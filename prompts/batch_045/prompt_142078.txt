I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and lately I have been writing about how research questions get chosen — who funds them, what makes a question look tractable to a committee, and how much of that is rhetoric rather than method. In that capacity I sit, as one of two non-academic members, on the program committee of a private foundation (I'll call it the Larkspur Foundation, which is not its name) that is about to make the largest single award in its history: roughly $45M over seven years to a consortium of economic historians and historical climatologists for a program whose working title asks why Britain and not China industrialised first. The proposal is impressive on data — new wage and price series, coal-transport cost estimates, paleoclimate reconstructions — and the science is not what I am worried about. We vote at the end of the month, and there are three live options: fund as proposed, decline, or fund with conditions that would force the consortium to restructure the first two years of its work. I have agreed to circulate a written opinion before the meeting, and I want it to take a position rather than summarise the discussion.

My unease is that the question in the title may not be the well-formed empirical question it appears to be. Philosophers of science distinguish asking why P from asking why P rather than Q; the second is contrastive, and what counts as an answer depends on the contrast class — on which Q you have smuggled in. This proposal inherits its framing from two places: the older question, associated with Joseph Needham, of why China, having led in science and technology for centuries, ceded that lead to the West; and the comparative literature of the last quarter-century, above all Kenneth Pomeranz's The Great Divergence, which deliberately compares England not to China but to the Yangzi Delta, and which pushes the decisive break forward to the nineteenth century, with access to coal and to New World resources doing much of the explanatory work. I read that literature as evidence for the worry rather than against it, because when you change the foil — England against the Yangzi Delta, Britain against the Netherlands, Europe against Asia — you get different explanations, and you get them because the foils differ rather than because the evidence changed. So in my notes I have been calling the minimal requirement this imposes the discrimination test: any proposed answer should have to name a foil and state what would have had to be different for the outcome to have gone the other way, and should then have to show that its favoured factors were absent, or present to a different degree, in that foil. A second, related worry is one of scale: Britain around 1800 was a state of some ten million people with an overseas empire and a captive export market, while Qing China was a continental empire of some three hundred million, and treating the two as comparable units of explanation may be like comparing a firm with an industry. One of the historians on the panel told us that asking why not China is simply asking what happened, dressed up in a rhetorical costume; I am fairly sure that is wrong, but I cannot say why, and I would like the memo to say it.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate: about 1,000 to 1,400 words, addressed to the committee, opening with the recommendation in a single sentence, followed by the reasoning, followed — if you recommend funding with conditions — by conditions stated concretely enough that the consortium could actually satisfy them and that foundation staff could verify compliance at the year-two review, including who checks what and what would count as failure. Two constraints matter to me. First, whichever option you recommend, give the strongest version of the option you are rejecting and explain why it still loses; in particular, engage the applicants' own defence, which is that a contrastive framing is precisely what makes a historical question answerable at all, since without a foil you have narration and nothing to test, and engage as well the board's worry that publicly deflating a flagship question looks less like methodological scruple than like political timidity, and that a foundation which cannot state its question in a headline will struggle to attract co-funders. Second, do not simply assert that the question is well posed: if you think it is, say what would have to be true of the contrast class for that to hold; if you think it needs restructuring, say what the restructured question actually is. Please keep citations and reading lists out of it — the committee will not chase references, and the argument has to stand on its own.
