I am a historical linguist, and I would like the cyclical-versus-linear debate about history translated into the vocabulary I actually work in, with a residue report.

My working vocabulary: the comparative method (cognate sets, regular correspondences, reconstruction, subgrouping); the Neogrammarian regularity hypothesis and the standard qualifications made to it; the family-tree and wave models of divergence and the ways they conflict; isoglosses, bundles, and dialect continua; the language-versus-dialect boundary problem; Sapir's notion of drift; grammaticalisation clines and the debate over unidirectionality; Jespersen's cycle of negation and the bleaching-and-renewal mechanism behind it; Meillet's way of describing that same pattern as a spiral rather than a circle; and my field's settled consensus that language change is neither progress nor decay, since no language is more complex or more expressive than another. Please assume full fluency in all of this and do not explain it back to me.

The positions to translate, one at a time: Polybius's anacyclosis, the rotation of regimes through their benign and corrupt forms and back to the beginning; Ibn Khaldun's account of group cohesion as strongest among peripheral or nomadic peoples, dissolving into factionalism and comfort within a few generations of taking power, so that dynasties have a natural span of roughly three generations; Toynbee's challenge-and-response mechanism, his creative minority decaying into a merely dominant one, and the passage of a civilisation through genesis, growth, breakdown and universal state (with, as I understand it, a more directional religious framing appearing in the later volumes); and Spengler's treatment of a culture as an organism with a fixed span and an incommensurable inner unity, such that its phases are stages of a single life and not points on a shared scale.

Two suspicions of my own that I want tested rather than indulged. First, I do not think these four are describing the same variable at all: one seems to cycle a form of government, one a property of the ruling group, one a scale or degree of institutional differentiation, one something like an expressive style. In my vocabulary these would be distinct state variables with distinct dynamics and distinct timescales, and if that is right then a good part of the cyclical-versus-linear dispute is not disagreement but talk at cross-purposes. Tell me whether this disentangling is forced by the texts or merely imposed by my framework. Second, my own field's canonical cycle is a renewal cycle: a form is worn down by use, reinforced with fresh material, and the fresh material is then worn down in turn, so the system returns to a structurally similar position without ever returning to the same position. I suspect cyclical-versus-linear is a false dichotomy in exactly this way, and that the real question is what the recurrence is a recurrence *of*. Test that against each of the four accounts.

Then the residue, which is the part I actually want. I would like three things adjudicated in particular. (a) The unit problem: regular sound change holds within a community defined by continuous transmission, apparent exceptions get diagnosed as borrowing or analogy, and the tree is drawn afterwards from the correspondences that the regularity assumption allowed you to see in the first place. Is the bounding of a civilisation circular in the same way, and what happens to the comparative use of many civilisations once one applies the areal-diffusion worry that cross-cultural anthropologists call Galton's problem, given that civilisations are not independent samples and parallel stages may be contagion rather than recurrence? (b) Commensurability: my vocabulary works with parameters shared across languages and cannot say that two units have no common parameterisation while remaining comparable. Is Spengler's incommensurability claim therefore untranslatable noise, or is it a denial of a precondition my framework needs and cannot defend? (c) Decline: my discipline spent a century expelling decline from its vocabulary. When you translate Spengler or Toynbee, does decline come out as a deflationary surrogate such as structural reduction or loss of differentiation, which they would repudiate, and is my framework's inability to express decline a refutation of them or a limitation of mine?

Be explicit wherever you had to fudge something to make it fit, and do not pass off an assumption of my framework as a translation. If I have attributed to one of these figures something they did not quite say, correct it in a clause and carry on. No reading lists, no citations, no intellectual history beyond what the translation needs. Finish by saying whether the residue reflects badly on the historiographers or on the vocabulary I asked you to use.
