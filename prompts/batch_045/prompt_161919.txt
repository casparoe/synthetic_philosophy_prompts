I have no training in philosophy and have been working through a popular book on philosophy of mind on my own, and I've reached a chapter I can't tell whether to trust. It summarizes a view it attributes to the philosopher Daniel Dennett, about "real patterns" and about three "stances" one can take toward anything whose behavior you want to predict: the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance. Below is my paraphrase of what the chapter seems to be claiming, followed by a conclusion the chapter draws from it, and then two cases from my own life that made me suspicious of the chapter. I'd be grateful for an accuracy audit of it.

My paraphrase of the chapter:

1. The three stances are competing accounts of the same events. The physical stance predicts from a thing's physical constitution and the laws of nature; the design stance predicts from what the thing was designed to do; the intentional stance treats the thing as a rational agent and predicts that it will do what it has most reason to do, given what it believes and wants.
2. Because they compete, the physical stance is the true one and the other two are shortcuts we resort to only because doing the physics is beyond us. Every intentional explanation is therefore provisional: a placeholder we will eventually replace with a real mechanism.
3. The design stance only applies where there was an actual designer. You can ask "what is this for?" only of things that were made on purpose, so the design stance tells us nothing about animals, organs, or ecosystems, which only look designed.
4. A pattern is "real" when there is a compact way of describing some stretch of the world — a formula or code — cheaper than spelling that stretch out detail by detail; the pattern is whatever that shortcut captures. The corollary the chapter draws is that a regularity no one has yet described isn't a real pattern yet, and that if you can find some way of compressing a batch of data — which you always can, with enough ingenuity — you have shown a real pattern is there.
5. Because the intentional stance is only an instrument for predicting, nothing strictly speaking believes or desires anything; talk of beliefs is a manner of speaking that works on creatures like us.

The chapter then concludes: no machine can ever literally believe, know, or understand anything, so the current fuss about whether AI systems "really" understand what they say is an empty question, and the answer is simply no.

Two things make me doubt this. First, the chapter keeps presenting the issue as a forced choice between "really out there in the world" and "merely projected by us," and I had the impression the whole point of the view was to reject that choice rather than to pick the second option. Second, the cases:

- I keep bees. When a colony swarms, beekeepers say things like "the colony decided the old queen was failing and chose to raise a replacement." One friend says that is literally true of the colony as a whole; another says insects don't decide anything and this is just loose talk. On the corrected version of the view, is there a fact of the matter here, and what would settle it?
- I play chess against an app on my phone. It sacrificed a knight in a way that looks exactly like it expected me to grab it, and my brother says it doesn't believe or expect anything, it just evaluates positions. But if the chapter is right about point 5, the same would seem to be true of my brother.
