So I'm defending a dissertation chapter in three weeks and I need Q&A prep, not another draft of the argument. Here's where I am.

Thesis: generational cohorts (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, whatever) are real social kinds, but not because the people in them share values or attitudes. They're what I've started calling infrastructural cohorts: what fixes which generation you belong to is which recording-and-replay technology was ambient during your late-adolescent world-formation window. The threshold that does the most work in my story is the "rewind point" — the moment ordinary life becomes routinely recordable and replayable, first audio, then video, then searchable on demand. So what a cohort shares is not a set of beliefs, it's a shared structure of what can be re-experienced: whether your adolescence is available to you as something you can watch again, quote exactly, search, and show other people. I claim most of the work that "generation" does in political opinion research runs through that, not through values.

The identification move is the part I'm proudest of and most scared of. I don't estimate the cohort boundaries; I date them externally, from when each infrastructure change actually arrived in a given country, and then I use the cross-country variation in arrival dates to break the age-period-cohort collinearity: a person who was 17 in 1988 in country A and a person who was 17 in 1997 in country B are on opposite sides of the threshold while sharing neither birth year nor calendar period. I also lean on a pattern I call onset-locking — if the mechanism is what I say, effects should track how old you were when you first crossed the threshold, discontinuously at the boundary, and shouldn't drift smoothly with period.

Who's in the room: two quantitative political scientists who do demographic decomposition and think cohort effects are mostly a modeling artifact; a life-course sociologist who thinks my whole "are they real kinds" framing is malformed and wants to talk about accumulated exposure instead; a survey methodologist who has already told me twice that you cannot compare a 1978 Likert item to a 2021 one; a philosopher of social science (the external) who is basically on my side but will push on whether a kind whose edges are set by a technology-adoption curve can be the same kind in two different countries; and a handful of grad students who work on non-Western cases and will be sitting there thinking that "Gen Z" isn't even a category where they work.
