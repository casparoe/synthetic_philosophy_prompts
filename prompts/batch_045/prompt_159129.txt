I am drafting a short essay arguing that the Fermi paradox has been systematically overstated, and I would like you to stress-test the argument before I commit to it. Please do not give me a survey of the candidate solutions — zoo hypotheses, rare earth, great filters, and the rest. I have read them and I am not writing about them. What I want is to be told where my own argument fails, precisely enough that I can judge whether the failure is repairable.

The setup. Let R be the mean rate at which technologically capable civilizations arise somewhere in the Milky Way, and L the mean length of time one remains electromagnetically conspicuous, so that the expected number present at any instant is the product N = R·L. My central claim is that discussions of the paradox spend all their effort on R and none on L, and that L has a hard physical ceiling. Call it the radiance window. A civilization is visible by leakage only while it is actively radiating, and an industrial economy runs on a finite stock of accessible low-entropy resources; the interval between the onset of radio technology and either collapse or a settled steady state that stops radiating is, I argue, at most a few centuries. Take L of order ten to the two years and R of order ten to the minus five per year — a middling, uncontroversial rate, I think — and N comes out of order ten to the minus three. The galaxy is then empty at essentially every moment even though it manufactures civilizations fairly often.

The argument has three parts.

First, and least interesting: since N is the number of civilizations contemporaneous with us, and N is far below one, the silence is what the numbers predict rather than something standing in need of an explanation.

Second, the objection I expect, and my answer to it. Over ten billion years of habitable history, a rate of ten to the minus five per year implies something like a hundred thousand civilizations in all, and one might ask why not one of them has left anything we can see. My answer is that a dead civilization is dark: leakage ceases when the transmitters do, and whatever was emitted a hundred million years ago has long since passed us. Detection requires that some other civilization's radiance window overlap our listening window — a few centuries out of ten billion years, a coincidence of order ten to the minus eight. Multiplied by a hundred thousand civilizations, that leaves an expected ten to the minus three detectable neighbours. This is the part of the argument I take to be new. The paradox dissolves not because civilizations are rare but because the windows almost never line up, and the cumulative number is a red herring because the dead leave nothing to detect.

Third, I want to add that even this small N is generous. The astronomical factors in the usual product are by now the best-constrained ones, so nearly all the uncertainty sits with the biological ones, and our own existence tells us nothing about them, since we could only ever find ourselves on a planet where the sequence ran to completion. The honest conclusion is therefore not that some filter has suppressed intelligence, but that we have no reason to expect the galaxy to be occupied at any particular time.

Three places where I want you to push.

One. I computed N by multiplying central estimates. A colleague objected that the numbers usually substituted into a product of this kind are central estimates on a logarithmic scale rather than expectations; that for a quantity uncertain over many orders of magnitude the mean can exceed the median by a large factor; and hence that the mean of the product can sit far above the product of the values I have used. I do not see why this matters here, since every combination of values I find defensible puts N below one, and the objection strikes me as a technicality about the tail. Is there a version of it that actually bites, or is my colleague wrong?

Two. Is the step from an expected number well below one to a probability of under one percent for a contemporary civilization licensed? And is the radiance-window framing genuinely contributing something, or is it, as another colleague put it, just N restated in different words? I cannot see how it could be, because it reaches the same number by a route that looks independent of it.

Three. I intend to close on the claim that the silence carries almost no information about how often intelligence arises, because it is dominated by an accident of timing. But I notice that this move would equally explain away the silence if N were a million, which makes me suspicious of it. I cannot locate the error, and I would rather find it now than in print.

If some part of this survives, tell me which part and what its strongest form is. If the whole argument quietly turns into one of the standard solutions, tell me which one, and at exactly which step the turn happens.
