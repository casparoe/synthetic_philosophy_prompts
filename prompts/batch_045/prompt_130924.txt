I chair the diagnostic-protocol committee at a community mental health centre. We revise the adult intake assessment every two years, and the current revision will govern roughly forty-five new assessments a week, carried out by nurses and master's-level clinicians in a forty-five-minute slot, with sign-off from the clinical director and one patient representative. I want a procedure my committee can run on each candidate criterion before voting it in — something usable in a two-hour meeting by people with no training in philosophy of science.

The problem I want operationalized is Ian Hacking's looping effects: our patients belong to the kinds we classify, many arrive having already read the criteria online, and the criteria themselves alter what patients notice and report about themselves, what their families notice, and what our own clinicians elicit and code. So a criterion can come to select for something different from what it was written to select for, without anyone making an error.

Please give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops with a redesign request rather than a yes. I have in mind distinctions I can't quite sharpen on my own: whether a criterion is transparent enough that a motivated person could satisfy it better after reading it with no change in the underlying condition; whether there exists any evidence source not exposed to the criterion's wording; what behaviour the criterion rewards once patients know it; and how we would detect, at the next revision, that its meaning has drifted. Tell me which of these distinctions are essential and which I can drop for our setting, and mark the points where the question stops being technical and becomes a judgement about values — for instance how much opacity toward patients we are willing to buy, and who pays for it.

Then run the procedure on three criteria from our draft: a self-report symptom count for adult ADHD, completed in the waiting room before the interview; the generic gate requiring clinically significant distress or functional impairment; and a criterion that asks the clinician to judge whether a patient's preoccupation with illness is disproportionate to the medical findings. I expect at least one of these to come out badly, and I want that stated plainly rather than softened. Finally, say what the procedure cannot tell us: in particular, whether a looping change is a distortion or an improvement, and whether the kind itself is real.
