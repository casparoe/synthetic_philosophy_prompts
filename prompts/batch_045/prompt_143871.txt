You are a professional philosopher of religion with a working command of empirical moral psychology and of the quantitative social science of prosociality. You have been retained as the scribe for the closing session of a two-year adversarial collaboration, and your job is not to adjudicate it. Your job is to draft the joint statement that both parties can sign.

The two parties, stated in the terms each would choose for itself:

Camp M (moralizing-monitoring, or indispensability). Large-scale cooperation among genetic strangers is not explicable by kinship, reciprocity, or reputation alone; what made it stable was the cultural spread of beliefs in morally concerned supernatural agents who observe invisible conduct and punish it, together with the costly rituals that make such beliefs credible. Secular societies have not refuted this; they are living off inherited moral capital, and the question is only how fast that capital depreciates.

Camp I (institutional-substitution). What did the causal work was never the metaphysics but the monitoring, the credible sanction, the costly signalling, and the dense community enforcement that religious practice happened to bundle. Those functions are substitutable: third-party arbitration, contract law, auditing, welfare-state provisioning, and reputational infrastructure can perform them, and in the places where they do, the observable markers of prosociality do not decay. The apparent effect of religiosity is largely confounding by community embeddedness, by cultural inheritance, and by self-presentation in self-report.

Before drafting: in your thinking, assemble a candidate list of agreed claims and grade each one (a) both camps would sign it and it is not trivial, (b) both would sign it but it is vacuous, (c) only one camp would sign it. Draft from (a) alone. Tell me how many (b)-grade claims you discarded and what made them vacuous, since the temptation to pad a joint statement with platitudes is the main failure mode of this genre.

Please stipulate the following as background, so that nothing in the document rests on a citation: assume the parties accept a stylized empirical picture in which religiosity tracks several measures of prosocial conduct across national samples, in which the tracking is stronger for self-report and for low-cost observable behaviour than for costly anonymous behaviour, and in which observed religiosity is heavily confounded with community embeddedness and cultural inheritance. Treat that as a stipulation for the purposes of drafting, not as a finding.

Two devices that the parties provisionally adopted and that I want you to use:

The first is a decomposition of "religion" into four separable components: belief in moralizing supernatural agents; costly ritual participation; embeddedness in a dense, monitoring community; and explicit moral instruction. Both camps agree that "religion makes people better" is ill-posed until one says which of these is doing the work, and they agree to disagree about that.

The second is a set of three operational metrics for "more moral", which I want used throughout in place of the undefined comparative: the low-cost anonymity gradient (how far prosocial conduct falls between a weakly observed condition and a strongly anonymous one); the motivation share (the fraction of prosocial acts attributable to other-regarding concern rather than to sanction-avoidance or reputational management); and the circle radius (how far the conduct extends towards out-group strangers). Much of the public quarrel, I suspect, is a quarrel about which metric is the right one, but not all of it: say which part is verbal and why, and say whether the residual disagreement is better described as a trade-off across metrics (one camp weighting circle radius, the other weighting motivation share) than as a disagreement about a single quantity.

Deliver the document in five sections.

Section A: numbered agreed claims, each non-vacuous, each in wording both camps would accept.

Section B: a disagreements table, with columns for the disputed proposition, the formulation Camp M would endorse, the formulation Camp I would endorse, and your own classification of the disagreement as substantive, verbal, or partly each -- with one line of justification.

Section C: the cruxes. For each crux, give the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp, a jointly acceptable test or case, and each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Use at least these four:

1. Component crux. The parties have a matched-inheritance natural experiment: two cohorts sharing ancestry, language, and a common religious heritage at the start of the period, which diverge in that one retains the full ritual calendar and community practice while dropping belief in the monitoring agents, and the other drops practice while retaining private belief. What does each camp predict about the anonymity gradient in each cohort after one generation, and about the ordering between the two?

2. Amortization crux. If Camp M is right, inherited moral capital should decay on a schedule; if Camp I is right, it should not decay at all once institutions have substituted. Extract a dated prediction from each camp: how many generations post-secularization, and what magnitude of change in which metric, before each would regard its own view as in trouble. Then tell me honestly whether either prediction is falsifiable in practice, given confounding and the timescales involved.

3. Substitution crux. There exists a mid-sized jurisdiction which, over roughly two decades, introduced near-universal low-cost third-party arbitration with reliable enforcement and a dense reputational registry, without any religious revival. What change in the anonymity gradient would Camp I count as vindication, what result would Camp M count as vindication, and is there a threshold both would accept in advance as decisive?

4. The transfer crux. Laboratory religious priming produces measurable short-term effects on anonymous giving. State both camps' reasons for discounting the field relevance of this, and then tell me whether this is a genuine crux at all or a shared assumption being mistaken for a disagreement.

Section D: what each camp counts as evidence against its own view, written so that camp would accept it, followed by anything both agree would genuinely move the debate.

Section E: a short note from you, marked as your own judgment, separating what is settled among people who know this topic from what is genuinely contested, and flagging any place where the framing I have handed you -- the four components, the three metrics, or the way I have characterized either camp -- is itself slanted in one camp's favour. Do not present a contested claim as consensus, and where you are unsure whether specialists would treat a claim as settled, say so rather than settling it quietly. Take your time and think carefully before writing; the value here is entirely in the exactness of the formulations.
