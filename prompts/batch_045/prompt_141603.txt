ok so – my friend Dave and I got completely stuck last night arguing about whether an ant colony could have a mind of its own, and I need something more structured than us shouting "its just a distributed algorithm" at each other. We were on the Aunt Hillary thing from Hofstadter: a colony you can actually talk to, whose "symbols" are ants and whose thinking is the shuffling of them around. He says the whole idea is obviously silly, I said "obviously silly" is doing all the work. Before I go read another paper about it, I want the spec.

So: what does a theory of colony-level mentality have to deliver to be worth taking seriously? Not "does it happen" – the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each one. Here's what I think it has to handle, but you fix the list and add what I'm missing:

- The census test. Suppose you have a complete real-time readout of every ant's internal state, every chemical gradient, every trail, every pile of debris and every tunnel in the nest. Nothing about the colony is hidden from you. Is that enough to fix what the colony believes and wants, or is there something colony-level left over? Whichever it says, the theory should say why, and should say whether that answer makes Aunt Hillary's mind a discovery or a bookkeeping convenience – and it can't just collapse into "well, the intentional stance is useful here."
- Quorum decisions. Rock-ant house hunting: scouts visit candidate nest sites, and the colony commits when enough of them are gathered at one site. Is a threshold rule a case of weighing evidence, or just a switch? What would the theory need to see to count it as the former?
- Stigmergy. The nest structure itself carries information – tunnels, middens, trail geometry. Is the architecture part of the colony's mind or part of its environment? I want a principled line, not a shrug, and I want to know what happens to a theory that draws the line generously (does a termite mound with the termites removed become a mind? a mind's memory?)
- Colony-level error. A trail to a food source persists after the food is gone and the ants keep walking it. Is that a false belief, or just inertia? The theory owes an account of colony misrepresentation or an argument that error isn't required for content.
- Fission, fusion and spread. A colony buds in two; a colony spread across several nests stops exchanging workers between them; two colonies forced together start operating as one. Give persistence and identity conditions, or argue the theory doesn't need them. Which half is the original Aunt Hillary, and does the answer track whether there's a mind there at all?
- Grain and timescale. Colony-level "decisions" take hours or days; the ant-level events that compose them take seconds. Does having a mind require processing in some bandwidth, or is rate irrelevant? If it matters, name the range and defend it.
- No bottleneck. No ant has the whole representation and no ant is in charge. Is that a defeater – the old "a whole population implementing a program" worry – or is it also true of brains? What does the theory have to add so that this isn't just assertion?
- Parts with their own interests. Ants reproduce, workers lay eggs and get policed, and there's conflict over sex ratios and over who gets to breed. Neurons don't have their own evolutionary goods. Does that asymmetry matter, and if the theory says no, what's the argument?
- Hijacking. A parasite – a socially parasitic queen, or one of those fungi that makes workers climb and clamp down somewhere convenient for the fungus – takes over the colony's behaviour and runs it for another organism's ends. Can a mind be hijacked like that, and does a theory that allows it also allow that it was never a mind in the first place?

Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp (consciousness-required vs intentionality-is-enough; decodable-from-individual-ants vs multiply-realizable-at-colony-level). Flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied – I have a feeling something like "sharp identity conditions" plus "the boundaries of a colony aren't conventional" is one, and maybe "no central bottleneck needed" plus "must not count a traffic jam or a stock market as a mind" is another, but you tell me. Then, in a couple of lines: which single requirement excludes the most candidate theories, and how the three obvious ones – superorganism/extended-functionalist, "it's just an aggregate, nothing there", and the pure-stance view – do against the parts of the list you think matter.
