A note on where I'm coming from: I have no academic background in philosophy. I trained as an electronics technician and have spent the last decade or so reading ethics on my own in the evenings, and I've ended up holding a strong version of suffering-focused ethics. I already know the standard objections people raise against it, and I've more or less made my peace with the ones I find cheap. What I want from you is a map of what the view actually risks: which findings, arguments, or cases would count against it, and how badly.

The position I hold has three parts.

First, the asymmetry in its strict form: suffering is the only thing that generates moral reasons. Creating happiness, preserving happiness, satisfying preferences — none of these is even a weak reason that usually gets outweighed. They are not reasons. Reasons run in one direction.

Second, what I call the eliminability thesis: no good essentially depends on suffering. For every good that looks like it is built out of suffering or is constitutively a response to it — being comforted, resilience, solidarity between people who are badly off together, the specific closeness of being cared for while you're in pain, the felt significance of having got through something — I claim there is a counterpart good available to a being who has never suffered. My test for this is a procedure I use on myself, which I'll call the substitution probe: take the good, strip out the unpleasant valenced part, keep whatever functional role that part was playing (the information it carried, the motivation it supplied, the attention it directed, the behaviour it produced in other people), and ask whether what remains is still recognisably the same good. My view predicts the answer is always yes; the suffering was never load-bearing.

Third, no offset and no threshold: no quantity of happiness compensates for any quantity of suffering, and there is no level below which further reduction stops mattering. I accept the consequences people find repugnant — that the view gives no reason at all to bring happy beings into existence, that the extinction of a contented species would not in itself be a loss, and that a world of permanently contented, undisappointed, unambitious minds is not worse in any respect for being that way.

Two things I take the view to be committed to beyond that, which I think of as its exposed surface.

The prediction. If we ever had a direct and reliable readout of valenced experience — call it a hedonimeter so we have a name for it — my view says the readout wins whenever it disagrees with what people say about their own lives or with what their choices reveal. More than that, it predicts the disagreements will be systematic and large: self-reports and revealed preferences will look mostly fine, while the readout shows a great deal of low-grade, habituated negative valence in lives that are described as happy, because the reporting mechanisms adapt and the experience does not. Competing views that give weight to happiness or to preference satisfaction have no particular reason to expect a gap of that shape or size.

The empirical hypothesis that would hurt. Call it the contrast law: suppose we discovered that the intensity of any positive state is set by its contrast with recent negative states, so that a mind with no history of suffering has a permanently low ceiling on positive experience. I don't believe this is true, but if it were, the eliminability thesis is false in a way I'd have to take seriously — some of the goods I'd be destroying really would be downstream of suffering, and my claim that nothing is lost would be a claim about a currency I've already said doesn't count. Relatedly, suppose the levers turned out to be a single dial: no intervention reduces suffering without also raising happiness by the same mechanism, and vice versa. Then my view would agree with classical utilitarianism about every real decision and would cease to be a distinct position in practice. I don't know whether to count that as fatal or merely deflating.

There is also a line of argument I half-understand and want pressed rather than repeated: that even at 99% confidence that only suffering matters, any nonzero probability that happiness matters, combined with stakes as large as the future seems to be, restores happiness promotion as the dominant consideration. My current answer is that I reject reasoning of that fanatical shape. I want to know whether rejecting it costs me more than it costs the utilitarian who invokes it, or whether the objection is symmetric and therefore not much of an objection.

So, the questions. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate what would genuinely count against the view from what is merely said against it. Say which of them a committed defender would concede as counting against them, and which they'd dismiss as question-begging. Tell me where I have insulated myself: have I defined suffering so broadly — every frustrated goal-state in any goal-directed system, say — that the view is either vacuous or much stranger than I think it is, and have I protected the substitution probe by stipulating in advance that any good that fails it wasn't really valuable? And then say plainly whether the insulation is a problem, given that this is supposed to be a thesis about what matters rather than a framework for interpreting whatever happens.

For what it's worth: I would be moved most by a substitution-probe failure for the good of standing by someone who is suffering, since that's a good I value independently of any theory, and by a demonstration that the view collapses into the utilitarianism I rejected. I would be moved least by appeals to the meaninglessness of a suffering-free world.

Please reason from the structure of the position and from cases; don't give me reading lists, citations, or a bibliography, and don't rely on my having characterised any school of thought correctly. Where the view is genuinely silent, say so rather than making it speak.
