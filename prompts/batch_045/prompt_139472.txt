Fifteen years into the debate about whether violence has declined, the two camps in my field have stopped exchanging arguments and started exchanging statistics: one side produces per-capita homicide and battle-death series running across centuries, the other produces absolute totals, twentieth-century body counts, the categories those series omit (domestic and sexual violence, deaths in colonial administration and famine, harm that is structural rather than interpersonal), and the tail risk of nuclear or engineered catastrophe; and nobody has moved. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My hypothesis is that this is three disagreements stacked under a single question. First: is there a fact of the matter about how violent a period was, or only a family of quantities we choose among — deaths per person-year, deaths per lifetime, absolute deaths, risk per hour of exposure — where the choice is settled by something like population ethics rather than by data? Note that as lifespans lengthen, a flat annual rate means a rising lifetime risk, so the two can move in opposite directions, and note too that both the numerator and the denominator drift with what an era is willing to record as violence at all. Second: is the quarrel over which harms belong under the word merely verbal, or does it show that the concept has no core? Third, the one I care most about: once the series are agreed on, catastrophic risks dominate any expected-deaths calculation while admitting no frequency estimate, so the two sides may simply be applying different standards of evidence under deep uncertainty. Say which of these, if any, is doing the work, and say what would count as progress — a distinction, a stipulated quantity, an agreed evidentiary standard for tail risks — or argue that no progress is to be expected and why.
