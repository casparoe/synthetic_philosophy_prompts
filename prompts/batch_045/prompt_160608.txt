I teach a graduate seminar on method in the study of the historical Jesus, and I want to close the unit on the criteria of authenticity with something better than another face-off between two readings of the same evidence. Please draft the joint statement that two scholars could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. One is a criteria-optimist: she holds that the standard instruments — dissimilarity (including its doubled form), embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, and the language-and-environment criteria (Aramaic or Semitic phenomena in the Greek, Palestinian realia, first-century Galilean plausibility) — can, when used with discipline and in combination, settle a good deal about what Jesus said, did, and intended, and that abandoning them leaves nothing but unconstrained intuition about the tradition. The other is a criteria-sceptic: he holds that these instruments are, at best, defeasible rules of thumb capable of excluding a tradition but not of establishing one; that they are not independent of one another, because multiple attestation presupposes source criticism which already presupposes judgements about which traditions are old; that dissimilarity is guaranteed to return a figure unlike either his Jewish environment or the movement that preserved him, so that even a criterion that never erred would yield an unrepresentative result; and that the appearance of rigour comes from applying several mutually dependent heuristics and treating the convergence as confirmation. Assume both know the material perfectly well and have simply been arguing past each other for years.

The document should begin with a numbered section of agreed claims — claims about what the sources are and how they reached us, about what would count as a successful inference to the best explanation here, and about what nobody seriously claims (for instance, that the tradition is transparent, or that we can recover Jesus' self-understanding in the way a modern biographer would want). State the residual disagreements in a table, each row being one crux, with the optimist's own formulation of her position in wording she would accept, the sceptic's in wording he would accept, the argument or test both agree would bear on that crux, and each side's prediction about it written down in advance. Among the cruxes I expect to see: whether the criteria are logically and probabilistically independent; whether the appropriate unit of assessment is the individual pericope or a broader narrative profile, with the criteria applied holistically; whether "authenticity" should give way to something like historical plausibility within a reconstructed Jewish context; whether the criteria can be calibrated at all — that is, whether one could run them over a body of sayings-tradition whose provenance is independently known, such as attributions to a later teacher or to a modern founder preserved through oral transmission, and measure a recovery rate, and what result each side would predict; and whether the thinness of the output is a defect of method or the correct epistemic posture toward this material. Where a crux is empirical, name the finding that would in principle settle it; where it is methodological, name the argument. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, mark it as such and say what either party would have to do to make it bite. Include, finally, a short closing section in which each side states what would make it abandon its own position.

Two constraints on how you write it. First, the statement should be self-contained and usable by a reader with no library: no citations, no bibliographies, no appeals to particular books, articles, or recent scholarship, and where a claim would depend on a contested textual or archaeological detail, flag it as depending on that detail rather than asserting it. Second, it should be written in a voice that gives nothing away: a reader who did not know should not be able to tell which of the two drafted it, and the agreed-claims section should contain nothing either side would regard as a concession smuggled in by the other.
