I'm in my last year of high school and I've been working through the old Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies. There's no philosophy at my school, so I've been doing it on my own, and I've ended up with a version of the argument that I think is valid and whose conclusion I can't accept. I'd be grateful if someone would tell me which step is broken, why that step looks fine when it isn't, and what each of the alternatives costs.

Here is the argument, as I've written it out.

(1) For something to be bad for a person, there has to be a time at which they are worse off because of it. Harm needs a subject, and a subject needs a time.

(2) When a person is dead, there is no time at which they exist. So there is no time at which their being dead is bad for them, and the state of being dead is not bad for them.

(3) The same holds for the stretch before they existed: no subject, no time at which they are worse off. And in that direction the conclusion seems right to everyone. Nobody lies awake troubled by the eternity before their birth.

(4) The two cases come out the same for the same reason, not by coincidence. Whether an interval during which you do not exist is bad for you depends only on what is in that interval, and there is nothing in it: no experiences, no subject, no feature that could be good or bad for anyone. Call this the Interval Principle. Steps (2) and (3) are two instances of it.

(5) So the interval after your death is not bad for you. Death does not harm the person who dies.

(6) If the interval itself is not bad for you, then neither is its length. Seventy years of posthumous nonexistence cannot be worse than one year of it, since a longer stretch is only more of something that is not bad.

(7) So dying at seventeen is not worse for you than dying at eighty-seven. The decades you would otherwise have had are not something you lose, because there is no time at which you lack them.

(8) So you have no reason of your own to avoid death: not to wear a seatbelt, not to take the medicine, not to step out of the road. The only self-regarding reasons left are to avoid dying painfully or slowly, and to avoid the distress of anticipating it. And nobody is harmed by being killed, so long as it is quick and painless.

(9) A further consideration in the argument's favour: if posthumous nonexistence were bad for you, then more of it would be worse, and since it has no end it would be infinitely bad, which is absurd. So it is not bad at all.

I cannot accept (8). When a seventeen-year-old is killed in a crash, my sense is that something has gone badly for her, and not merely for the people who loved her, and that it has gone worse for her than it would have gone for her grandmother who died at ninety. That sense is stronger in me than any of the premises.

But I also built a case that seems to break the argument somewhere in the first three steps, and I can't tell which step it breaks. Suppose a seventeen-year-old is put into total suspended animation: painless, with no experiences at all for the whole fifty years, nothing that it is like for her during that interval. She closes her eyes and the next thing she knows it is fifty years later, she is sixty-seven, the people she loved are old or dead, and the life she was going to have has been used up. My intuition is very loud that this is a disaster for her, that she has lost fifty years, even though during the interval there is no subject and no time at which she is worse off — exactly the situation steps (1) and (2) describe. So either the suspended animation is not bad for her, which I don't believe, or steps (1) and (2) don't by themselves get you to (5), which I can't square with step (4).

I've tried three escapes and none of them work for me. Denying (4) looks like refusing the question, because (4) reads to me as a generalisation of (2) and (3) rather than an extra assumption smuggled in. Denying (6) is what a friend recommends: he says that adding a stretch of nothing is not the same as taking away a stretch of life. I don't see the difference. It is the same stretch of years, described from one end or the other. And I can't see how to deny (7) or (8) while keeping (5), since (7) looks like a restatement of (5) applied to a longer interval.

If it matters for the diagnosis: the claims I would most like to keep are (1) and (2), which seem to me to be the actual content of the Epicurean position, and I would give up (9) before anything earlier, since (9) was only ever support and not part of the main chain. If I have to choose between (5) and (8), I keep (8) and drop (5).

So: which step should I reject, and how? What makes that step look sound when it isn't? And what do I have to pay if I reject it — is there a way to hold that death harms the person who dies without being able to say when it harms them, and is there a way to treat the time before birth as harmless and the time after death as not, without that being special pleading?
