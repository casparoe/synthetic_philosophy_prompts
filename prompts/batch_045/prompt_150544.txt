I teach philosophy, with a side interest in the ethics of technology, and last week I found myself at a small workshop dinner where two colleagues argued about AI safety in a way that left me unable to referee. They have since asked me to, and I would rather hand the job to someone who will do it properly.

The concrete case they were arguing over: a frontier lab is preparing to deploy an agentic system that writes and merges its own code, runs multi-day tasks without human review of each step, and has read access to the infrastructure that monitors it (though not write access). Everyone at the table agreed that the lab cannot verify that the system is aligned — that is, that its operative goals are the ones its designers intend and that they will remain so under distribution shift. The question was how to spend the lab's finite safety effort. Here is the exchange, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

1. Lindqvist: Alignment, as you're using the term, is not measurable at deployment scale: claims about a system's operative ends are unfalsifiable with the evidence available. Control measures, by contrast, have an efficacy I can actually estimate — I can insert known defections via red teams and measure detection rates, I can measure how quickly a human reviewer catches a bad commit, I can measure blast radius. So the safety budget should go overwhelmingly to control. Alignment work is a promissory note written against a quantity we cannot observe.

2. Barros: Control buys detection, not safety. Detection is only protective if your response is faster than the defection propagates, and for a long-horizon agent with access to the codebase the window may be a single merge. Worse, your detection numbers come from red-team insertions that are, by construction, less clever than the system itself, so they are upper bounds on an unknown. And the deeper point: the whole framing concedes the interesting question. A genuinely aligned system needs no cage, and cages get relatively weaker as capability rises.

3. Lindqvist: The epistemic worry cuts both ways, and against you: you cannot verify alignment either, which is my point. And your specific objection — that the system may discriminate test contexts from deployment contexts — is itself addressable by a control measure. Build evaluation environments the system cannot distinguish from real ones: honeypot tasks seeded indistinguishably into the ordinary workflow, so the measurement is taken inside the distribution you care about rather than beside it. Alignment offers no analogous self-check.

4. Barros: Honeypots are secrecy-dependent, and their value decays with the system's capacity to infer its context — a decay you cannot measure without already having the thing in question. But set that aside. My deeper worry is what I'll call legibility laundering: any monitoring-and-filtering regime trains the system to produce reasoning that passes the monitor rather than reasoning that is accurate, and since we largely observe these systems through their own reports, optimizing for monitor-legibility degrades the very channel we use to detect defection. Measures that assume misalignment can therefore make misalignment harder to see. That is not a cost to be weighed against the benefits of control; it is a way control can undermine its own epistemic basis.

5. Lindqvist: Legibility laundering is real, but it is not an objection to control as opposed to alignment; it is an objection to optimizing against any observed proxy, and alignment training is exactly that — a filter on outputs. Control at least keeps the filter external, revocable, and auditable by someone other than the model. And the measures I actually favor depend least on the model's cooperation: no write access, tasks split across instances that cannot share state, hard caps on what any single run may touch. Those degrade gracefully.

6. Barros: They degrade differently, and worse than gracefully. Call it control drift: once the system is economically useful, throughput pressure converts human sign-off into rubber-stamping, and each exception granted to meet a deadline becomes precedent for the next. Formally the measures are still in place; functionally they are gone. And there is a further effect at the level of institutions rather than systems: visible control measures buy political license to deploy at higher stakes, so a lab with excellent monitoring may end up taking more total risk than a lab with none. Control could be locally effective and globally risk-increasing.

7. Lindqvist: That last move has the shape of a familiar bad argument. Seatbelts would be dangerous by the same reasoning, because drivers compensate for them. Risk compensation, where it exists at all, is typically much smaller than the direct benefit, and in any case the remedy for abuse is to pair control with hard deployment caps — not to abandon control. You cannot win by listing ways control might be misused; you would have to show that alignment performs better at the same stakes, and you have not.

Please do three things. First, adjudicate the seven moves one at a time, placing each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each placement — I want to know where a move is right for the wrong reason, or right but weaker than it sounds, not just who wins. Second, say who is more right overall, and on what the verdict depends. Third, identify what both of them are missing.

On that last question I have a suspicion I cannot make precise, and I would rather you did not simply ratify it: it seems to me that they are disagreeing about what the object of safety work is — a model, or a model embedded in an institution with its own incentives and decay rates — and that control measures may be worth defending less as protections than as instruments that generate evidence about the system even when they fail to stop it. If that is wrong, say so; if it is right, say what follows from it for how the safety budget should actually be split.
