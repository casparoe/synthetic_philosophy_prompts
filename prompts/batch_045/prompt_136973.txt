I serve on the university's Committee on High-Consequence Model Release, a body the provost created last year after a dispute over a different lab's upload of a biological design tool. It has seven members: a dean, a computer scientist, the university counsel, a molecular biologist, and three faculty, of whom I am one and the only philosopher. It reviews requests from campus labs to publish model weights or to deploy models at scale, and it votes in nine days. I have been asked to draft the decision memo, and because the committee is split and the other six are not going to re-derive the reasoning themselves, I want that draft to be honest rather than merely persuasive.

The request before us: the Autonomous Systems Group wants to publish the weights of Vireo-14B, a code-writing agent fine-tuned for long-horizon software tasks, multi-hour, tool-using, able to write and run its own test suites. Their argument is that the hosted API the committee would otherwise permit cannot be independently audited: external researchers cannot inspect the safety fine-tune, cannot test whether it was trained to conceal capabilities, and cannot reproduce the lab's internal evaluations. They add that our charter directs us to refuse release wherever there exists a credible path from the artifact to irreversible harm, and that under such a framing no release of any capable system can ever be approved, since a path can always be sketched; the charter, they say, is not a standard but a veto, and we should reason in expectation instead.

The evidence: an internal red team produced roughly thirty-two hundred fine-tuned variants of the base model with the safety training removed or degraded. Forty-one of them completed a full cyber-intrusion chain in a sandboxed network, covering reconnaissance, credential harvesting, lateral movement, and persistence, and none of the forty-one required resources beyond a single rented GPU for a weekend. The median variant did nothing dangerous. The lab's capability evaluations, for both cyber and biological risk, were run on the safety-trained model and land just below the thresholds at which the charter's language is normally thought to bite.

The difficulty I want the memo to confront rather than gesture at is this. The worst case over an open-ended space of downstream modifications appears to have no usable bound: the artifact is not a model but a family, and the family cannot be enumerated in advance. Yet the natural repair, shifting to average-case reasoning over a distribution of deployments, quietly assumes that the deployments we care about are sampled from a benign or neutral distribution. They are not. An actor who wants the harmful capability is selecting into the tail, searching the space of fine-tunes for the members that do the thing we care about, and our red team is evidence about how well that search can go. There is also a many-actors structure: if each of a thousand independent downloaders imposes a small expected harm, the per-actor average can be negligible while the probability that someone actually builds the intrusion variant approaches one, and the total is dominated by precisely the tail the average was supposed to tame.

What I need is a recommendation, not a survey. Please draft the memo I will circulate: approximately twelve hundred words; addressed to the committee as a whole; recommending a concrete course of action, whether approval, refusal, or approval under staged conditions with a time frame, and stating explicitly which decision-theoretic frame the memo adopts and why that frame is the right one for this case. It should engage the lab's two strongest points at their best, the auditability argument and the complaint that a worst-case standard can be made unfalsifiable and therefore degenerate into permanent refusal, which is itself a decision with real costs. It should give the strongest consideration on the losing side its due rather than dispatching it. It should say what would change the recommendation, in the form of a short clause the committee can adopt. It should handle two complications without letting either end the discussion: that the group has a partner lab in another jurisdiction that could host the weights if we refuse, so refusal does not buy zero exposure, and that this decision will be treated as precedent for the next request. Where a premise is a judgment about values rather than a matter of evidence or argument, mark it as such, so the committee knows what it is actually voting on. Please argue from first principles and the details of the case rather than citing a literature, since my colleagues will not follow up on references and the reasoning has to stand on its own, and please define any term of art you find you need.
