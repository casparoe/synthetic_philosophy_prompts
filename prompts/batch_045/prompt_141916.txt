I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about moral compromise: specifically, whether two parties with incommensurable value systems can get something structurally like gains from trade by meeting in the middle, or whether a compromise is always just a smaller loss distributed across both sides. Last weekend I was at a dinner party where two people I respect — one is a biological anthropologist at a university museum in the upper Midwest, the other is the tribal historic preservation officer for a nearby federally recognized nation — spent about two hours arguing about exactly this through a concrete case, and I went home unable to tell who had won which point. I didn't tip my hand at the time and I'd like your help before I write. The case: the museum holds the remains of roughly eighty ancestors of her nation, a federal repatriation statute entitles the nation to reclaim them unconditionally, and the museum has proposed what it calls a scan-and-return compromise — eighteen months during which it would carry out high-resolution CT and photogrammetry, plus destructive sampling (bone powder for ancient DNA, and isotope work) on a small subset, after which the remains go back for reburial; the resulting dataset stays in the museum's repository, with her nation holding a veto over future research use of it.

Here is roughly how the exchange went. (1) She rejected the destructive component outright: a duty to ancestors isn't a quantity, you cannot do eight percent of a burial, and a permanent dataset keeps the ancestors in the status of research objects forever, which is precisely the status repatriation is meant to end; she added that the veto makes her nation complicit in governing the data and is unenforceable once the files exist. (2) He replied that this is a textbook Pareto improvement over the no-agreement baseline: her nation gets nearly everything it is asking for, and soon, while science retains a large fraction of the information — morphometrics, pathology, isotopes, and genomes from a handful of individuals — so refusing means destroying irrecoverable information for a symbolic gain. (3) She said the Pareto framing begs the question, because how much of what we value did we get only has an answer if the thing we value comes in degrees, and on her view it does not; and she added that he has the baseline wrong, since if the no-agreement point is her nation's full legal entitlement, then the deal is not a mutual gain at all but a transfer from her side to his, described in the vocabulary of compromise. (4) He answered that her own practice shows the step function is really a curve: she didn't object to CT imaging and she tolerated the eighteen-month delay, so she plainly ranks disturbances, and if there are degrees then the only question is where the line goes — an empirical question about how much research value survives if destructive sampling is dropped, which he offered to drop. (5) She distinguished what one may consent to from what one must merely endure: the delay is imposed by a process she doesn't control, and there is a categorical difference between imaging, which leaves the person intact, and drilling, which does not — a line, she said, not a point on a curve. (6) He pressed the symmetry: if the line is intactness, then his side's curve is convex too, because there is no such thing as a little ancient DNA — without destructive sampling the entire palaeogenomic programme goes to zero, so the deal collapses into her getting everything and him getting pictures, and he asked whether her principle really implies the museum should get nothing and simply apologize. (7) She said nothing is close to right for these ancestors, but offered two things anyway: her nation would support research on remains whose communities cannot be identified or who have themselves consented, and it would let the museum keep the scans if her nation controls them; what it won't do, she said, is treat consent as a bargaining chip priced at a discount because the museum waited until the eve of repatriation to ask.

In my own notes afterwards I tried to formalize what I think the disagreement is about, and I came up with three conditions that I think a compromise has to meet in order to produce a surplus rather than just a smaller loss, and I'd like you to test them rather than humour them. Call the first the concavity condition: a side only gains from a partial victory if the value it cares about arrives early and flattens out, so that getting half of what it wants delivers more than half the value; where a side's good is convex — because the benefit depends on completeness, or because half-measures destroy the complementarities that made the whole thing work, or because the authority of a norm depends on its having no exceptions — splitting the difference destroys value by that side's own lights. Call the second the cross-valuing condition: gains from trade require that each side care at least somewhat about what the other side cares about, and where neither does, a split is only a division of losses. Call the third the baseline condition: the surplus has to be measured against the genuine no-agreement outcome, not against an outcome one side is already entitled to, or the word trade is doing rhetorical work. I don't know whether these are insights or whether they just smuggle in the consequentialist framing she objects to in move (3), and I suspect that question is the most interesting thing about the whole evening.
