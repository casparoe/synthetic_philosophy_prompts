I work at a research-funding organisation that allocates part of its programme budget using an internal forecasting market: around sixty staff trade contracts on questions like whether a given grant portfolio will hit its replication target by a stated date, and once a week we hold a deliberation meeting where the same questions are argued out verbally. A senior colleague, who reads widely in this area, wants to replace the meetings with pure trading, and he argues for it via Aumann's agreement theorem. As he puts it, the market is a machine for pooling dispersed private information: the common prior is the shared understanding of the question, each trader's private evidence plays the role of the private signal, placing an order plays the role of announcing a posterior, repeated trading plays the role of the iterated exchange that drives convergence, and the price plays the role of the common posterior the agents would reach if they were honest and rational. From that he draws three conclusions: the price should be treated as the house view when we make funding decisions; the deliberation meetings are redundant Machinery; and any forecaster who keeps a persistent stake away from the price is either irrational or profiting from the disagreement, and should be investigated.

I want to audit the analogy rather than dispute the theorem, which I take to be fine within its own assumptions. Two features of our setup make me suspicious of the transfer. First, payoffs: we pay from a fixed bonus pool split among the top quartile by log score, so what matters is relative performance, and every trade is visible to the people who write the annual reviews. Second, much of our disagreement is not about how much weight to put on a piece of evidence but about which model class to trust at all — some of us think replication rates are driven by field-level research practices, others by selection in the publication pipeline — and that looks like disagreement over how to carve up the possibilities, not over which cell we are in.

So I would like the mapping laid out explicitly — what is supposed to correspond to the prior, the private signal, the announcement, the iteration, and the convergent posterior — and then the disanalogies, separated into those that matter for each of his three conclusions and those that do not. Concretely: the price is one number, while a posterior is a distribution over a partition, so does the dimensional collapse break the identification, or can repeated trading recover what a single price cannot say? Does strategic shading break it? I have half-remembered a result usually called the no-trade theorem which seems to say that under just the conditions that would make the price the group posterior there should be no trade at all — which would make our trading volume evidence against the premises rather than for them — and I would like to know whether that is the right result, whether I have the idea right, and how it differs from the separate point about informationally efficient markets being impossible. Relatedly, my understanding is that the theorem assumes each agent's information partition is itself common knowledge, so that all that is unsettled is which cell obtains; if that is right, our model-class disputes attack the setup at a different place than the incentive problems do, and I would like those two failures kept apart. What, on the corrected picture, can a residual spread legitimately be read as — heterogeneous priors, risk aversion or budget limits, residual private information, or something structural? Does the close-the-meetings conclusion survive if the analogy only partly holds, given that meetings transmit likelihoods and reasons rather than posteriors? And if the market is not a conversation, is there a better analogy for what it does, or is the honest answer that it is a different kind of object that merely outputs a number in the same format as a probability?
