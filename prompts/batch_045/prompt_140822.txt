I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about whether infinity breaks consequentialism — the longtermist angle: a grantmaker at an EA-ish funder who has to pick between two bets, one of which has a small chance of improving the lives of an infinite number of future people. Before I ring up anyone for comment I want the spec: what would a decision procedure for infinite worlds actually have to deliver to be worth putting in that person's hands?

Give me the desiderata as a table, columns exactly:

- Requirement — one line, imperative
- The case it's there to handle — the smallest example that forces the requirement, described concretely
- Non-negotiable or camp preference — and whose preference, if the latter
- What it kills — the popular move or family of views the requirement rules out
- Collides with — other requirements on the list it can't obviously be combined with

Things I want on the list, and I genuinely dont mind if you tell me some of them are barmy:

- It has to say something to a bounded agent: no supertasks, can only ever affect finitely many people, but the choice settles which of two infinite worlds obtains. I keep hearing about infinitarian paralysis and I want to know whether it's a defect of the theories or just what the situation is like.
- Drip worlds: population finite at every time but unbounded across time — an ever-expanding civilisation that never stops. Don't let the theory dodge by only handling the tidy case where every location already exists from the start.
- Granularity invariance: the verdict shouldn't flip depending on how you carve the same world up into lives, lives into moments, or space into regions.
- It mustn't collapse "the world contains infinite value" into "your action makes a difference" — and it shouldn't collapse whether something matters into how much it matters.
- It should degrade gracefully when the agent doesn't know whether the world is infinite at all, which the grantmaker doesn't.
- It should avoid fanaticism — or tell me fanaticism is actually forced once you've accepted the rest.

Then the bit I care most about: is some subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable? If the list eats itself, that's the story, so say which subset and why. And name the single requirement doing the most work in excluding candidates, with a sentence on why it excludes so much. One line each at most on the usual suspects — straight aggregation, threshold or catch-up style views, lexical or leximin style views — I'll do the scorecard separately with people who can name papers, so keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases.
