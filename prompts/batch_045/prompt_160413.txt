I am writing a chapter on the analysis of social convention and need a careful comparison of two accounts of what a convention is. Please work from the characterizations given below rather than from anything you may recall about the secondary literature: assume you have no library access, so do not cite, attribute, or reconstruct bibliographies, and do not bring in further named theories beyond the two accounts specified.

Account A is David Lewis's analysis in Convention (1969). Roughly: a convention is a regularity in the behaviour of a population that solves a recurring coordination problem, where nearly everyone conforms, nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform, those expectations give each person a reason of the right kind to conform, there is some alternative regularity that would have solved the problem just as well, and all of this is common knowledge in the population.

Account B is the evolutionary/dynamic account in the tradition of Brian Skyrms: a convention is a self-reinforcing behavioural pattern that a population falls into and stays in because individual-level learning, imitation, or reproduction reinforces what has succeeded. What Lewis explains by mutual expectation and common knowledge is here explained by precedent, correlation, and path-dependence; arbitrariness is explained by which basin of attraction the population happened to start in.

The comparison I want is not about which account better predicts behaviour, but about which gives the better identity condition for conventionhood: which one better separates genuine conventions from other stable regularities. Run both accounts on three variants of one scenario.

Two competing mining companies run driverless ore trains through a shared single-track tunnel. A regulatory firewall forbids any data exchange between their control systems; each train can, however, receive a public time broadcast and, after the fact, consult a public log of which train entered when.

Variant 1 (no meeting of minds): over some months the pattern settles into trains with even unit numbers entering on the hour and half-hour while odd-numbered trains hold. The reverse pattern would have served just as well. The companies never communicate; each firm's engineers infer the pattern from the logs and eventually rely on it.

Variant 2 (the vestige): an interlocking signal is installed, so two trains can never be in the tunnel together and the coordination problem disappears. The even/odd pattern nonetheless persists for years, because rewriting the scheduler is expensive and everyone has grown to expect it.

Variant 3 (pure correlate, day one): from the first encounter, before any history, each train settles entry by the parity of the minute in the public broadcast — enter on even minutes, hold on odd. Coordination is perfect immediately, with no precedent, no learning, no expectations about the other firm, and no communication.

For each variant, give a verdict under each account (convention / not a convention / indeterminate), one line of reasoning each, and name the single clause or condition doing the work. Then say where each account respectively under-generates and over-generates, and whether the residual disagreement is substantive or merely about what the word convention ought to be used to pick out.
