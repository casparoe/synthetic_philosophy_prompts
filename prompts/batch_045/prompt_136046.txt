I had a long argument with a friend last night about the doctrine of doing and allowing, and I would like to settle it in a seminar room rather than over another bottle of wine. Her position was that the distinction is at bottom a causal one: killing and letting die pick out different causal relations between an agent and a death, the causal facts are never what is in dispute, and the only genuinely open question is whether that causal difference carries the moral weight the doctrine assigns it. I disagreed, and I think I disagree at the first step rather than the second. Whether an agent counts as having killed or as having merely let die is fixed only relative to a contrast case — what would have happened had she acted otherwise, or had she not been there at all, or had everyone else behaved as they normally do — and nothing in the physical sequence of events tells you which contrast to apply. She said this was just scepticism about causation and that no one really believes it. Since I cannot browbeat her, I would like to try it on twenty undergraduates.

The setting: a third-year seminar on the ethics of life and death, twenty students, one seventy-five-minute session, one room with a whiteboard and no technology. They have already worked through the standard cases — the runaway vehicle, the surgeon and the transplant, the switch, the footbridge — and I do not want any of them again. If a vehicle, a hospital, or a large man on a bridge appears in your design, I will not use it. What they have not done is notice that the distinction is applied against a baseline the case does not supply.

What I want them to come away having seen: that the same physical sequence can be correctly described as a killing or as a letting die depending on which contrast is made salient; that the contrast is not settled by the causal facts; and that the doctrine therefore cannot do the work it appears to do unless something else — a view about what we owe people, or about what an agent may do with her own body, time, and attention — has quietly been built into the choice of baseline. I do not want them to conclude that the distinction is worthless. Several of them will want to conclude exactly that, and I would like the activity to make that route harder rather than easier.

Constraints on the design. I want something the students do rather than something I describe to them: real choices made in sequence, with small stakes — chips, points, or a public tally — so that they commit to a classification before they can hedge. The disagreement should arise out of the rules and out of what the students do to each other, not out of anything I announce. I would rather not name the doctrine, the baseline problem, or anything else until the debrief. The scenarios should be ordinary and non-medical: a workplace, a climbing party, a shared house, a hiring committee. And the cases should be structurally awkward in a specific way: the outcome should depend on several people's ordinary behaviour, while any one of several others could individually have prevented it and none of them did — so that a student who wants to say that someone killed the victim has to say it of a person whose own act or omission made no difference by itself, and a student who wants to say that everyone merely let the victim die has to say it of people whose combined behaviour was necessary for the death. I have index cards, a pack of playing cards, poker chips, a whiteboard, and a flipchart.

What I want from you: the rules, the materials, the setup, and a minute-by-minute timeline; the scoring or payoff scheme and what it is meant to make visible; the debrief, mapped onto the moving parts of the idea, including where I should press and where I should stop; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover; and a frank statement of which part of the philosophical point the activity cannot deliver and which I will have to say in my own voice afterwards.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether an activity of the kind I have described can actually make the point I want, or whether this is a point I should stop trying to stage and simply argue for — and then give the design and the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for.
