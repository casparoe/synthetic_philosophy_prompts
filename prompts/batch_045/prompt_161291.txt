I'm retired now — I spent most of my working life doing solution NMR, so I'm used to asking how faithfully a reconstruction reproduces the thing it was reconstructed from, and to being perfectly happy with answers of the form "to within two per cent". A few months ago I drifted into a philosophy reading group that meets in the back room of a pub, and last month we did the teletransporter case — the version from Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons, 1984 I think the handout said, where you're scanned on Earth, destroyed, and rebuilt on Mars. The group has one line it reaches for every single time anyone asks a question, roughly: identity isn't what matters. It got wheeled out on me twice in one evening and I definately want to know what it was actually doing there.

Here's what happened. I said the standard case is too clean for me, so I made up a messier one: the machine sends the scan down two channels. Channel A builds a replica at full resolution; channel B builds one from a heavily compressed scan, with the fine detail — the exact synaptic weights, or all the sub-cellular detail, pick whatever does the work — filled back in by a reconstruction algorithm, so B is right in the big features and off in a small fraction of the small ones. Both wake up, both feel entirely normal, both pass any behavioural test you could run in an afternoon, and neither can tell which one it is. My instinct from the lab is that A is me and B is me to some high percentage, and that there's a real if vague quantitative question there. The group said the line settles it: there's no fact of the matter about which one is me, so the question shouldn't even be asked, full stop. Two other people used the same line for two completely different jobs in the same hour — one said it shows stepping into the destructive machine is no worse than going to sleep, and another said it shows death isn't really bad for the person who dies.

What I'd like is a disambiguation, not a ruling. Lay out the different precise claims that line could be standing for, ordered from the boring to the wild: identity isn't the only thing that matters; identity matters but isn't some deep further fact over and above the ordinary facts; what matters is psychological continuity or connectedness, which comes in degrees and can hold one-to-many; in the branching case there's no further fact, so the which-one-is-me question is empty rather than hard; identity doesn't matter at all, so special concern for your own future is unjustified; nothing about survival matters at all, so death isn't a harm. For each, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or just unargued — and this is the bit I actually want — whether that reading would really do the job it was being used for: does it support telling me to stop asking about my lossy copy, does it support the going-to-sleep claim, does it support the death-isn't-bad claim, or does the line only sound like it settles those? If the degrees reading is the live one, I'd also like to know whether it can handle a wrinkle I thought of on the walk home: suppose B is built first and lives for a week before A is built, so by the time both exist they've got different histories. Does anything in the menu shift? Keep it in plain language with no reading list — I can't look anything up, and I want to be able to say something coherent at the next meeting rather than just report what the book says.
