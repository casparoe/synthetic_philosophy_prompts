I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Slate Star Codex era and I run a small reading group that keeps getting stuck on the same argument: whether the patterns we pick out with the design and intentional stances are *real* in the same sense that the patterns of the physical stance are, or whether stance-talk is a压缩 trick we perform on a world that contains no such joints. Everyone in the group can recite the standard moves, and I want to break the deadlock the only way that ever works on us — by making people commit to concrete cases and then showing them which position their commitments add up to. So I need a sorting instrument, not an essay.

What I want from you is a diagnostic tree for the question *when is a pattern real, and what are we committed to when we say that it is*. Please build it in this order: first the diagnostic questions, then the space of answer combinations they allow, then the named positions located in that space, with the empty cells marked and briefly diagnosed.

Constraints on the instrument:

**Build from the logical space, not from a list of names.** Fix the questions first — I'd like ten, each with two or three options — and only afterwards say which combinations of answers are jointly satisfiable and which position lives in each occupied cell. Where a cell is empty, give me a one-line verdict on *why*: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where a cell is occupied by nobody famous, invent a label and state the position's most awkward commitment in one sentence, rather than bending my cases to fit a canonical view.

**Every question is a case, never a label.** No item may ask whether patterns are real, whether the intentional stance is merely instrumental, or which stance the reader prefers. Each should be a concrete system or regularity the reader either does or doesn't count as a pattern, phrased so that someone who has never heard the terminology can still answer. Cases I want used as nodes (use at least six, add more if the space needs them):

- a glider moving across a Conway's Game of Life board;
- a billion-bit output stream from a cryptographically secure pseudorandom generator with a 128-bit seed — tiny Kolmogorov complexity, but no computationally bounded observer can recover the seed or compress a single bit;
- the implied probability of a rate cut that a market price seems to encode, and the S&P 500 index level considered as an object;
- a frontier language model's apparent aim of answering the user helpfully, given that what actually exists is a pile of matrices shaped by gradient descent;
- a thermostat that turns on the furnace and whose behaviour is equally well predicted by saying it believes the room is below 18°C and by describing its circuit;
- a honeybee colony arriving at a nest site after scout dances;
- a regularity that holds reliably in the data but collapses the moment anyone trades on it;
- a regularity that compresses beautifully and predicts perfectly but supports no counterfactual at all, because the variable you'd intervene on is causally inert and merely perfectly correlated with the real cause;
- a system whose behaviour you can steer and control but which admits no shorter description than a raw listing of its states;
- a bit sequence that is algorithmically incompressible.

**The wrinkles that actually separate people.** These are the ones my group argues about, and a tree that ignores them won't sort anyone:

1. *Budget relativity*: whether the pseudorandom stream case forces patternhood to be relative to a compressor's computational budget, or whether in-principle compressibility is enough and infeasibility is merely an epistemic misfortune.
2. *Loss-function relativity*: two observers with identical data and identical compute but different loss functions draw the pattern boundaries in different places. Is there one pattern there or two, and does the answer change if neither clustering compresses better than the other?
3. *Fragility under being acted upon*: whether a regularity that no agent can intervene on (because it is too distant, too slow, or already past) can still be a pattern, and whether one that vanishes when acted on was ever a pattern.
4. *Cross-cutting and stacked patterns*: the same system admitting two incompatible optimal compressions at the same error rate, and patterns defined over other patterns — a glider simulated inside another cellular automaton. Does the tree let both members of a conflicting pair be real, and does patternhood iterate?

**Leaves.** I want at least these as terminal nodes, plus whatever the space demands: Dennett-style compressibility realism about patterns; the structural-realist or Platonist view that patternhood is mind-independent and not a matter of description length; the instrumentalist view that only the physical stance describes what is really there and the higher stances are useful fictions; the view that accepts the design stance as carving something real but eliminates the intentional stance; the interventionist view that a pattern is real exactly when it licenses counterfactuals and supports control; a pragmatist view on which patternhood is relative to an observer's loss function and compute budget; and a quietist or deflationary view on which calling a pattern real adds nothing beyond its predictive usefulness.

**Honesty about what the tree cannot do.** Flag any pair of leaves that my case set cannot separate — for instance, if compressibility realism and the interventionist criterion give the same verdict on every case I listed, say so, and either supply one further case that pries them apart or concede that no offline case I can pose will do it. Also flag any question on which two leaves that differ elsewhere collapse.

**Scoring key.** Map answer combinations onto leaves, including mixed profiles that straddle two positions; for each such profile, name the nearest position and say which single verdict the reader would have to change to land cleanly inside it.

Audience and format: people who have read some Dennett, throw around map-territory and Goodhart, and have not read the technical literature. Keep each question to a case and two or three plain options. Everything must be answerable without looking anything up — reason from the cases and distinctions above, and don't ask me to consult or cite anything.
