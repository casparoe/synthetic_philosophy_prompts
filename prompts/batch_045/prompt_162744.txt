And here's the answer, verbatim apart from spellings I left alone:

The situationist critique starts from social psychology, and it is worth saying at the outset what it is not. It is not skepticism about free will or agency, and it is not the accusation that people are hypocrites who don't really believe what they say they believe. It is an empirical claim about what explains behaviour. In Milgram's obedience experiments, whether a subject kept administering what he took to be painful and dangerous shocks varied with how physically close the learner was and whether the experimenter was in the room; in Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, whether a seminarian stopped for a stranger slumped in a doorway was predicted by how much of a hurry he was in, and not by how religious he was by his own report, or by the fact that he was on his way to give a talk on the Good Samaritan; in the phone-booth study, subjects who happened to find a dime were much likelier to help someone gather up dropped papers. In each case the thing doing the explanatory work is a feature of the situation rather than a disposition of the agent.

Harman's version of the argument is the strong one. Character traits are never observed directly — we see behaviour and infer the trait behind it — and since nothing in the data requires that inference, we have no reason to believe in character traits at all: what looks like character is really the fundamental attribution error, our habit of reading situational effects off as facts about persons. Doris's version is the same thesis set out more carefully. He argues that there is no such thing as character: the global traits virtue ethics needs — traits reliably manifested across a diversity of trait-relevant eliciting conditions and stable across iterated trials of similar conditions (Doris 2002, 22) — turn out on examination to be an empty posit, and what actually exists are the narrow, situation-specific dispositions that psychologists can measure. Both are situationists in the sense that both hold situational factors to do most of the explanatory work.

On the distinction from lecture: I think it is shallower than it looks. Bandwidth failure is when the trait doesn't appear across variation in features irrelevant to what it requires; cost-tolerance failure is when it doesn't appear once compliance is costly or unpleasant. But every study above is a case of the first, Milgram included. In the remote condition the subject bears no cost by continuing — he is responding to the learner being out of sight and to the experimenter's instruction being salient — and in the proximity conditions he stops for the same reason; nothing there turns on cost. The same goes for the hurry condition in Darley and Batson, since being late is an irrelevant feature of the situation rather than a price the seminarian must pay in order to help. And cost-tolerance failure, examined closely, is just bandwidth failure under another description, because whether some feature of a situation counts as a cost at all depends on what the agent happens to attend to. So the situationist evidence is all of one kind, and one reply should be able to handle all of it.

The reply I'd defend is what I'll call the scaffolding reply. Virtue ethics has never been only a theory of individual psychology; from Aristotle onward it has insisted that character is produced and held in place by the right laws, the right education and the right company, which is why the Nicomachean Ethics spends so long on upbringing and legislation. So the situationist findings don't refute virtue ethics — in a sense they confirm its sociological claim, and they tell us which features of an environment do the damage. If we design institutions that strip out the irrelevant frictions, the hurry and the ambiguity about who is responsible and the noise, then ordinary people will do well enough, and nothing in these experiments says otherwise. A second and independent point supports this. Even if the critique went through entirely, all it would show is that virtue is rare, and no virtue ethicist has ever claimed that most people are virtuous; an ideal can be the right ideal even if hardly anyone meets it. The critique is therefore at best a claim about how virtue is distributed, not about the theory. One standard reply I am setting aside: the methodological point that traits show up only in behaviour aggregated over many occasions, so that one-shot laboratory trials cannot detect them. I take that to change the subject, because the situationist claim is precisely about how people act on particular occasions.
