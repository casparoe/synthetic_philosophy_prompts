I am writing my Extended Project essay on Andrew Niccol's Gattaca, and on whether the film is really an argument against liberal eugenics – by which I mean the position Julian Savulescu named procreative beneficence: that parents who are going to have a child anyway have a moral reason, indeed an obligation, to select from among the children they could have the one expected to have the best life. Almost everything I can find online treats the film as a flat refusal of that view, which seems too quick in both directions, so I would like a reading that takes the philosophy seriously and takes the film seriously.

Four things I would like you to work with, because I have not seen them put quite this way:

1. Selecting and sorting are separable. Gattaca's horror depends heavily on genetic data being available to employers, insurers and the police; a society could permit clinic-based selection while making it a serious crime for anyone outside the clinic to see the results. If the dystopia survives only in the sorting half, does the film actually test procreative beneficence at all, or only a disclosure regime bolted onto it?

2. Expected well-being is not independent of the institutions that reward it. What counts as the best life prospects for a child is partly made by the hierarchy the film depicts, so a population-wide practice of selecting for currently favoured traits entrenches the very arrangement that makes those traits pay. That looks like a collective-action or arms-race structure: each family's reason may be sound while the aggregate practice is self-defeating, and I want to know how much weight that carries.

3. The film cannot easily be making a claim about Vincent's parents. Had they selected, Vincent would not have existed, so the choice it is tempting to criticise cannot have been wrong for his sake; this is the non-identity problem in narrative form. So the critique, if there is one, has to be about the society that closes off his options rather than about procreative selection as such. Does the film see this, or does it smuggle in an argument it has not earned?

4. Where the film may rig the case, and where it may land a hit the journal articles miss. Its selected characters are drawn as conspicuously unhappy, and the murder plot and its resolution are melodrama; but it also offers something the literature mostly gestures at, namely that a private parental reason may be unable to stay private once the goods at stake are positional.

I would like to know whether the dystopia follows from procreative beneficence or only from a neighbouring package; which two or three scenes you would build the essay around – I am thinking of the swimming races with his brother, and Eugene, the silver medallist, at the end – and what a defender of the view should concede to the film.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, the single claim your essay would defend, and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Roughly 900 words, in British English. I do not need a bibliography or citations, and please describe scenes rather than quoting dialogue.
