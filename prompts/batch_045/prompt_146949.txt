I sit on the Medication Safety Review Committee at a 420-bed teaching hospital, and I have been asked to draft the memo the committee will vote on at its next meeting. Two nurses are before us, and the case has divided us in a way I think is philosophical rather than clinical.

Nurse A and Nurse B each skipped the required independent double-check before hanging a bag containing a high-alert medication. In every respect we have been able to reconstruct — training, hours into shift, patient-to-nurse ratio, the wording of the order, the position of the bags in the fridge, and the fact that both had performed the check correctly hundreds of times before — the two episodes are indistinguishable. A's infusion reached the patient, who turned out to have an undiagnosed sensitivity that no one on the team had reason to suspect; the patient suffered lasting harm and has filed a complaint. B's bag was stopped by a smart-pump guardrail about twenty minutes in, before a clinically significant dose was delivered; no one was harmed. That guardrail had been installed on B's unit two weeks earlier as part of a staggered rollout; the installation on A's unit was delayed by a supply-chain problem, not by any decision of A's or of her manager. So both resultant luck and circumstantial luck are doing work here, and part of our discomfort, I suspect, is about the second rather than the first.

Two further wrinkles. A's personnel file is exemplary; B's contains two prior documentation lapses, unrelated in mechanism but not obviously irrelevant. And the state board requires us to report serious reportable events, which our own policy defines as events that reached the patient and caused harm, or that would have caused harm had they reached the patient; it is genuinely contested in our meetings whether B's intercepted error falls under that second clause, and our counsel agrees the language is ambiguous. Reporting, if we do it, must occur within thirty days.

Please draft the memo. It should be addressed to the committee, put its recommendation up front, and be between 1,000 and 1,200 words. It is an internal document, so no citations or references are needed — what I need is reasoning the committee can act on and both nurses can read without being insulted.

The memo should do the following. First, recommend concrete dispositions for A and B, with a time frame: start by stating what you would recommend under the stipulation that the two files are identical, and then say what, if anything, changes once B's prior lapses are on the table, and why. Second, engage seriously the argument that outcome is itself evidence: a process that lets a wrong bag actually reach a patient may be a worse process than one in which something catches it, so differential treatment may be epistemically rational even if the difference in outcome is, morally speaking, a matter of luck. Treat this as the strongest consideration on the other side and give it its due rather than dismissing it. Third, distinguish — and tell us which one the committee is doing at each point — between judgments of blame or culpability, decisions about sanction and discipline, the hospital's reparative response to the harmed patient, and what we owe A as a person who now has to live with what happened. Say explicitly whether these can come apart, and where you think the committee's authority stops. Fourth, evaluate two procedural proposals that have been floated for our standing rules: an outcome-blinded protocol, under which the reviewing panel sees a redacted timeline with the patient's outcome withheld, records a provisional disposition, and only then unseals the outcome, with any departure from the provisional disposition requiring written justification; and a veil-of-outcomes test, under which we may adopt only those rules we could endorse without knowing whether we would end up being the nurse whose error hurt someone or the nurse whose error was caught. Recommend adopt, adopt-with-modification, or reject for each, with reasons a skeptical member could answer. Finally, give me one short paragraph, written to be read aloud, that I can use when we meet the patient's family and are asked what the hospital did and why.

Two ways of resolving the case are unavailable to me, and I would ask the memo not to take them. The first is to say that the law compels us: on this language it does not, and we have discretion. The second is to say that because luck is morally arbitrary, outcomes must be irrelevant to what we do; that may be right in some register, but the whole difficulty of the case is that we are an institution with duties to a harmed patient, a workforce to deter and instruct, and a limited ability to see process directly. The interesting work is in the space between those two answers, and that is where I want the memo to live.
