I do advance care planning at a community mental health clinic — the psychiatric kind, where somebody who's doing okay right now writes down what should happen the next time they aren't. I'm the one who sits with them and drafts it; a psychiatrist signs off on the clinical bits but honestly she rubber-stamps whatever I put in front of her. I've done maybe three hundred of these over four years, fourty minutes a session, and I also get the 2am call from the ER when one of these documents is the only thing anybody has to go on. So: high stakes, almost no time, and the person applying it is often a resident who has never met the client.

The story I absorbed from rationalist blog posts years ago is too crude for what I actually see. It says: the person who wrote the document is the real agent, the person in the room is impaired, so the document wins. But the hard cases dont look like that. From the spreadsheet I keep, they split into at least four different things. (1) Plain capacity dips — the person can't reason right now and thanks us later. Easy, maybe sixty percent of calls. (2) Value drift — they're reasoning fine, they've just changed their mind, and they say the old self was scared, or brainwashed, or just wrong. Sometimes they're right! (3) New information — a reason the earlier self never weighed: a side effect that showed up years later, a new diagnosis, a baby, a breakup. The earlier self didn't reject this reason, they never heard it. (4) Trigger gaming — the earlier self wrote a precise trigger and the current self stays just inside it: four hours of sleep instead of three, gives away two hundred dollars instead of whatever the number was, refuses without quite using the words the document keys on. Technically it hasn't fired. And then there's a pattern I only noticed recently: some directives bind the outcome (force the shot if I say no) and some only bind the process (call my sister, make me wait a day, don't let me sign anything), and the process ones basically never blow up in my face.

What I want is a procedure, not a theory dump. Ordered questions I can run in a session or in ninety seconds on the phone; what counts as a good enough answer to each; where the procedure just stops and says no, honor the document (or no, ignore it); and where it has to hand the decision to a human being because the question is values, not facts. Tell me what to do when I can't get an answer — client won't articulate reasons, nobody who knew them is reachable, it's 2am. Assume I have no philosophical training and I have to be able to explain the result to a sibling who thinks I steamrolled their brother. Please don't give me a reading list; I know the Ulysses stuff and the precommitment discourse, I need something I can run.

Then run it on three. (A) A client with bipolar I whose directive says treat me as unable to refuse meds if I've slept under four hours for three nights and I'm giving money away. She's on night two, maybe four hours, gave two hundred bucks to a church raffle, perfectly coherent, refusing. Does the trigger fire? (B) A directive written eleven years ago by someone who has since had a serious religious conversion and now says the person who wrote it was a different and worse person and wants the whole thing voided — coherent, not manic, sober. Do I void it? (C) Different scale: our clinic adopted a policy in 2016 after a bad incident, never call the police on a patient, and the same policy made repeal require a two-thirds vote. Everybody currently on staff thinks it's too strong and it's never been repealed. Last week a patient broke a nurse's arm. Does the policy bind us, and is the group case even the same shape as the individual one or am I importing the wrong analogy?

One of those should give me an answer I don't like. I'd rather have that than a rule that always sides with the earlier self. And be straight about what the procedure can't do, where it'll misfire, and the cheapest way to catch a misfire before it hurts somebody — an expiry date, a re-signing ritual, a single question I always have to ask.
