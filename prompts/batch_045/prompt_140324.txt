I should say at the outset that I have no academic background and no university affiliation. My first degree was in electrical engineering, I have spent fifteen years working as a location sound recordist, and for about three years now I have been reading about perception on my own — mostly secondary summaries, encyclopedia entries, whatever I can get hold of without a library login. I am applying to a small foundation that funds nine-month, part-time projects by independent researchers with no host institution. They want a two-page proposal plus a one-paragraph summary for a general reader. I can write the prose. What I cannot do is tell whether my question is a real open question or something the field closed years ago and I simply failed to understand. So part of what I am asking you for is a scoping sanity-check alongside the document itself.

The thing I keep coming back to is how the argument from illusion is normally set up: you take a hallucination as vivid as a perception, note that the subject cannot tell the two apart from the inside, and then either conclude that the good case and the bad case share some common mental factor — the thing experience is really of — or refuse that conclusion and hold that perceiving is a relational success state and hallucinating is not the same kind of thing at all. Both moves, as far as I can see, help themselves to the assumption that contact with the world is binary: either you are in touch with the object or you are not.

What made me doubt that is a set of cases that fit neither slot. Seeing an object in near-darkness or through a badly distorting medium; the patterned visual hallucinations that people with serious sight loss report (Charles Bonnet syndrome, as I understand it, though I may have the mechanism wrong); my own tinnitus, which is not an audible sound and is not nothing either; phantom limb sensation where some residual nerve activity remains but no limb is there; cochlear implants and visual prostheses, which restore a kind of contact through an entirely artificial channel; and camera-to-skin sensory substitution devices, where you genuinely pick up features of your surroundings through a channel nobody would call natural. In all of these there is some causal and informational contact with the world, but partial, degraded, or rerouted. They are not good cases and they are not hallucinations.

My half-formed idea is to take that seriously and treat contact as admitting of degrees — roughly, the extent to which the character of your experience at a time is being controlled by, and tracking, the particular thing you seem to be aware of — so that hallucination is the zero point on the scale and the good case is the top of it, rather than the two being different kinds of state. Call it graded disjunctivism for the purposes of the proposal.

The question I want to work on, and the part I most want you to sharpen, is whether grading it rescues the disjunctivist's central claim or quietly hands the argument back to the other side. My worry is that a degree is exactly the sort of thing a hallucination and a perception can share: if contact is a scalar quantity, then a hallucination at zero and a badly degraded perception at some small non-zero value differ only in the value of a variable, which starts to look like the common factor the disjunctivist wanted to deny. Against that, maybe the scale is only definable by reference to the good case — zero being what you would have if the object were removed — in which case the good case stays metaphysically prior and the disjunctivist keeps what she wanted. I can see both answers and I cannot see what would settle between them, which is either a sign that it is a genuine question or a sign that I have missed something.

What I want from you is the proposal document itself, and in it: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the standard disjunctivist and common-factor positions both leave it open, given that they share the binary assumption; the method — what I would actually do with nine months at roughly a day and a half a week, with milestones and what would be true at each one; the two or three possible conclusions and the considerations that would decide among them; what the finished thing is (I am imagining a long essay plus a worked catalogue of the partial-contact cases, but tell me if that is the wrong shape); and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that it fails because the question dissolves under pressure. Please also include the plain-language paragraph, and a short, direct answer to the question of why a foundation should fund a self-taught person with no department and no track record to do this rather than someone inside the field.

Keep it to two pages.
