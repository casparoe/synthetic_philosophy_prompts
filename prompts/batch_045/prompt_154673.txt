I host a monthly discussion evening at my local library: usually eleven or twelve adults, ages from the early twenties to the late seventies, almost none with any science training, and two or three who read popular physics and will say so within the first ten minutes. I have two hours in a room with movable tables, a whiteboard, and no projector. I should say plainly that I have no academic background in philosophy or physics. I became interested in the direction of time on my own about two years ago, and I am now fairly sure that the thing I believed at the start was wrong, but not at all sure what should replace it.

Here is the shape of what I have talked myself into, and I would rather the evening tested it than illustrated it. First: I no longer think the puzzle is why disorder increases. I think the puzzle is why anything was ever ordered, and why the ordered-ness is all at one end. Second, and this is the part I am least sure of: I have come to suspect that the asymmetry we actually live inside is not one fact but two, and that the second one quietly depends on the first in a way that makes the usual story feel too cheap. The one everybody talks about is that mess comes after tidiness. The other is that we have traces of the past and none of the future — notes, photographs, memories, footprints. I can see how to tell a story in which the first explains the second. What I cannot see is how to tell it without helping myself to the very thing I was trying to explain, because the reason a photograph is evidence of what happened before it was taken seems to be a reason about causation, and causation seems to point the same way time does. I may be confusing myself here. If I am, I would rather find out from the design of the evening than from being told.

So here is what I want from you. Design a runnable activity for that room and that group — a simulation, a game with scoring, a staged deliberation, or some combination — in which people run into these things by doing them, before anyone names them. No lecture at the front, and the words entropy, second law, and arrow of time should ideally not be spoken by me until the last twenty minutes. Concretely, I want:

One, the rules, materials and setup, cheap and reproducible from a supermarket and a printer, with someone with no preparation able to run it.

Two, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, including what participants are told at each stage and — just as important — what they are not told.

Three, the mechanism by which the first lesson lands on its own. My own guess is a deck of cards and a coarse way of scoring how tidy a deck is, with rounds in which teams must manipulate a deck by hand into a tidier state and discover that nothing in the rules stops them, and then a betting round in which a team is shown a middling deck and asked to wager on what it looked like five shuffles ago and what it will look like in five more. If a pure statistical argument pushes them to give the same answer in both directions, I want them to feel their own resistance to giving it, because that resistance is the thing I want to put on the whiteboard afterwards. But if there is a better mechanism, use it.

Four, a second phase that puts pressure on the traces point without naming it: something that makes a team win or lose depending on whether a record they are handed is trustworthy, and then makes it visible that they had no way, from the record itself, to tell a genuine trace from one that was manufactured after the event. I want them to notice that their confidence came from somewhere outside the activity.

Five, the debrief: which moments in the play map onto which part of the idea, and how you would draw that out with questions rather than assertions.

Six, an honest account of what will go wrong the first time. I can foresee some of it — the two readers will supply the vocabulary early and flatten everything, someone will shuffle badly enough that the deck never mixes, someone will decide the whole exercise is about card tricks. Tell me how to handle these, and what else to expect.

Seven, equal honesty about what the evening cannot do. I assume it cannot show that our own universe in fact has the boundary condition the argument needs, and I assume the difference in scale between a deck and a cosmos makes the analogy worse in some specific way I should be upfront about rather than paper over. Tell me which parts I will simply have to assert on my own authority, and say what the shortest honest assertion is.

Finally, tell me how you would cut it if only six people came, or if the room turned out to be full of people who have never thought about any of this at all.
