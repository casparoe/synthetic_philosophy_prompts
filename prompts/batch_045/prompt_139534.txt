I'm on the board of a very small foundation — five of us, and a budget that covers one two-year postdoc plus maybe two small workshops — and we've been arguing about whether to spend it on the cluster of questions around Aumann's agreement theorem. Bit of background on where I'm coming from: I retired a few years ago from a career in ocean–atmosphere data assimilation, so I spent decades with ensemble methods where nobody ever sends anybody a full posterior. You send ensemble members, or some compressed summary, and you spend half your time on fudges like covariance localization and inflation to stop the filter from collapsing onto a single overconfident answer. Reading about the agreement theorem as a retirement hobby, I keep having the feeling that the philosophy literature has mostly studied an idealization — agents who share a prior, who have common knowledge of each others' posteriors, and who can transmit a number without cost, noise, or strategic distortion — and that everything interesting for the people who actually run expert panels, forecasting platforms, or replication projects lives in what happens when you relax those three things and the conclusion stops going through.

So here's what I need. For someone who wants this money to change how real groups of inquiring people are run — not to add another theorem to the shelf — which open questions in this area are most worth two years of one person's time, and in what order would you fund them? The rough families I can see are: (a) approximately common knowledge and bounded-depth reasoning — does the agreement conclusion degrade gracefully as you move away from full common knowledge, or does it fall off a cliff, and does the answer depend on the topology of who talks to whom; (b) bandwidth — what happens when agents can only exchange a few bits or a handful of samples rather than a posterior, and does it matter whether they can agree in advance on a compression scheme, which in my old field was the difference between a filter that worked and one that didn't; (c) no common prior, or worse, no common state space — agents with genuinely different variables, different model classes, or plain unawareness of what the other one is tracking; (d) what the result is even supposed to imply normatively for real human disagreement, including whether it gives anyone a reason to think actual mass polarization is irrational, or whether that inference is one the literature keeps half-making and should stop. Please reorganize, split, merge, or throw these out as you see fit, and add whatever I've missed.

What I actually want is the defence, not the survey. For each question: how much of the rest of the agenda depends on getting an answer — which questions are upstream of which; whether it's genuinely tractable in two years with pencil and paper plus maybe some simulation, as opposed to being a career; whether it's neglected because it's hard or because nobody wants the answer; and what a result one way or the other would license a practitioner to do differently — a different way to run a panel, a different rule about what to record, a different notion of when to stop deliberating. Be explicit about which of these I'm probably overweighting because they sound deep, and which I'm underweighting because they sound like plumbing. And tell me what kind of result in eighteen months would move something up or down the list — a proof, a clean negative result, a working simulation — because I have to go back to four other board members and justify an ordering, not just a topic.

Two constraints. We are not funding anyone to do empirical ML, and I'm not looking to be pointed at papers or a reading list: I can't tell a good paper from a bad one in this field yet, so argue it out in your own words.
