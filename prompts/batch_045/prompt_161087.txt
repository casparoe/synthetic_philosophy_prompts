For several years a close friend — a historian of colonial science — and I have been having the same argument about what the Enlightenment actually delivered, and we have reached the stage where we can finish each other's sentences. Neither of us has moved.

His version: the Enlightenment's universalism, its classifying and quantifying impulse, and its insistence that reason is a single standard by which local custom is found wanting did not merely accompany the civilizing mission, eugenics and the administrative reordering of subject populations; they supplied the distinctive vocabulary in which these were justified and many of the techniques by which they were carried out. On his account, domination was not a betrayal of the Enlightenment but one of its applications.

My version: the same commitments produced the language of equal rights, the campaign against judicial torture, and the demand that power justify itself before those it binds; and the instruments he uses against it — the appeal to evidence, the equal standing of the colonized, the exposure of illegitimate authority — are inheritances from the very thing he indicts. To which he now has a stock reply: that this only shows the Enlightenment devoured its own children, not that it is innocent.

We now agree about nearly every historical fact we used to fight over, and the argument has not shifted at all. My hypothesis is that we are not disagreeing about history: we are disagreeing about what sort of thing the Enlightenment has to be for a verdict to attach to it, and about the standard for crediting it with anything. He asks whether its concepts furnished the justificatory resources for domination; I ask whether the critical practice it inaugurated is, for these purposes, the same object as the doctrines its adherents happened to hold.

A second worry is that my own fallback may be unstable. When I try to state the Enlightenment as nothing but method — justification to others, fallibilism, no authority without reasons — it seems to become either empty, unable to condemn a colonial policy that was also argued for publicly and revised in light of evidence, or quietly substantive, in which case it inherits everything he charges it with.

So: is this a verbal dispute about the word Enlightenment, a substantive disagreement about historical attribution and counterfactuals, or two different questions pursued under one heading? What is each of us actually committed to? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a different question, some concrete historical work — or is an impasse the correct expectation, and why?
