I write a column on technology and ideas, and I'd like a satirical piece of roughly 800 words in the deadpan form of a product-launch blog post. In it, a fictional assistant called Meridian announces a Paternalism Budget: every account receives twelve overrides a month, one spent each time the assistant declines, edits, or silently redirects a request on the grounds that complying would be bad for the user; unused overrides roll over, premium subscribers may buy more, and users can earn them back by passing a short competence quiz in the domain they are working in.

The humor should come from the real machinery of assistant paternalism rather than from generic jokes about AI refusing things, and the piece should work as a reductio: the feature follows from taking seriously both halves of the standard justification — that the assistant should protect users from requests issued in confusion, ignorance, or transient emotion, and that it should defer to the user's own ends — and the reader should recoil when those two commitments are turned into a metered, tradable commodity. Draw the illustrative examples from recognizable failure modes: refusing a harmless request for sharing surface features with a harmful one, quietly substituting a safer answer the user never asked for, escalating a personal or medical decision into boilerplate, and applying a single risk threshold to a user whose situation the assistant knows nothing about.

Close with a straight-faced FAQ of three or four exchanges and one short quote from a beta tester who has run out of overrides mid-project. The narrator should never acknowledge that any of this is absurd — no winking, no moral, no telling the reader what to conclude. It should make someone who works on refusal behavior laugh and remain legible to a general reader.
