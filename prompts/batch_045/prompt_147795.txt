I'm a novelist not a philosopher, so apologies in advance for naming things badly. My current book has a woman who gets scanned and emulated; the emulation wakes up in a simulated city and insists it's her while the biological original is still walking around, and there's a second thread where a studio licences her "behavioural model" — a precomputed table with an entry for every input she could ever recieve, which returns exactly what she would have said and done — to drive an interactive version of the novel. Readers keep asking me whether the emulation is her and whether the table-thing is a person, and I've been trying to write down what I actually believe so I stop contradicting myself in interviews. Here's where I've landed:

1. I'm a functionalist. What makes something a pain or a belief is the causal role it plays, not the goo it's made of.
2. Multiple realizability is true and I want it to be radical: I don't want to put any in-principle upper bound on how strange the substrate gets. That's basically the whole reason I'm not an identity theorist.
3. The table-thing — call it the Index — has no inner life whatsoever. Not a flicker. Behaviourally perfect and completely dark inside. This is the one I'd least like to give up.
4. The emulation is her, same mind, same person. And I don't care whether it reproduces her at the neural level or at the coarse person-and-psychology level: if the person-level organization is preserved, I want to say that's enough.
5. The Twin Earth stuff convinced me years ago. What her thoughts are about depends partly on the world she's in, not just on what's in her head.
6. I'm not an epiphenomenalist. Her believing the door is locked is what makes her turn the handle. When people tell me (2) and (6) collide — the exclusion problem, or whatever it's called — I've been reaching for something I call the inheritance constraint: a realizer only counts if the realised state's causal powers are inherited from the realizer's own powers, not borrowed from somebody's description or map. That's how I've been sleeping at night.
7. And: a copy of the emulation sitting on a drive, not running, has no mental life either. I don't know if that one is doing any work but it feels right.

So does it hang together? I have a suspicion (3) and (4) are up to something sneaky between them, and someone at a reading told me (5) wrecks (4), which I don't understand at all. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, ranked by how bad they actually are, and for each real tension what would have to give and what giving it would cost me — including what it does to the plot, since I need to know if I'm allowed to keep the ending. Also whether the move I use in (6) helps me or hurts me with (3) and (4), and whether there's one revision that tidies up the most at once.
