I am defending a suffering-focused axiology in a graduate seminar next month. In preparing for it I wrote out what I took to be the strongest argument for the view's practical upshot. The conclusion is one I do not accept, and I cannot locate the step at which the argument fails. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The axiology I am defending — call it *pure negative axiology* (PNA) — has three claims:
(i) Suffering, understood as experience that is unpleasant for the one who has it, is the only thing that is intrinsically bad.
(ii) Its badness is not indexed to anyone beyond the fact that it is someone's: how bad an episode is depends on how intense and how long it is, not on whose it is.
(iii) Happiness and the relief of suffering are not intrinsically good. Removing suffering makes an outcome better; adding happiness does not make it better. Nothing is intrinsically good.

The argument. Fix some future stretch of time, and let an outcome be a specification of which sentient beings exist, when, and what their experiences are like for them.

(1) Outcome A is better than outcome B if and only if A contains less total suffering than B; if they contain equal suffering they are equally good.
(2) So for any outcome containing suffering, there is a better outcome.
(3) A bit of suffering can be absent from an outcome in either of two ways: no one undergoes it, because it is prevented, relieved, or never arises; or the one who would have undergone it is not there.
(4) By (ii), it does not matter which way: the ledger records amounts, not owners. Call this the *lever-substitution* step. I have been treating "reduce the suffering per sufferer" and "reduce the number of sufferers" as two levers on one quantity.
(5) The same holds where we cannot control the outcome: what we ought to do is minimize expected total suffering.
(6) Suffering scales with the number of sufferers, and we have far more reliable control over how many sentient beings there will be than over what their lives will be like.
(7) So the highest-expected-value interventions are those that reduce the expected number of future sentient beings, including ones that cause some suffering now.
(8) There is no stopping point: value decreases with total suffering and with nothing else, and every being that exists is a possible site of suffering, so the optimum is zero sentient beings, permanently.
(9) Therefore we ought to bring about the extinction of sentient life as quickly as possible, by whatever route minimizes expected suffering, including routes that impose enormous suffering on people now alive.
(10) Corollary: since extinction is the optimum, anything that makes extinction less likely is pro tanto wrong, even if it relieves an enormous amount of suffering — so a permanent, world-wide, highly effective analgesic programme would be wrong if it made human extinction less likely.

Two reasons I suspect the flaw is structural rather than in PNA itself.

The mirror. Run the same moves on the mirror axiology, on which happiness is the only intrinsic good and nothing is intrinsically bad. Out comes: we ought to fill the accessible universe with as many maximally happy beings as our causal powers allow, at the cost of any finite amount of suffering, and we wrong possible future people by not creating them. I reject that as well. Whatever is broken is broken in both arguments, so the fault cannot be in (i) through (iii).

Two cases on which I cannot get a stable answer.

*The Mute.* Suppose an intervention — I do not care whether it is medically possible — that leaves the sensory intensity of pain and the subject's capacity to report it entirely intact while removing the unpleasantness. The subject still says, "that is a severe pain in my left hand," and is entirely indifferent to it. If PNA says this removes the suffering, then suffering is one detachable component of experience, and lever-substitution fails in a second way: the ledger can be driven to zero while every sufferer is left in place, which means the route from (2) to (9) depends on an empirical premise the argument never states. If PNA says it does not remove the suffering, then PNA is committed to the intrinsic badness of something no one minds.

*The sparse world.* One hundred beings each in lifelong agony, against a billion beings each with an unremarkable mild chronic discomfort. The ledger says the sparse world is better. I can say that out loud. But notice that in the sparse world every single person is far worse off, and the populations do not overlap, so no one in either world is benefited by the choice.

Where I think I may be confused, without being able to say how: my supervisor says that "expected total suffering" is not well defined when the number and identity of the people is itself what the act changes, because there is no fact of the matter about who the expected sufferers are; and she says this is a different problem from the one about lever-substitution. I half follow. There may be two faults, or one fault I am describing twice.

Constraints on what I would accept. I would rather give up (ii) than (i); I would rather give up either than accept (9), (10), or their mirror. But I do not want a view with nothing to say about future suffering, so "we have no obligations at all regarding people who do not yet exist" is a price I would rather not pay either.
