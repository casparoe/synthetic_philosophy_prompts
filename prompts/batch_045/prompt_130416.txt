I work on latency compensation for a cloud-rendering service, so I spend my days worrying about when a frame counts as having been "seen" — whether a correction to a frame lands before or after the commit to the display, and what the user can be said to have been shown at a given millisecond. A few weeks ago I joined a philosophy-of-mind reading group with a friend and came across the color phi phenomenon: two dots of different colors are flashed in quick succession at different positions, observers report a single dot moving between them, and the color appears to change abruptly partway along the path — at a point in time before the second dot has actually been presented. Looking for the standard discussion afterwards, I found Dennett's distinction in *Consciousness Explained* (1991) between an "Orwellian" reading, on which the subject has one experience and the record of it is subsequently revised (as in the rewriting of the past in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*), and a "Stalinesque" reading, on which the conflicting content is reconciled before it ever reaches consciousness (as in a show trial whose verdict has been fixed in advance). The summary I read quotes him as saying that the two theorists agree about everything that happens in the brain and that "Subjects should be unable to tell the difference between misbegotten experiences and immediately misremembered experiences."

That much I can accept. I would no more ask a viewer to introspect about whether a frame was dropped than I would ask a subject to settle this by verbal report. What I cannot follow is the further claim, which the same sources make, that there is therefore no fact of the matter about which one happens. To me this looks like an ordinary architectural question with two candidate designs, decidable in principle from the outside. Call the first the *displayed-then-patched* design: a frame containing the uncorrected color is committed to whatever counts as presentation, and the corrected content is written afterwards, so the subject's later report reflects a buffer that was already shown. Call the second the *patched-then-displayed* design: the very same content-fixation occurs at the very same point in the causal pathway, but upstream of the commit, so nothing is ever presented with the uncorrected color in it. My proposal is to instrument this rather than argue about it: (i) train a decoder on MEG or intracranial data to detect the neural signature of the second color, and take the moment that signature first appears as the write time; (ii) present a probe — a click, or a briefly flashed marker — at a variable delay between the two flashes, and ask subjects to judge whether the color change came before or after the probe, taking the discrimination threshold as the display time. My intuition is that the decoder gives me the write, the probe gives me the display, and the sign of the difference tells me which architecture the visual system actually implements.

So: which of these measurements, if either, actually discriminates the two hypotheses, and what would I have to see in the data to be justified in calling the result Orwellian or Stalinesque? I am prepared to be told that my probe is useless — but I would like to know specifically why, because as far as I can tell my friend's objection is just that I have helped myself to a place where the frames get shown and a moment at which they get shown, which she says is precisely the "Cartesian theater" Dennett is attacking, and I genuinely cannot see what is wrong with that: some stage of the pipeline has to be the output. Is the right conclusion that the two hypotheses are not different mechanisms at all but two descriptions of one mechanism, so that no instrumentation could ever separate them even in principle? Or is there a real difference that is only accessible from the first-person side? Or is there a fact of the matter here and Dennett is simply wrong to dissolve it — and if so, which philosophers hold that, and on what grounds? Finally, if the correct answer is that there is nothing to be right about, what would count as evidence for that claim as opposed to evidence for our own ignorance? "We currently have no way to measure it" and "there is nothing there to measure" look like very different claims to me, and I would like to know which one I should be defending at the next meeting.
