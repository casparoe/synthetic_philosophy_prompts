hey — I'm an ML engineer and I spend most of my time on uncertainty quantification for a deployed risk-scoring model (think: should a case get auto-cleared, or go to a human). I'm fluent in the stuff I actually use — deep ensembles and disagreement between independently trained models, conformal prediction and coverage guarantees, calibration, proper scoring rules, robust/minimax optimisation, distribution shift, abstain-and-refer options — and I've got basically no philosophy. That's the setup: I want the imprecise-credence position translated into *my* vocabulary, and I want the residue report honestly.

The position, as I half-understand it: a rational agent's uncertainty isn't always captured by a single probability function. Sometimes the evidence pins down only a *set* of admissible distributions, and — this is the bit that seems load-bearing — no member of the set is rationally *required*; every member is *permitted*. The motivations I've heard are the classic ambiguity setup (one urn with known composition, one with unknown composition, and people won't treat the second as 50/50 even though symmetry gives you nothing else) and an arbitrariness worry: what argument is there for 0.5 exactly rather than 0.49 or 0.51, if nothing in the evidence distinguishes them. The costs I've heard: the set can get *less* precise after you condition on new information (dilation, I think it's called), and there's apparently no nice decision rule for acting on a set — worst-case-optimising, Pareto-style admissibility, and just picking some member all come apart, and things get worse once decisions are sequential.

Here's my concrete situation, which is why I care. We run two models trained on different cohorts; for a subgroup they disagree about risk and nothing in our evaluation tells us which cohort's distribution is the right one. Right now we do what I call shrink-to-consensus: report a precision-weighted mean plus a band, and a 'dispersion gate' that routes the case to a human when the models' spread exceeds a tolerance. And I've noticed something I call confidence laundering — downstream consumers read the midpoint as the forecast and treat the band as decoration, so the width does no work in the decision at all. My hunch is that our tolerance setting is secretly doing philosophical work, and that shrink-to-consensus is quietly converting a *set* into a *mixture*, which may be exactly the move the imprecise-credence people think is illegitimate.
