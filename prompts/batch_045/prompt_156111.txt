I'm in a small graduate reading group that has been working through the contemporary free will debate — the leeway-versus-sourcehood split, Frankfurt-style manipulation cases, the luck worry about indeterminist accounts — and last night, after everyone else had gone home, I spent about three hours arguing with a friend who audits the group and has very firm views about whether determinism, if true, would deprive us of anything worth caring about. He kept steering the whole conversation back to a single sentence that he must have repeated a dozen times: "the only free will worth wanting is the kind determinism rules out." He used it to reach three separate conclusions — that compatibilists have quietly changed the subject, that it would be a genuine loss if determinism turned out to be true, and that without the ability to do otherwise deliberation changes nothing and is therefore pointless. When I pushed back, he sharpened the claim with a test of his own invention: a freedom is worth wanting only if, holding fixed the entire history of the universe up to the moment of decision and re-running it, the outcome would sometimes come out differently, and differently because of me rather than because of chance. I said that this variety seems to arrive packaged with a cost, since the less my choice is settled by my prior deliberation, reasons, and character, the less it is my deliberation, reasons, and character that explain what I do, and that a reasonable person might prefer the smaller freedom once the package is described honestly. He replied that this only shows I don't really want freedom, that wanting a property is different from wanting to believe you have it, and that my preference therefore settles nothing. Somewhere around hour two we also noticed we had been sliding between wanting it for myself, wanting it to be true of other people so that blame is fair, wanting it so that legal punishment is justified, and wanting it only because it makes a life feel authored by the person living it.

I'd like to send him something written up rather than continue shouting across a table. Please lay out, as a menu with verdicts rather than as one verdict defended, the distinct precise things his sentence could be claiming — I've already spotted the differences between a freedom that is worth wanting because it does the normative work free will was traditionally supposed to do, one that is worth wanting because its absence would be a loss to the agent, one that is worth wanting because it licenses desert or the reactive attitudes, one that is worth wanting because it makes deliberation non-vacuous, one that is worth wanting because it is simply what people mean by "free will," and one that is worth wanting even after its costs are subtracted, so please go beyond these where you can. Do the same for "could have done otherwise": set out the range of precise theses it might express, from the conditional or hypothetical readings through ability-versus-opportunity through "could have tried" through the strong reading his replay test seems to require. Then say, for the combinations that matter, whether the resulting claim is a platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or question-begging; whether it would actually deliver each of his three conclusions; and whether it survives the objection that the variety he wants comes at the price of reasons-tracking. I also want you to flag any point where his argument, as I've reconstructed it, quietly shifts from one reading to another between premises, since I suspect that is where we were talking past each other. Finally, tell me which of these readings is the one people are usually expressing when they say this sort of thing in print or in seminars, and which of them I could concede without losing what I actually wanted to claim.
