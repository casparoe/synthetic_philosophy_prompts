Me and a friend who's doing a PhD in evolutionary biology got into an argument after a department seminar last week and I've been chewing on it ever since. I ended up defending something close to Popper's notorious verdict on Darwinism: that as it is actually used, the theory of natural selection isn't a falsifiable empirical theory so much as a metaphysical research programme, a framework for generating testable claims rather than a testable claim in its own right, roughly because fitness isn't pinned down independently of who actually survives, so any outcome can be redescribed after the fact as the fitter variants having left more offspring. My friend says this was a confusion that got cleaned up decades ago: fitness is a propensity or expected reproductive success in a specified environment, measurable without looking at who survives, and selection hypotheses make risky quantitative predictions all the time. I'm not convinced by that reply, but I want to know how exposed my position really is, so: what would count against it?

To make it concrete, here are three things I keep circling, though don't feel limited to them. First, imagine someone proved what I'll call the replacement lemma: for any finite record of trait or genotype frequencies in a population, and any description of the environment, there is some assignment of relative fitnesses plus some demographic model that renders that record a product of selection, and conversely whatever a selection model predicts can be matched as closely as you like by a neutral model with different parameters. If something like that were true, would it vindicate the Popperian verdict, or is it just the ordinary underdetermination that any curve-fitting has and therefore no special verdict about Darwinism? Second, suppose we had the technology for what I'll call shadow populations: replicate lineages in environments characterised in advance well enough to compute an engineering-style estimate of how each variant ought to do, from biomechanics, metabolic costs, whatever, before anyone looks at who actually survives and reproduces. If across hundreds of independent lineages the frequency trajectories tracked those pre-registered estimates tightly and quantitatively, would that refute me? I can already hear the reply that what is really being tested is the engineering model plus a pile of auxiliary assumptions rather than selection itself, and I'm not sure that reply is illegitmate, but I want to know how much work it's doing and whether it differs from the move any theory makes when a prediction fails. Third, stasis: lineages that sit there unchanged while their environment demonstrably shifts. In my experience the answer is always stabilizing selection or a moving optimum, which smells like the very same insulation by redescription.

The thing I'd personally find most decisive is a historical case where a selectionist prediction was made in advance, was genuinely risky, failed, and the field treated it as counting against selection rather than against some auxiliary assumption, so if those exist tell me about them and how good they are. I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including what the position predicts that its rivals don't, which ones a defender of my view, or Popper himself, who I gather walked some of this back later, ought to concede rather than explain away, and which they'd rightly dismiss. And be honest about the meta-question: my claim is itself a claim about falsifiability, so what would falsify it? If it turns out to have been qualified into something no observation could dislodge, say so straight, and say whether that's actually a problem for a claim of this kind or whether it's just what claims in the philosophy of science look like.
