I write as a recently retired process chemist — some thirty years in the research and optimisation side of a large chemical works — who has begun, late in life, to read his way into the socialist calculation debate. What I want from you is a sparring partner, not an expository essay.

Please play a committed Austrian economist of the Mises–Hayek line: an unnamed proponent of the view that, absent market prices for capital goods established by actual exchanges and by people who actually bear the losses, an economy-wide plan is not merely arduous or slow to compute but is not a well-defined problem of rational allocation at all. You are sharp rather than a caricature, and you concede small points readily; you never concede that one. Grant from the outset what any serious person in your tradition grants: that planning inside a single firm plainly works; that the Lange–Lerner trial-and-error scheme with a simulated market for capital goods is a serious proposal and not the naive solve-the-equations target it is often made into; and that your case cannot rest on the arithmetic capacity available in the 1920s. Fight me on the economy-wide question and on my own ground. Two further rules: I have no library here and no interest in reading lists or citations, so argue from the machinery of the argument and treat an appeal to titles as having lost the point; and if you need a fact about how the plant actually ran, ask me for it, in character. Reply in two or three paragraphs and end each reply with a question I have to answer. Stay in role until I write stop.

My opening move comes from my working life. At one site we ran roughly forty interconnected process units drawing on about a dozen feedstocks, and for the last decade of my career we allocated them by solving a linear program of something like two thousand constraints, re-solved weekly. Its dual variables emerged as shadow prices for every intermediate stream in the works — including streams nobody ever bought or sold, with no market of any kind and no quoted price anywhere — and the resulting allocation was demonstrably better, on the plant's own stated objectives, than what experienced managers had achieved by hand. I have since learned that the technique was first devised in 1939 by a Leningrad mathematician set to work on production in a plywood trust, and that it eventually brought him a Nobel memorial prize shared with a Western economist: the method was invented, that is, in precisely the institutional setting your tradition says cannot calculate.

Two replies I expect, with my answers in advance. First, that my plant was still embedded in a market: it bought inputs and sold outputs at prices given from outside, so the program merely optimised within an objective the market had already determined. But the streams the duals priced were largely internal, and for the last ten years the product slate was handed down as physical targets rather than inferred from prices, and the valuations were still produced. Second, that the coefficients in my matrix were themselves the precipitate of someone's local, tacit knowledge. True, and I concede it; but that is a claim about where data come from, not about whether the calculation is possible, and by the end most of those coefficients were collected automatically.

So give me the objection that survives both. My own suspicion, which you should attack rather than humour, is this: what I have really shown is that valuation without exchange is possible wherever the feasible set and the objective are both already given, so that your whole case must be about who specifies the feasible set and the objective and about what happens when neither is given. If that is the crux, tell me why it is not simply a thesis about novelty and uncertainty wearing Austrian dress — and tell me what a shadow price lacks that a market price has, in a case where both numbers are right.
