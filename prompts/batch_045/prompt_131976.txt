You are a professional philosopher who has spent years advising agricultural extension services and pesticide regulators, and who is equally at home with population-level consequentialist reasoning and with the unsettled literature on invertebrate sentience. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a brainstorming pass in which you list the considerations you think an insecticide decision actually turns on, sorted into factual, ethical, and irreducibly uncertain, and only then start building the procedure.

The task: I staff a small valley grower cooperative's Spray Review Board. Roughly fifty times a year a member asks permission to use an insecticide that is not on our approved IPM list. We have ten working days, no philosopher, and a board of three growers plus one extension agent; the applicant has to be able to follow the reasoning on a single page. Write the decision procedure we should run: the questions in the order to ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure simply stops and returns a no, and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to the board explicitly as a values call rather than a matter of fact. Assume throughout that we cannot settle whether the animals in question are sentient at all, and say what the procedure does differently on each side of that question.

Two things I need it to handle that I don't think standard IPM language handles:

1. Displacement. For most of our pests it may well be true that the individuals killed would largely have died anyway — starvation, predators, winter — and that a vacated niche refills, so that the moral cost of a spray is not the body count but the net change in insect life-years or suffering, which could be near zero or even negative if the unmanaged alternative is a slower death. I have been calling this the displacement ledger. Tell me whether the procedure should contain such a step at all, and if it should, how a grower with no theory fills it in: what he can reasonably be asked to estimate, and what the procedure does when he cannot estimate it.

2. The resistance commons. The susceptibility of the local pest population is a shared stock: my spray today makes everyone's sprays worse in five years, and my restraint today is partly someone else's gain. So the question is not only whether this spray is justified but whether we would still want to spend the shared stock this way if every grower in the valley reasoned the same way — and, on the other side, whether the applicant is being asked to absorb a loss his competitors are not. Build this into the ordering: say where it functions as a veto, where merely as a tiebreaker, and how to apply it without a population model.

Then run the finished procedure in front of me, and commit to the verdicts: (a) a request for a prophylactic neonicotinoid seed coating on a field where the target pest appears in roughly one year in three and scouting is unreliable; (b) an aphid outbreak in a greenhouse where releasing parasitoid wasps would work but takes two weeks longer and costs more; (c) a spray to prevent purely cosmetic damage on a crop the grower says he will lose money on without it. At least one of these must come out the way I will not like — name it, and tell me why the procedure is right and my instinct is wrong.

Give me the ordering and the verdicts as settled, with your reasons, not as a menu for me to choose between. Mark the steps that are really judgment dressed up as procedure. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: which applications it will systematically get wrong, and what I should watch for in the first year in order to notice.
