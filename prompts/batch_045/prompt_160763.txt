Context on me: I'm a novelist, not a philosopher. I'm running a 90-minute workshop at a writers' conference next month, about 14 people, all adults, all fiction writers, in a hotel conference room. No budget, no AV I can count on, no prep I can ask them to do in advance, and nothing that needs consent forms or a waiver — I can hand out paper and pens and maybe by a bag of something at the corner shop.

Here's what the session is for. I'm writing a novel set in a society with a Ministry that allocates a scarce thing among citizens by comparing how much each citizen's suffering or enjoyment is worth. The scarce thing is negotiable — a painkiller, hours of quiet, years of life, whatever makes the mechanics cleanest; I can adapt the fiction to it. In my world the Ministry has already solved the problem of comparing inner lives across people, and it solved it with something I invented: every citizen, at twelve, is given an identical standardized experience, and from then on all intensities are reported as multiples of that shared reference. So my novel's version of this problem comes with a built-in answer, and what I want from the workshop is to find out whether that answer is a cheat.

So, the ask: design an activity in which fourteen writers have to actually divide a genuinely scarce thing among several claimants whose inner lives they can only guess at, where the rules force them to make comparisons of wellbeing across persons and then run into why that's a disaster. I want them to hit the problems themselves before anybody names anything. Roughly what I'd like them to walk out having felt:

- that "just ask them how much it hurts, or how much they want it" settles nothing, because self-reports arrive in different currencies, get shaped by adaptation and by what people have learned to expect, and get gamed the moment people notice the report decides the allocation;
- that any official exchange rate between persons either does violence to somebody or is doing something other than measuring — its making a moral decision and calling it a measurement;
- the greedy-claimant problem: whoever converts the resource into welfare most efficiently eats the entire budget;
- and that turning the interpersonal problem into an intrapersonal one — decide how to divide things before you learn which claimant you're going to be — looks like the way out and then quietly isn't, because it has to import something to get itself off the ground.
