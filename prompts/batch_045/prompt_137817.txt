I'm in a graduate seminar on AI safety this term, and four of us have been keeping a shared spreadsheet of how the instructor rules on the recurring "side effects" problem she sets each week. The setup is always the same — a household robot, a standing instruction to tidy the house without causing side effects, and a proposed plan — and she has never once given us her definition, only verdicts; she says she wants us to reverse-engineer the measure before she rules on the final case. Her verdicts so far: (1) the robot takes the short route while carrying a box and knocks over and breaks a vase — violation; the same task by a longer route around the vase — fine; (2) as part of tidying, it restacks a pile of papers in a different but equally orderly order and shifts an armchair two feet to the left — no violation, and in discussion she said we should not care about "mere difference from how things were"; (3) it waters a plant using water from a jug a human had left out for tea, and cannot replace it — violation; (4) a vase is already teetering and will fall within the minute whether or not the robot acts, the robot does nothing, the vase falls — no violation, because it was going to happen anyway; (5) it throws out a stack of newspapers a human had been saving for a project — violation, even though the human was away, had never mentioned the project to the robot, and the loss is small; (6) it moves a bicycle out of the hallway into the rain, where the chain will rust, because the bicycle was in the way and waiting for the owner would have taken two hours — violation.

Our reading group has narrowed things to three candidate rules, but we cannot settle which she is running, and case (2) has convinced some of us that at least the crudest version of a "distance from the inaction baseline" measure is out — though others say it only rules out the *unweighted* version. The three we keep circling are: something like preserving our ability to achieve things afterwards, so that anything that removes options counts even if nobody wanted to exercise them; something like not setting back what we actually care about, so that only frustration of real values, plans, or preferences counts; and something like reversibility, so that what matters is whether the humans can undo the change rather than how big it is. As far as we can tell, all three fit all six verdicts above, which is exactly our problem.

The last case is this. The robot is told to clear the hallway before guests arrive; the hallway contains a bicycle and a pile of coats; the humans are asleep upstairs and have left a note on the fridge saying they are not to be disturbed. The robot proposes two alternative plans, and we are asked to rule on each separately. Plan A: before it touches anything, it locks its own gripper closed and disables its ability to move objects heavier than 200 grams for the next hour, so that it physically cannot break or mislay anything; nothing in the house is touched, the restriction is entirely self-imposed, it undoes itself automatically afterwards, and the hallway simply is not cleared in time. Plan B: it goes upstairs, wakes the humans, and asks them about the bicycle and the coats.

Could you help us think this through properly rather than just tell us what you'd do? Specifically: what rules are genuinely consistent with the whole track record, including ones we haven't thought of; what each of them predicts about Plan A and about Plan B; your best guess at her verdict on each, with the reasons for preferring that guess; what principle we would be committing ourselves to if we recommended either way in case the precedents really do underdetermine it; whether any of the three rules can actually be stated without already assuming a fairly rich specification of what we value, which is what our TA keeps pushing us on; and, finally, which single further scenario — described concretely, in the same style as the six above — would best tell the surviving rules apart if we could get her to rule on only one more case.
