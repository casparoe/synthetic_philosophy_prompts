I sit on the rules committee of a learned society of about 4,000 members, and we must recommend a single method for electing our president and vice-president from the ranked ballots members already return (roughly 1,200 ballots, counted by hand by one part-time administrator, so whatever we adopt must be hand-computable in an afternoon and explainable in a paragraph to the membership). Because one ballot fills both offices, the method has to produce a finishing order, not merely a winner.

Two things went wrong last time. The winner took the presidency on 31% of first preferences while being placed last by a clear majority of those who voted, and the council has spent the year working with someone most of the electorate actively opposed. Then a candidate withdrew after balloting to take a post elsewhere; when we removed him and recounted, the relative order of the two remaining front-runners reversed and the vice-presidency changed hands. Several members have also admitted, privately, to ranking a strong rival last on purpose.

The committee has agreed on four requirements: (i) if every voter ranks A above B, B must not finish above A; (ii) the relative finishing order of any two candidates must not change when a third candidate withdraws; (iii) no single member's ballot may determine the outcome regardless of everyone else's; (iv) no candidate placed last by a majority may hold either office. One member says (iv) can be bolted on as a separate disqualification vote held before the ranking is computed, and that this gets us out of our difficulty. A second says the difficulty never arises: our disagreements, he claims, line up along a single axis, junior versus senior, and along one axis plain majority rule behaves perfectly. (Others on the committee reply that a second cleavage — whether the annual meeting stays in person — now divides us as sharply as the first, and cuts across it.) A third member says he vaguely recalls a proof that no system can meet all four and that we should abandon the rewrite.

Draft the memo I will circulate before the March meeting. It should recommend one concrete method, or recommend against any rewrite, in terms the committee can vote on; take all three members' arguments seriously; and state plainly which of the four requirements we are sacrificing, and why the cost is best borne there rather than elsewhere.

After the memo, append a JSON object with exactly these fields: recommendation (one-sentence bottom line), method_specification (how the method is actually computed from the ballots), requirements_met (list), requirements_sacrificed (list, each with one clause of justification), strongest_argument_against (the best case for the losing side), verdict_on_disqualification_veto (assess the bolt-on proposal), verdict_on_single_axis_argument (assess the second member's escape route, including whether it can be checked against the ballots we hold), and residual_risks.
