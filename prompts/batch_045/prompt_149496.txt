hey — I need a thought experiment built to spec, and I want it to be the centerpiece of a short talk I'm giving to a hospital's clinical governance committee. The line I want to land is that "if you're unsure about a diagnosis, get a second opinion, and if you're still unsure, a third" is not automatically good advice, even when every clinician you ask is better than chance and nobody is rubber-stamping anybody else. I know the Condorcet jury theorem says majority accuracy climbs toward certainty as you add independent, better-than-chance voters, and I don't want to contradict that. I want a case where the theorem's headline conclusion holds and the advice still fails.

So: build me a realistic case — a ward, a committee room, an on-call handover, whatever, not an urn or a coin — where two things come apart in exactly this direction:

(a) the probability that the majority verdict is correct is HIGHER with nine voters than with one;
(b) the expected cost of acting on the majority verdict is WORSE with nine voters than with one.

Constraints, and these are most of the work:

- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Real decision, real stakes, no science fiction, no trolleys, no stylized urns. The committee's verdict is binding and gets acted on, nobody abstains, odd numbers only so there's no tie-breaking.
- Two states of the world, and you give me a competence number for each: the voter's probability of being right conditional on that state. Justify each in a sentence, so I read it and think yes, that is about how good a tired senior resident actually is. The rare state has to be rare for a reason that's in the story.
- Every voter has the SAME two numbers. No heterogeneity, no weak-link voters dragging the average down. Whatever is doing the work, it is not "a few bad reviewers."
- Overall competence, averaged over the base rates, comfortably above 50%.
- Votes independent conditional on the state. Secret ballot, no deliberation, no deference, no cascade, nobody sees how anyone else voted. So the mechanism cannot be "they all looked at the same misleading scan" — I know that one, it isn't interesting, and it can't be "average competence is below 50%" either.
- The voters are voting on the same proposition the committee is deciding. No gap between what the voter is reliable about and what the vote is about.
- Here's the bit I haven't seen handled anywhere: each voter is well calibrated — when they say they're 90% sure, they're right 90% of the time — and the aggregation is a plain headcount that throws that confidence away. Nobody states a probability, nobody weights by one.
- The two error types have genuinely different costs (a missed something versus an unnecessary something), and the asymmetry has to fall out of the scenario rather than be asserted.
