Here is the background and the thing I have noticed. I work in the second line of defense at a large insurer, and for the past two years I have sat on the internal committee that assigns each deployed model one of two governance labels before it goes live. Roughly: "tool" (the system produces recommendations; a named human reviewer with real authority and real capacity to override signs off on each one; accountability stays with the human; light oversight regime) versus "agent" (the system is allowed to carry a multi-step task through without per-action human approval; heavier regime; the deployer carries the exposure).

What I keep watching happen is this. A system earns the "tool" label because the override is genuine: in the first months reviewers change a meaningful fraction of recommendations, and the committee can point to that as its evidence. Then the model improves and the override rate falls. Nothing is wrong yet. But three things follow. First, the skill that made the override meaningful gets exercised less and decays — a reviewer who now sees two hundred recommendations a day, of which perhaps one is wrong, and who is measured on throughput, is not in a position to catch that one. Second, the organization acquires a strong interest in the "tool" label, since the label is what keeps the system in the light regime and leaves the residual liability with the human; so the review interface, the time budget per case, and the documentation requirement all get shaped in ways that preserve the form of review. Third — and this is the part I take to be distinctive — the artifacts of the eroded practice are then offered as evidence that nothing has eroded. The file shows 99.7% of decisions "human-reviewed." That figure is cited back to the committee as the reason to renew the tool classification, and to the auditor as the reason the control is effective.

So: the conditions that justified the label are consumed by the consequences of holding it, and the accounting trail left by the degraded practice is what certifies that the conditions still hold.

Three further instances, in case they help you see the shape rather than my generalization:

- Hospital imaging: a model flags possible findings and the radiologist "reads" the flagged study. The hospital reports that every flagged study is read by a physician. Reading has quietly become confirmation.
- Public benefits eligibility: the analyst must type a justification in order to deviate from the model's recommendation; writing one up takes twenty minutes and attracts supervisory attention. Deviation rates go to near zero. The program reports a human determination in every case.
- Software: a developer reviews a generated diff line by line, accepts it, and merges. When the resulting bug ships, the developer is the person asked about it, on the grounds that a human put their name to it.

What I have already tried and set aside: "Goodhart's law" is about a measure ceasing to track its target once it becomes a target; here the measure is not so much gamed as left reading the same number while the underlying capacity quietly disappears. "Automation bias" and its neighbors get the psychology of over-trust but not the governance loop, and they say nothing about the third step, where the output of the degraded practice is offered as proof of its own integrity. "The responsibility gap," as I understand it, concerns cases where no one can be held responsible; here responsibility is assigned very precisely and even advertised, which is part of what stabilizes the arrangement.

So my question is whether there is an established concept for this exact configuration, and if not, how it should be carved.
