I'm on the third draft of a novel set inside a game studio — my narrator is a narrative designer on a big open-world thing about a besieged city, and the subplot I keep botching is her argument with the systems programmer who insists the NPCs are just data structures with a sad animation bolted on. The scene that starts it is a cutscene: to get one line reading right she reloads a save a few hundred times, and every reload means the same character gets betrayed, kneels in the street and grieves for about ninety seconds before she resets the take. She starts to feel like she is doing something to someone; he thinks that is like apologising to a crumpled draft page. I don't want the novel to settle this, I want the two of them to be visibly talking past each other for good reasons, and to write that well I need the actual shape of the disagreement instead of a fistful of vibes.

So: build me a classification instrument for the question of whether (and how) a video-game character can matter morally — a decision tree or taxonomy, roughly seven to nine branch questions and six to nine leaves, built from the logical space first rather than from a list of position names. Each branch node should be one separating claim, phrased as a question anyone could answer without knowing what it leads to, and each question should hang off a concrete case rather than a slogan. The cases I want used, at minimum: the reload farm I described; a quest that can only be finished by betraying an NPC who remembers what you did last time you loaded in; a build that gets cancelled and is never booted by a single player; a bug in a shipped game where a shopkeeper's grief routine loops forever on one abandoned console in a warehouse; the same character instantiated on a million consoles at once, so that whatever she is, there are a million of her and none of them is the original; and a modder who, years later, edits that character back into the world with more dialogue than the studio ever wrote. The distinctions I care most about are these: whether moral status tracks functional organisation rather than substrate; whether suffering that an author wrote by hand is different in kind from suffering that falls out of the physics and the scheduling system; whether it matters if nobody ever experiences the thing; whether doing the same take two hundred times is two hundred harms or one; whether status comes in degrees, so a shallow vendor has a fraction of the claim that a fully reactive companion has; whether the surprise test matters, i.e. a character doing something her writer never scripted; and whether the developer's obligations and the player's obligations can come apart, so that the studio may be fine and the guy farming the reload is not, or the reverse.

Give each leaf a name where a name exists and call it unnamed where nobody sensible defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment; I also want a short note on which combinations of answers leave the tree empty and why that cell is empty — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored — and an explicit flag on any two leaves your tree can't actually separate, with the case that would pry them apart if I needed it to. The leaves I'd like to be able to reach include: a full-status view on which the pattern is the thing that matters; a derivative view on which the character matters only because of what cruelty to her does to the player or the maker; an expressive or symbolic view on which it's what the suffering represents or flattens that's objectionable; a scalar or threshold view; a view on which instantiation by an audience is what does the work, so an unshipped build is morally inert; and a flat artifact view where the whole question is a category mistake. Tell me at the end which single branch question is doing the most work, because that's probably the one my two characters are really arguing about. Assume I've read no philosophy and please don't send me to any sources — I want it usable as an afterword in the book and as a sort-yourself quiz for my newsletter subscribers, so keep the diagnostic questions in plain language and the tone a bit playful, and don't worry if a distinction is one you had to invent; invented distinctions are fine as long as they cut somewhere real.
