I’ve been rewatching Arrival (the 2016 Denis Villeneuve film, based on Ted Chiang’s 1998 novella Story of Your Life) and I keep getting stuck on the Hannah stuff. Quick setup in case it’s needed: Louise Banks is a linguist brought in to talk to the aliens; as she learns their language she starts having what look like flashbacks of a daughter, Hannah, who dies at twelve from an incurable illness; the reveal is that these are not memories but premonitions of a child who hasn’t been born yet, because the alien language lets a person experience future events out of order. The weapon the aliens offer turns out to be the language itself, and the way I remember the ending the story seems to move towards Louise embracing the future she has seen rather than trying to prevent it.

I want a reading of the film through suffering-focused ethics / negative utilitarianism: the broad idea that intense suffering matters more, maybe lexically, than happiness, and that knowingly creating a being who will suffer and die young looks like a serious moral cost even if the life also contains love and joy. No plot summary needed and no citations. What I do want is this: does Louise’s apparent choice to go ahead and have Hannah count as a rejection of that ethical outlook, or does the film’s weird time structure give her an excuse?

To make it less hand-wavy I’ve been toying with a made-up principle, call it the already-mourned-child move. Normally, if someone is deciding whether to conceive a child and knows in advance that the child will undergo intense suffering and an early death, a suffering-focused view says that expected suffering is a heavy cost, perhaps decisive. But Louise does not decide in ignorance: she has already lived through the grief of Hannah’s death as a kind of memory before Hannah exists. The already-mourned-child move says this pre-lived grief changes the moral picture: either it partly offsets the badness because the parent has already paid some of the emotional cost, or, in a stronger retrospective-negative-utilitarian form, it means the suffering is in a sense already actual and accepted, so the choice is not one of producing suffering but of consenting to a future that is tenselessly there. Does that move actually hold up, or does it trade on a confusion between Louise’s anticipatory grief and Hannah’s own suffering? Does Arrival dramatise the move honestly, or does it smuggle in a sentimental thought that the value of the parent’s love somehow outweighs the child’s pain?

I also want the reading to say where the film resists the lens. Maybe Arrival is not really a procreation-ethics story at all: it's non-linear time is a metaphor for grief, love, and mortality, and a hard-nosed suffering-focused ethicist would say the heptapod gift is monstrous, because it removes ignorance without removing the suffering, and the right response would be not to have the child. Where would you say the film adds something to the ethics that a straight negative-utilitarian argument misses—maybe about what we owe to futures we can foresee but not change, or about whether love for a future person makes their suffering matter less to the lover but not less impersonally—and where does it get the ethics wrong or just change the subject? And, as a seperate question, does the ambiguity of the aliens message (the bit rendered as something like offer weapon, which China reads as use weapon) matter here, or is that just a thriller macguffin?

Please write this as a piece of criticism: faithful to the film (no distorting it into a mere illustration) and faithful to the ethical view (no watering it down to fit the movie). If the honest verdict is that the film’s resolution is morally evasive, say so, and say which scenes or plot turns you’d point to.
