I'm auditing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and I keep turning over an argument I overheard between two of my classmates at the pub after class last week. They were arguing about whether epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experiences are produced by physical processes but themselves cause nothing further, either physical or mental — can survive what is often called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I took notes on my phone and have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can below; I have no stake in who wins, and honestly both of them sounded right at different moments. (1) Ana opened with the paradox itself: if experiences cause nothing, then my saying 'I am in pain' is fully explained by the neural state that produces the utterance, and the pain itself plays no role in bringing it about. So the view seems to saw off the branch it is sitting on: the reason either of us believes there are experiences at all is that we report having them, and on this view a physically identical being with no experiences whatsoever would emit exactly the same reports. That doesn't look like knowledge of one's own consciousness; it looks like a correlation one has no right to trust. (2) Ben replied that the correlation is not brute but lawlike — the psychophysical laws guarantee that neural state N occurs if and only if experience E occurs, so the report is a perfectly reliable indicator of E — and that knowledge does not in general require the fact known to cause the belief: we know mathematical truths and facts about the future, neither of which causally prod us into believing them. To insist that the pain must cause the report is, he said, to smuggle in a causal theory of knowledge as a premise. (3) Ana said the general point is too quick, because in those other cases there is no rival explanation of the belief that leaves the fact known out of the picture, whereas here there plainly is: the zombie case she had just described. And she pressed a second, deeper problem that she said reliability does not touch — reference. What makes 'pain' in my mouth pick out the feeling rather than the neural state or the functional role? Causal and informational theories of reference fix reference by a link running from the referent to the speaker, exactly the link epiphenomenalism forbids; and if you retreat to acquaintance — I am directly acquainted with the feeling, so I can name it — then acquaintance starts to look like just the sort of causal transaction the view says never occurs. (4) Ben answered that acquaintance is not a causal relation but a constitutive or intentional one: my thought about the number seven is about seven without seven doing anything to me, and nothing in epiphenomenalism prevents a state from being directed at an experience it does not cause. Content is thus secured without any causal work, and lawlike reliability then converts that content into knowledge. (5) Ana's next move was a dilemma. If content is fixed by acquaintance and the neural-to-phenomenal mapping is lawful, the view predicts a first-person infallibility we plainly do not have — people mislabel their experiences constantly, taking anxiety for hunger or nostalgia for contentment. The tempting escape is what she called 'inward-only epiphenomenalism': keep the claim that experiences cause nothing physical, but allow experiences to cause further experiences, so that the pain can cause the conscious judgment that one is in pain and the paradox dissolves. But, she argued, this is unstable: that judgment is itself a physically realized state whose physical causes are already complete, so the phenomenal cause is either redundant — the causal exclusion problem reappearing in a new costume — or it is doing genuine work, in which case the view has quietly ceased to be epiphenomenalism. (6) Ben said the exclusion worry only bites if phenomenal-to-phenomenal causation is competing with physical causation for the same job, and there is no reason to assume that; it can be treated as a distinct, non-competing relation. Mislabelling, he added, is an error in applying a learned concept, not a failure of acquaintance, so it costs him nothing. He then floated, without fully endorsing it, a last resort: first-person utterances are better understood as avowals or expressions rather than descriptions, so no truth-tracking relation needs to be secured in the first place. (7) Ana closed by calling that last resort a trap. The main reason anyone takes epiphenomenalism seriously is the knowledge argument about the neuroscientist who has never seen colour and seems to learn something new when she sees red for the first time — and that argument needs her to acquire a genuine fact. If first-person speech is mere expression, there is no new fact acquired, and the argument that motivated taking experiences seriously as something extra collapses. So, she concluded, the two standard defences pull in opposite directions: the reliabilist one preserves knowledge but only by making it look like an unexplained correlation, and the expressivist one dissolves the paradox but undercuts the motive for holding the view.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who is right about what — ideally rating each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification each time, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing. My own thought, such as it is, is that both may have been assuming that the judgment about an experience must be a state wholly distinct from the experience it is about; if that assumption were dropped — if a conscious judgment could be partly constituted by the very experience it concerns — would the paradox even get off the ground, or does it survive in another form? I'd be grateful for your view on whether that is a real way out for the epiphenomenalist or just a relabelling of Ben's acquaintance move.
