I sit on a small grantmaking committee that funds work on the governance of advanced AI, and I have noticed that our deliberations are not merely illustrated by historical analogies — they are settled by them. Two do all the work. When a member wants to argue against controls on frontier models, the argument is that this is the printing press, not the bomb: a technology that multiplies a general-purpose human capacity; suppression that fails and discredits the suppressors; harms that arrive through diffusion rather than detonation; and a historical lesson that societies coped by raising literacy and rebuilding institutions around cheap print rather than by restricting the presses. When a member wants the opposite, the argument is that this is the bomb: a handful of actors, a dual-use capability, consequences that cannot be undone, and therefore a nonproliferation-style regime of inspections and controls on the scarce input. Both arguments are convincing in the room and they point in opposite directions, which makes me suspect the analogy is carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it.

Please audit both analogies as arguments, not as decoration. I would like:

1. An explicit correspondence table for each — what plays the role of the scarce input, of the state, of the press or the warhead, of the tract or the blast, of the censor or the inspector, and of the adaptation that is supposed to have saved us. Where a slot has no plausible filler in the target case, say so; an empty cell is a finding.

2. A sorting of the disanalogies into those that bear on the conclusion each analogy is used for and those that are merely vivid. The test I want applied is counterfactual: if the target were made to match the source on that feature, would the recommended policy have to change? If not, the disanalogy is decorative. Two features I suspect are load-bearing are the legibility of the capability to outside observers (a test detonation is public; a training run is not) and whether the relevant actors are states with territories or firms with balance sheets.

3. An assessment using a distinction I have been trying out, and which you should refine or replace if it does not hold up: technologies can be additive (they let humans do something new), substitutive (they replace a human input, as with mechanical power), or delegative (they take over the choosing that humans previously did, not merely the executing). Locate the printing press, nuclear weapons, the factory system, and advanced AI on this three-way distinction, and say what each analogy's power depends on: my suspicion is that both of our house analogies are substitutive-era stories being used to reason about a delegative technology, and that this is where they quietly fail.

4. A discriminating prediction for each analogy. If the printing-press frame is the right one, what should we expect to observe about advanced AI over the next decade that we would not expect if the bomb frame is right, and vice versa? If an analogy licenses no observation that could tell against it, I want it flagged as unfalsifiable rather than balanced.

5. A candidate for a better frame, audited to the same standard rather than merely asserted. The one I keep coming back to is the industrial fixation of nitrogen: a technology that removed a binding natural constraint on a critical input, with consequences that were enormous, delayed, diffuse, poorly attributed to their cause, and never concentrated in a single catastrophic event; dual-use in the most literal sense; and governed, such as it was, long after the fact. Tell me what that mapping gets right, where it breaks, and whether it supports any governance conclusion — or propose a better one.

6. A bottom line: which conclusion, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and if neither analogy can carry a conclusion on its own, what kind of argument would actually have to be made directly.
