You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of the metaphysics of propositions and of the arguments that cluster around Bradley's regress; you have also done editorial work, so you know how to draft wording that two mutually impatient parties will both actually sign. Read the whole brief before writing. Begin with a clearly marked scratch-work section in which you decide, provisionally, which of the disagreements below are substantive and which are fights about labels, and note any place where my own framing begs a question. Then draft the deliverable. Do not adjudicate: where the two sides cannot be brought to agree, make the residual disagreement exact rather than resolving it.

The situation. I am convening a three-session working group on the unity of the proposition, and I want the two participants to end with a joint statement instead of another exchange of papers. Call them the structuralist and the primitivist.

- The structuralist holds that propositions are structured complexes built from constituents by modes of combination, and that the regress is generated by an illegitimate assumption: that the relation is a saturated item which must be hooked to its relata by something further. Once the relation is treated as unsaturated, or the combining is done by a typed mode of combination (or by an act-type of predication) rather than by an additional constituent, the regress stops. Unity is explained rather than assumed.
- The primitivist holds that no combinatorial story explains unity: every candidate unifier is either a further constituent, in which case the regress resumes, or it is a primitive unity smuggled in under a name like mode of combination, unsaturatedness, or predicative act. The right moral is the priority of the whole: a proposition is a primitive unity, and its constituents are abstractions from it rather than items out of which it is assembled.

The argument on the table, as I want it stated. (1) The proposition that Brutus stabbed Caesar has Brutus, stabbing, and Caesar as constituents. (2) Those constituents can exist without composing it; a list, a set, or a mereological sum of them is not the proposition and says nothing. (3) Insufficiency premise: therefore something beyond the constituents and their mere coexistence is needed. (4) Parity premise: whatever does the uniting must itself be something that stands to the constituents as they stood to the proposition, and so needs uniting with them in turn. (5) Hence an infinite regress of ties, no completed proposition, and no account of what makes the complex a truth-evaluable, directed whole rather than a heap.

Three diagnostics I have been using, which you should apply or reject. (a) The insufficiency/parity split above: I want to know which premise each camp rejects, because I suspect the structuralist's answer is parity while the primitivist's own answer would have to be neither, and I want to see whether that asymmetry is principled or evasive. (b) Thick versus thin regress: on a thick reading each level of the hierarchy adds genuine constituents and genuine requirements on anyone who grasps the proposition; on a thin reading the levels are virtual, adding no constituents and nothing a finite thinker must entertain. Classify regress under each camp's view, and say whether a thin regress is any kind of solution or only a redescription. (c) The abstraction test: can the constituents be specified independently of the proposition, or are they recoverable only by abstracting from the whole? I take independent specifiability to be the structuralist's signature and abstraction-only to be the primitivist's, and I want to know whether either camp can switch signatures without cost.

What I want in the document.

Part I, agreed claims, numbered, each with a one-line note on what work it does. Start from my candidates, keep the ones that are not vacuous, and delete or sharpen the ones that are. (i) The proposition that Brutus stabbed Caesar differs from the proposition that Caesar stabbed Brutus, and both differ from any aggregate of the same items; an account must say where that difference lives. (ii) No unordered set, list, or sum of constituents is the proposition. (iii) The naive view on which the unifier is an ordinary further relation among the constituents is rejected by both, on pain of regress. (iv) Any solution must generalize beyond the true dyadic case: it must handle the monadic proposition that Brutus was ambitious, the negated proposition that Brutus did not stab Caesar, and false propositions generally, which have unity without a corresponding fact. (v) A set-theoretic coding of propositions is not by itself an answer unless accompanied by an account of what makes the coding truth-evaluable and about its constituents. (vi) A finite thinker's grasp of a proposition cannot require entertaining infinitely many levels; both camps accept this constraint and use it differently.

Part II, a disagreements table with columns: crux; the formulation the structuralist would accept for their own view; the formulation the primitivist would accept for theirs; and genuine or verbal, with a one-line justification. I expect at least these cruxes: whether the regress is generated at all, or only generated given a saturated-constituent assumption; whether the monadic case raises the same problem or is solved for free; whether a mode of combination, an unsaturated entity, or a predicative act-type is a principled stopping point or a primitive unity under another name; whether the directedness of the proposition reduces to a primitive of ordering that is itself as brute as unity; whether explaining truth conditions suffices, or whether aboutness is the real thing to be explained; and whether the dispute is verbal at the point where the primitivist says unity is primitive and the structuralist says the applicability of a mode of combination is a primitive it is happy to accept.

Part III, for each crux, one test or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Candidate tests, which you may replace: run each account on the monadic case and see whether it needs the same machinery as the dyadic case; locate the asymmetry between the proposition and its mirror and ask whether locating it there reintroduces a tie; the false-instance test, especially for a primitivist tempted to inherit unity from facts; the comprehension question of what restricts which combinations of constituents form a proposition, and whether any proposition can be a constituent of itself; and the question of what a thinker must grasp in order to grasp a mode of combination, if that is not itself grasping a further proposition.

Part IV, concessions: one claim each side should concede that it currently resists, and what each would count as evidence or argument against its own view.

Part V, a short coda telling me which of my three diagnostics is misleading, question-begging, or should be sharpened, and what should replace it.
