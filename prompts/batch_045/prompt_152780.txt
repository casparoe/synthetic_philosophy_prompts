Last year I was told I have perhaps a decade of good health left, and ever since I have been unable to settle an argument with my brother about how to spend it. He says — and I cannot locate the flaw — that since I will be dead either way, and since humanity's eventual extinction will erase everything anyone does, my urge to put my remaining money and energy into an eighty-year forest restoration project is sentimentality wearing the costume of principle; I should travel while I still can.

What unsettles me is not that he may be right about the project, but that his argument leaks. Taken seriously, it seems to drain next month's concert with my daughter of its point as well, and that feels like a reductio whose mechanism I can't name. I have also tried three variations on myself. In the first, humanity ends thirty days after I die; in the second, three hundred years later, long after everyone I know is gone. The second is clearly less disturbing to me, which is strange, since in neither case will I meet or be remembered by a single survivor. In the third, humanity continues for millennia, but no one born after a certain date has any use for forests, music, or anything else I care about; that one hollows me out nearly as much as the first, though differently.

Is my brother's reasoning sound, and if not, where does it break? Is my stake in a future I won't inhabit best explained as self-interest, sentiment, or as a background condition of valuing anything at all? Are my three variations really doing different work, or am I mistaking one fear for two? Please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, flag the places where your answer depends on a contested reading rather than a consensus one, and don't send me off to read anything.
