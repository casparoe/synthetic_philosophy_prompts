I work as a backend engineer (mostly Go and Python) and have no formal philosophy background; I ended up in a reading group where we got onto metaethics, and the view called expressivism struck me as a design decision I have seen before: moral sentences, on this story, are not in the business of describing how things are. They are more like structured exclamations — sophisticated versions of boo and hurrah — whose job is to vent or coordinate attitudes rather than to state facts. Then someone raised what the group called the Frege–Geach problem, and it sounded exactly like a composability bug: the same sentence has to contribute the same thing whether it is asserted on its own or embedded unasserted inside a conditional, under a negation, in a question, after Sam believes that, or inside a quantifier — but on the expressivist story there is no attitude being expressed in those embedded positions, so it is unclear what is composing. The canonical casualty is the argument whose first premise is a conditional (if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong), whose second premise is the antecedent (lying is wrong), and whose conclusion is the consequent (getting your little brother to lie is wrong). That inference looks valid, and it looks valid for the boring reason that it is an instance of modus ponens. If the two occurrences of lying is wrong do not mean the same thing in the two premises, the inference has nothing to lean on.

I would like you to write a mock internal engineering RFC that makes this predicament funny by taking it seriously.

**Form and voice.** A design doc in the house style of a large software company: a header with status, author, and reviewers; sections for Summary, Motivation, Guide-level explanation, Reference-level explanation, Drawbacks, Alternatives considered, and Unresolved questions; and a discussion thread at the bottom with two or three reviewer comments. About 900–1100 words, markdown, with small Python-ish or pseudocode samples in fenced blocks. The voice is a senior engineer who is entirely confident, entirely reasonable, and wrong in a way they cannot see: deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, no footnotes outside the fiction explaining what is being parodied.

**What the RFC proposes.** That the team refactor its moral vocabulary: since moral sentences only express attitudes, they should be typed as side-effecting expressions that return nothing, and calls to them should be hoisted out of any position that requires a value. This works until it meets embedded occurrences. Please make the failure reproducible, with a failing unit test (something like a test asserting that modus ponens over the moral module still passes) and a note that the failure is not a regression introduced by the refactor.

**What the satire has to get right.** The comedy should come from the real shape of the view and of the workarounds its defenders actually reach for, not from a strawman:

- The negation case: if the assertoric use of a moral sentence is an attitude of disapproval, the negation has available to it only the absence of that attitude, which is not the same as approval — the RFC should notice this and move on briskly.
- The higher-order workaround: the standard repair is to say that a moral conditional expresses an attitude directed at a *combination* of attitudes (disapproval of accepting the antecedent while rejecting the consequent, roughly). Have the RFC present this as a wrapper type, a thunk, or a monad that restores composability, and have the Drawbacks section admit in passing that the wrapper has never been proved to validate modus ponens — merely that it feels like it does, and that the team is comfortable shipping it.
- The quasi-realist ending: the final section should recommend that, the refactor being complete, the team continue to use the words true, fact, and believe exactly as before, now with a thinner meaning that costs nothing, and resolve the original bug as working as intended. A lint rule for inconsistent attitudes can stand in for the whole of moral reasoning.

**The reviewer comments.** One from a representationalist who refuses to approve because they think the entire premise is a category mistake; one from a colleague who thinks the module should be deleted outright, since nothing is ever morally anything at all; optionally one drive-by from someone asking whether any of this changes what anyone does on Monday. They should sound like engineers in a pull request, not like philosophers.

**Constraints.** Do not use bibliographies, citations, or quotations from real philosophical works, and do not refer to any specific article, book, or author by name — a reader should need nothing beyond what is above. Do not have any character state the moral of the piece plainly. The target is the position and its usual escape hatches, not a person. Someone who works in metaethics should finish it feeling that the parody was fair and that the author understood what is hard about the embedding problem; someone who has never read any of this should still follow the engineering story and find it funny.
