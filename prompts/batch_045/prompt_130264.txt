I am trying to work out what I actually think should be equalized — welfare, resources, capabilities, or something in the same neighbourhood — by working case by case rather than by first adopting a theory. Below are my verdicts, given on gut. I would like to know whether a single principle underwrites them, and where it has to overrule me.

(1) Louis and Marta begin with identical resources and identical prospects. Louis deliberately trains his palate on vintage champagne over two years, fully aware of what it will cost him; Marta is content with ordinary wine. Equal resources now leave Louis less happy. Verdict: he has no complaint and is owed nothing.

(2) Marie develops an unforeseen metabolic condition. Without a costly diet she cannot function or feel as others do, and she could not have avoided it. Verdict: she is owed enough extra to bring her to the level others reach as a matter of course.

(3) Ahmed's religion requires an expensive annual pilgrimage and a restricted diet. He endorses the religion, but he did not select it from a menu, and leaving it would cost him his family and his sense of who he is. Verdict: no claim to additional resources, but the state would wrong him by, say, taxing the pilgrimage or scheduling inescapable civic duties on his holy days. I cannot say why this differs from case (1).

(4) Kay belongs to a community whose norms of hospitality require her to host expensive feasts; to abstain is to lose the relationships that make her life what it is. She knew the cost and goes on paying it. Verdict: some claim — weaker than Marie's, stronger than Louis's.

(5) Priya uses a wheelchair and is, by her own account, perfectly happy. Her income equals everyone else's, but the streets and buildings were not built for her. Verdict: she is owed something, and it is not satisfiable in cash: the infrastructure has to change. Her happiness is beside the point.

(6) Rosa was raised to expect very little and is cheerful on a fraction of what others have. Verdict: she is not thereby equal to them, and we still owe her more. Her happiness is again beside the point.

(7) Two equally resourced people take the same actuarially fair risk; one loses and ends up far worse off. Verdict: the loser has a claim not to fall below a decent floor, but no claim to equality, and the winner owes nothing back. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine principle or squeamishness — Marie sits at the same level by brute luck, and there I compensated her fully.

(8) A world in which some people are blind, blindness is incurable, and the sighted could be blinded at no cost and to no one's benefit. Verdict: there is no reason whatsoever to blind the sighted. Equality is not worth pursuing for its own sake.

The distinction I have tried and cannot make work: requirements that are expensive merely to have the option of meeting (Ahmed's pilgrimage must be available whether or not he goes) versus requirements that are expensive only when exercised (Louis must actually drink the champagne). It fails to separate Marie from Louis, so I suspect it is the wrong cut.

The case I keep reversing on: Noor converts as an adult, after full reflection, to a faith with costly requirements she did not grow up with. She sits somewhere between (1) and (4), and I do not know which way to go.

My tentative principle: people are owed equality in the capabilities they can actually exercise; a shortfall generates a claim only when it is not traceable to the person's own choices; and equality is not itself a goal, only the removal of involuntary shortfall is. The middle clause and the last clause already look to me as though they are fighting each other, and I doubt the first clause can handle (6).

So: state the strongest principle you can find that fits these verdicts, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether you would have me give them up; tell me what it implies for Noor; and give its verdicts on a kind of case I have not listed — people whose requirements become expensive because of other people's uncoordinated choices, such as housing in a city everyone is moving to, or goods whose price is driven by what others bid for them.
