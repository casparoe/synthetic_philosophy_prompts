My roommate's a molecular biologist, I'm a philosophy grad student, and we keep going round on whether DNA is really a code or whether that's just a lab habit of speech. She finally wrote her argument down for me and I think it proves way too much, but every step looks fine to me individually. Here it is, roughly in her words:

(1) A string of symbols is a code only if the mapping from symbols to what they stand for is arbitrary — set by convention, not forced by the physics of the symbols themselves. (Her example: the word "dog" doesn't resemble a dog, and a photograph of a dog isn't a code for a dog.)
(2) The mapping from codons to amino acids is arbitrary: nothing about the triplet's chemistry calls for its amino acid, the link is made by tRNA synthetases and tRNAs, which are physical machinery, and the code even varies a bit between lineages (mitochondrial tables differ from the standard one, and I half-remember something about a yeast where a leucine codon actually specifies serine).
(3) So the genome is a code and genes carry semantic information: a stretch of DNA means an amino acid sequence. That's the bit she wants, and I basically want it too.
(4) But now notice what fixes which amino acid a codon means: just what the translating machinery does with it. The machinery is the "convention," and it operates by chemistry, not by interpreting anything.
(5) So take any physically specific structure that some downstream mechanism reliably turns into a specific product — a synthetic random-sequence mRNA in a cell-free extract, the shape of a substrate relative to the enzyme that acts on it, the maternal mRNAs and proteins dumped into an egg cytoplasm that the embryo then acts on. By (4) each of these is a code for its product.
(6) And the meaning looks context-bound anyway: the same string gets different readings under alternative splicing, RNA editing, recoding or a shifted frame, so meaning isn't sitting in the string.
(7) Here's the case I made up that bugs her: suppose we synthesized a lineage whose translation table is a uniform permutation of ours, and rewrote its genome to match. It builds exactly the same proteins, lives exactly the same life, but every codon "means" a different amino acid. Nothing changed except the code. To me that says the code is extrinsic and (3) is doing no work; she says it shows two different codes can express the same message, which is exactly what you'd expect if it were a code.
(8) So either "code" collapses into mere causal specificity — true of tree rings, thermometers and riverbeds, and hence not something that marks genes out as special — or nothing literally carries genetic information and half the vocabulary of molecular biology is metaphor.

Both horns look wrong to me: I don't want to say the genome is just another riverbed, and I don't want to say molecular biologists are speaking figuratively. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? A couple of things people in my reading group have thrown out, which I want priced rather than repeated: one person says premise (2) isn't clean, because there are stereochemical-affinity results and something about the actual code being unusually good at buffering translation errors — if that's right, does it undercut (2) or only the jump from (2) to (3)? Another says codes need proper functions supplied by selection, which handles my test-tube case, but then I want to know whose selection — the gene's or the organism's, and haven't I just walked into the units-of-selection swamp I was trying to stay out of — and what we'd say about the first self-replicators, before there was selection to supply any functions. And please don't just tell me it depends what I mean by information; tell me which sense of information each premise needs and why the argument can't have all of them at once.
