Some context, so we are on the same page. I am not an academic — I test accounting software for a living and have been thinking about this on my own for about two years. I gather some philosophers call the arrangement I am about to describe "moral trade," though I arrived at it independently. About forty of us, mostly strangers who met through a giving forum, run what we call the exchange: instead of pooling our money into one fund and fighting about priorities, we each agree to spend some of our own giving budget on things other members care about more than we do, in return for them doing the same for us. Someone who cares most about animal welfare pays for a malaria program; someone who cares most about global health pays for a cage-free campaign; both end up with more of what they wanted than if they had each spent on their own cause. A rotating three-person panel reviews any trade a member flags, and can approve it, reject it, or approve it with conditions. Panel reasons are not formally binding, but in practice members cite them constantly, and we are all fairly embarrassed about ruling inconsistently.

Here are the eight rulings I have managed to dig out of the old meeting notes, with the short reasons the panel gave.

1. APPROVED. Two members swapped EUR 400 each: bednets one way, a cage-free corporate campaign the other. Both described the other's cause as "genuinely fine, just not what I would pick." Payments were verified by receipt within the cycle. Reason: nobody was asked to fund anything they thought was bad, and both sides came out ahead.

2. APPROVED (one dissent). A wealthy member put in EUR 4,000 to a literacy nonprofit a student member cared about; the student put in EUR 1,000 to the wealthy member's priority. The amounts and the sacrifices were both lopsided. Reason: what matters is the gain on each side, not matching amounts or matching effort; dissent argued that a trade this lopsided is really a donation wearing a costume.

3. REJECTED. A member who believes a particular industry lobbying group is doing net harm offered to donate EUR 600 to it if another member funded his animal-welfare charity. Reason: we do not approve trades where someone spends money on something they themselves think is bad.

4. APPROVED. A member who thinks a community arts program is worthless but harmless funded EUR 500 of it in exchange for EUR 500 to a crisis-hotline charity. Reason: thinking something is a waste is not the same as thinking it is bad; case 3 was about harm.

5. REJECTED (one dissent). A member offered to fund another member's deworming charity. The second member had said in the group chat that being covered on deworming was what let her move her own EUR 1,500 to her church's overseas mission work, which the first member regards as actively harmful. Reason: if your money only frees up her own budget for something you think is harmful, you are paying for that thing. The dissent: she might have done the mission work regardless, so his money causes nothing and he should simply take the deworming.

6. REJECTED. A member offered to change careers into biosecurity research — a ten-year commitment she could not prove she would keep — in exchange for two members funding her animal-welfare charity over the same period. Reason: we cannot verify a decade of someone's career, and we do not accept promises about how someone lives as the currency of a trade.

7. APPROVED WITH CONDITIONS. A philanthropist outside the group, who funds space settlement (most of us think this is close to useless), offered to fund EUR 5,000 of a member's cause in exchange for EUR 5,000 to hers. Approved on condition that both payments passed through the group's escrow account and were released only on proof of payment. Reason: we do not need to share someone's values to trade with them; we need to be sure they actually pay.

8. REJECTED. A member offered to fund a literacy program that the other party's own foundation had already publicly committed EUR 20,000 to that year. Reason: this buys nothing that would not have happened anyway.

The case in front of us now. Ana, a senior engineer, cares almost entirely about farmed-animal welfare. Tomas, a freelance translator, cares almost entirely about global health. Proposed trade: Ana gives EUR 2,500 to a deworming program; Tomas gives EUR 2,500 to a cage-free campaign. Ana thinks deworming is genuinely good, just far lower priority than her own cause. Tomas is neutral-to-positive on cage-free work. Four complications, all of which came out in the thread:

- Tomas has already earmarked EUR 1,000 of his own money for that very cage-free campaign, so part of his side would have happened anyway.
- Tomas says that with deworming covered he will redirect "a few hundred euros" — he puts it at maybe half of what he frees up — to a ballot measure that would block farmed-animal welfare regulations. Ana regards that measure as harmful; by her own estimate money to it does roughly three times as much damage per euro as cage-free money does good.
- We keep a public ledger of who funded what for whom. Tomas's allies intend to cite Ana's deworming contribution as evidence that even committed animal advocates put global health first when spending their own money. Ana thinks that will cost her credibility with other funders in her own cause, and she cares about that a lot. I have started calling this "credibility transfer": your money travels with your name, and your name carries weight with your own side, which the other side can then spend against you. Nothing in our precedents addresses it directly.
- The EUR 2,500 is to be paid in three annual instalments, each verifiable by receipt. It is a trivial sum for Ana and a significant one for Tomas.
