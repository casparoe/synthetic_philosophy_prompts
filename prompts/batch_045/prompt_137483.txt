I am a high school student in an advanced ethics seminar, and we have been discussing whether the extinction of the human race would be bad in a way that goes beyond the enormous number of deaths it would involve. After class, a friend who had read a popular article on Samuel Scheffler’s afterlife conjecture tried to explain the view to me, and she summarized it like this: ‘Scheffler’s central claim is that our ability to regard our activities as meaningful depends on a largely unnoticed assumption: that human life will continue after we die. His main thought experiment asks us to imagine learning that humanity will go extinct thirty days after our own death. In that case, we would see that nothing we do now can be part of an ongoing human story or be appreciated by anyone later, so we would lose our conviction that our projects, relationships, and everyday pursuits have any point. This shows that the badness of human extinction, over and above the deaths it involves, is that it would destroy the point of everything we value by leaving no future people to benefit from or enjoy what we do. The deeper moral Scheffler draws is that we are not nearly as selfish as we usually assume: the survival of the human race matters to us much more than our own survival, which is why we ought to stop fearing our own deaths and instead dedicate ourselves to making sure that humanity has a long future.’ I found the summary both clarifying and slightly too pat, but I do not know enough to tell where it goes wrong.

I am suspicious of two parts in particular. The claim that extinction is bad because it would leave no future people to benefit from or enjoy what we do sounds to me like it makes Scheffler’s view more straightforwardly consequentialist, or more dependent on an audience, than I remember from seminar. And the practical takeaway—that we should stop fearing our own deaths and simply serve the species—strikes me as in tension with what we read about Scheffler’s view that the fear of death can be reasonable and that personal immortality would not even be desirable, even though he also thinks the survival of humanity matters in a distinctive way. Still, I cannot tell whether these are fair readings of the article’s summary or just my own confusion.

I have also been turning over a variant case of my own, which I call the ‘cultural dark age’ case: suppose that humans do not go extinct, but that within a single generation every language, record, and cultural tradition is destroyed, so that the species survives biologically but there is no continuity of human practices, memory, or shared forms of life. I am not asking for a bibliography, citations, or a reading list; I just need an accuracy audit I can use in my paper. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a defender of Scheffler would accept, which are subtle overstatements or conflations of claims he keeps distinct, and what a defender would object to. Then tell me whether the article’s practical conclusion—that we should stop fearing death and simply serve the species—follows from the accurate parts of the summary. Finally, if it is relevant, explain how a Schefflerian should handle my ‘cultural dark age’ variant, and whether thinking about that variant helps show why the summary is unfair.
