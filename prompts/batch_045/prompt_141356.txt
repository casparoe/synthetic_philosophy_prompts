I help run my school's philosophy club, and the teacher who advises us rules on the example cases we bring in. She keeps separating a reason to have some attitude from a reason to want to have it or to get yourself into it, and her verdicts so far are these. A stranger offers you fifty dollars to believe that the cafeteria serves pizza on Fridays: a reason to get yourself to believe it, not a reason to believe it. The smell of smoke in the hallway: a reason to believe there is a fire, and a reason to be alarmed. The fact that being alarmed would get you out of the building quickly even if there is no fire: not a reason to be alarmed. A friend's joke really is clever: a reason to be amused; the fact that laughing would get you added to the group chat: not a reason to be amused. A classmate who practised all summer and still tutors the ninth-graders: a reason to admire her; the fact that admiring her would make you practise harder: not a reason to admire her.

The case I have written for next week is one I cannot predict. My lab partner has been unreliable all year, but she is the sort of person who becomes reliable exactly when someone visibly counts on her and who confirms low expectations when nobody does; grant that if I trust her with this project she will come through, and that if I withhold trust she will not. Is the fact that trusting her would make her trustworthy a reason to trust her?

I care less about the verdict itself than about the rule she is running — whether a consideration has to bear on whether the attitude fits what it is about, or whether some other relation between the attitude and something good can do the same work — which rules fit her past verdicts just as well, what each of them predicts here, and what further case I should bring her that would force a choice between them. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine this one, I would also like to know what she would be committing to with each answer. One thing I suspect matters: trust looks to me like partly a prediction and partly a relationship, and I cannot tell whether that should split her rules.
