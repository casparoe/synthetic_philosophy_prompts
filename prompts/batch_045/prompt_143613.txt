I write about science and ideas, and I am finishing a long piece on how large philanthropic funders decide which problems to work on. Much of the piece turns on the three-factor scoring scheme that circulates in that world: a cause area is rated for importance, tractability and neglectedness, and the three ratings are multiplied to produce a score. I have become convinced of a thesis about that scheme, I have an interview next week with practitioners who use it daily, and before I commit the thesis to print I want to know what would make me abandon it.

The thesis is that the multiplication has no independent normative content. It is an epistemic workaround that dissolves under full information, and it dissolves in a specific, arithmetically demonstrable way.

Set it up as follows. For each cause area let x be the cumulative resources already committed to it and let V(x) be the cumulative value produced, so that V'(x) is the marginal value of the next dollar. Assume for the moment that causes are separable and that the budget is fixed. Then the correct target is simply V'(x): put the next dollar where the marginal value is highest. Now write the three factors the way practitioners write them. Importance is the scale of the remaining problem, call it S. Tractability is the fraction of that problem solved per dollar at the margin, which is V'(x)/S. Neglectedness is the inverse of the resources already in the field, 1/x (some versions divide by problem size first, which only makes what follows worse). Multiply: S cancels, and the score is proportional to V'(x)/x, not to V'(x). The framework is charging crowded fields twice for the same thing -- once through the curvature of the returns curve, which is already inside V', and once again through neglectedness. The two rankings coincide only when the candidate causes happen to be equally funded, which is the one case in which nobody needs a prioritisation framework. On this reading the scheme's famous outputs -- it points away from fashionable causes toward obscure ones -- are artefacts of a proxy rather than discoveries about value.

I am aware of what I think is the strongest reply, and I want it developed rather than repeated: that neglectedness is not a second charge for diminishing returns but a proxy for counterfactual displacement. If the relevant question is not what my dollar produces but what my dollar produces that would not have happened anyway, and if crowded fields are crowded with funders who would have financed the next project regardless, then the naive V'(x) target is itself wrong and the extra factor is correcting it. I do not think this reply is free: funding levels also track how tractable a field is and whether competent actors exist in it, so 1/x may be conflating crowding with difficulty while purporting to measure only the former.

What I want from you is a map of the exposure of my thesis, not a general verdict on the framework. Please do not give me citations, the literature, or claims about who has said what; I cannot check them and they will not survive into the piece.

1. Rank the things that would count against the thesis from fatal to merely awkward, and say which are live rather than merely logically possible. I have named displacement and separability; I suspect there are others, including cases where the returns curve is not concave at the relevant scale -- threshold effects, minimum viable scale, tournament-like payoffs where a few large wins dominate and expected marginal value is the wrong guide -- and cases where spending itself manufactures tractability.

2. Separate what a committed user of the three-factor scheme would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss as a misreading of what the scheme is for. I need this distinction for the interview: I want to know which of my points they can fairly wave away.

3. Give me one worked pair of cause areas where the marginal-value ranking and the three-factor ranking come apart, and where the three-factor answer is the one that seems intuitively right. A single such case, with the arithmetic shown, would move me more than any methodological argument, and its absence would raise my confidence considerably.

4. What does my thesis predict that its rivals do not? I think it predicts that as estimates of returns curves improve, the weight given to neglectedness should fall, and that within-cause comparisons, where curvature can actually be estimated, should not use the factor at all. Both look checkable in principle. What else?

5. Tell me honestly whether I have insulated the thesis. If my response to any apparent counterexample is that the practitioner has mis-specified one of the three factors, I have made my claim unfalsifiable; I want to know where the line between that manoeuvre and a legitimate reply falls.

6. Finally, assess the available retreat. If defenders say the scheme was never a decision rule at all but a prompt to think with, a way of forcing funders to ask questions they would otherwise skip, is that a fair description of how it is used or a demotion forced by objections like mine -- and if it is a fair description, does my thesis still have a target?
