I am a novelist, and I have written myself into an argument I cannot get out of. I would be grateful for a diagnosis, addressed to someone with no formal training in philosophy but with a strong interest in getting this right.

The novel, now in its third draft, turns on a figure I call the Sink: a young woman in a small provincial town whose only unusual property is that her capacity to be benefited has no ceiling. Give her a loaf of bread, an hour of someone's company, a room with a window, and she draws from it a quantity of wellbeing that no hundred of her neighbors could draw from the same loaf, hour, or room. There is no saturation point. Her returns are not merely high; they are, by stipulation, unbounded.

I built her this way deliberately, in order to close the exits that the familiar versions of this creature leave open. I gather that philosophers have a type-name for figures like her, but every version I have encountered is constructed so that some easy reply is available, and I wanted the case where none is:

- She begins badly off, so she cannot be set aside as someone who already has enough.
- She is not responsible for her constitution. It is congenital, not cultivated, so the reply that we may disregard needs a person has brought upon herself is unavailable.
- She is psychologically one of us: continuous over time, self-aware, capable of love and of long projects, and her wellbeing is of the same kind as everyone else's rather than some alien currency we cannot place on a common scale. So she cannot be excluded from the moral community or dismissed as incomparable.
- She is unique, and her condition is neither imitable nor transmissible. No one can become like her and no one can make another. So the incentive-based and expressivist replies — that feeding such a being would invite others to grow monstrous appetites, or would express contempt for ordinary human life — have nothing to work with here.

What is left, it seems to me, is only the bare structural feature: an extremely high and, I have stipulated, unbounded rate at which resources convert into welfare.

Against that, the argument.

(1) When one person would gain more from a resource than another would lose by giving it up, and the transfer can be made, the transfer is morally required. This seems to me, in its plainest form, to be the whole reason anyone thinks anything is owed to the badly off.
(2) What makes the gain or the loss matter in (1) is welfare and nothing else; the comparison is exhausted by how much welfare is at stake on each side.
(3) The Sink gains more from any unit of resource than anyone else in the town gains from it or loses by it. This is true by construction.
(4) Therefore every transferable good ought to go to the Sink.
(5) Concretely: the town owes her its harvest, its medicine, its school, its instruments; and since her returns have no ceiling, nothing short of that is a stopping point.
(6) And that is not merely a demanding conclusion. On (4), no other life in the town may flourish for its own sake. Every marriage, craft, and piece of music becomes at best an instrument for producing her states, and the ordinary moral world my readers are meant to care about has no standing of its own.

I want to keep (1). It is load-bearing for everything else I believe about what we owe one another, and without it I cannot see why the town owes the badly off anything at all. So I want to reject (4). But every route I try seems either to be closed in her case or to take (1) down with it:

(7) One route: weight benefits by how well off the recipient already is, giving priority to the worse off. But a merely diminishing weight on benefits will not stop her. If her returns are unbounded, then however steeply I discount, some finite gift to her still outweighs everything else on offer. To stop her along this route I need something stronger — a ceiling on how much any single life's welfare can matter, or a lexical ordering.
(8) And a ceiling proves too much in the opposite direction. Past the ceiling, relieving an enormous suffering would count for nothing whatsoever, which looks like the same absurdity transposed.
(9) Another route: run the case in reverse. Suppose instead a figure I call the Reservoir, who converts small deprivations into enormous suffering — the same unboundedness, pointed downward. Everything that blocks the Sink by appealing to her psychology, her responsibility, or the priority of the worse off is equally available against the Reservoir, and there I find the conclusion impossible to reject. So whatever rescues the town must be asymmetric between benefits and burdens, and I cannot see why it should be.

So: which step should I give up? Please name the step, explain why it looked safe — I have reread (1) through (4) many times and each one seems to me to say exactly what I meant it to say — and price the alternatives, in particular telling me whether, having paid, I still get to keep a duty of aid to the badly off. If there is a move that blocks the Sink while leaving the Reservoir's claim intact, I especially want to know whether it is principled or whether I am smuggling a stipulation into the fiction and calling it an insight.

Two further requests. First, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I supplied it in the fourth draft, would lead you to a different verdict about where the argument fails, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to be mistaken. I would rather have that than a confident answer. Second, please reply in American English.
