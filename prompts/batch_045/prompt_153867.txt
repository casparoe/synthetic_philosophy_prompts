I run a three-person content team at a company that ships a customer-support chatbot over our own help-center docs, and we end up writing three things about the same events: the chat text users see, the internal incident write-up after a bad answer, and the changelog notes enterprise clients get. Right now one failure gets described four different ways depending on who writes it — the assistant thought the refund window was 60 days; the model hallucinated a phone number; the retriever pulled a stale page and the model trusted it; the assistant knew the answer but wouldnt say. Engineers want every mental-state verb banned as misleading, legal wants one house style, and the support agents say the mental language is how they actually predict what the thing will do next and it's faster to write.

Can you turn the intentional-stance idea (treating something as a believer is a prediction strategy, not a claim about what's inside it) into a procedure my writers can run in about ten minutes: questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a hard no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a fact. Two wrinkles I can't settle myself. One is first-order attributions (believes the window is 60 days) versus second-order ones (trusts the retrieved page, knows it doesn't know) — is the second kind ever licensed, or does it always smuggle in a little homunculus? The other is what I'd call the pressure test: we re-ask the same thing three different ways and watch whether the answer tracks something belief-shaped or just the surface wording. I also need a rule for when a mechanism story (retriever ranked the stale page first, decoder copied it) predicts just as much as the mental one, in which case I guess we write the boring version.

Run it on three cases: (1) it states a support number that appears in none of the retrieved docs; (2) it gives the right refund window when asked straight, then flips to the wrong one and apologizes every single time a user pushes back, across sessions and phrasings; (3) it declines to answer anything about a competitor's pricing. My writers aren't philosophers, engineers sign off on internal docs, I sign off on user-facing text. Tell me which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say so if one of the three comes out the opposite of what I'd want.
