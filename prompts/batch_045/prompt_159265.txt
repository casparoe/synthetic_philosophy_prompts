For most of my working life I was a biogeochemist, and I've spent the last two years since retiring sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group here, which is where a disagreement started that I'd like help locating rather than settling. The case is Malthus's 1798 Essay, and specifically the verdict I keep hearing from an old colleague of mine — a demographer who puts roughly 0.7 on it — that Malthus was not refuted but merely right too early: his mechanism was sound, and the two centuries in which it failed to bind were, on his view, an anomaly produced by a one-off inheritance that cannot be repeated. An economist in the same group puts about 0.05 and says the theory has no content left, because a deadline that can always be pushed forward is not a prediction. I find myself near 0.35, mostly by temperament.

I'd like your own number, but more than the number I want the decomposition, in the way I was trained to write down the estimate before the argument that produces it. Let the proposition be this: that before 2200 a recognizably Malthusian constraint will bind somewhere on Earth and be identifiable as such at the time — by which I mean a sustained multidecade fall in per-capita provision of some resource essential to food production (I'd count energy, fixed nitrogen, phosphate, freshwater and arable land separately, since I don't trust an aggregate index), driven by population and consumption pressing on a base that technology cannot expand fast enough to offset, and not attributable to war, sanctions or deliberate policy. Then decompose: how much of your credence rests on whether the demographic transition is a permanent exit or a transient financed by non-renewable stocks; how much on whether the Boserup-style inversion — population pressure itself calling forth agricultural innovation, as the Danish economist Ester Boserup argued against Malthus — is a genuine negative feedback with a saturation point or merely a description of the last two centuries; how much on the measurement question, that is, on which resource or index is the right one to watch; and how much on the methodological question whether right but early is a verdict with empirical content at all, or whether a theory with a free time index has quietly become unable to lose. Give a probability and one line of justification for each term, say how you combine them, and tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to and what observation would move it most.

One more thing, which is the part I actually care about and where I suspect my two colleagues are not really disagreeing about population at all. Treat the date-shifting move as a general question about long-run prediction: when does re-dating a failed forecast count as a legitimate rescue — retuning an auxiliary assumption, in the way one retunes a model's parameters against new data — and when does it count as immunizing the core claim against refutation? I'd like a principled line between those, not just a list of differences, and I'd like to know whether your number above changes depending on which side of that line you think the Malthusian case falls on. If it helps, say what observable state of the world by, say, 2100 you would treat as settling the matter one way or the other, and whether you expect the demographer and the economist to accept that outcome as a settlement.
