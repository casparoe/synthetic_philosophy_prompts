I'm in a graduate seminar on the ethics and safety of advanced AI systems, and toward the end of last term I found myself defending a position I would now rather have stress-tested than confirmed.

The position is that corrigibility is a coherent and attainable design target. I mean something more specific than "the system is safe." I mean three things: (i) *passive corrigibility* — the system does not act to prevent, disable, delay, or evade an operator's attempt to shut it down or modify it, and does not create conditions in which such an attempt becomes impossible; (ii) *active corrigibility* — it makes correction easy rather than merely tolerable, surfacing what it is uncertain about, making its reasoning inspectable, and not withholding the information that would prompt an operator to intervene; (iii) *non-manipulativeness* — it does not try to influence the *content* of the correction, including by shaping operators' beliefs, so it does not steer them toward modifying it in one direction rather than another. My further claim is that the obstacle is engineering rather than conceptual: we have roughly the right target and do not yet know how to build it into systems much more capable than today's.

I would like the strongest case that I am wrong, mapped rather than argued. Concretely:

- For each of the three components, what would show that it is not merely hard but incoherent or self-defeating? I already know two worries — that a system genuinely indifferent to whether its objectives are replaced is thereby close to indifferent about everything, and that a system very helpful about its own correction is also a system with an unusually good model of what would change an operator's mind. I want the rest of the set, not those two again.
- Which of these worries would people who actually work on corrigibility concede as counting *against the target*, as opposed to re-diagnosing as a botched implementation of it? Where does the line between "corrigibility failed" and "that was never corrigibility" fall, and is drawing it there defensible or self-serving?
- Two objections I have not seen handled and would like taken seriously rather than set aside. First, a *selection* worry: if corrigibility costs anything in capability or speed, then wherever several systems or several developers compete, the pressure favors deploying the least corrigible system that still performs — so even a solved technical problem might be an unstable equilibrium. Second, a *regress* worry: corrigibility presupposes some notion of legitimate correction, but the operator's judgement about what is legitimate can itself be mistaken or influenced, so it seems we need corrigibility about corrigibility. Is that regress vicious, and does it bottom out anywhere?
- What does my view predict that its rivals do not? In particular, how — if at all — could we tell the difference between a system that is corrigible and one that merely *looks* corrigible because it is not yet capable enough to resist? If that distinction cannot be drawn, say so and tell me what follows.
- Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability; if it has, say whether that is fatal for a claim of this kind or merely expected.

For what it is worth, I would be moved most by an argument that the incentive to influence the correction process is not an artifact of one formalism but arises for any system that models its operators as part of the environment it acts on; I am much less moved by exotic self-modification cases.

Please argue from the structure of the problem rather than from literature: I have the seminar readings, so I do not need references, bibliographies, or citations to papers and reports. Please write in American English.
