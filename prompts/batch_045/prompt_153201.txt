I am writing a novel whose hinge is a single afternoon in a solicitor's office in Wells, and I want the deadlock at the centre of that scene to be a genuine one rather than a matter of temperament. The situation is this: a reclusive novelist has died leaving a single typescript of an unfinished, sexually explicit novel, and her will permits exactly three arrangements — it may be read by her daughter Miriam, a straitlaced churchwarden who is ashamed of it; or by her son Rafe, a libertine who would enjoy it; or by nobody at all, in which case it is burned. Miriam's real ordering is: burn it; failing that, read it herself, since she would rather shoulder the corruption alone than loose it on Rafe; and worst of all, hand it to Rafe. Rafe's ordering is nearly the mirror image: he would most enjoy Miriam being made to read it; if she will not, he wants it himself; and destruction is to him the worst of the three. Neither of them is being merely stubborn. Each appeals to two principles that sound, to me, beyond reproach: first, that over any pair of arrangements differing only in what that person themselves does, that person's own preference should settle the matter; and second, that if both of them prefer one arrangement to another, that arrangement should win. Yet when I work through the scene with those two rules I get a circle — handing it to Miriam beats handing it to Rafe, handing it to Rafe beats burning it, and burning it beats handing it to Miriam — and the afternoon simply goes round and round. A friend who teaches social choice tells me there is a formal result of exactly this shape, but she could not recall its name, and in any case I would rather understand the argument than be pointed at a paper.

So my question is what my characters, and I, should make of it. I have drafted several ways out, and I need to know which dissolve the difficulty and which merely conceal it. Rafe proposes adding a fourth arrangement — that they both read it — on the grounds that a larger menu must be easier to settle; is he right, or does a circle among the original three survive however many further options are added? The solicitor, who chairs the meeting, proposes a simple majority with a casting vote for himself, and I have noticed that the outcome then seems to depend on the order in which the options are put; is that an accident of my drafting, or is it in the nature of the problem that whoever frames the agenda decides? He also offers to draw lots among the three, which feels evasive to me, but I cannot say why: does randomising genuinely respect both principles, or does it only move the circle somewhere less visible — and if what Miriam objects to is Rafe's reading rather than the chance of it, does a coin toss answer her at all? And Miriam proposes the most attractive escape: let each of them simply decide for themselves whether to read, and let whatever happens happen, since then no one can complain that their own conduct was settled by somebody else. I find this very appealing for the scene, but I suspect it does not end the argument — that it yields an outcome which both of them would have ranked below another available one, so that the clash between respecting each person's own sphere and never leaving a jointly preferred arrangement on the table has not been resolved but only relocated.

I would be grateful for an answer that stays inside the scene rather than surveying a literature: reconstruct, in these characters' own terms, why the two principles cannot both hold when people have preferences of this shape; tell me, for each of the escapes above, whether it is a real solution, a restatement of the problem, or a change of subject; and advise me what, if anything, the chapter can honestly conclude — whether Miriam and Rafe should accept that no procedure is fair to both principles, and if so which principle a reasonable person in that room would give up. One further thing: Rafe also offers to buy Miriam's consent with a cheque, and Miriam counters with a cheque of her own. If you think bargaining of that kind is the real key to the scene, please say so, and say also whether the size of those cheques can be settled before the entitlement they are supposedly buying has been assigned.
