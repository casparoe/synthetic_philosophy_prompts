I'm in the second year of a PhD and my supervisor — call her D — has been handing me off-the-cuff verdicts on cases for the identity of indiscernibles all term, one per coffee, and I've realised I cannot tell what principle she is actually running. I have to give a work-in-progress talk to our reading group on Thursday and I'd rather find out now than in front of everyone. Her track record, as nearly as I can reconstruct it: (1) Black's two spheres, exactly alike, some distance apart in an otherwise empty universe with no absolute space — she says there are plainly two of them and that this isn't even a counterexample to the principle, because the symmetric but irreflexive distance relation does all the discerning work anyone could want; (2) two fermions in a singlet-type state, opposite spin along every axis and no fact of the matter which has which — again two, and again she says the principle survives, this time on a relation that is physical but purely symmetric; (3) two bosons in exactly the same state, same mode, nothing orthogonal, no spin to appeal to — here she bites the bullet and says the principle genuinely fails, but that nothing we need is threatened, because the occupation number is a determinate physical magnitude and 'how many' does not have to be 'which ones'; (4) the cheap reply, where the discerning relation is just numerical distinctness or some gerrymandered disjunction cooked up to fit — she rejects it flatly, on the ground that the relation has to be qualitative or physical rather than logical or already loaded with the distinction at issue.

The case I have built for Thursday is meant to sit between (1) and (3). Suppose that all that exists is two island universes: no space, no time and no causation relates them to one another, and each contains nothing but a single sphere, the two spheres exactly alike in every intrinsic respect. There is no distance between them, no causal order, no shared spacetime, and no quantum-mechanical state in which they are entangled or antisymmetrised. Two further stipulations. First, the count is determinate and not an artefact of description: the laws fix the number of spheres at exactly two, and the total mass of everything there is is twice the mass of a single sphere. Second, there is a law of nomic covariation linking them: every qualitative change in one is matched by an exactly similar change in the other, and the law is stated without singling out either as source or recipient. So there is a candidate relation available — covariation under a law — but it is perfectly symmetric, it is a law rather than a spatiotemporal or causal link, and nothing in the world grounds any difference between its relata.

What I want from you is a prediction about D rather than a survey of the literature. Is there, on her view, a fact of the matter that there are two spheres; are they distinct; does the identity of indiscernibles hold here, fail here, or fail to apply; and does the covariation law do any discerning work, or is it the same kind of cheat as the relation in (4)? Then set out the two or three rules that fit her verdicts (1) through (4) equally well — my own candidates are some form of weak-discernibility defence of the principle, some form of haecceitism with the principle demoted to a methodological rule of thumb, and a view on which cardinality is a qualitative matter and there simply is no fact of the matter which is which — say what each predicts about my case, tell me which she is most likely running and why, and be explicit if her earlier verdicts genuinely leave it open and about what she would be committing herself to either way.

Finally, I want a test I can put to her on Thursday. My candidate is the same setup with the covariation law expiring at t, after which the histories diverge: one sphere acquires a dent and the other does not, so that from t on they differ qualitatively. Question: are 'the sphere in the first island universe gets the dent' and 'the sphere in the second island universe gets the dent' two different possibilities, or the same possibility described twice? Tell me whether this really does separate the rules you have identified, what each of them says about it, and, if it doesn't separate them, what case would. And tell me where the rule you attribute to her is weakest — the objection she should most hope nobody raises. Something I can read in five minutes, please.
