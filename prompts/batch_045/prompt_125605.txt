I'm retired now — thirty years in a molecular biology lab — and I've started going to a philosophy reading group down at the library. Last week one of the regulars ended a discussion with "we're living in the most peaceful era in human history," and used it to wave away my worry that violence actually seems to be getting worse. I'd like to take the line seriously rather than argue about tone.

Can you set out the different precise claims that sentence could be making, as a list I can take back with me? I can see some of the axes myself: per-capita against absolute numbers, what gets counted as violence in the first place, deaths against non-fatal harm, maybe intended violence against harms nobody aimed at. But I'd also like you too include a couple of measures I came up with that nobody there would engage with: one is the expected number of violent deaths a randomly chosen person will hear about in a lifetime, which I think can rise even when the rate per person falls, just because there are more of us and we're more connected; the other is violence per social encounter rather than per person-year, since how many strangers you pass in a week is not constant across periods.

For each reading say whether it's roughly true, whether it's close to vacuous, whether it's really a claim about definitions rather than about the world, and — the bit I actually care about — whether it would justify brushing off my worry that things are getting worse. And if the whole thing turns out to depend on which measure you pick, is there still any fact of the matter here, or is the argument just a fight about bookkeeping?
