I am writing a short essay on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and I want critical comments on the notes below before I develop them into a full draft.

**Notes: the stakes are not one thing**

The debate is usually set up over a single datum. In the familiar bank case, the subject's evidence is held fixed while the practical cost of being wrong varies, and our willingness to attribute knowledge varies with it. Pragmatic encroachment (interest-relative invariantism) says the epistemic standard is invariant and the subject's practical interests partly determine whether it is met: knowledge attributions are interest-relative but not relative to a context of assessment. Epistemic contextualism says the standard itself shifts with the attributor's context, so one and the same subject, with fixed evidence and fixed interests, can be truly said to know by one speaker and truly said not to know by another. The standard diagnostic tries to separate them by asking whose situation sets the standard: hold the subject's stakes fixed and vary the attributor's, and conversely.

My thesis is that this diagnostic cannot settle anything, because both views have misidentified the practical factor. Each treats it as a single scalar, the size of what is at stake, when what actually does the work is the route by which a purpose depends on the proposition.

Call the alternative purposive encroachment. Knowledge is a three-place relation: S knows that p for the purpose of φ, where φ is some course of action or inquiry. The epistemic strength required varies with the cost of error along the specific route by which φ's success depends on p. Three consequences.

First, route-sensitivity. Hold fixed the subject, the proposition, the evidence, and the overall stakes, and vary only the route. Bela has tested a wire once, carefully, and found it live. She is about to touch it, where error is catastrophic, and she is also about to record the result in a maintenance log that no one will ever act on, where error is trivial. Any view on which the subject's stakes determine a single verdict must say either that she knows the wire is live or that she does not. The purposive view returns two verdicts, and I claim that ordinary speakers can be brought to hear both, once they are asked not whether she knows but whether she is in a position to touch the wire and whether she is in a position to log the result.

Second, a case that I think discriminates between all three views. A stranger has wagered his life savings on whether the wire is live. Bela knows nothing of the wager; we, who are attributing knowledge to her, know all about it. The stranger's outcome depends on p, but not on anything Bela does. Subject-stakes encroachment says Bela knows. Attributor contextualism, if the attributor's context is doing the work, says she does not. The purposive view says she knows, because no purpose whose success is routed through her agency is at risk: stakes that bypass the subject's agency are not her stakes in the relevant sense. I take this as a point in favour of the purposive view, since my intuition about the case is firm, though I do not want the essay to rest on intuition alone.

Third, a reconciliation claim. Contextualism and encroachment are not so much false as each other's special case. When the attributor's conversational purpose coincides with the subject's dominant practical purpose, contextualism returns the right verdicts; when the subject has one salient purpose and the attributor is tracking it, encroachment does. The dispute survives only as a question about which purpose sets the default when the two come apart, and I want to argue that this residual question is genuine but sociological rather than epistemic: it concerns what we are normally doing when we say that someone knows.

Two problems I have not solved. (1) Gerrymandering. If purposes may be chosen freely, then for any true belief there is some purpose for which the subject knows — for instance, the purpose of believing truly that p — and knowledge becomes trivial. I want to require that the purpose's success depend on p in a way that is not merely the purpose of getting p right, but every formulation I have tried either smuggles in the notion of knowledge or excludes too much. (2) Cost of inquiry. Sometimes the danger lies not in acting on a false belief but in gathering evidence: testing whether the wire is live by touching it. Both rival views are silent here, since they are theories about the cost of error rather than the cost of evidence, whereas the purposive view at least has room for the case, because inquiry is itself a φ. I am not confident what it should predict.

Give me exactly five numbered critical comments on these notes. Each comment should be one or two sentences long. Include no introduction and no conclusion. Among the things I would particularly value: whether the wager case really discriminates between the three views, or whether a contextualist can accommodate it as cheaply as the purposive view does; whether route-sensitivity amounts to anything more than the familiar idea that knowledge is relative to a question or a contrast; whether the reconciliation claim is substantive or only restates the fact that both views fit the original bank data; and what you take the single weakest step in the argument to be.
