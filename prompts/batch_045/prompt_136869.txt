Next month I'm giving the opening paper at a one-day closed-door workshop on the moral equality of combatants that a defence academy is co-hosting with an international law institute, and I want to walk in with a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than my usual habit of improvising. The room is not a philosophy audience: about fifteen people — six or seven IHL practitioners and ministry-of-defence legal advisers, two revisionist moral philosophers of the McMahan stripe who think an unjust combatant has no permission to kill at all, one traditionalist who holds the independence of jus in bello from jus ad bellum as close to a fixed point (Walzerian war-convention stuff, plus a bit of natural law), a political scientist who works on compliance and audience costs, and a serving colonel who teaches ethics at the staff college and regards soldiers second-guessing the justice of their war as a discipline problem first and a moral question second. Roughly: the lawyers care about rules that a nineteen-year-old can apply under fire and about not importing ad bellum politics into targeting law; the philosophers care about whether my view is just revisionism with an epistemic fig leaf; the political scientist cares about what my view predicts and how anyone would measure it; the colonel cares about what he's supposed to tell his troops on Monday.

Here's the thesis, sketched so you can aim at it. I want to defend what I've been calling the "epistemic entitlement" reading of the moral equality of combatants: equality is not a substantive permission that floats free of the justice of the war, and it isn't merely an excuse for soldiers who happen to be ignorant either — it's a defeasible evidentiary presumption, and the thing that warrants it is the state's discharging a jus ad bellum duty of transparency toward its own combatants. Where a state makes the justice of its war reasonably accessible (open parliamentary debate, published legal advice, a free press arguing about it, a professional volunteer force trained in the law of armed conflict), the presumption holds and combatants on both sides are morally on a par. Where a state systematically hides or falsifies that information, the presumption weakens; and where an individual combatant has privileged access to it — the legal adviser who has read the classified advice that the war lacks Security Council authorisation, the senior commander in the room when the assessment was made, maybe the drone crew that sees the full intelligence picture rather than a fragment — it lapses for that person, who then fights at greater moral risk than the conscript in a closed society who couldn't have found out. I want to hold two further things: that the law should stay flat (keep a single IHL rule, don't make targeting law turn on anyone's epistemic position), so my grading is a moral grading only, and that states acquire a real duty of transparency with teeth, breach of which shifts some moral risk back onto their own political leaders. I've been sitting on this paper for to long and I know where it's softest — the whole thing looks like it collapses into revisionism once you grant that the informed combatant lacks permission, and like it gives the lawyers a rule nobody can administer if I let the law move — but I'd rather you tell me which objections actually land in that room than the ones that would land in a philosophy seminar.

So: draft me the prep sheet. Give me eight or so objections in the order I'll actually get them (by likelihood and by damage together, not one at a time), each phrased the way that person would phrase it and not the way a philosopher would — the legal adviser's objection should sound like a legal adviser, the colonel's like a colonel, the political scientist's should be about operationalisation and observable implications. For each: a reply I can say in about ninety seconds, where that reply is weakest, and whether I should concede it, hold it, or concede a piece and keep the rest. I also want two or three things I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks, the one reply that has to land or the whole paper dies, and the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, with a fallback for if they do. Return it as a JSON object, no preamble, with this schema: top-level keys "opening_concessions" (array of strings, each a concession I should make up front, in the terms of the room), "objections" (array of objects with fields "rank" (integer, 1 = most likely and most damaging), "raiser" (which person or group in the room, by role), "objection_as_they_would_put_it" (string, in their voice, ideally with their vocabulary), "damage_1_to_5" (integer), "reply_90_seconds" (string), "soft_spot" (string, where that reply breaks or is question-begging), "concede_or_hold" (one of "concede", "hold", "concede_partly"), and "the_real_problem_underneath" (string, the deeper commitment of mine the objection is really aimed at)), "must_land" (object with "objection", "reply", and "why_the_paper_dies_without_it"), "question_i_hope_nobody_asks" (object with "question", "who_would_ask_it", "why_it_scares_me", "fallback_if_asked").
