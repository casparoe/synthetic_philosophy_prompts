Hey — I write historical novels (the last one was set in Dublin in 1798, the one I'm slogging through now is about a family of glassmakers in Bohemia around 1880) and I keep having the same argument with myself, and ocassionally with a reader, about what I'm allowed to invent. I've dipped into the history-theory stuff — the Rankean ideal of showing the past as it really was, Carr being sceptical about whether facts even arrive unselected, White and the narrativist crowd saying the shape is imposed anyway — and none of it seems to be about my actual problem. Selection isn't my problem, obviously I select. Invention is. So here's my list of gut verdicts, and what I want from you is the principle underneath them, if there is one, plus which of them I have to give up.

Fine, no guilt at all: inventing a minor person who isn't in the record — a furnace boy at the glassworks, a maid, a clerk. Nobody thinks there's a roll-call.

Not fine, and this is the one that wakes me up: putting a made-up sentence into the mouth of a real, documented person, in a scene I've invented anyway.

Fine, and I do it constantly: putting that same real person in a room he was almost certainly not in, on a day when he was documented in another city, for a scene that's invented anyway.

Not fine, and it feels worse than the furnace boy even though both are "adding a person": fusing two real minor figures into one composite character.

Not fine: moving a real, datable event by nine days so it collides with my plot.

Fine: reordering two private quarrels whose real order the record doesn't settle at all, so that the second one lands where I need it.

Fine, obviously, everyone does it: a real person's thoughts, motives, what they wanted and were ashamed of — unless the record actually says otherwise, and then it flips to not fine.

Fine, no problem at all: a whole counterfactual novel, clearly signalled as one on the jacket.

The worst thing on my list, worse than the made-up sentence: one counterfactual event slipped into a book that otherwise tracks the record, with nothing signalling it.

Torn, leaning fine: turning something we only have as a paraphrase in somebody's letter into direct speech.

Now, the two that I think are genuinely hard to hold together. The made-up sentence and the wrong room are the same move — I'm placing a real person where the record doesn't place them — and I condemn one and don't even hesitate over the other. I have a story: it's about quotation, that words are different from whereabouts, that a sentence in quotation marks is a claim about a document while a body in a room is a claim about a staging. But I don't definately believe it, because a made-up scene between two real people is already a hundred made-up sentences, and I'm fine with those in free indirect style — it's only the ones between quote marks that bother me, which smells like formalism rather than anything about truth. And the counterfactual pair I can't unify at all: if invention is licensed by signalling, then the jacket copy is doing an enormous amount of moral work, and most readers never read jacket copy.

My novelist friend says the whole list is just tracking whether the reader is *licensed* to take a sentence as testimony, and that the licence is set by the form of the text and not by how big or small the fact is. I half believe her, but I think that gets the composite wrong — the composite isn't about what the reader is invited to believe, it's about someone being deleted, and I don't see why deletion is worse than addition on a licence story.

So: state a principle, or two or three candidates, precisely enough that I could actually apply it at my desk at 7am. Tell me which case motivates each clause. Tell me what it says about the cases I haven't put on the list — a novel narrated in first person by a real historical person; a real person who is recognisable but renamed; two real events merged into one; a real documented quotation given to the wrong real person; an author's note at the end that lists everything I changed; and whether it matters if the invented thing I make a real person do is vile rather than trivial. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn — I want reflective equilibrium here, not a principle that saves the list by being infinitely adjustable.
