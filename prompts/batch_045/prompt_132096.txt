Hey — I'm in a philosophy-of-computation reading group, and I keep bumping into the same conversation-ender. Someone brings up hypercomputation stuff: the accelerating "Zeno" machine that does its nth step in 1/2^n of a second so it finishes infinitely many steps within a bounded time, analog setups exploiting infinite-precision reals, that general family of ideas. And our convenor just says: "the Church–Turing thesis is a definition, not an empirical claim" — and then behaves as though the whole literature is confused rather than merely betting on a contingent falsehood. I dont think that's obviously right, but I also can't tell what fight we're actually having, and I suspect the slogan is doing more work than its content licenses.

Can you lay out, as a menu I can bring to the next session, the different precise things that one-liner could be asserting?

- That in ordinary mathematical usage "computable" now just *means* Turing-computable, so any alleged counterexample is by stipulation not computation?
- That the pre-theoretic notion of an effective procedure was vague, and CTT is one precisification among possible others — a proposal, not a discovery?
- That it's an empirical claim about what any physically constructible device can do (in which case: could we test it, and what would a failed test look like)?
- That it's a claim about what *human beings* can do following a rule, i.e. partly psychological?
- That its real content is the convergence result: many independent-looking formalisms all turned out to define the same class, which is evidence for something — but evidence of what, exactly, and about what?
- Something else I havent thought of?

For each reading: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false / question-begging? And most importantly — which of them would do the work he wants, i.e. show that the hypercomputation papers are *confused*, rather than just wrong if a contingent claim happens to be true?

Two cases I want every reading tested on, because I think they pull the slogan apart:

(1) Non-uniform families. For each input length n there are only finitely many n-bit inputs, so you can build a circuit (or a lookup table, or a physical contraption) that answers the halting problem correctly on all of them. The family of circuits "decides" an uncomputable set even though no single machine computes the whole function. Now imagine a lab that can manufacture the nth device on request, but with no recipe relating the devices to each other. Does the thesis, on each reading, rule this out? Is it even the kind of thing the thesis was ever about?

(2) The one-shot oracle. A single black box that, once, correctly tells you whether one specific program halts, and then breaks. It's finite, it's physical, it isn't doing anything "infinite". Which readings say that's impossible, and are they saying anything more than "no uniform effective procedure computes this"?

Also: are there readings that these cases *support* rather than undermine — e.g. ones where the thesis survives because the interesting claim was always about uniformity? And tell me honestly which reading you think someone who says this in a seminar usually means, cause I suspect its the trivial one doing untrivial work.
