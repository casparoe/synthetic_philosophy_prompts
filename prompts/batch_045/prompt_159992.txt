I work on the public inbox at a national health agency — me and two junior comms officers on rotation, a few hundred messages a week, a big chunk of them from people who don't just disagree with a claim but insist the data was fabricated, or that trials can't show anything, or that the thing we're talking about doesn't exist at all. Right now we have no rule for how to respond, so the default is either a long, patiently footnoted evidence dump that never lands, or silence, and both feel bad. Each officer has maybe ten minutes per message and has to log a one-line reason code, and I don't want a framework that only works if you've read the philosophy.

A philosopher friend told me I should look at Wittgenstein's On Certainty and the hinge stuff — that some propositions aren't supported by evidence but are held fast, they're the scaffolding that makes evidence possible, and the riverbed can shift over time. Her punchline: when someone is rejecting a hinge rather than a claim, more evidence is the wrong answer, and past that point you aren't really arguing at all. That sounds both genuinely wise and like a wonderful excuse for never answering anyone, and I can already tell my team would use it to close tickets early.

So: turn it into something we can actually run. The questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "don't send evidence," and where it hands off to a human because it's a values call — whether to keep engaging at all, staff time and burnout, amplification, and the fact that our replies are read by lurkers, not just by the person who wrote in. Two things it has to handle or it's useless to me:

1. Most messages aren't pure hinge rejection. They mix a first-order factual dispute with doubt about a specific institution or instrument — this registry, this trial design, this lab, peer review generally — and only sometimes with the global stuff. So it needs a way to say which layer is actually doing the work before we decide what to do.
2. Sometimes the doubt is reasonable. Some of the communities we write to were genuinely lied to or experimented on by health institutions, and their hinges legitimately differ from ours. I don't want a procedure that can't tell that apart from conspiracy thinking, and I really don't want one that can't flag the case where *we* are the ones treating a live, contested question as settled bedrock.

Then run it on three real-ish messages: (a) "the flu vaccine doesn't work, the trials were designed to produce whatever the manufacturer wanted"; (b) "viruses don't exist, people get sick from toxins and terrain"; (c) a message from a community health group citing a real history of non-consensual experimentation and saying our whole evidence base is untrustworthy. I suspect (c) is where your procedure tells me something I won't want to hear, and I'd rather you say it than soften it. Also please be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the procedure just can't settle no matter how well we run it.
