My roommate and I got into a four-hour arguement last night about whether punishment can survive once you drop desert, and I think I lost it on the wrong terms. His package is the one you'd expect from the free-will-skeptic side: nobody ultimately deserves anything, so stop asking who merits what and rebuild sentencing as a forward-looking, public-health-style institution — risk management, incapacitation, quarantine, deterrence, repair. Every time I said "your replacement keeps smuggling desert back in through the side door," he said that's just leftover vocabulary and would wash out once the institutions were actually redesigned. I didn't buy it, and instead of writing him another message he won't read, I want to make the point as a document.

So: write me the internal house style guide of a corrections authority, roughly 800–900 words, dated in the third year after the legislature abolished desert as a basis for sentencing. Format it as numbered entries preceded by a short memo header from something like the Office of Sanction Design, and include at least one two-column table of "Desert-era phrase → approved post-desert equivalent," plus worked before/after examples where a real sentencing statement, victim-impact statement, or press release is rewritten into compliance. Invent the jurisdiction's details (I keep picturing a "Northvale Correctional Authority" with a risk instrument called the Halberd-7, but use whatever you like) and give me one recurring case the guide keeps returning to: I'd suggest a low-risk offender being held for much longer than his old tariff because the "general signal requirement" needs a body, plus an entry on the offender who *insists* he deserves to be punished and objects to being "managed," and an entry on what you do when there's no treatment for the condition and the risk score never falls.

The constraints that matter to me: the guide's authors should be sincere, competent, humane people trying to write clean prose, not villains — the horror has to come from the position's own logic, not from anyone twirling a moustache, so a free-will skeptic should be able to read it and think "yes, that really is the pressure point" rather than "straw man." Keep the irony deadpan and never let the document wink: no snarky asides, no "as if," no character saying the quiet part out loud. Each euphemism should fail for a *structural* reason rather than just sounding silly — the proportionality constraint creeps back in because ungraded sanctions lose legitimacy and compliance; deterrence has to be stated in terms of the harm imposed on this person in order to change other people's behaviour, which is exactly the using-persons-as-means worry; the quarantine model has no release condition keyed to what the person did, and indefinite detention with no cure on offer is hard to distinguish from punishment; a sanction still has to *say* something, so censure keeps reappearing as "the expressive dimension of the intervention"; and if risk is all that matters, the high-risk innocent becomes detainable. I'd also like one entry that quietly shows the collateral damage on the other side of the ledger — what happens to the vocabulary of praise, achievement, and holding-people-responsible once nothing is deserved, that compatibilist worry that responsibility practices are partly what constitute someone as an agent. By the last entry the guide should have reconstructed retributivism almost exactly, under new names, without anyone in the document noticing, and no entry may state that thesis. It should be funny to someone who knows the just-deserts literature and readable to someone who's never opened a word of it, so no unexplained jargon and definitely no citations, footnotes, or real philosophers' names inside the document — my roommate definately won't read anything with footnotes.
