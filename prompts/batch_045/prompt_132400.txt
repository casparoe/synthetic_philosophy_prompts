Take the 2019 Varsity Blues admissions scandal — Rick Singer's operation, where parents paid him to fabricate athletic-recruit profiles and to inflate entrance-exam scores for their kids, charged federally as conspiracy to commit mail fraud and honest-services fraud against the universities — and analyse it through the lens of meritocracy and desert. I don't want the standard "merit is a myth" essay; I want a mapping. Line up four candidate justifications for giving selective places to the "meritful" — (a) desert proper, the place is owed to whoever earned it; (b) the role view, offices have purposes and should go to whoever will do best in and after them; (c) the luck-discounted version where talent is brute luck and only effort can ground desert; (d) the procedural view, where what matters is just that the sorting mechanism isn't buyable, whatever it measures — and for each say what exactly went wrong in this case, in its own terms.

Then run each one against the control cases I actually care about: paying for test prep, attending an elite private school, being a legacy, a parent endowing a building, being a genuinely recruited athlete, and finally bribing a coach for a fake one. Wich of those does each justification have to condemn, and is there any view that manages to condemn only the last?

Then the other direction — what the episode does to the theories. Two things I'm stuck on. First, my "swap test": if you substituted a randomly chosen rejected applicant for the kid who got the seat, would the college be any worse off by its own lights? If usually no, the seat was never tracking merit and (a) and (b) are idle here — but does that argument prove too much, e.g. about hiring? Second: prosecutors charged fraud, and the universities were treated as the victims. I read that as the one wrongmaking feature everyone could agree on being the lying and the bribes, not that the kids didn't deserve the places. If so, is the public outrage evidence for or against desert-based meritocracy — does it suggest people really care about procedural fairness in a competition — and what would each of the four views say about a variant where the parents openly gave the money to the college for the seat, no deception at all? Also tell me where the theories just go silent on the case instead of forcing them to speak.
