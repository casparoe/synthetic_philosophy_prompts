Write a parody internal strategy memo of roughly 900 words, addressed by a junior associate in Product Development to the managing partner of a firm whose flagship client experience is the following arrangement. Clients are shown two transparent boxes: box A plainly contains $1,000; box B contains either $1,000,000 or nothing, having been filled the previous night on the basis of a five-second video scan. The firm's contract states that the money is never moved after the scan, and clients may watch the loading on a live feed, a clause the legal department regards as the firm's principal defence against fraud claims. The scan is published as 90 percent accurate, symmetrically, over a client population in which one-boxing and two-boxing are equally common.

The novel product the associate is proposing is the Quiet Room: for a $5,000 fee, a client may spend an hour deliberating behind a one-way mirror before choosing. Because the firm's model is trained on five seconds of micro-expression, deliberation degrades the prediction to chance. The room is also recorded, and the transcripts are the firm's actual product, the boxes being a loss-leader. The brochure's own disclosure page gives the resulting figures, and the memo must respect them: outside the room, taking only box B is worth $900,000 in expectation and taking both boxes $101,000; inside the room, before the fee, the two options are worth $500,000 and $501,000. The memo should also explain, in the vocabulary of retention and churn, that clients who take both boxes do worse and complain afterwards, and that the firm's intake questionnaire sorts clients by their response to a short vignette — a question the associate admits misclassifies the annoying minority who bring up the medical case in which an inert lesion produces both the behaviour and the bad outcome.

Three constraints govern the voice. First, the associate is a scrupulous causal decision theorist: he must never claim that a client's choice causally influences the prediction, and the comedy of his proposal lies in his having to explain, without ever contradicting himself, why a person with his scruples should pay $5,000 for a room that merely degrades his own future evidence and thereby changes what his future self will do. Second, he must never use the slogan with which philosophers taunt people who take both boxes; the same thought has to be expressed as a client-satisfaction metric. Third, he must not notice that, on his own numbers, selling the room raises the firm's expected payout per client by nearly $400,000; any such figure should surface as a line item labelled data-acquisition cost and be waved through.

Across the numbered sections the proposal should escalate, as straight-faced proposals do: from the room, to marketing it only to clients the questionnaire identifies as causally inclined while quietly steering the others out of the building, to pre-commitment packages, to a final recommendation that the firm abandon the choice entirely and pay clients according to the type the scan assigns them, reclassified as a character dividend. He should not register that the endpoint has eliminated the decision it was supposed to advise.

The piece should name no philosopher and cite no paper; the two theories should be identifiable purely by what the firm does with them. It should be funny to someone who works on decision theory because the arithmetic and the verdicts are right, and legible to someone who has never heard of either theory, who should finish able to say what each one recommends and why the firm has ended up selling the destruction of its own customers' evidence. The author should never wink: the associate believes every word.
