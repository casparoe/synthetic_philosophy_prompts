I'm a retired marine biologist — thirty years of North Sea benthic surveys — and now I finally have time to read, I've got into philosophy, mostly social choice theory after a shouting match at our natural history society. Here's what I actually need.

I sit on the council of a small conservation trust, about forty members, and we settle maybe six things a year by vote: where the year's budget goes, which of three sites we bid on, who gets our little research grant, where the AGM is held. Whenever a result is close somebody says the rule was gamed, and we keep shopping for a voting rule that can't be gamed. Then I ran into the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and worked out there probably isn't one, not with three or more options and no dictator. So don't explain the theorem to me again — what I want is something the council can run.

Give me a procedure for choosing and stress-testing a voting rule given that every rule is manipulable somewhere: the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us because it's a values call rather than a technical one. Constraints: doable by volunteers in an afternoon with a spreadsheet, no software and no consultants; it has to work when we barely know how members will vote, since we vote by email all at once and the ballots are visible to the whole council afterwards; and it has to be honest about which assumption we're giving up when we give it up — squashing the options down so preferences line up on one dimention, using money or a lottery instead of a straight choice, cutting one decision into two, whatever else there is. It should also keep apart the question can this rule be gamed in principle from will it be gamed by people who don't know how anyone else is voting.

Then run it on three of our recurring votes: picking the AGM venue, where everyone basically wants it near where they live and the options really do sit on a map; splitting the grants budget between three unrelated causes, where I can't see any single dimension they line up on; and our two-round system for the research grant, shortlist first then winner — I'm rather fond of that one and I suspect you're going to tell me it doesn't help. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't tell me, where it's silent, and where I'd be kidding myself if I thought it had settled something. And say plainly which step is doing the real work, because my guess is that most of it comes down to guessing what our members actually want rather than to anything in the theory.
