Bit of an odd one. I sit on the committee of a tiny local charity that hands out micro-grants — a few hundred quid each, a couple of dozen applications a time — and we've always just argued it out and produced a ranked list. Last round we somehow ranked A above B, B above C, and C above A, and none of us could find the mistake, which is how I ended up reading about whether all-things-considered betterness has to be transitive and about those spectrum arguments where you walk from something obviously better to something obviously worse in a long chain of tiny improvements. It's got under my skin, and now I've been asked to run a 45-minute session on it for the new volunteers at our AGM in a fortnight: a retired GP, a maths teacher, a data scientist, two arts grads, no philosophy between them, one flipchart. I don't want to hand them a better ranking procedure and I don't want them to leave thinking philosophy is just clever sophistry; I want them to feel the force of the argument and to see what the exits actually are.

So plan the session rather than explaining the argument to me. What has to be in place before the puzzle even bites, and in what order — I'm assuming they need to have 'better than' separated from 'better in respect of x' first, and to have accepted that each link in the chain is supposed to be an unambiguous improvement, before any of it lands. I was going to carry the whole thing on two graduate job offers, walking sixty-odd steps from a fifteen-minute walk and a dull but much-loved job to a ninety-minute commute and a nightmare team, each step trading three more minutes of travel for a small gain in the work itself; but I've a nagging worry that with essentially two dimensions someone will say 'fine, pick an exchange rate and add it up' and the thing collapses. Is that the right vehicle, what does it need to have and to lack (my guess: many independent dimensions, nothing lexically dominant, and no aggregation over people and no probabilities, since I really don't want to end up doing population ethics or expected utility by accident), and what would you swap in instead? The misconceptions I'm braced for are that this is only the sorites and 'better' is vague, that 'better than' is like 'taller than' so of course it chains and intransitive preferences just get you money-pumped, and that if the first option isn't better than the last then the two must be equally good. Tell me which of those I've got wrong, what else that particular mix of people will throw at me, and where exactly in the hour to defuse each one.

Two more things, because a friend who does think about this stuff threw them at me and I couldn't answer them, and I'd like the session to be able to absorb them rather then dodge them: first, that if every step is an improvement you can apparently run the whole chain in reverse and end up threatening the asymmetry of 'better than' as well, so the cheap 'just drop transitivity' exit may cost more than it looks; second, that our panel never actually has the intermediate options on the table — we only ever choose between the applications in front of us — so maybe betterness among real options stays transitive even if it goes intransitive once you start inventing hypothetical ones. Give me the honest verdict on both and say whether either is worth putting to the group at this level or whether it'll just muddy things. Finally: what should I openly refuse to explain in 45 minutes (I'm assuming the Repugnant Conclusion and anything with subscripts), and give me one thing to do or ask in the last five minutes that would show they've grasped the structure of the argument rather than just been made uncomfortable by it — and if there's a version of that check that doubles as something useful for a committee who has to hand out real money on Monday morning, so much the better.
