hi — I'm defending my dissertation chapter on Tuesday (three-hour viva, 20 minute talk then questions) and I badly need a Q&A prep sheet, because I've been staring at this thing for way to long to see it straight anymore.

My thesis in short: "genetic information" is not a metaphor, but everybody has been cashing it in with the wrong primitive. The primitive isn't reference or representation or instruction — it's *error*. A stretch of DNA means what it does because there is maintenance machinery in the lineage (proofreading, mismatch repair, recombination-based repair, that whole apparatus) that treats departures from it as things to be corrected rather than merely as things that happen. So DNA is a code in the sense that an error-correcting code is a code: a protocol for keeping a message intact across an indefinite number of copies and across a lot of noisy chemistry. It is not a code in the sense that a language is a code, and it isn't a blueprint, a program, or a description of the organism.

Three things I claim this buys, which is where I expect the damage to come from:
(1) We get to call a mutation an *error* without sneaking in a designer or a goal for the organism — "error" is defined by what the maintenance system does, not by what would have been good for the creature.
(2) It explains why information talk feels like it thins out at the edges: in systems with little or no maintenance machinery (most RNA viruses, say, or a stretch of DNA in a cell that's about to chew it up) there just isn't a fact of the matter about what the sequence "says," only about what it does when something reads it.
(3) It answers the parity argument — the old move that genes can't be privileged carriers of information because sunlight and gravity and the mother's cytoplasm are also causally necessary for the trait. On my view the genome is special not because selection blessed it but because it's the only thing in the cell that's on the receiving end of machinery whose job is to distinguish correct tokens from incorrect ones.
