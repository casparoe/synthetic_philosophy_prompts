I sit as the student representative on my university's Behavioral Insights Panel, which has to clear every student-facing change to choice architecture before it goes live. We have no written charter; we vote, and the minutes carry a one-line reason, and after two years I still can't tell what principle we think we're enforcing. Our record so far: (1) approved — rearranging the main dining line so fruit and hot vegetables sit at eye level and the fry station moves behind a partition, with everything still on the menu at the same price; (2) rejected — auto-enrolling all undergraduates in a seventy-five-dollar-per-semester emergency textbook fund, where opting out required an in-person appointment at the bursar's office during weekday hours; (3) approved — pre-checking the box that lets the career centre see a student's CV on the course-registration form, with the unchecked state visible on the same screen and one click away; (4) rejected — a twenty-second interstitial with a somber photograph and a be-sure-you're-certain prompt that appeared only before finalizing a course withdrawal, even though withdrawing stayed fully available and easy and the panel agreed many withdrawals are impulsive; (5) approved after changes — a graduation-pledge campaign, but only once the pledge cards were made private rather than posted on a public wall and signing was made genuinely skippable; (6) rejected — defaulting students into the university's own high-deductible health plan, even though our analyst said most students' expected out-of-pocket costs would be lower under it and switching out took one click, the recorded reason being that the university saves money when students stay in its plan.

The new proposal comes from the financial-aid office. Federal student borrowers here already have to complete an online entrance-counselling module before their loans disburse; the office wants to insert, near the start, a single unscored screen that is not recorded anywhere and not enforced in any way, asking the student to type into a text box the monthly amount they expect to repay after graduation and then to tick a statement that they intend to repay on time. Nothing else changes: the same information, the same options, the same number of clicks to finish, plus a plainly visible skip link. In a pilot randomized across last year's cohort, borrowers who saw the screen were meaningfully likelier to be current on their payments six months later, and almost everyone completed the screen rather than skipping it. Two complications. First, a second pilot arm added one sentence saying the question was asked because answering it makes people likelier to repay — and in that arm the effect disappeared entirely. Second, the effect is concentrated in exactly the borrowers the office flags as highest default risk, and the university's cohort default rate, which bears on its federal aid eligibility, improves as a result; over the period studied the students did not take on more debt or pay more, and avoiding default does protect them from garnishment and wrecked credit.

What I want from you is a decision memo for us, not a literature survey. First, reconstruct two or three genuinely distinct rules that would generate all six precedents — including the ones that surprised people at the time, like the withdrawal interstitial and the health-plan default — and be explicit about where the precedents leave the rule underdetermined. Second, say what each rule predicts about the repayment-intention screen, with the reasoning shown. Third, give me your recommendation: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analogue, and — if our record really does underdetermine it — what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Fourth, tell me how much work the pilot's second arm is doing: does the panel's verdict properly depend on the effect dying once the mechanism is disclosed, or only on whether we actually disclose it, and would your recommendation flip if the pilot had instead shown the effect surviving full disclosure? Finally, name one further case we could put in front of ourselves that would separate the rules you found. Argue it out from the precedents and the case; I don't need citations or further reading.
