I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of action, and last week a student raised a version of the toxin puzzle that I hadn't considered before, so I've decided to build the final around it. For anyone who needs the reminder: in the standard case a billionaire puts a vial in front of you that will make you miserably ill for a day but do no lasting harm, and offers you a large sum if, by midnight tonight, you have formed the intention to drink it tomorrow afternoon; you needn't actually drink, and the money is already in your account hours before the time for drinking arrives, so that when the moment comes you will have no reason at all to drink. The question is whether you can now form that intention, given that you know perfectly well you won't act on it. I don't want to test whether my students remember that case, though — I want to test whether they can do something with it. The variant I have in mind goes like this: the billionaire makes the same offer every year for twenty years, and tells you the whole schedule up front; a perfectly reliable intention-reader does the detecting, and it is calibrated to the functional profile of intention rather than to any private feeling of resolve, so it pays out only if the state it detects is one that would issue in your drinking if the money had not already been transferred; and there is a no-crutches clause, under which any year in which you arrange an external enforcement mechanism — hiring someone to force you, taking a drug that compels you, signing a contract with a penalty — the reading comes back null and you forfeit that year and the next three. My suspicion is that the serial structure changes the case in a way that the one-shot version hides: repetition might make intending easier, because you can train yourself into a disposition or adopt a standing policy of forming such intentions, or it might make it harder, because each passing year is another piece of evidence that you never drink, and because a state that has been formed nineteen times and never once moved you to action starts to look less like an intention and more like a wish, an avowal, or a bit of play-acting in your own head. There is also a further wrinkle I want them to confront: if the only reliable way to collect the money is to become the kind of person who is disposed to act against her own considered judgment, then succeeding at the game seems to require making yourself somewhat worse, and I want them to say whether that matters for whether the intention is available to her.

What I need from you is a complete exam package. First, the question itself: 250 to 350 words, written in a closed-book, self-registering style, so that it states the puzzle and the variant in the stem and defines any theoretical label it uses (if it invokes something like a rationality constraint on intention, the constraint gets spelled out in the question), and so that a student who has never encountered the puzzle's provenance or its secondary literature can still answer it. It should force a decision on a specific, crisply stated thesis — something along the lines of the claim that one can intend to act only if one believes that, when the time comes, one will have sufficient reason to act — and ask whether the serial case gives us reason to reject that thesis, to reinterpret it by separating the deliberative question from the state that answers it, or to conclude that the successful player has not acquired an intention at all and that the thesis survives untouched because the phenomenon has been explained away. There is more than one defensible answer, and I don't want the question rigged toward one; but it must be gradeable, so the question should be built so that certain moves have to appear whichever side the student takes. Reciting the standard dialectic about the one-shot case, or observing that intentions are not simply beliefs and desires, should get a student nowhere.

Second, a model answer of roughly 800 words that takes a side and argues for it rather than surveying the options, and that explicitly handles at least one strong objection to the side it takes. Third, a marking scheme worth twenty marks in total, organised around five must-hit moves with the marks assigned to each, plus a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a short closing paragraph distinguishing an A answer from a B answer and saying what earns a C. Please don't build any part of this around citations, reading lists, or named authors and papers: I share the marking with a colleague who hasn't taught the material, and the scheme has to be usable by someone who has read only the question text and the model answer.
