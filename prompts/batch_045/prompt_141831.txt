I'm a philosophy professor, and I've got a senior seminar on Rousseau and the arts this term. I'm prepping the section on his side of the quarrel with d'Alembert about whether Geneva should build a theater, and I've realized I have a bunch of gut verdicts about imagined Genevan spectacles that I can't quite systematize. Before I inflict them on my students, I want to know what principle, if any, captures them, and which verdicts I should drop.

As I teach the official view: a permanent professional theater is bad for a small republic. The reasons usually given are that it creates a class of actors who make a living by appearing to be someone else, which looks like a dishonorable trade built on hypocrisy; it turns citizens into a passive audience that feels pity for imaginary sufferings while ignoring the real needs of neighbors; comedy in particular feeds amour-propre and social comparison by letting the audience laugh at fictional or local faults; the whole institution is tied to luxury, aristocratic manners, and the wrong kind of polish; and, most importantly, it substitutes representation and spectatorship for the transparent, participatory public life a free people needs. His positive alternative is the civic festival: outdoors, communal, non-commercial, where everyone is at once actor and spectator, and the community recommits itself to its laws and mores. There's also the awkward fact that he himself wrote plays and a little opera, so if the ban is on mimesis as such he's definately a hypocrite; if it's on a certain social package, maybe not.

Here are my verdicts, as a reasonably sensible Rousseauian.

1. A professional company stages a neoclassical tragedy in a Geneva playhouse: people buy tickets, sit in the dark, watch heroes suffer, cry, and go home. Not fine.

2. A professional comedy of manners that holds up local shopkeepers and wives to ridicule and lets the audience feel superior to their neighbors. Not fine; if anything worse than 1, because ridicule seems to me a motor of amour-propre.

3. A village harvest dance in the open square: everybody dances, the old and the magistrates watch, nobody is disguised, no one is paid. Fine.

4. A civic festival with processions, songs, and gymnastic displays where the whole city celebrates the republic and its laws, and everyone is both participant and witness. Fine.

5. A neighborhood amateur production of a simple patriotic play about a legendary ancestor: no tickets, no professionals, a plain stage in the square, and the neighbors rotate roles so that over the week almost everyone is at some point an actor and at some point a spectator. This is the one I keep going back and fourth on. My gut says it is fine, or at least nowhere near as bad as 1, even though it still has a script, costumes, imitation, and a stage.

6. A comic song at the civic festival that names real local drunkards and shames them in front of everybody: no costumes, no actors, the crowd sings it together. Fine, actually good; the ridicule here feels corrective rather than corrosive.

7. A few friends in a study privately reading a play out loud for moral discussion, no costumes, no audience, no money. I want to say fine, but I'm not sure; call it unsure.

My tentative principle is this: what he really objects to is not representation or fiction as such, but a social form. The target is the professional, commercial, indoor playhouse that splits a population into passive spectators and paid imitators and that runs on amour-propre, especially ridicule and pity for imaginary woes, instead of active civic identification. The festival is the opposite package: participatory, transparent, outdoors, non-commercial, and aimed at the community's own laws. If that is right, then 1 and 2 are out; 3, 4, and 6 are in; 5 is allowed because rotation destroys the passive-spectator split, even though imitation remains; and 7 is a weird case because it keeps representation but drops the social package, so maybe it is fine too.

Test that against the cases and repair it if it breaks. If no single principle captures them, give me the smallest set of candidate principles and tell me which of my verdicts each one sacrifices. I especially want to know whether I should instead say that any scripted imitation is already a fall, in which case I have to overturn my verdict on 5, and maybe rethink 6.

I also have two further cases I haven't decided, and your principle should tell me what to say: a traveling puppet show for children at a fair, with no human actors, a crowd of laughing kids and parents, and a bit of commerce; and a magic-lantern show of optical illusions in a dark room, no actors and no script, but pure passive spectatorship and trickery. What does the principle say about those?

Please answer in four sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: 1. The principle, stated plainly, about 100–150 words. 2. How each clause handles my cases, about 300–400 words. 3. Hard cases I didn't list, about 200–300 words. 4. Which of my verdicts has to go, about 100–150 words.
