I am a novelist, and I need a case I can print.

Next year my publisher is reissuing a novel of mine with a short author's note attached, and they would like me to say something about a passage that readers now take in a way I did not write it. I would rather say something true and exact than something conciliatory or vaguely self-deprecating, so I need a model case: not my own book, but a case with the same shape, clean enough to set in front of a general reader with no jargon, no footnotes and no scholarship.

The shape is this: a book finished in one situation and published in another. I want you to construct a case in which three views of what a text means come apart in a particular direction. Call them what you like; I will define them as I need them.

(A) Actual intentionalism: what a text means is what its author actually intended to mean in writing it — on the version I want, only where that intention was successfully executed, in the sense that the words she chose did express it, in the language as it stood when she wrote, to the readers she was writing for.

(B) Hypothetical intentionalism: what a text means is what an appropriately informed ideal reader would most reasonably hypothesize the author to have meant, working from the text and from everything publicly available about the text, its author and its moment at the time the text appeared — and from nothing else.

(C) Conventionalism: what a text means is what the words, read by the conventions of the language in their textual context, say. What the author intended, or can be conjectured to have intended, settles nothing about meaning.

The specification: (A) and (C) must agree, both giving the same answer about what the passage means — including what it says about the world — while (B) must give a different answer.

Constraints, and these are the ones that matter:

1. The divergence must fall out of the gap any novelist can suffer between finishing a book and seeing it appear: an ordinary delay of publishing, a shelved list, a change of imprint. Not a private key, not a cipher, not an allusion only the author could recover.
2. The author must not be convicted of a failure of craft. No slip, no malapropism, no irony that misfires, no word used wrongly. Her intention must be one that (A) has to count as successfully executed at the time she wrote it.
3. You may not manufacture the divergence by withholding from (B) what (B)'s own view entitles it to. Its reader gets the full public record as of publication.
4. What shifts across the gap should be the world rather than the words: the conventional meaning of the sentence in its context should be stable, while what it is reasonable to attribute to the author moves.
5. The passage must be determinate under each view. A case whose real moral is that the sentence was ambiguous all along is not a case.
6. Ordinary prose fiction, one paragraph, in prose I would be willing to have read aloud. No genre furniture, no invented languages, no real names, and nothing a reader would need to look up.

What I want back: the passage and the stipulated facts; then, view by view, the verdict and the reasoning that produces it, so I can see the verdicts generated by each view's own criterion rather than stipulated; then which feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; then the best a defender of (B) can say to stop the case from counting, and whether they can say it without quietly altering their view; then which temporal slice of the author's intention your (A) verdict relies on — the intention at the moment of writing, the one she held when she saw proofs, or the one she would avow at the reissue — and why that slice is the right one; then what the case becomes at the reissue, thirty years on, when the original reviews and three decades of reading are themselves part of the public record; and finally the one sentence I could actually set in the note.
