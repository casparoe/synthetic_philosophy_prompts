Question:
In Nicomachean Ethics V Aristotle analyses exchange as proportionate reciprocity rather than strict equality, introduces money as a middle term that measures and renders goods commensurable, and claims that money exists by nomos and not by nature, being able to be altered or made useless at will. He also says that demand or need, chreia, is what really holds an exchange community together, that money represents demand by convention, and that money serves as a guarantee of exchange in the future. In Politics I he distinguishes the two uses of possessions, most famously a shoe, criticises the unlimited pursuit of money in retail trade and chrematistics, and condemns lending at interest, tokos, as the most unnatural form of wealth-getting because interest is money born of money.

Some interpreters draw one of two conclusions from this material. Reading A holds that, because money is conventional, Aristotle must think exchange ratios are merely conventional and politically revisable, with no grounding in the goods or in human needs. Reading B holds that, because chreia is the real standard, money is a merely passive veil and its conventionality is philosophically unimportant.

Assess these two readings. In your answer, explain the role of commensurability and proportionate reciprocity in NE V; say precisely how money's conventionality and chreia are related; explain whether Aristotle thinks goods are already commensurable in themselves or are made commensurable; and show why, on this account, usury is singled out as especially unnatural. Be exact about the steps of the argument and about where each reading goes wrong.

Student's answer:
Aristotle's treatment of money and exchange has two complementary parts. In Politics I the household's natural art of acquisition, oikonomia, is directed to self-sufficiency and is limited by real needs; by contrast chrematistics, especially retail trade and lending at interest, is directed to unlimited monetary gain and is therefore unnatural. The distinction is illustrated by the example of a shoe. Every article of property has two uses: the use peculiar to it, such as wearing a shoe, and the use not peculiar to it, such as exchanging the shoe for money or food. Barter that exchanges a surplus for a missing necessary, for instance wine for corn, is not contrary to nature; it exists to replenish natural self-sufficiency and is not yet an unnatural art of wealth-getting. When communities begin to import and export, however, money is devised: a portable commodity such as silver, at first measured by size and weight and later stamped to indicate amount. This makes possible the art of business aimed at profit, and ultimately usury.

In Nicomachean Ethics V justice in exchange is proportionate reciprocity. A builder and a shoemaker do not exchange identical products, so their association requires that their different products be equalized. Aristotle represents this with the proportion builder : shoemaker :: house : shoes, so that the relative worth of the producers is mirrored by the relative worth of their products, taken in the same order. The exchange is just when the diagonal proportion holds. Because a house and a number of shoes are not naturally commensurable, the community introduces money as a middle term. Money measures all things and thus makes them commensurable, allowing the proportion to be expressed and exchange to take place. Money also serves as a guarantee of future exchange, since a person with no present need can accept money now and use it later.

Crucially, Aristotle says money is nomisma: it exists by nomos, not by nature, and can be altered or made useless at will. This shows that money is a human convention. The real bond of exchange, however, is chreia, need or demand: if neither party needs anything from the other, no exchange occurs. Money is a conventional symbol that represents this demand. Yet because chreia is itself the natural need that goods answer to, the measure is not wholly artificial: the ratios are fixed by the natural usefulness of the goods in relation to need, and money, as a conventional measure, simply registers a commensurability that is already present in nature. Thus Aristotle rejects the crude conventionalist reading, Reading A: exchange ratios are not arbitrary or merely revisable, because they track real demand. But he also rejects the opposite reading, Reading B: money is not a passive veil, because without a conventional measure the incommensurable products could not be equalized and exchange would not happen. Money's conventionality is therefore essential to its measuring role.

Given this, Aristotle's condemnation of usury follows directly. Money was invented to serve exchange and to satisfy needs indirectly. Interest, or tokos, is money born of money: the lender receives back more money than was given, as if money could breed like livestock. Since money's only natural function is to serve as a measure of exchange, and not to produce offspring from itself, usury is the most unnatural form of wealth-getting. The borrower's need may be real, but the lender's profit is a limitless increase of the measure itself rather than a return for any use of a real good. Aristotle therefore hates usury most because it mistakes a conventional measure for a natural source of growth.

In sum, the two readings each capture half of the truth. The conventionalist reading rightly notes that money is not natural, but wrongly infers that exchange value floats free of need. The veil reading rightly notes that need is the real bond, but wrongly treats money's conventionality as unimportant. Aristotle's own view is that a conventionally instituted measure is required to make need effective across goods that are not strictly commensurable, and that treating this measure as if it had a nature of its own, capable of growth, is the root error of usury.
