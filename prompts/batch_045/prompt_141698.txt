I'm putting together a small, very unofficial anthology of short fiction that does philosophy by other means — pieces where the idea lives in the setup and in what the characters are willing to do, not in anybody's mouth. Here's the commission I want from you.

The topic is the meta-problem of consciousness: instead of asking why there is experience at all, you ask why we are so disposed to *say* there is, to insist on it, to find it puzzling, to write papers about it. The interesting move is that this question looks tractable in a way the first one doesn't, and then people disagree about whether solving it would hand you the first question for free or would quietly dissolve it. I'd rather you not have anyone lecture about this. I want it in the plot.

The premise I keep failing to make work. A lab has a technique — call it whatever you like — that can reversibly uncouple, in the same subject, first-order sensitivity from the disposition to judge and report that one is in a state. With the device on, a subject discriminates a target at essentially normal accuracy in forced choice, but when asked whether they saw anything says no, flatly, with no confabulation, no surprise at their own score afterwards, and no sense that anything is missing. With the device in its other mode, a subject is at chance on the same discrimination but produces rich, confident, emotionally loaded reports of having seen something, and will argue with you about it. As I understand it, nature already gives us rough one-sided versions of both (blindsight-ish cases, and confabulation in anosognosia-ish cases), but the fictional bit is that it's a *switch*: the same person, both ways, ten minutes apart, and they can be asked afterwards which period they'd rather repeat.

Here's the complication I want at the centre, and please don't sand off its edges. One subject — the only one — reports, after the decoupling is lifted, that during it "something was there and I couldn't say it." She is insistent, and she is the person the realist on the project has been waiting for. But when they go back through the trial log, her forced-choice accuracy in that session was at chance. She was guessing. The ineffable residue shows up in exactly the subject whose first-order performance was worst, and it shows up in the form of a sentence about how the words fail. I want that fact to cut both ways and I want the reader to feel it cut both ways: the realist will say the report system was tracking something it couldn't access behaviourally; the other character will say the report system is a self-contained generator that produces ineffability out of noise, and that this session is the cleanest demonstration of that you could ask for.

What I'm after, concretely:

- 1000–1500 words. A short story, not a scene from a novel, not a dialogue, not a story with a philosophical appendix stapled to the end.
- Nobody explains the field. No character says, in effect, "as you know, the hard problem is..." No closing paragraph where the narrator tells us what to think. If a character is a philosopher by trade, make that legible from what they do with the data — what they want logged, what they refuse to sign, what they delete — rather than from what they say about their commitments.
- At least two characters who want genuinely different things, and neither of them should be a caricature. The person who thinks the reports are all there is should be competent and should get something right. The person who thinks the reports track something extra should notice a real problem with the experiment that the other one can't wave away.
- The ending should be an image or an action, not an argument, and the reader should come away genuinely unsure which of them was right — unsure, not confused. There's a difference and I care about it.
- Give it a title.
- One mundane detail I'm oddly attached to: somebody in this building is fighting with a coffee machine, and it keeps happening. Do what you want with that, or ignore it.

I'll be reading it partly for whether I can hand it to someone with no philosophy background and have them feel the disagreement before they can name it, so if a piece of jargon definately has to stay in, earn it in one line rather than explaining it.

Oh — and no aliens, no uploads, no simulations, no dying AI. I've recieved nine of those already.
