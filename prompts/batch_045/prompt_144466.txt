I sat on a jury for nine days this spring — a fraud trial, almost all of it documents — and we retired with the room split eleven to one. We came back unanimous before lunch. What I have not been able to settle since is whether we reasoned our way to that or simply wore each other down into it: from the inside the two feel exactly alike, and the difference seems to matter rather more than the verdict did. In a few weeks I am introducing a community screening of 12 Angry Men for a local film-and-philosophy series, and I would like to use the slot to get at that question with an audience who have never encountered any of this formally.

What I want from you is a reading of the film through Aumann's agreement theorem: the result that agents who start from a common prior and who come to know one another's posteriors cannot settle into a stable disagreement, but must end up with the same number. I would like both the film and the theorem taken seriously — the film not reduced to a charming illustration of a result, and the result not stretched until it says something it does not.

Some things I would particularly like you to work with.

First, the assumption that tends to go unmentioned: besides a common prior and common knowledge of posteriors, the theorem assumes a shared and fixed space of possibilities over which those probabilities are defined. My sense is that what the holdout actually does in that room is not report a credence at all. He opens up possibilities the other eleven had not partitioned on — a second knife of the same make, whether the old man could have reached his door in fifteen seconds, whether the woman across the tracks was wearing her glasses when she claimed to see the killing. On the theorem's terms that move has no name: it is not updating within a model, it is changing the model. I would like that developed, and told me if it is wrong.

Second, the currency of the exchange. The film's deliberation runs on reasons, demonstrations and re-enactments rather than on numbers, and there is a real difference between telling someone your posterior and showing them what you saw or staging a small experiment — the knife produced in the room, the walk to the door, the timings acted out. Later work on agents who trade evidence rather than credences treats convergence as a separate and harder question. Is the film depicting that, and does it get it right?

Third, the common prior as the actual villain. Everyone in that room shares more than a prior; they share a presumption of guilt, and the theorem is entirely silent on the quality of the posterior they converge to. Unanimity can be complete and worthless. Is the film's real subject the danger of a shared starting point rather than the value of agreement?

Fourth, belief versus verdict. At the end they do not agree about what happened; they agree about what to do. Aumann's result concerns a shared posterior over states of the world, whereas a verdict is a decision taken under a grotesque asymmetry of costs. Can the film be read as showing that a jury can have all the agreement the law requires without any of the agreement the theorem describes — and if so, what does that say about what my own jury actually achieved?

Finally, where the film resists you. The holdout's first vote is not backed by any private evidence of his own, and on the theorem's own logic the other eleven votes are themselves evidence he ought to update on. By its lights he is being irrational, and he is the only reason the boy lives. I want you to tell me plainly whether that is the point of the film, or whether I am misreading it, and to say what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the formalism cannot.

What I need is the argumentative spine for a twenty-minute introduction, with the scenes to point the audience towards and a closing note on what the formalism leaves out — the unanimity rule, the locked room, the heat, fatigue, the cost of being the last person to move. Please describe scenes rather than reproducing dialogue; I have the disc and will check any wording myself. Around a thousand words, and I would be grateful for an honest flag anywhere you think the reading is being forced.
