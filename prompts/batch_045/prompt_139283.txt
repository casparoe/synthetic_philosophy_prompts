Hey — I write a column on history and the social sciences, and I've drifted into a position on the Hitler counterfactual that I'd rather have stress-tested than cheered on. Bear with the setup, its a bit fiddly.

My view is that "great man vs. structural forces" is the wrong axis. The question I actually care about is how much outcome-variance there was across the real candidate pool. By 1930 the structural preconditions for some kind of authoritarian, revisionist, violently antisemitic regime in Germany look pretty robust to me — Depression, the Treaty settlement, elite panic about the left, a conservative establishment and civil service that was never really reconciled to a republic. What Hitler uniquely supplied, on my reading, was coalition-breadth: he was simultaneously acceptable to the conservative elites around Hindenburg, to the party's radical base, and to the streets. None of the plausible substitutes — Goebbels, Göring, Strasser, Himmler — had that triple constituency. So without him you don't get a surviving Weimar; you most likely get a right-authoritarian regime that fails to consolidate, either a military-conservative dictatorship or a Nazi-led coalition that splinters into factional infighting. Which means no war in the shape of the one that came in 1939, and no industrialized genocide on that scale in that decade. And note the mechanism I'm appealing to for that last bit isn't personal demonism: the Final Solution came out of a chain of cumulative radicalizing decisions across 1939–41 — deportation schemes like the Madagascar idea that stalled, then mass shooting, then camps —每一个 needing an arbiter who could settle disputes without the coalition coming apart.
