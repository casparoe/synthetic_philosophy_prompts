I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I have never once handled van Fraassen's Reflection principle well. My usual habit is to put the principle on the board — that my credence now in a proposition, conditional on my credence in that proposition at some specified later time being x, ought itself to be x —, remark that it looks like conditionalization viewed from a distance, run through a diachronic sure-loss argument, and move on before anyone notices that this argument is doing something quite different from the synchronic Dutch book argument they met two weeks earlier. This term I finally have a full ninety-minute slot for it, the room has two blackboards and no projector, and a colleague from a machine learning group down the hill has asked whether his students may sit in. I can write the lecture myself once I have its architecture; what I want from you is the architecture. Please give me a teaching plan for the session, not an explanation of the principle, and please write it in American English with notation limited to what I could put on a board in one pass.

The audience is genuinely mixed, which is part of the difficulty. The philosophy students — MA and early-stage PhD, most of them — have had Bayes' theorem, conditionalization, and one synchronic Dutch book argument; they are quick with symbols and impatient with anything that smells like a paradox, and several of them arrived in the program already convinced that credences are just subjective and therefore beyond normative criticism. The visitors use Bayesian updating daily as an engineering tool, have no prior grip on a diachronic requirement as a requirement at all, and in my experience convert any principle about an agent's later opinions into an implementable design rule within about ninety seconds. So, first: tell me the order in which the components must appear and what depends on what. In particular I need a decision on whether conditionalization is to be presented as something Reflection delivers or as an independent norm set beside it, and on where in the sequence the assumption that an agent can be certain of what her own future credence function will be has to be introduced — I have a suspicion that my past lectures failed precisely because I introduced that assumption sideways. Second: give me the one case that should carry the whole session, and say what a carrying case must contain and what it must lack. My worry about the examples I have used before is that all of them are evidence-gathering cases — blood tests, horse races, urns — so that Reflection collapses into the advice to wait for more data. A case that carries it, as I now think about it, has to be one in which the agent foresees a shift in her own opinion that is not simply the arrival of evidence, without being so exotic that it smuggles in manipulation, madness, or self-deception. There is a familiar Ulysses-style case in which an agent can foresee being swayed and can foresee what he will think afterwards; tell me whether that should carry the session or whether it imports the very confusion I am trying to head off, and if you would replace it, specify exactly what the replacement must have and must not have.

Then the part I am worst at, the misconception map. Here is my list of suspects, which I would like you to correct, reorder, and add to; for each item that survives I want the precise point in the sequence at which it should be blocked and the move that blocks it: that Reflection is just conditionalization restated; that it says to defer to your future self because she will know more; that it says your future credences will be more accurate; that it is a principle of self-trust or of intellectual humility; that it forbids you from ever expecting to be misled; and — the one I most want placed carefully — that the diachronic sure-loss argument settles Reflection the way the synchronic Dutch book settles probabilism, when I take the diachronic argument to be the genuinely contested one and I want my students to feel why it is contested without my having to adjudicate it in class. Finish with two things: a single question I can put to the room in the last ten minutes that would let me tell apart a student who has the principle from one who has only the slogan about trusting your later self, and a recommendation about what I should refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I am refusing. I am thinking specifically of the fact that the principle is widely taken to break down once the propositions involved are self-locating, and I cannot decide whether a five-second aside about that is intellectually honest or merely a way of opening a door I have no time to walk through.
