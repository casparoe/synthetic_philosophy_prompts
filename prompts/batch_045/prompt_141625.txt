I'm teaching week 6 of an upper-level "Rational Choice" seminar — 14 students, mostly philosophy majors with a handful of econ ones, 75 minutes, movable chairs, and they already have the poker chips we used last week. Last session was the synchronic Dutch book: violate the probability axioms and a bookie can stitch together a set of bets that loses you points in every possible outcome. They followed it, but it landed as a parlor trick — the vibe was basically "well sure, if you're dumb enough to take both sides of anything at your own fair price, you get burned, so what." What I actually want them to feel is the diachronic version: that the thing getting criticized is an *update rule*, that the target is a plan rather than a single belief, and that the whole argument is way more hostage to a few background assumptions than the clean version on the handout suggests. And I dont want to stand up and say the c-word for the first forty minutes — they should walk into it and only hear it named in the debrief.

Here's roughly what I'm imagining, but I cant get the mechanics tight. Pairs: one student is the punter, one is the bookie. The punter fills out two price sheets for the same set of tickets — a ticket on A pays 100 points if A is true, 0 otherwise, and for each ticket they name a single price between 0 and 100 at which they'll take either side. Sheet 1 is before any evidence. Sheet 2 has to be filled out in advance, with one column per cell of an evidence partition: a die gets rolled under a cup and all they'll ever be told is whether it came up odd or even, so they owe me a "price if I'm told ODD" column and a "price if I'm told EVEN" column. The bookie then gets to see the punter's whole completed plan and places some trades now and some after the announcement, and the punter is bound by what they signed — no reconsidering on the spot. If the two sheets dont line up conditionalization-wise, the bookie should be able to lock in a gain no matter how the die landed and no matter which cell gets announced; if they do line up, the bookie should be stuck. I want the bookies to find the "sell it to them at the high price now, buy it back at the low price later" move on their own, and I want the punters to feel picked apart by their own signature.

Then I want to break it, and this is where I'm most unsure — three variants, and I don't know how to sequence them or what question to put to the room after each: (a) let punters post a bid-ask spread instead of one price; (b) let them renege on any pre-signed trade after the announcement by paying a small fixed withdrawal fee (3 points?), which I'm hoping makes them notice that once walking away is cheap nothing is exploitable at all, and reframes the argument as being about what your future self is on the hook for; (c) a "forgetful punter" role where the ODD/EVEN column gets filled in by a classmate who never saw sheet 1, so nobody — not even the punter — can predict the later prices.

What I need from you: exact rules and a ticket set on a die roll simple enough to do the arithmetic on paper in real time, including a set that will actually tempt people into non-conditionalizing pairs (my worry is they'll derive everything from one prior and the sheets will line up by construction — is there a way to nudge them toward over- or under-updating without it being obvious what I'm up to?). Plus a bookie worksheet, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes with the role swap so everyone plays both sides, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific moving parts of the argument. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be straight with me about which part of the diachronic argument the game just cant stage — my guess is the "evidence you didn't see coming" problem, since I'm handing them the partition up front, and maybe something about points not being utility, but tell me if there's a bigger hole than those.
