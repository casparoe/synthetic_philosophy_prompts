Bit of an odd one. I work in channel coding — LDPC mostly, plus some weird stuff for storage systems — and a freind in philosophy of biology talked me into giving a short slot to her lab group on whether the genome is literally a code or whether that's just a metaphor we got stuck with in 1960 and never shook. I said yes before thinking about it, and now I'd like to actually say something true instead of something that sounds good.

Here's my problem. I'm fluent in sources, messages, encoders, decoders, codebooks, block length, redundancy, Hamming distance, synchronization and framing, and in the fact that Shannon deliberately threw meaning out of the theory in order to get a theorem. What I can't tell is how much of the philosophy-of-biology argument survives being said in that vocabulary. So please do the translation. Take the two or three positions you'd actually defend rather than the straw versions — the deflationary one where it's all causal correlation or mutual information and there's no content anywhere, the one where content comes from selection history / what the mechanism was selected to do, and the one where the codon-to-amino-acid lookup is a real code because the mapping isn't forced by the chemistry (frozen-accident flavoured) — and restate each one in my terms, as faithfully as you can. Don't attach names or papers to them and don't smuggle coding theory's assumptions in and call it translation.

Three things I specifically want handled rather than gestured at, because I think that's where the residue is: (1) in my world the encoder and decoder are built by someone standing outside the system and fixed before any message goes through; in a cell the tRNAs and the synthetases that implement the mapping are themselves products of the DNA they decode, so the codebook is written in the code and the decoder has to be manufactured by the message before any message can be read. (2) A code in my sense is normative — a string can violate the code, and a decoder can mis-decode in a way that isn't just channel noise — and I genuinely can't tell whether a mis-incorporated amino acid is that kind of failure or just a chemical accident that the code has no opinion about. (3) My framework is mute about aboutness by design, so if "information" in the biological claims is doing semantic work, maybe it has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — but I also suspect that a lot of the time it's mutual information with some extra romance, and I want you to tell me which is happening where. One more practical angle, since the room will be full of synthetic-bio people: people down the hall really do rewrite mappings, reassigning stop codons and slotting in noncanonical amino acids. Is the fact that we can do that evidence that the mapping was conventional all along, or does it only show that it was chemically contingent, and is that distinction load-bearing for "is it a code" or just a striking fact about stereochemistry?

Give me a table with three columns — the claim in its home vocabulary / your translation / what got lost or had to be fudged — and then at most four sentences underneath answering whether the residue is blind spot in my framework or a sign the biological claim was less clear than it sounds. Under 700 words total, no footnotes and no citations: my library login expired years ago and I can't check anything you point me to, so argue instead of referencing.
