Hi — I'm a reporter on the science-and-ideas desk, and I'm trying to finally squeeze a piece out of a dinner argument from last month, so I need your help pressure-testing it before I start writing. Deadline is Monday, my editor wants roughly 1100 words, and please write in American English.

The setup: I did a physics undergrad before I switched to journalism, and I still get dragged to these things. At dinner my friend Priya, an experimental condensed-matter person, rolled her eyes at the whole Wigner business — you know, the famous essay about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences — and said there's nothing mysterious here at all: mathematics is like a filing cabinet we built to hold the files we already had. We measured the folders, we cut the drawers to fit, so of course nothing rattles; the wonder is just us forgetting who did the carpentry. My other friend Marcus, a number theorist, came back with the opposite picture: mathematics is like a mountain range that was there before anybody showed up. We make the maps and the climbing gear, but the ridges decide which routes go through and which dead-end, and every so often a route somebody plotted for fun turns out to lead exactly to the pass people needed two centuries later. Each of them was leaning on their picture for a conclusion: Priya's to say there's nothing here that needs explaining, Marcus's to say something platonist-ish is more or less forced on us.

What I want from you is an audit of both analogies rather than a verdict on platonism itself. Give me the correspondence tables — what plays the role of the file, the drawer, the carpenter, the ridge, the map, the climber — and where each mapping is doing real work versus just being atmosphere. Then the breaks, and, more importantly, which breaks matter for the conclusion each of them wants and which are just noise. I've also got two things of my own that I want run through both pictures, because I think this is where the argument actually lives and I definately can't get them straight on my own. First, I dont think effectiveness is one thing. I've been sorting the stock examples into three tiers: math that merely compresses data we already had, math that predicts something nobody built it to predict, and math that conscripts a new ontology into physics, where the formalism tells you the world has to contain some object you had no prior use for. Call them bookkeeping, prophecy, and conscription. My suspicion is that the filing-cabinet picture handles bookkeeping fine, starts looking uncomfortable at prophecy, and is basically silent at conscription — but check whether that's right, grade a few of the usual cases against the ladder (the Greek conics everybody brings up; the episodes where people got to a new planet and to new particles by following the equations first, before anyone had seen the thing), and tell me which tier the wonder actually has to be about. Second, there's the survivorship worry: we keep the formalisms that worked and we mathematize the phenomena that were mathematizable in the first place, so the cabinet looks custom-built partly because everything that didn't fit got thrown out. Does that deflation go all the way down, or does it stop somewhere — and if it stops, does it stop at the same tier where Priya's analogy starts coming apart?

Please give me your actual reasoning rather than a tour of the literature, and don't hedge everything into mush at the end: if one of the two pictures is basically defensible and the other one isn't, say which, and if both are carrying more weight than they can hold, give me a better analogy — or tell me honestly that the serious version of this argument has to be had without one.
