I sit on the ethics working group that advises our municipality's autonomous shuttle pilot. We rule on stylised emergency scenarios, and the engineers then have to implement each ruling as a constraint on the motion planner. We are down to one scenario that has resisted a decision, and I want the ruling, not a survey of the literature on trolley cases. Unless I say otherwise, in every scenario the service brakes have failed, everyone involved is an adult, no one is responsible for the emergency, no one has consented to anything, and the shuttle owes no one a special duty.

Our seven precedents:

1. *Divert.* Five pedestrians in the crosswalk ahead. The shuttle can swerve onto a wide sidewalk where one pedestrian is standing; a wall beyond her stops the shuttle before it reaches anyone else; she is killed by the swerve. Ruled: MAY swerve.
2. *Plow.* No swerve available. A side-mounted plow could sweep a bystander into the shuttle's path so that his body stops it; he is killed. Ruled: MAY NOT.
3. *Loop.* Swerve onto a service lane where one worker is standing. Nothing beyond him stops the shuttle: the collision with him is what halts it before it can continue to the five. He is killed. Ruled: MAY NOT.
4. *Sandbags.* Steer into a sandbag barrier. The shuttle derails, and flying debris kills a bystander standing several metres away. Ruled: MAY.
5. *Even numbers.* One pedestrian ahead, one pedestrian on the sidewalk, no other difference. Ruled: MAY NOT swerve.
6. *Doomed worker.* As in *Loop*, the collision with the worker in the service lane is the only thing that will stop the shuttle — except that an unrelated scaffold is collapsing and will crush him two seconds later whatever we do, so the collision costs him nothing. Ruled: MAY.
7. *Passenger.* The shuttle can save five pedestrians only by swerving into a concrete barrier, which kills its own passenger. Ruled: MAY NOT.

The undecided case:

*Trigger.* The mechanical brakes have failed, but the shuttle carries an automatic emergency braking system that engages only on a confirmed pedestrian detection within three metres. The five are around a bend, outside sensor range. The only pedestrian the shuttle can detect is a worker in the service lane. If the shuttle enters the lane, the emergency system fires as it closes on the worker and stops the shuttle before it can reach the five. The planner estimates roughly one chance in three that the shuttle still strikes him before stopping, with death near-certain if it does. His body contributes nothing to stopping the shuttle — the brakes do all the work — but his presence in the lane is what makes them fire.

Several members keep reaching for the doctrine of double effect and its distinction between harm intended as a means and harm merely foreseen as a side effect. I do not think that settles anything here, because the case sits exactly where that distinction is ambiguous: the rescue runs through the worker, but arguably not through any harm to him.

What I want from you:

1. The two or three principles that fit all seven precedents. State each precisely enough to be implemented, and say which precedent does the work for it and which precedent it has to explain away — in particular how each handles *Doomed worker* and *Passenger*.
2. What each principle predicts about *Trigger*.
3. The ruling you recommend, with your reasons, and the closest analog among precedents 1 to 7. I take it that *Trigger* has something in common with both *Divert* and *Loop*; I want to know which one controls and why.
4. What we would be committing ourselves to either way — including whether a MAY NOT commits us to anything about cases in which the person the manoeuvre runs through suffers no harm at all.
5. The one further variant that would separate the surviving principles. We can construct whatever you need: a version in which the strike probability is effectively zero, a worker merely detected from a safe distance behind a fence, a consenting test worker, a worker who caused the brake failure. Take whichever does the most work.
6. Whether the principle you recommend can be stated without appealing to anyone's intention, since the engineers cannot implement a constraint on intentions — and, if it cannot, what we lose by recasting it in causal or structural terms.

If you think one of the seven is bearing more weight than it can bear, say so. And if it helps, add where you expect the group in fact to land, as opposed to where consistency pushes it.
