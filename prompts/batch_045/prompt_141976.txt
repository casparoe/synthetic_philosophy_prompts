I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of action, and I'm drafting the take-home final. I need one question on Kavka's toxin puzzle that does more than reward students for restating the puzzle and its usual moral. The background: last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend — an economist who works on commitment devices — about whether the puzzle shows anything about the nature of intention at all. Her line was that the whole thing is an artifact of our lacking direct voluntary control over our own motivational states. You can't form the intention, she said, for the same reason you can't decide to find a joke funny: human beings simply can't switch an intention on at will. Hand someone a reliable commitment device, she claimed, and the difficulty evaporates. I said no — the availability of the device doesn't help, and if you try to use it, the same failure reappears one level up. We didn't settle it, and it occurred to me that the disagreement is exactly what a good exam question should force students to adjudicate.

Please write the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Constraints:

- Role and process: you are a professional philosopher who specializes in the philosophy of action and has taught this material many times. Before drafting anything, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: list (i) the two or three things a genuinely strong answer must do, (ii) the moves a merely competent answer makes that should earn a B, and (iii) the plausible-sounding dodges students use to escape the puzzle (for example, deciding to deceive yourself about whether you'll drink, or arranging now to be drunk or coerced at 6 p.m.). Then write the question, then the model answer, then the marking scheme.

- Level and format: upper-level undergraduate seminar; take-home; nominally 90 minutes; 1,500-word limit for students. The question should be self-contained — students see the case in full.

- The case: a variant I'll call Case S, the toxin puzzle with a surety device. As in the original, an eccentric offers you $1M, transferred instantly and irrevocably, on the sole condition that at noon you now form a sincere intention to drink a vial of toxin at 6 p.m.; the toxin causes a day of nausea and nothing worse; you need not drink, and the payment does not depend on whether you drink. The addition: before you answer, the experimenter shows you a capsule. Swallow it at noon and it guarantees, by direct intervention in your motor systems, that you drink at 6 p.m. — you would drink knowingly and unwillingly, but you would drink. It is free and otherwise harmless. The terms are then amended: the $1M is paid if and only if at noon you now intend to drink at 6 p.m. and you do not swallow the capsule. The experimenter's scanner reads both your intention and whether you swallowed, infallibly. Build into the statement of the case whatever clauses are needed to block the dodge-list you brainstormed: no self-deception, no arranging to be drunk or otherwise unable to resist later, and the capsule is the only device available.

- Structure: three parts. (a) Does the mere availability of a device that would guarantee the action make the intention formable? (b) Could you intend to swallow the capsule — intending it in order to make it true that you will drink, in order to be able to intend to drink? If not, is there a regress, and what does the regress show? (c) Use the comparison between the original puzzle and Case S to adjudicate among at least three diagnoses of what goes wrong: a doxastic view (intention requires, or is constrained by, the belief that you will perform the action); a reasons view (the consideration that justifies the intention must also be a reason for the action, and here it is not); a planning or functional view (intention is a commitment whose job is to settle and coordinate future action, so forming one you know in advance you will discard is idle); and my friend's skeptical view (nothing about the nature of intention is at stake — only our inability to manipulate our own attitudes at will). Students should say which diagnosis the pair of cases supports, and say what would have to be true, or what kind of case would have to be possible, for the skeptic to be right.

- Make the question unanswerable by summary: require students to distinguish intending from predicting, to state precisely why the reason for intending fails to transmit into a reason for drinking, and to take a position on whether intentions can be formed at will.

- Model answer: about 1,000–1,200 words.

- Marking scheme: 20 marks total, organized around five or six must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the typical way students get each one wrong. Separate the A from the B explicitly: name the move a B answer omits or muddles.

- Assume closed-book conditions with no library access. Neither the question nor the model answer may depend on any citation or on knowledge of the secondary literature; name positions descriptively (the doxastic view, the reasons view, and so on), not by author.

- Finally, after the marking scheme, add a note of no more than 120 words on where the model answer itself takes a contestable stance. Say how confident you are in that stance and name the single point you are least sure of, so that I know where a student may disagree with the model answer and still deserve full marks.
