Please write a short story, for my senior philosophy elective, that dramatizes what our textbook calls the boundary problem: before a group can decide anything, it has to settle who counts as part of the group, and that prior decision can quietly determine the outcome. Set it at a high school that must settle, in one student-body vote, whether to ban phones for the next four years.

Two invented complications should drive the plot. First, the school's stakeholder rule: anyone who will live under the decision for at least a quarter of its duration gets a full vote, so graduating seniors get a quarter of a vote and the incoming eighth graders, who were not even enrolled when the vote was announced, get a full one. Second, a procedure called opting out: any group that expects to lose may declare itself outside the student body for the purposes of the vote, in which case it keeps its claim on shared goods such as the buses and the library but is not bound by whatever passes.

I want the regress to be felt rather than tidied away — every repair to the voter roll is itself challenged, and the repair to that challenge is challenged in turn — and I want the story to end with the narrator, who has spent the whole story arguing that membership should be settled by those with the least stake in the result, having to cast the tie-breaking vote on a motion that would disenfranchise her own group.
