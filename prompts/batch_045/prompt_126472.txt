Here is the practice I want reconstructed.

In ordinary medical settings a patient's own numeric rating of how much they hurt — the 0 to 10 number recorded alongside pulse and blood pressure — is treated as the measure of the thing that matters, and it is what doses are titrated to. Yet the same institutions quietly abandon that deference in two directions. First, retrospective ratings are treated in court as authoritative accounts of how much a patient suffered, even though it is well documented that a patient's global rating of a painful episode tracks its worst moment and its final moments far more than its duration or total burden: the number is apparently constructed by a compressing heuristic rather than read off a running total. Second, suppose a hospital system (as I am told some are moving toward) pilots a monitor that continuously scores nociception from facial and autonomic signals, displays that score to the patient on the same screen where they are asked to enter their own number, and instructs clinicians to record the midpoint of the two whenever they disagree by more than three points — with the result that a patient who rates 9 and sees a machine 2 on the screen will often rate 6 an hour later.

I am genuinely puzzled by the shape of this. The practice seems to presuppose that a subject's report has a special authority that no third-person measurement can override, and simultaneously to treat that report as one noisy channel among several, correctable by a machine and contaminated by what the machine says.
