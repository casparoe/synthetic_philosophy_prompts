I am deputy director for public programmes at the National Archives, and next month I have to give the opening remarks — six minutes, roughly 800 words — at the ceremony marking the handover of the Sembera District record books (1921–1959) from the cultural relations agency of the former administering power, which paid for their digitisation, to us.

The room will be genuinely mixed: a junior minister and a small delegation from the donor government; our own director and staff; professional archivists from both countries; a party of historians; the press; and, in the front row, the descendants of the man listed first in the document the whole ceremony is built around — a 1947 Statement of Hut Tax Defaulters for the district, whose facing page the programme reproduces under the caption "restoring the names of those history forgot." The family has asked to speak after the minister, so my remarks come first and must not pre-empt or ventriloquise what they will say.

I want the speech to be shaped, not decorated, by a distinction I keep coming back to. My old teacher, who ran the colonial records room for twenty years, called it "the ask of the file": every document in a collection is the answer to a question some office needed answered, so an archive is less a text with gaps in it than a questionnaire with a fixed set of items. The 1947 statement answers a question about arrears; the correspondence around it answers questions about whether the arrears were collectable. Nothing in it answers a question about what the people listed thought, wanted, or feared, and no amount of additional material from the same office supplies that — more files just mean more copies of the same question. Reading the roll "against the grain" gives you inferences that the question constrains, not a voice. Alongside this I use a working distinction between three kinds of silence: the unasked (no procedure ever required the fact to be recorded), the unkept (it was recorded and then weeded, lost, or destroyed), and the unfindable (it is legibly on the page and simply cannot be reached — no index, no catalogue entry, one reading room on another continent). Digitisation addresses only the third.

I need the speech to honour three constraints at once. It must not say that the silences are being filled, that voices are restored, or that the named are now "in the record," because on my own account none of that is happening — the names in that ledger were in the record all along, as defaulters, and what changes is only who can reach them. It must not say that the files are merely instruments of rule, because I think they are plainly valuable and I want the families to be able to use them, and because a five-year digitisation programme depends on the goodwill of the people sitting in the second row. And it must land as hospitality rather than as a lecture or a scolding: it has to be sayable by a state employee, in front of a minister who is about to take the podium, without jargon, and without insulting anyone in the room.

Write the speech. Take a definite position on the hardest choice in it — whether I say in plain words, in front of the donor, that what is being handed over addresses only the third kind of silence, or whether I encode that some other way — and give your reasons; if you think the speech should decline to name the point directly, say what it should do instead and why that is not cowardice. Then add a note of no more than 200 words: what line you committed the speech to, where it leans on a contested reading of "the ask of the file" or of the three silences, and the strongest objection you would expect from someone in that room.
