I'm on a volunteer climate advisory committee for our mid-sized city — which is a fancy way of saying I'm the person who actually reads the spreadsheets. Our draft climate plan does three things with time that, set next to each other, look like they can't all be right, and I'd like help working out what's going on underneath.

- The whole plan is built around a cumulative carbon budget: one number of tonnes we're allowed to emit between now and 2050, and as near as I can tell it makes no difference inside that window whether a tonne goes up in 2026 or in 2049. A tonne is a tonne.
- Part of how we hit the number is offsets. One project on the table protects a forest for a hundred years and then the protection lapses (and it presumably gets cleared later). The protocol gives it about half the credit per tonne it would get for permanent protection, via this "tonne-year" calculation that treats a century of delay as worth roughly half of not emitting at all. A similar project nearby with a permanent easement gets full credit.
- Separately, when we convert methane and the other short-lived gases into CO2-equivalent for the inventory, we use a multiplier that as I understand it adds up the warming a pulse of the gas causes over a fixed hundred-year window and then simply stops counting.

So inside the budget, timing does nothing. In the tonne-year rule, timing does a lot. In the equivalence multiplier, timing matters right up to a cutoff and then stops mattering completely. None of us think that's three names for one idea, but none of us can say what the difference is meant to be, and one member keeps saying "the people in 2125 will be richer than us, so of course delay is fine," which I suspect is a fourth thing again.

I don't want any reading list or citations — I'll go find the literature myself, and honestly I mostly need this before a meeting thursday. What I want is the conceptual map. What view of what we owe to people who come after us does each of those three rules fit with? Start with the tonne-year one: is that half-credit secretly just a discount rate in disguise, i.e. a claim that later people's welfare counts less, or can it be defended with no discounting of anyone's welfare at all?

My half-formed idea is that the honest version goes through rate rather than level: what actually hurts a society isn't a temperature but the gap between how fast conditions are changing and how fast it can adapt, so delay is genuinely worth something even if future lives count exactly as much as ours — and on that story it should be worth more or less than half depending on what else is happening, not a flat fifty percent. Does that hold up? And is it the same argument as the "they'll be richer" one, or a different argument that happens to point the same way?

Then the two things I really need for the meeting: which of these three numbers actually commit us to a substantive view about the future, and which would look exactly the same under several quite different theories — i.e. where can our committee stop feeling guilty? And if we took the rate-vs-level idea seriously instead, what concretely would change in how we score the forest project and in how we treat that hundred-year cutoff?
