I teach a small upper-level seminar, cross-listed between philosophy and computer science, on self-reference: Gödel's 1931 incompleteness argument, Kleene's recursion theorem as a fixed-point result, von Neumann's sketch of a self-reproducing automaton, and the chapters on Typogenetics in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. The final is a ninety-minute in-class paper: one essay question, answers of roughly 900 words, marked out of 20. The block I still have to write is the last one, on Hofstadter's claim that there is a deep isomorphism between DNA and Gödel numbering, and my worry is that the strong students will all hand in the same paragraph: DNA is a code, the cell is the machinery that reads the code, the code therefore describes the machinery, so a self-replicating strand is just a Gödel sentence in biological dress. I want a question that forecloses that move without turning the exam into a test of who memorised Hofstadter's enzyme table, so the seminar has worked throughout with a stripped-down system I stipulated myself, Typogenetics_0: strands are finite strings over the four-letter alphabet A, C, G, T; a pool is a finite multiset of strands; any strand in the pool can serve either as a substrate that an enzyme edits, as a template that is copied symbol by symbol, or as a program that is translated into an enzyme; translation reads the program strand in consecutive pairs of letters, each pair selecting one operation from a fixed repertoire that includes appending the current symbol to a growing output strand, moving one position along the substrate, inserting or deleting a symbol, cutting the substrate, and copying it; an enzyme may act on any strand in the pool, including the strand it was translated from; and the only conditional primitive in the whole system is a search instruction that finds the next occurrence of a specified letter and continues there, or halts the enzyme if no such occurrence exists, leaving whatever partial output has been produced. There is no negation, no arithmetic, no variables: the halt-on-failure branch is the only way a strand can register the absence of something. Students have been told to count a strand as self-replicating just in case translating it and running the resulting enzymes on a pool containing it causes a second copy of that very strand to exist in the pool.

What I would like from you is one essay question whose crux is the difference between a causal fixed point and a diagonal one. It should make students say what the correct formal analogue of a self-replicating strand actually is — a quine, that is, a fixed point of Kleene's recursion theorem for a program that merely outputs its own description, as opposed to a Gödel sentence, which is a fixed point taken against a representable predicate such as provability — and then say what would have to be true of Typogenetics_0, or what would have to be added to it, for it to contain the second kind of thing rather than only the first: whether the search-and-halt primitive is enough to diagonalise against a property of strands, what it would mean for a strand to predicate something of its own description rather than merely to cause a copy of it, and what the difference does to Hofstadter's isomorphism claim. It should also force them to say where the DNA–Gödel isomorphism genuinely holds — the substrate/enzyme or data/machine duality in the cell mirroring the way arithmetisation collapses the distinction between a formal system's objects and its syntax — and where it breaks, given that nothing in the typo system has truth-conditions and whatever aboutness a strand has is conferred on it from outside by the translation and folding rules. Three constraints: the question must be answerable from the stipulation above and from the seminar readings, with no recall of Hofstadter's actual enzyme table required; it must not require a student to settle in ninety minutes whether the enzyme repertoire is computationally universal, but should let a strong student point out what would follow either way; and it must not be answerable by the sentence DNA is a code, so DNA is a Gödel number. A colleague in computer science, who thinks the whole business is the recursion theorem restated, and a colleague in philosophy, who thinks it is barely more than a metaphor, have both offered to read the draft, so the question needs to make each of them do work rather than letting either rest on their position.

Please give me: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with whatever short lead-in or definition it needs; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 organised around five must-hit moves, with the marks assigned to each and a one-line note on the characteristic way students miss it. Finish with a short explicit statement of what separates an A answer, at 17 or above, from a B answer in the 13 to 16 range, phrased as the presence or absence of specific moves rather than as a difference in polish.
