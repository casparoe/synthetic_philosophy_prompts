The setup is an invented empirical pattern, so just grant it for the sake of the argument. Imagine intracranial recordings in epilepsy patients where, on some trials, you get what we've started calling dark ignition: the stimulus produces the full global-workspace signature — late, sustained, widely distributed activity, the standard ignition marker — and the content is demonstrably available downstream. The patient can pick it out on a forced-choice test well above chance, it primes their decisions minutes later, and it even shows up in what they say if you ask them to talk for a minute about whatever was going through their head. But asked directly, at the time, they say they saw nothing. Not in a hesitant, "I'm not sure" way either: they'll take a bet that no stimulus was presented, they stick to it when you encourage them, and when you tell them the recording says otherwise they say things like, well, the electrode must have picked something up, I didn't see anything. On other trials with the very same stimulus — echo trials, we call them — you get ignition plus a later, reverberating prefrontal component, and those ones get reported as a perfectly ordinary visual experience. And if you re-present a dark-ignition stimulus a few seconds later, it usually flips into an echo trial and gets reported.

What I want you to argue is that my friend is wrong to read dark ignition as evidence for a real gap between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness.
