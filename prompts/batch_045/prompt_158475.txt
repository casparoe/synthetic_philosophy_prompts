I'm a TA for an introductory decision theory course, and I have one eighty-minute session on the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if the first heads falls on the nth toss the payoff is (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n — so the expectation, summed in the natural order, is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − … = ln 2 ≈ 0.69, while other orderings of the very same terms yield any real number you please. I am not asking for an explanation of the paradox; I am asking for a plan for teaching it.

The audience: one semester of probability, no analysis (to them "convergent" just means "adds up to something finite"), and they have already met the St. Petersburg game and taken away the moral that bounding the utility function makes the problem go away.

Please give me: (1) the order in which the components have to arrive, and what specifically breaks if two of them are swapped — in particular where the absolute/conditional distinction has to enter, given that I have no vocabulary for it; (2) the one example that should carry the session, and the properties such an example needs. My candidate is the alternating harmonic series reordered as 1 + 1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/7 − 1/4 + … = (3/2)ln 2, but I worry that a purely algebraic example lets them file the whole thing under "infinite series have tricks" rather than "expected value is not a well-defined quantity here"; (3) the misconceptions in the order they will form, and the point at which each should be headed off — I expect three: "just bound utility, it is St. Petersburg again," "no casino can pay, so truncate the tail," and, the one I think actually matters, "the order of summation is fixed by the game, so the rearrangements are cheating"; (4) what I should openly decline to settle at this level; and (5) one final question that separates a student who has the real lesson from one who has merely learned that infinity is weird. My candidate is: what is the expectation if the outcomes are indexed by payout size instead of by number of tosses? Does that question do the work I want?

One further thing I would like the session to land, and do not know how to stage: that choosing a summation order, a truncation, or a principal value is not discovering a number but importing a choice about how fast the tails get discounted — closer to a risk attitude than to a fact about the game. Where in the sequence should that go, and how do I say it without leaving them thinking that expected value is simply arbitrary?
