So I somehow ended up on this little volunteer committee that writes the wall text for a museum exhibit in my town about the big flood we had a few years ago, and I've gotten way more into it then I expected. The argument on the committee is about who or what the text should say "did it." My neighbour, whose basement still floods every spring, wants the county named, because they approved a subdivision and a parking lot upstream on land that used to soak the water up. The older people on the committee want the text to just say the river flooded — "the river did it," the same way you'd say a person did something. Meanwhile the adjuster who handled my insurance claim used the phrase "act of God" about that exact same event, which seems to mean the opposite thing: that nothing with a mind was behind it, so nobody pays. And then there's the naming business — storms get human names, and the really destructive ones get their names retired forever like they've been disgraced, but in a courtroom the same storm is just a brute natural event with no agent attached to it.

My sister, who did a history degree, is no help at all, because she says that citing non-human causes is precisely the move historians are trained to distrust: explaining why some region is poor by pointing at its climate or its soil is supposedly the classic discredited move. But when I pointed out that the popular history books about the European conquest of the Americas basically give germs top billing, and everyone treats that as enlightened rather than creepy, she just got annoyed and said it's different. None of us can say what the difference actually is. (There's also a volcano in one of the books I've been reading that supposedly gave Europe a year without a summer, and it gets written about like a full-blown historical character with plans.)

So: what does our everyday blaming practice here actually presuppose — the personal names for storms, retiring the names of the bad ones, "the river did it" talk sitting right next to act-of-God clauses, and the unwritten rule about when you're allowed to make a germ or a drought or a volcano the star of a historical explanation and when doing that is taboo? Does talking as if a storm or a pathogen were an actor commit us to anything real about agency and causation, or is it just a manner of speaking that several quite different theories would happily allow? Is the practice even consistent with itself, given that we hand out agency in the storytelling and then take it back in the courtroom and in the textbook? And here's the test case I keep getting stuck on: a friend proposed what he calls the "someone could have said no" rule — you may cite a non-human cause as a historical actor only when no human decision anywhere nearby could have prevented the outcome, and otherwise you have to name the decision. Is that really the rule the practice is running on, or is it doing something else, like reaching for non-human causes exactly when that shifts blame away from people we'd rather not blame? If we did adopt it, what would have to change — would we stop naming hurricanes, would our wall text have to name the county, and would my adjuster still get to say act of God?
