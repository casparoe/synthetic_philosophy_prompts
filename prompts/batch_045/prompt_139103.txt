I am putting together a seminar session on whether advanced AI systems should be built as tools or as agents, and I want a scripted dialogue to use as the discussion text. Everything it needs should be available to a careful thinker working from memory: please lean on conceptual argument and on facts about machine learning that are common knowledge, and do not reach for citations, paper titles, or author names. If a load-bearing claim turns on an empirical guess about how such systems will in fact behave, have the character making it say so rather than assert it flatly.

Set the scene. A lab has the capability to build one of two systems and must commit its next two years to one architecture:

The first is a mandate-bound tool. It plans only within a task specification the operator writes down; it declines open-ended objectives; it is deliberately engineered not to build a model of the operator's unstated aims; it hands back intermediate results and waits.

The second is a delegated agent. It infers what the operator wants from sparse instruction, decomposes that into sub-goals, acts in the world for weeks at a time without checking in, and is designed to be corrected, redirected, and shut down mid-task.

Two senior people at the lab, who agree about the empirical situation and about the size of the stakes, argue about which to build. Write them as equally competent: neither should be a naive accelerationist nor a nervous abstainer, and each should be able to state the other's position in terms the other would accept. Real back-and-forth, please — each should respond to the actual argument just made and concede at least one substantive point, rather than delivering two adjacent monologues.

I want the exchange to work through the following, which I take to be where the interesting action is:

First, the interpretive threshold. Past a certain level of usefulness, a system cannot do what is asked without reconstructing aims the operator never stated — and reconstructing someone's aims requires representing them as an agent whose behaviour is to be anticipated, which already gives the system something like a point of view from which the operator figures as a resource or an obstacle. The tool proponent needs an answer here: either the tax imposed by staying below the threshold is smaller than it looks, or it is worth paying.

Second, the steering problem. A corrigible system has to treat human intervention as evidence about what is wanted rather than as interference to be routed around. But the very capacity to read an intervention as evidence is arguably the capacity to have a view about whether being intervened on is desirable. Have the agent proponent face this squarely rather than wave at it.

Third, the relocation objection. Agency may be a property of the human-plus-system loop rather than of either component, in which case a tool does not eliminate agency but relocates it: the human remains the principal in name, while the system increasingly determines the beliefs and options on which the human's decisions depend. If the tool's influence runs through the operator's epistemic state, it may be harder to notice and to audit than an agent's overt actions. This cuts against the tool proponent, and I want to see whether they can answer it.

Fourth, the instability problem. The cost of architectural restraint is borne by whoever exercises it, while the benefits of crossing the threshold accrue to whoever crosses first. So even if restraint is collectively better, it may not be stable. I want the tool proponent's institutional answer — standards, procurement rules, audit regimes, interface requirements — pressed on the question of whether it can bind the parties who would gain most from defecting.

Do not end in a stalemate. The lab has to choose, and I want both characters to feel the pull of the other's case and still land somewhere: end with each stating what they would actually recommend to the lab, and let at least one of them have shifted their recommendation, or their confidence, in the course of the conversation.

Aim for roughly 1,400 words of dialogue.

Then add a note of about 250 words in which you take a side: say whose argument you think is stronger and why, identify the single strongest objection to the verdict you have just given, and name the one contested empirical assumption the whole exchange rests on most heavily. I want a committed reading, not a survey of the dialectic.
