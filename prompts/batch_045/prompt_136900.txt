I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, finishing a dissertation chapter on moral residue and the ethics of public office. At a workshop last week I presented a case I've been using: a minister of health, mid-epidemic, who authorizes a coercive quarantine she knows will fall hardest on a neighbourhood the state has already failed, and who knows too that the compliance of that neighbourhood was bought with promises the state is now breaking. I argued that even if her order is, all things considered, what she ought to do, something is left over afterwards that is not discharged by the fact that she did the right thing. A senior commentator replied, without ceremony, that this is just a case of you can't make an omelette without breaking a few eggs, and that whatever I was calling residue is either ordinary regret about a cost or a failure of nerve about consequences. The room moved on.

I want to take that reply seriously rather than resent it, which means I want to know what the proverb could actually be claiming. Could you lay out, as a menu I can work through, the distinct precise theses it might be expressing: the near-trivial ones (some choices impose costs; every action forecloses others; nothing of value is achieved without loss), the substantive ones (the metaphor recategorizes a violation as a production input; permission by outcome extinguishes residue; causal authorship of a necessary harm is morally different from the cost of having authored it; the justification of the act is inherited by the agent's subsequent condition), and the ones that look simply false (nothing is ever owed to those who are harmed necessarily; an agent who acts for the best has no reason to do anything at all afterwards). For each, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false, and — this is what I most need — whether it would do the work my commentator wanted, i.e. dissolve the residue rather than merely redescribe it.

Three further things would help.

First, the metaphor's own logic. The proverb invites a picture in which the broken things are inputs rather than victims, the cook is not soiled by the breaking, the mess is consumed by the finished dish, and the dish settles the accounting. Which of these features is doing the philosophical work, and what happens to the proverb's force if we shift to the vocabulary of dirt that the literature actually uses — where dirt accumulates, transfers to the office and perhaps to the polity, resists being absorbed into the outcome, and may call for something ritual rather than compensatory?

Second, a colleague offered a neighbouring proverb in the same spirit: if decent people won't do it, worse people will. Am I right that this is a different animal — a counterfactual claim about who will occupy the role, not about the moral shape of the act — and that if it is true it relocates the whole discussion into a comparative-harms frame, where my residue claim has to be reargued rather than asserted? Say whether that variant does the dismissive work better or worse than the omelette, and why.

Third, I have been trying out a distinction between residue-as-debt (the agent owes the victim something, dischargeable by apology, repair, or public acknowledgement) and residue-as-stain (a change in the agent's moral standing that no repair clears, and which may be the real point of the dirty vocabulary). Can either reading of the proverb express the second? If not, is that a defect in the proverb or in my distinction?

Finally, tell me where the disambiguation itself is most likely to fail. For each verdict give a confidence level, flag the reading you are least sure of, and name the consideration — an argument, a case, a distinction I have not drawn — that would move you to reclassify a reading from platitude to substantive, or from false to defensible. I would rather have an honest menu with error bars than one confident reading, and I need to know which items I should not lean on when I reply to the commentator in writing.
