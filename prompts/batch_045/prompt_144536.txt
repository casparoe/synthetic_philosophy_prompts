I host a long-form interview series on political philosophy — one guest, ninety minutes, no panel — and next month's guest is the political theorist Dr. Ellen Brace, who has spent two books arguing that Chesterton's fence should be read neither as a counsel of timidity nor as piety toward ancestors, but as an epistemic principle: social practices are causally opaque to the people who live inside them, so the survival of a practice is defeasible evidence that it is doing work its participants cannot see, and the burden of proof falls on whoever would take it down. I'd like eight questions for her, each with a note on the reply she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed specifically to that reply rather than to the question.

I've read enough of her work that I don't want anything that lets her restate the parable or explain why she isn't simply a reactionary; the questions should press at the joints. Please include one each on: whether the thing the reformer is supposed to understand is the purpose the practice was erected for or its present effects (I expect her to answer that it is neither — that the point is only that there was some reason, so removal is an unmodeled intervention — and I want the follow-up to press whether the principle then collapses into ordinary causal inquiry, leaving the rhetoric of ignorance about origins doing illegitimate work); on a symmetry worry I raised at a dinner party and couldn't get past, namely that the same test applied to the innovation — know why it was never put up — paralyzes reform unless she can ground the asymmetry in something other than mere existence (her likely reply is that the status quo has survived an adversarial filtering process that proposals have not, so I want the follow-up to force her to say what to do about durable practices under no such pressure, liturgical vesture or sumptuary rules or degree requirements, and who then carries the empirical burden of showing the filter was operating); on what she would count as evidence that a fence is genuinely dead, with a historical case she would concede, and on whether the principle can fail a test at all; on Burke's own defence of the 1688 settlement as restoration rather than innovation, and what distinguishes a restoration from an innovation if any reformer can claim the former; and on the analogy she draws between inheriting a practice and inheriting a language, where I expect her to lean on Burke's image of a partnership among the living, the dead and the unborn — the follow-up should press that a grammar is not a command, and ask what carries normative authority across the analogy when the inherited item is a rule enforced by sanction.

I'd also like one question on her proposal to institutionalize the principle by requiring a documented account of a rule's history before repeal, pressing whether that burden is discharged by perfunctory archival work and why no equivalent burden falls on those who keep the rule. Close with a question about what would actually move her — a class of cases, an episode, an empirical finding that would lead her to restrict or abandon the principle — together with a version to use if she concedes nothing at all. Please keep the register exacting but not hostile: she is a generous interviewee who gets bored by softballs, and I would rather risk a hard question than a flattering one.
