Two friends of mine got into it after dinner the other night and I said I'd referee, but honestly I couldnt tell who was winning. The question they were fighting over was whether the physical size of an animal's brain has anything at all to do with how much the animal matters morally — not as a rough shortcut for guessing how smart it is, but in itself.

They set it up with a made-up case so the usual facts couldn't settle it. Imagine we meet a species — they called them the Sarn — whose entire brain is about the size of a walnut, but whose neurons are much smaller and packed far tighter than ours, so they end up with roughly as many neurons and synapses as a human brain, they run faster because the wiring distances are shorter, and on every behavioural and language test we can devise they come out exactly as mentally capable as we are. Question: does the fact that their brains are tiny give us any reason at all to count them less?

(1) Nadia: No, and the reason is that brain size was never the thing doing the work. What does the moral work is what the organ lets the creature do — suffer, have a life that can go better or worse, care about what happens to it, stand in relations to others. Once a walnut-sized brain does all of that as well as ours does, size contributes nothing. Insisting otherwise is like saying somebody matters less because their liver is smaller.

(2) Wes: That's too fast. In real life we never get to inspect "what the organ lets the creature do" directly. For the cases we actually agonise over — octopuses, fish, insects, anything built very differently from us — what we reach for is some measure of neural hardware, and size, or neuron counts, which track size, is basically all we've got. And it isn't mere laziness. There's a real reason to think size carries information: doing the same job in less volume means compressing, and compressing plausibly costs you redundancy and fine-grained detail. So size isn't the fundamental variable, fine, but it's our best window onto a richness we can't measure any other way.

(3) Nadia: But we stipulated the compression away — same neuron count, same connectivity, faster signalling, identical performance. If your reply is "maybe there's some further hidden cost we haven't thought to measure," then you've conceded my point in the only form it was ever made. Size itself isn't doing anything; you're just betting on some unmeasured quantity that happens to travel with size in the animals we happen to know about.

(4) Wes: Okay, but turn it around, because I think your view has the bigger problem. Look at how we treat our own species. Somebody with advanced dementia, or who's lost a chunk of cortex to a stroke, doesn't get counted as a fraction of a person. And nobody thinks a hypothetical super-intelligent being with ten times our processing power is owed ten times as much consideration. So the "how much capacity" axis is one we deliberately refuse to grade along inside our own species — and yet across species almost everybody does grade: killing a chimpanzee strikes people as far worse than killing a mouse, and a mouse worse than a beetle. If moral status were a flat threshold, those gradations shouldn't be there at all. If it's something like neural equipment, they're exactly what you'd predict.

(5) Nadia: Those gradations are real, but they're not gradations in status — they're gradations in how much there is to lose. A chimp has a longer future, more projects, a richer mental life, so its death is a bigger harm, and that's perfectly compatible with the chimp mattering fully and the mouse mattering fully. Same reason we think killing someone with decades ahead of them is worse than killing someone at the very end of a long life, without thinking the first person has a higher moral status. So the flatness above the threshold is a feature of my view, not a bug, and it leaves brain size with even less to do than you thought.

(6) Wes: Last thing. Suppose I grant you the harm-magnitude picture. You still have to say magnitude for whom. If what you're counting is total processing, then one human and a barn full of mice with the same combined neuron count come out as equal harms, which nobody accepts — so you need magnitude per subject, not in aggregate. And now size creeps back in, because per-subject hardware is roughly what distinguishes a brain that can lose a lot from one that can only lose a little.

After that they just repeated themselves in different words. Can you go through the six moves and rate each one — clearly mistaken, weak, decent, strong, knock-down, whatever scale you like — and say briefly why? And I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing, since I have a feeling theyre both sliding over something and I can't put a name to it.
