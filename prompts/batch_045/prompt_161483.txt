had a long and kind of dumb argument with my friend last night about the problem of logical omniscience — the way the standard modal treatment of knowledge (truth at all the accessible worlds, closure under known entailment, every tautology known, and knowing the same thing twice no matter how you phrase it) seems to commit you to saying that every agent already knows every consequence of what they know, including consequences nobody could actually compute. His position is that it's a pseudo-problem: read the box normatively, or swap in some resource-bounded proxy for knowledge, and whatever's left over is just the agent being irrational, not the formalism failing. My position is that the failures aren't all of one kind and that some of them survive even if you hand the agent unlimited time and unlimited scratch paper, but I couldn't make him feel that, we were just trading made-up examples at each other. So I want to built something where he runs into it himself.

The setting: I run a fortnightly workshop on formal epistemology, about 14 people, mostly grad students with a few advanced undergrads, everyone's seen the basic possible-worlds semantics for a knowledge operator but nothing beyond that. One 75-minute session, whiteboard and printed handouts only, no laptops, and I can hand out counters or candy but there's no real money. He's agreed to show up as a participant. What I want is a game — rules, materials, payoff scheme, timeline — where the scoring does the work. My rough idea, improve or replace it as you like: hand everyone a short booklet of boring relational facts about some invented domain (seedings in a fake sports league, twelve cousins and a will, a train timetable, whatever generates facts whose consequences are mechanical but tedious), then individually and under time pressure answer a deck of queries, each marked yes/no/can't tell plus a confidence and plus a tick for "I actually derived this" versus "I guessed." Then score them against the full logical closure of the booklet, with "can't tell" and lucky guesses and careful derivations all scored the same way, so that people get credited with knowledge they demonstrably didn't have and punished for not having produced it. The deck needs to contain at least four different kinds of query so the failures come apart: one entailed only by a long tedious chain, one that's a tautology or independent of the premises entirely, one where the same content is asked twice in different words (and don't tell them, I want them to notice their own answers disagreed), and one that follows only given some background fact that half the room wasn't given. Then, after the reveal, I want them to switch sides and try to fix it: they write down the rule for when to attribute knowledge to an agent like themselves, and then I hit them with a held-out query set they haven't seen so they can't just enumerate the things they happened to work out. Nobody should hear the name of the problem, or the taxonomy, until afterwards.

What I need from you beyond the runnable thing: a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the problem (the computability ones, the awareness one, the rephrasing one, and the "it's just irrationality" move my friend wants to make); an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each — I'm especially worried about someone just grinding through the long chain and beating the game, someone saying "can't tell" to everything, someone spotting the rephrased pair and meta-gaming it, and someone in the awareness condition shouting that it wasn't fair, which I actually want them to do but I want to know what to do with it when they do; and a frank note on which feature of the problem the game can't reproduce and which therefore I have to just say out loud. He's going to be in the room being smug, so if there's a way to set the payoff scheme so that his "just make it normative" move has to pay rent during the activity rather than only in the discussion, do that.
