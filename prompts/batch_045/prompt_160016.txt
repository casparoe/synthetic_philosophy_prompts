I coach a competitive debate team at a large public high school, and in the same building I moderate a monthly discussion group for adults who read philosophy on their own. In both settings I run into the same move several times a month, and I have never had a consistent way of ruling on it. Instead of offering evidence, a speaker says that the opponent could not even make the claim without presupposing its opposite. You cannot argue that there are no reasons for belief, because to argue is to offer a reason. You cannot say that every truth is relative, because that very statement presents itself as true and not merely relative. You cannot deny that you acted freely, since raising the denial is something you did. You cannot claim that nothing is objectively valuable while writing a speech that urges the audience to change its mind. Sometimes the move deserves to win the point. Sometimes it feels like rhetorical judo, a way of punishing someone for speaking at all. I have since learned that philosophers apparently have a name for this shape of argument and a long dispute about what, if anything, it establishes, but I have no training in the subject and I am asking you rather than reading about it.

What I need is a decision procedure, not an essay. My students debate every other weekend, and whether this move counts decides rounds; I also have to give the adults something they can use the same evening. So I want six or seven questions in a fixed order, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, a point at which the procedure returns a clear no, and a point at which it stops and hands the decision to the judge because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. It has to fit on one side of an index card and be runnable by a fifteen-year-old in about ninety seconds in the prep room, with no technical vocabulary. Where a step needs a piece of theory in order to be understood, say so and say what the plain-language version gives up. The judges are community volunteers who will not accept anything that sounds like theory, and neither will half my students.

Three things I have noticed on my own that I want built in, but corrected if I have them wrong. First, these arguments are supposed to start from something the opponent cannot deny; yet some of them start from something the opponent is perfectly willing to give up, such as their own continued participation in the exchange, since they can stop talking or say they are only venting a feeling rather than asserting anything. That difference seems to matter, and I want a question that catches it. Second, there is a reply that deflates many of these arguments for me: granted, we have to think that way, but is that any reason to think things are that way? Some arguments I hear seem to collapse under that reply and others seem untouched by it, and I cannot say which is which. To me this looks like the deepest issue in the whole neighbourhood, so I would like the procedure to contain an explicit test for vulnerability to it, and to say what an argument would have to add in order to be immune. Third, some of these arguments strike me as circularity in costume, where the starting point quietly contains the conclusion the argument was meant to establish. I want a question that catches that without rejecting every argument of this shape on principle, since a few of them clearly do real work.

Please then run the finished procedure on three cases and be blunt about the results. Apply it to the relativism case above, where a speaker says that the claim there is no objective truth refutes itself. Apply it to the case of reasons for belief, because I have been using that argument myself for about ten years and I would rather be told honestly that it establishes only something weaker than I have been claiming, if that is what it establishes. And apply it to a case I expect to fail outright, such as the claim that no one can deny having free will, since anyone who says so could have stayed silent instead. Where the procedure hands a decision to the judge, say so explicitly and say what the judge is being asked to decide.

Finally, tell me which of the questions are really judgment calls wearing procedural clothing, and what the procedure cannot do. In particular, I want to know whether it can ever certify a conclusion as true of the world rather than merely as something we are committed to in virtue of thinking or speaking at all, and how a judge should weigh a move that survives the whole procedure against a single piece of ordinary evidence pointing the other way.
