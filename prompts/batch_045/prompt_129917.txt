I teach a final-year seminar on formal epistemology, and I am drafting this year's final. A large share of my students arrive having spent years reading rationalist blogs and forum comment threads, and they bring a habit with them that I want the exam to break: they treat *Dutch book* as a conversation-stopper. Someone's credences look strange and the verdict comes down that they are not a coherent probability distribution, therefore Dutch-bookable, therefore irrational — as if the word itself carried the normative weight, and as if the absence of an actual bookie with a satchel were an empirical accident rather than part of the design of the argument.

I want one question, in three parts, that forces students to cash the argument out rather than invoke it.

Constraints: closed book, ninety minutes, twenty-five marks, pitched at final-year undergraduates and MA students. Nothing in it may require library or web access: the paper should supply every definition the student needs, and I want the question itself to say that no marks are awarded for naming authors, papers, or theorems, and that a student can take full marks without them. The mathematics available is elementary — finite sets of worlds, real-valued credences, expectation, dominance.

I have been playing with a device I would like the question built around, though you may improve on it. Call it the posted-quote bookie. The bookie never learns the agent's credences. Instead the agent is asked to post, for any proposition the bookie names, a number: the price at which she is willing to take either side of a unit bet on that proposition. The bookie then chooses the side and the stake, subject to a fixed cap on the stake, and may repeat this for further propositions, choosing them in light of the quotes already given. The agent is free to decline to quote at all, or to quote something other than her real fair price. Please work out what this mechanism does to the argument: which of the usual idealizations it removes, which it leaves untouched, what exactly it shows when the posted prices are incoherent, what happens when the agent quotes strategically rather than honestly, and whether the argument now constrains the agent's credences or only her quotable, actionable dispositions. I also want the small-stakes cap to be load-bearing rather than decorative.

I would like three things from you.

First, the question itself, written out as it would appear on the paper, with any stipulations and definitions, and with a short paragraph setting up the dialectical situation so that a student cannot answer it by restating the definition of a probability measure. The crux should be whether and how the move from exploitability to irrationality is supposed to go, and what is left of the argument once you notice that the losing package is one the agent could simply decline.

Second, a model answer of about a thousand to twelve hundred words. It should commit to verdicts rather than surveying positions: I will penalise a model answer that merely lists what various people have said about the gap between pragmatic and epistemic arguments.

Third, a marking scheme. Marks per part, totalling twenty-five, with the part built on the posted-quote bookie worth the most. For each part, name the specific moves that earn the marks, and give one line on how students typically go wrong on each. Then give band descriptors that separate an A from a B from a C by what the answer does and does not contain: a C answer should be attainable by someone who has read the Sequences and absorbed that incoherence means a guaranteed loss, while an A answer should require seeing why that gloss is not yet a normative claim.
