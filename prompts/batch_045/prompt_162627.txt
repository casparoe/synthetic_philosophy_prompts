**Candidate 2214, Question 4**

The case: there is a vial in my fridge; drinking it makes me ill for a day and does no lasting damage. If at midnight tonight I intend to drink it at four tomorrow afternoon, a million lands in my account; by midnight the money is already there, hours before I would have to drink, and nothing I do later affects whether I get it.

(a) The argument that I cannot collect: at four o'clock tomorrow drinking has a cost — a day of illness — and no benefit, since the million is already mine whatever I do and the illness has no further effects. So drinking would be irrational. I know I am rational and I know all this, so at midnight I know that I will not drink. But intending to φ entails believing that one will φ — call it the Belief Constraint — and believing that I will drink while knowing that I will not is like believing something I know to be false. So I cannot intend, and so I cannot collect. What makes the case work is the payoff mechanism: the billionaire is in the position of the predictor in Newcomb's problem, since the million is already in the bank before I act, and my drinking later would only ever be evidence about a state of the world that is already fixed. So this is a decision-theory problem in disguise, and the evidential decision theorist has the better of it: the right response is to treat the intention as carrying news value rather than as causing anything.

(b) The premise the argument needs is the Belief Constraint. Anyone who thinks the intention can be formed has to give that up: they will say that intention is a plan-like commitment rather than a prediction about myself, so I can intend to drink without believing that I will, and my knowledge that I shall change my mind does not defeat the intention. There is a further problem for them, though, about how the intention is supposed to get formed at all, since you cannot intend at will. A second option is the move associated with Gauthier's reply: once the intention is in place it constrains what counts as the best available option, so at four o'clock drinking would in fact be what I have most reason to do, and I could believe that I would drink after all.

(c) This is where the puzzle bites in real life. A deterrent threat — a threat to retaliate after a nuclear first strike, say — works only if the threatener genuinely intends to carry it out, and intends rationally. But once deterrence has failed, retaliation kills millions for no gain: it is ex post pointless, exactly like drinking the toxin once the million is in the bank. So by the argument in (a) no rational agent can form the intention to retaliate, and the structure is the same whether the intention is conditional on being attacked or not. It follows that rational deterrence theory is finished: a rational state cannot intend to retaliate, so any threat it issues is a bluff, and since the other side is rational too and can run the same argument it will see the bluff for what it is and will not be deterred. The only way to have a credible deterrent is to arrange things so that retaliation is no longer up to you.

(d) The Second Bidder makes the intention available again. At four o'clock tomorrow I would have a million-dollar reason to drink against a day of illness, so drinking would clearly be worth it; so at midnight I can believe that I will drink; so, given the Belief Constraint, I can intend. This shows that the original puzzle was never really about intention at all: it is just a fact about payoffs, that when the moment for acting arrives there is nothing in it for you, and once you fix that the problem vanishes.
