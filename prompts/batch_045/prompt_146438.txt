In most of experimental consciousness science a subject's verbal report that she saw a stimulus is the gold-standard evidence that she consciously perceived it, yet the method the field officially teaches for handling such reports — Dennett's heterophenomenology — instructs the researcher to record the subject's utterances, suspend judgment on whether what she says about her own experience is true, and assemble out of them a third-personally describable heterophenomenological world that her behavior and her neural recordings are then said to be about. At the same time the field has been moving toward no-report paradigms, in which conscious perception is inferred from eye movements or other involuntary indicators rather than from what the subject says, on the explicit ground that demanding a report confounds perception with metacognitive and decision processes — that is, on the ground that reports are systematically unreliable as measures. The same researchers also defer to subjects about whether they saw something while refusing to treat their claims about what it was like — that their red is your red, that their visual field contains no blind spot — as evidence about experience at all.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of first-person report that this combination of practices embodies, and separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Three things I want answered: whether the bracketing move is merely methodological and compatible with realism about experience, or whether it commits the practitioner to there being no fact of the matter about a subject's experience beyond what is heterophenomenologically accessible; whether one coherent view of report can make it both the gold standard for whether a stimulus was seen and no evidence for what seeing it was like, or whether the practice is really running together two different things under the word report; and whether the turn to no-report paradigms is in tension with the rest of the practice or only discredits reports made under task conditions. Finally: if the strict view is right, what would a lab using this method have to change, and if it is wrong, is there anything the method could in principle turn up that would distinguish a real experience from its heterophenomenological world — or does the method's inability to do so settle the question by itself?
