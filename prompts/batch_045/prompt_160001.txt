I am writing a chapter arguing that the standard generational categories we use in Anglophone social science and policy — the Silent Generation, Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z — are not real kinds in the sense required for cohort membership to do genuine explanatory work. My view is that they are administrative partitions imposed on a continuous birth distribution: retrospective labels that get stabilized by the survey instruments, marketing categories, and journalistic conveniences that deploy them, and that they behave far more like interactive kinds, in the sense made familiar by work on looping effects, than like natural kinds with a discoverable joint in nature at the boundary.

The objection I cannot get past is not an argument but an analogy, and I would like it audited rather than dismissed. A senior colleague in my department, at a workshop where I presented the chapter, put it like this: a generation, she said, is like a wave of immigrants arriving in a country. The group arrives at a particular time, encounters an institutional and technological environment that was not built for it, acquires a shared repertoire of habits and reflexes that natives of the receiving country never acquire, remains marked by that encounter for the rest of its life, and is partly constituted as a group by being treated as one. She drew the familiar natives-and-immigrants picture of technology adoption and generalized it from technology to formative environment as a whole. She then used the analogy to license three conclusions: first, that cohort membership can carry genuine causal weight in explaining attitudes and behavior; second, that where the boundaries fall is discovered rather than stipulated, whatever round numbers appear in the codebook; third, that the fact a category is also used administratively no more undermines it than the fact that immigration authorities classify arrivals undermines the reality of an immigrant cohort.

Please give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the country of origin, the crossing, the port and date of arrival, the receiving country, the natives, the accent, naturalization, the second generation — and then tell me, separately for each of the three conclusions, whether the mapping can carry it, where it strains, and where it breaks outright. I have a list of disanalogies I want on the table, and I want them sorted by whether they bear on the claims she is making rather than by how vivid they are. Immigrants arrive from somewhere, share a prior culture, and can in principle return; a birth cohort arrives from nowhere and stays put. Immigration crosses a border that was antecedently marked on a map, and an individual's arrival is datable to the day, whereas births are continuous and the cutoffs are round numbers chosen for administrative convenience. The receiving country exists independently of any particular wave, while the environment a cohort encounters is partly built by earlier cohorts and is partly rebuilt by the cohort itself once it is large enough to have a market aimed at it. An accent is audible at the time to anyone who listens, whereas generational markers seem to be named retrospectively, often by journalists, and to be contested by the people assigned to them. And an immigrant cohort is, at least initially, a minority inside a continuing population, whereas a generation eventually replaces the population it was defined against.

Tell me which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports into the target that cohorts do not have — discreteness of arrival, a decision to migrate, a shared antecedent culture, an independent receiving context, third-person observability of the mark — and which of those imports are load-bearing for her three conclusions. If the immigration picture cannot carry the weight, is there a better one: a year-group passing through a school system mid-reform, a conscript intake year, a vehicle vintage exposed to one model year of safety regulation? Or does every available candidate import a discreteness that birth cohorts lack, and what would that tell me? Finally, what would my colleague have to show for the analogy to be vindicated rather than merely illustrated: if I were to look at data around a boundary year such as 1965 or 1997, what pattern — a discontinuity, a threshold effect, a durable divergence in trajectories — would actually make the immigrant picture true of generations, and what pattern would leave it as a metaphor doing the work of an argument?
