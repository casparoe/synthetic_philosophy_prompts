Two people in my department have been going at each other for years about whether the poverty of the stimulus argument actually shows anything, and I want the document they should have produced instead of the arguments they've actually had. One of them — call her the nativist — thinks some version of the argument still does real work: children's input underdetermines the grammar they end up with, so at least some of what they end up with isn't learned from the input, and the interesting question is just how much and what shape it is. The other — call him the rich-input guy — thinks every concrete version of the "poverty" claim has quietly dissolved once you look at actual corpus statistics, that domain-general learning operating over genuinely structured input plus social and pragmatic scaffolding can do the job, and that the nativist keeps moving the goalposts between "no procedure could learn this" and "no procedure anyone has actually built has yet learned this."

A distinction I've been using between *evidential* poverty (the input literally doesn't contain the information) and *procedural* poverty (the information is there but no learner of the child's computational budget and data quantity could get to it).
