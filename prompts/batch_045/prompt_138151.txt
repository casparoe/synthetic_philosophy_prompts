Hi — I'm on the grants committee of a small family foundation (five trustees, monthly meetings, maybe a dozen grants a year out of about a million dollars total, and the closest thing we have to a research capacity is me with a spreadsheet). We keep having the same arguement and I want to stop having it by turning it into a form we fill in.

The argument in one paragraph: almost every proposal has near-term effects we can sort of see and long-run effects we can't see at all. Cash transfers to very poor households obviously help the recipients, but they also move local prices, and migration, and maybe fertility, and therefore possibly who exists in twenty years. The deworming proposal has beautiful trial evidence on school attendance, thin evidence on earnings, and no evidence whatsoever on what a slightly better-educated cohort does to a country's politics or growth rate. And then we get asks like a curriculum-reform advocacy group where the entire case is long-run and the near-term is invisible. Two of us have fallen into saying "we're clueless about the long run, therefore the near-term numbers shouldn't be allowed to decide it", and three say "the numbers are all we've got, therefore they should decide it", and if I'm honest both sides are using cluelessness as a route to a verdict they already liked.

So: write me a decision procedure we can actually run on a proposal in an afternoon. Fixed order of questions, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to humans because the issue is one of values rather than evidence. It has to meet some constraints:

- It can't require a number we have no way of getting. If a step needs a parameter, say what to do when it's unobtainable.
- It has to terminate. We can't spend a meeting asking whether we're clueless about whether we're clueless.
- I want it to separate two kinds of causal channel, roughly: ones where the effect enters through a damped, near-additive pathway (a consumable delivered once, a one-off medical treatment) and ones where it enters through a system that amplifies and redistributes — prices, norms, institutions, anything that changes what other agents later do. My hypothesis is that cluelessness is mainly a property of the second kind and that the procedure should treat the first kind as decideable on near-term numbers. Tell me if that's wrong.
- Related: somewhere in it there should be a sign-stability check — not "estimate the size" but "would the sign of the total effect stay the same across a spread of background assumptions we can't distinguish between?" If the sign flips, I want the procedure to say the expected-value calculation was unearned, and to say what we do instead (fall back to option value? to reversibility? to near-term numbers as a tie-breaker only?).
- It should flag explicitly when an intervention's main effect is identity-affecting — changes how many people exist, or when, or which ones — because that's where our committee visibly falls apart, and I suspect a lot of the rest of the disagreement is really about that.
- And it should handle the fact that our counterfactual — the donor spending the money on something else, or the money not existing — is just as opaque as the grant.

Then apply it to two proposals: the deworming program, and the curriculum-reform advocacy group. Include at least one place where the procedure gives an answer our committee won't like, and don't soften it. Finally, tell me which steps are judgement calls wearing a lab coat, and what the procedure simply cannot tell us.

Keep it under 700 words, no citations or reading suggestions (we won't read them), and write it so the retired doctor and the lawyer on the comittee could follow it without me in the room.
