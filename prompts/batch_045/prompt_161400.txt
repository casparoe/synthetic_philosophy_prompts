I am giving a thirty-minute talk plus questions at an invitation-only workshop on long-term cause prioritization next month, and I want to spend my remaining preparation on the question period rather than on the slides.

The thesis I am defending: suffering risks — the risk that the very long-run future contains astronomically large quantities of suffering rather of happiness, including suffering far beyond anything produced so far — belong in the same priority tier as extinction-risk mitigation, and they belong there for a reason that does not require any claim that suffering matters more, unit for unit, than happiness. The argument is an asymmetry, but not the familiar population-ethics asymmetry about bringing happy versus unhappy lives into existence. Call it the production asymmetry: for a broad class of mind designs, learning architectures and large-scale optimisation processes, intense suffering is the cheap, default, highly replicable output, while intense happiness is expensive, fragile and requires simultaneously satisfied conditions that are easily undone. Suffering arrives as a byproduct of error signalling, of competition over scarce resources, of incentive structures that use aversive states as control signals, and it scales with the number of minds and the length of time they run; flourishing requires architectural and institutional fine-tuning that can be lost far more easily than it was won. On this view, the accessible future — the set of futures we might realistically reach — is dominated in expectation by suffering-dense regions, and that is an architectural and empirical claim about which futures are likely, not a claim about the comparative moral weight of a unit of pain and a unit of pleasure.

The practical upshot I will defend is not negative-leaning utilitarianism and not anything that treats extinction as a solution. It is an agenda of three parts: research aimed at upper bounds on the intensity and duration of aversive states in constructed minds, so that extreme suffering becomes architecturally unavailable rather than merely disincentivised; standards, norms and treaty-like instruments that make it costly to build very large numbers of systems with unconstrained aversive states; and differential technological development, favouring technologies that reduce the chance of large-scale conflict and delay those that make cheap mass replication of suffering states easy. I will explicitly deny three things: that suffering has lexical priority, that human extinction would be a good outcome, and that the future is more likely bad than good.

The room: about twenty people. Six or seven are researchers at long-termist organisations who do quantitative prioritisation, trained in physics, computer science or economics; they have all built expected-value models of the future and will want orders of magnitude, and will want to know why my asymmetry is not already inside existing extinction-risk estimates. Two are philosophers with population-ethics backgrounds — one total utilitarian who will suspect the production asymmetry is a way of smuggling in a suffering-focused axiology without defending it, and one prioritarian who will grant the asymmetry and ask why it does not simply collapse into giving priority to the worst off. Two come from effective animal advocacy and think wild-animal suffering is the largest suffering risk that actually exists now, and that a future-oriented framing is a way of changing the subject. One is a visiting moral philosopher of broadly contractualist sympathies who thinks expected-value reasoning over unidentified future persons is a category error. One is a programme officer at a funder, who will ask what the marginal dollar buys this quarter. And the organiser, who is sympathetic but worried that a suffering-focused framing will split the community and draw bad press.

What I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defence of the view. Please give me:

- The eight or so objections I will actually get from this specific room, ordered by likelihood multiplied by damage rather than by philosophical depth, and attributed to which person or group raises them.
- Each objection phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine — the modellers should sound like modellers, the contractualist should sound like a contractualist, the animal-advocacy people should sound like people with a spreadsheet on wild-animal suffering.
- For each: the reply I should give in about two minutes standing up, what I should concede inside that reply, and one sentence on where the reply is soft.
- The two things I should concede up front, before anyone objects to them, and the one reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the session.
- The objection that is strongest in itself even if it is not the most likely to be raised, since I would rather spend my preparation there.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks — please diagnose what it is rather than have me name it — together with a sentence on what I say if it comes anyway.
- Any term of art in my own framing that I should drop or translate for this audience.

One constraint on the answers: assume I am standing at a whiteboard with no notes and no ability to look anything up. Every reply has to be a self-contained argument I can deliver from memory; no citations, no reading lists, no appeal to what some paper showed. Where a reply would normally lean on a source, give me the reasoning the source was carrying.
