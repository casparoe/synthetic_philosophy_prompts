hey — I'm in a formal epistemology seminar (mostly grad students, we've done Bayesian conditioning and a bit of game theory), and there's a line that keeps getting thrown around in section: rational agents can't agree to disagree. Last week my TA used it to shut down a discussion. We'd each written down our probability that a replication attempt of some psych result would succeed, we went around the room once, nobody moved much, and she said: if that disagreement was common knowledge, then either one of us was being irrational or the assumption that we all started from a common prior is just false.

That feels like it's doing way more work than one sentence can do. Can you seperate out the precise claims hiding in it — what exactly has to be shared by whom (a prior over hypotheses, or also a prior over which evidence you'd end up seeing?), what exactly has to be common knowledge (that I know your number, that I know you know mine, ...), and what rational is even doing there — and say which of those readings are trivial, which are substantive, and which are just false?

Two specific things I want to be able to raise in section. First: we only went around once and people talked over each other, so we probably had second- or third-order knowledge of each other's numbers, not the full infinite hierarchy. Does the result actually need common knowledge, or do finitely many levels already force the posteriors together, or at least put some bound on how far apart they can be? I've heard both and can't tell which is right. Second: someone said the no-trade thing we skimmed is the same point in different clothes — with a common prior nobody can expect to gain from a purely speculative bet, so people actually betting shows the priors differ. Same claim wearing a hat, or a different claim that just sounds similar?

Also: is there a third option besides irrational or no common prior? One person in class said her prior was over which theory is true while the guy next to her was assigning probabilities to what the data will look like, and it seemed to me there's just no common space for the theorem to bite on. Is that a real escape route or am I missing something?
