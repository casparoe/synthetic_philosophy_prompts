I am a novelist, and before I commit to a structure I would like an audit of the view of moral change my current book is built on. Please answer in British English.

The novel is set in a fictional Baltic trading republic in the 1830s. The practice whose collapse it traces is a form of pistol duelling over commercial insults, the satisfaction as the book calls it; the protagonist is a merchant in his fifties who has fought twice and acted as a second a dozen times. My commitments are these:

(1) The satisfaction is wrong, and wrong independently of what anyone in the book believes about it. I do not want the ending to imply that the practice merely became wrong.

(2) What ends practices of this kind is not the force of the better argument but a shift in what the relevant people find honourable. Honour, not morality, is the lever — roughly Appiah's picture.

(3) Nonetheless, the case against the satisfaction is genuinely sound, and I want a sound argument to be what turns my protagonist. I do not want to write a book in which reasons are only retrospective rationalisations of interest, as in the Williams-ish story.

(4) The turn should occur in a single scene between two people.

(5) The defenders of the practice, by contrast, are moved by material interest and dress it up as principle.

Which of these actually pull against one another, and in what order of severity? Which pairs merely look as though they conflict? For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what that would cost me dramatically, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
