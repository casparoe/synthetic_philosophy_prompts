For the past nine months I have been my mother's principal carer — she has vascular dementia and now needs help with almost everything — and in the evenings, when there is nothing left to do, I have started reading about moral worth. My question is a practical one, and I hope you will allow that it is also personal.

The difficulty is that I act as I do for a tangle of reasons at once: I love her; I should be ashamed in front of my brother if I did less; I feel guilty about the years I spent abroad; and I also think, quite steadily, that this is what a son owes. From the inside I cannot separate these or weigh them against one another.

The test I have arrived at, which I call the Subtraction Test, is this: strip away from an agent's motivation everything except the thought that the action is required of her — if the action would still be performed, it has moral worth, and if it would not, it does not. On my own case the verdict seems clear and unwelcome. Were the love, the guilt and the concern for my brother's good opinion subtracted, I honestly do not know that I would continue; and on the easy days, when I most want to be there, the part I can credit to duty is smallest — so it appears to follow that the more willingly I do it, the less it is worth. The familiar case of the tradesman who gives correct change only because honesty is good for custom seems to point the same way.

So: is the Subtraction Test the right way of making precise the thought that an action has moral worth only if it is done for the right reasons? I ask because I have begun to act on it. I have been taking on the tasks I find most repugnant on purpose, and trying to let the affection and the guilt drain out of what I do, on the assumption that whatever survives the subtraction is the only part that counts. Is that the correct practical upshot of the view? If the test is wrong, I should be grateful to know exactly where it fails — whether in treating worth as something that comes in degrees, in reading done because it is required as a causal claim about what actually moves me, or somewhere else — and what a better criterion would say about a case like mine, where the motives are thoroughly mixed and I could not tell you which of them I am acting on. And if it is wrong to be trying to engineer the purity of my own motives at all, I should like to know why.
