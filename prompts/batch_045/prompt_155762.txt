My dad had a stroke about six weeks ago and came out with something the neuro team called pain asymbolia — he'll say "yes, that's pain, it's sharp, it's in the left knee", give it 8 out of 10, and then just sit there happily, not shifting, and last week he actually laughed when the physio pressed on a bruise. It isn't only pain either: he sat in the sun till he was burnt without moving, and he'll hold on when he needs the loo. The care home have asked me to do ten minutes at Monday handover for the carers, who mostly know him as the man who never complains, and there's really only one thing I want them to get — that "he's in pain" and "he's bothered by it" have come apart, and that this is not bravery, or denial, or confusion.

Can you plan the ten minutes rather than just explain it to me? What has to be in place first for that distinction to even be thinkable to someone who's never met it; which single example should carry the whole thing (and should it be a pain example or one of the non-pain ones, and what would make an example useless here); the wrong readings they'll form in the order they'll form them, with where in the ten minutes to head each off; what to openly refuse to get into at this level; and one question at the end that shows me they've got it rather than just learnt two long words. Also — he still sometimes asks for painkillers, saying the pain is "bad for the knee", and I can't tell whether that's a contradiction I should definately deal with or a detail to leave out.
