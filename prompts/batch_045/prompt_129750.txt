I work on quantitative prioritization for a small funder that splits its giving between near-term global health and longer-horizon catastrophic- and extinction-risk work, and I have been asked to write an internal memo on how we should handle heavy-tailed payoffs, including cases where the expectation fails to converge. Before I commit anything to paper I would like someone to audit the position I have ended up with, because my colleagues keep telling me it is a grab bag and I genuinely cannot see which part is load-bearing. One request up front: we have essentially no journal access from here, so please argue the points out rather than pointing me at papers — I need the reasoning and the arithmetic, not references.

Here is where I have landed. (1) In ordinary finite cases I am a straightforward expected-utility maximizer, and I do not want to give that up, because every alternative I have looked at — weighting probabilities nonlinearly, ignoring probabilities below some threshold, sorting options into lexical tiers — seems either to cost me statewise dominance or to destroy the fine-grained discrimination we need when two grants differ in expected value by a factor of three. (2) Value is unbounded, and for the parts that matter to my job it is roughly linear in lives saved: ten-to-the-thirty lives saved is about ten-to-the-thirty times as good as one, and there is no ceiling past which further lives stop adding value. This is the whole reason the long-term half of the portfolio is worth taking seriously. (3) But I am not a fanatic. Take a physically clean lottery — one ticket drawn from a well-mixed urn of ten-to-the-twelve, with no funny business about where the probability estimate came from — that saves ten-to-the-thirty lives with probability ten-to-the-minus-twelve and does nothing otherwise, and compare it with saving ten-to-the-four lives for sure. I do not believe the lottery is the better option and I do not want a theory that tells me it is; I want to be able to keep funding the second kind of thing. A colleague says I can have both by putting a ceiling on value, and my worry is that any ceiling low enough to help with (3) also makes (2) idle, but I cannot tell whether that is right or whether I am just attached to (2). (4) On the St. Petersburg game itself I think the entry fee is small, somewhere in the tens of dollars, and I notice that I expect that verdict to be nearly the same for me as for someone a thousand times richer. My explanation has two parts: diminishing marginal utility of money, and the fact that any real counterparty has a ceiling on what it can pay out, so the schedule is truncated somewhere. I know the concavity part alone does not settle it, since you can re-denominate the payoffs in utils and the paradox returns; I am relying on the truncation part to do the real work. A friend adds that explaining a wealth-insensitive fee by appeal to concavity is incoherent, since a concave utility function is precisely a wealth-sensitive object, and I would like to know whether that objection lands. (5) I also think that when an expectation genuinely fails to exist, it is legitimate to stop doing expected-utility arithmetic and be guided by what happens in the overwhelming majority of cases; this sounds like it contradicts (1), but it does not seem to me to, since expected-utility maximization has no verdict where there is no expectation. (6) One theory, not two: I do not want one rule for my own money and another for lives saved, since almost every decision I actually make is a trade-off between them, and I do not want verdicts to shift when the same outcomes are re-described in different units.

(7) And here is the case I cannot get a stable verdict on, which I made up and call the Lifesburg game: the same fair-coin structure, but the payoff on the nth toss is two-to-the-n lives saved rather than two-to-the-n dollars. My judgment is that I should not liquidate everything I own to play it once — that strikes me as a plainly bad trade, and I am more confident of that verdict than of any argument I can construct for it. But when I try to derive it from my own framework I come apart: with linear value in lives the expectation is infinite, and if I truncate at the number of people who could ever exist — say ten-to-the-forty, the sort of figure that gets thrown around at work — the expectation is roughly 133 lives, which at any cost-per-life we would actually defend is still more money than I have.

So: which of these commitments are really in tension with which, and which are only apparently in tension? Please rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say what specifically would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me, given that I would rather revise one thing than three. In particular: can a single revision deal with both the Lifesburg verdict in (7) and the anti-fanaticism verdict in (3), or do they need different fixes, and is there any fix that handles both without gutting (2)? Tell me which items I have wrongly flagged — I suspect at least one of the (1)-versus-(5) worry and the wealth-insensitivity worry in (4) is a non-issue, and I would rather not revise more than I have to. And if the honest answer is that no coherent package delivers my (7) verdict, say so and tell me which of (1), (2) and (7) is the weakest link.
