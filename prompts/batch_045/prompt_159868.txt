I've been on our university's animal research ethics committee for a couple of years, and we are rewriting the rule that decides which animals trigger full welfare review rather than a rubber-stamp registration. The existing rule is roughly *vertebrates plus cephalopods*, which I suspect nobody can defend except by inertia, so I have been trying to get clear about what I actually believe by going case by case instead of by theory. A friend who keeps bees and a colleague who works on decapods have both been pushing on me, and I have found that my confident verdicts do not line up with any principle I can state. Here is where I have landed: a pig, obviously yes, as confident as I am about other people; an octopus, yes, and fairly confidently so even though most of its neurons sit in its arms rather than in a central brain; a zebrafish, yes to something — pain, I think — but probably not to anything like a persisting self; a honeybee, genuinely torn, maybe 60/40 toward yes; a crayfish, no, or at best 20/80 toward yes; a roundworm with a few hundred neurons, no; a human whose cortex has been entirely destroyed, no, which I take to show that I am not simply deferring to species membership. The crayfish and the bee are the pair I keep returning to: a colleague pointed out that the evidence for the two is the same *kind* of evidence — nociceptors, learning that reverses when the contingency changes, responses dampened by analgesics, willingness to trade a shock against a preferred shelter — and I have no account of why they come apart for me, and I don't want to just stipulate that they do. Three invented cases also pull on me. First, an animal whose nociceptors drive avoidance and whose responses are dampened by the same analgesics, but which never shows trade-offs and never gives up a learned avoidance once the shock is switched off: my gut says there is nothing it is like, or at least that it feels no pain. Second, an animal with no nociceptors at all, but which learns flexibly in many domains, reverses its learned preferences, and trades costs against benefits in a way that looks like it is weighing how things are going for it: my gut says yes, something matters to it — which would mean nociception is not required for sentience after all. Third, an animal with clearly integrated, recurrent, widely broadcast perceptual processing but nothing anywhere that looks like valence: I find myself wanting to say that there is something it is like but nothing is good or bad for it, and I am not sure that is coherent. Hibernating animals and animals under general anesthesia I treat as no, or as so close to no that the difference does not matter for policy.

The principle I keep trying out is something like: a creature is conscious to the degree that it has a single integrated system which represents how things are going for its own body, as better or worse, and uses that representation to arbitrate among competing motivations over time. I like this until I notice three things: it seems to hand me a yes for the crayfish as readily as for the bee, given the trade-off evidence; it gets hibernation and anesthesia right only if I can say what it is for such a system to be *offline* rather than *absent*, and I do not know how to say that inside the principle rather than outside it; and it is not obvious that it delivers anything for the nociceptor-free learner.

So: can that principle be stated precisely enough to yield verdicts and repaired, or do I need a different one, or a small set of candidates? I want each clause of whatever you propose tied to the case that motivates it, and I want to know honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you think I should give up. Please also say what it predicts for cases I have not listed — a brain organoid grown from human cells, a computer simulation of an insect nervous system run in enough detail to be functionally equivalent, and the colony as a whole rather than the individual bee, where I genuinely have no idea whether the hive is a candidate at all — and what it implies for how I should weight an uncertain case in committee: if I am 60 percent on bees, do ten thousand bees count as six thousand, and does your principle say anything about that or stay silent? I am not looking for a tour of the leading theories of consciousness or for anything to read; bring in a named theory only where it does the work of delivering a verdict. Please write in American English.
