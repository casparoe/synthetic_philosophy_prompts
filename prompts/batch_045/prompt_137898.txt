**Question 2. Some philosophers have argued that spectrum arguments show that "better than" is not a transitive relation. Discuss.**

**(a)** Set out the Simple Spectrum argument as a valid argument, stating its premises precisely. Be explicit about which relation the transitivity premise concerns, and about what the argument does and does not immediately establish. **[30 marks]**

**(b)** Consider the following case, which I shall call the Loop. There are three outcomes, P, Q and R, ranked along three independent dimensions of value — call them T, E and A. Each dimension yields its own transitive ordering of the three outcomes: by T, R is better than P and P better than Q; by E, P better than Q and Q better than R; by A, Q better than R and R better than P. Suppose that one outcome is better than another overall just in case it is better along at least two of the three dimensions; and suppose that every pairwise margin along every dimension is negligible — no larger than the increments the Simple Spectrum treats as too small to matter. Does the Loop create a difficulty that the Simple Spectrum does not, or is it only the same difficulty in a new costume? In answering, you should say what you take the difference to be, if any, between a betterness relation that is merely intransitive and one that contains a cycle, and what turns on that difference. **[40 marks]**

**(c)** The Essential Comparativist proposes that "better than" is not a two-place relation between outcomes but a three-place one: A is better than B only relative to a comparison set S to which both belong. Does this proposal resolve the difficulties raised in (a) and (b), or does it merely restate them? In answering, take care to say what the proposal does and does not commit its holder to. **[30 marks]**

**(a)** The Simple Spectrum can be set out as a valid argument with three premises. Let the spectrum be a series of outcomes X₁ … X₁₀₀₀, where X₁ is eighty years of very intense happiness and X₁₀₀₀ is eighty years of the mildest contentment, and where each outcome differs from its predecessor only in that the intensity of each moment's pleasure is very slightly lower — by a margin too small for any observer to notice. Then:

> (Tol) If B differs from A only by a negligible decrement in some respect of value, then B is at least as good as A.
> (Trans) "At least as good as" is transitive.
> (NT) X₁ is better than X₁₀₀₀.

(Tol) gives us, for every i, that Xᵢ₊₁ is at least as good as Xᵢ. Iterating (Trans) along the series gives us that X₁₀₀₀ is at least as good as X₁. But (NT) says X₁ is better than X₁₀₀₀ — that is, X₁ is at least as good as X₁₀₀₀ and X₁₀₀₀ is not at least as good as X₁. Contradiction. The three premises are jointly inconsistent, so one of them must go.

Two clarifications are worth making. First, the transitivity premise must be stated for the weak relation, "at least as good as", and not for "better than"; the argument does not go through if (Trans) is stated for the strict relation alone, since from "A is not better than B" and "B is not better than C" nothing at all follows about A and C. Second, the conclusion is therefore not immediately that "better than" is intransitive. What the argument strictly establishes is an inconsistency; a theorist who surrenders (Trans) must then do further work to show that the strict relation inherits the failure, and how much work that is depends on how the strict relation is defined in terms of the weak one. It is better to say that the argument forces a choice between Tolerance, Transitivity and Non-Triviality.

**(b)** The Loop is, I shall argue, the same difficulty in a new costume — a useful one, because it makes explicit how much is lost by abandoning (Trans). The structure is that of a majority vote over three orderings. Along T, R beats P beats Q. Along E, P beats Q beats R. Along A, Q beats R beats P. Taking the dimensions two at a time: P beats Q on T and E; Q beats R on E and A; R beats P on A and T. So the overall relation generated by the two-out-of-three rule is cyclic: P is better than Q, Q is better than R, and R is better than P. And nothing here trades on vagueness — all three orderings are perfectly sharp, and the imperceptibility of the margins is incidental.

Is this a new difficulty? Some writers distinguish transitivity from the weaker condition of acyclicity, and claim that a theorist may give up the first while keeping the second, and so escape the money pump. I do not think the distinction will bear the weight. To require that a relation be transitive just is to require that it contain no cycles: a cycle is simply the case in which A is better than B, B is better than C, and A fails to be better than C — which, given that the options are comparable, can only mean that C is better than A. Intransitivity is cyclicity. Once cyclicity is on the table the damage is done. An agent with such a relation will pay to trade P for Q, Q for R and R back for P, and will end where she began and poorer; and nothing in the menu {P, Q, R} is best, since each is bettered by another. The moral generalises back to (a): the cost of surrendering (Trans) is not a local failure of composition but the collapse of deliberation. If betterness does not compose, there is no guarantee that any option before an agent is choice-worthy, and practical reason has nothing to take hold of.

Nor can the Pluralist escape by blaming her aggregation rule. The Loop shows that she has only two options: a lexical ordering of the three dimensions, or the admission that overall betterness is not a genuine relation at all. The first is not credible here — it would make one dimension infinitely weighty, and nothing in the case supports that — so she is driven to the second, which is just the scepticism about value comparison that the Simple Spectrum already threatened.

**(c)** The Essential Comparativist's proposal is best understood as a version of that scepticism, and should be answered as such. If whether A is better than B can shift with which other options happen to be before us, then betterness is not a feature of A and B at all; it is a projection of our own attitudes on to the world. That cannot be right, for two reasons. First, it makes genuine disagreement impossible: if my comparison set differs from yours, then when I say A is better and you say B is, we are not contradicting one another. Second, it does not solve the Simple Spectrum, it dissolves it — the comparativist does not tell us which premise is false, she only declines to let the premises be combined, and declining to combine premises is not an answer to an argument. To be fair to the view, it does explain the pull of (Tol): in a comparison of neighbouring items taken narrowly, on their own, very little separates them, and that is why tolerance feels compulsory. But an explanation of the pull of a premise is not a defence of it. The proposal neither resolves the difficulty nor restates it; it abolishes the subject matter.
