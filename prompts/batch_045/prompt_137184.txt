I run a 90-minute session of a decision-theory reading group: about 18 undergraduates, one long table, no real money. They are comfortable with expected-value maximization and have already discussed Pascal's Wager as a historical curiosity, but they have not met the mugging case, and I would rather they run into it than hear it named.

Design a runnable activity — rules, materials, a timeline, and any roles you want — in which the students are made to feel the pressure of Pascal's mugging rather than of the Wager, and in particular the feature that separates the two: that the mugger sets the size of the stake after seeing how resistant you are, so that no credence threshold or stake-discount you fix in advance is safe from being outbid. The rough mechanic I have in mind: an opening round in which they allocate chips to an ordinary, well-evidenced high-impact ask; then the Mugger, whose demand stays at a single unit while the threatened or promised stake is multiplied by a large fixed factor each time the group declines; then, once they have settled into a pattern, a Counter-Mugger who pays them for refusing the first. But design it as you think best, and don't let the facilitator name any view up front.

The debrief should map each moment of the game onto one of the four ingredients doing the work — unbounded stakes, expected-value maximization, no floor on credence, and an adversarially chosen stake. It should also get participants to write down a decision rule before seeing the next stake, then apply it to a stake chosen in light of the rule, so the cost of each escape route — a utility ceiling, a probability floor, stakes-insensitivity below a threshold, or abandoning expected value — surfaces as a loss somewhere else in the game.

Finally, be honest about what the exercise cannot reproduce and that I will have to state myself: above all that, in the game, the stakes are stipulated rather than genuinely uncertain, so nothing the students do can show them why a prior should be structured so as to be exploitable in the first place, or why the mugger's claim deserves any credence at all. And tell me what will predictably go wrong: how do I keep the room from simply refusing to engage, from insisting that the probability is literally zero, or from deferring to whichever student does arithmetic fastest?
