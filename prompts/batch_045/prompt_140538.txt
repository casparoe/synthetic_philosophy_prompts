I am writing a paper on the debate about whether the world has become less violent, and I have ended up with an argument I do not trust, because it seems to work equally well for questions where I am confident there is a determinate answer. I would like a diagnosis of the argument, not a verdict on the topic.

Set-up. Call an *operationalization* any procedure that assigns to a historical period a quantity, or an ordering of periods, intended to represent how much violence there was. Say an operationalization is *admissible* if it is coherent, measures something we have independent reason to care about, does not double-count the same harm, and is not gerrymandered to a conclusion.

(1) "Violence" is a contested concept with no sharp boundary. It covers at least intentionally applied physical force, credible threatened force, coercion backed by institutions, and slow harms that disable or kill without anyone striking a blow. For any of these you may count events, victims, episodes of victimization, person-years of suffering, or life-years lost; and you may normalize by population, by person-years lived, by number of social interactions, or not at all.

(2) So no operationalization of violence is uniquely privileged: for any period there is more than one admissible operationalization.

(3) And the admissible ones disagree about direction, not merely about magnitude. On the figures usually cited in this literature (I do not need them to be exact, only to be roughly as advertised), interpersonal homicide rates per capita in Europe fell by something like an order of magnitude or more between the late medieval period and the twentieth century, while the absolute number of deaths deliberately inflicted by organized states in the twentieth century is larger than in any earlier period; and because people now live roughly twice as long, switching the denominator from population to person-years lived shifts every ratio again, in a way that has nothing to do with how dangerous anyone's life was.

(4) A claim about whether the world is becoming less violent is a claim about how one period compares with another with respect to violence, and there is nothing to such a comparison beyond what some operationalization delivers.

(5) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent. There are only facts of the form: by operationalization O, period A was more violent than period B. Realism about the trend is false; what we actually have is relativity to an operationalization.

(6) But run (1)–(5) on anything else. "Is the world getting warmer?" — surface air temperature, sea-surface temperature, satellite readings of the lower troposphere, and ocean heat content are all admissible, all measure something we care about, and they disagree in detail; no unique admissible operationalization. "Did crime fall?" — crimes recorded by police, victimization surveys, and hospital admissions for assault come apart in well-documented ways. "Is the population of X growing?" — residents, citizens, and persons present on census night come apart. And, embarrassingly, "has Bob become less violent?" — punches thrown, injuries caused, people credibly threatened can move in opposite directions if Bob has grown weaker. Either all of these lack a fact of the matter too, in which case my argument has proved much more than I want, since I think the warming claim and the Bob claim have determinate answers; or one of (1)–(4) is doing work only in the violence case, and I cannot see which.

There is a second wrinkle I cannot place. Nobody alive in 1300 is alive now, so a per-capita comparison across the two periods does not describe anyone's risk changing. The reading of "the world has become less violent" that I actually care about is the biographical one: a life like mine would have been more dangerous then. But that is a counterfactual claim about persons, and it neither follows from nor is refuted by the rate comparisons in (3). I cannot tell whether this wrinkle is the reason (4) is false, or an independent confusion I have smuggled in on top of a separate flaw.

What I want from you:

- Name the step where the argument breaks, if it breaks. If the failure is a quantifier shift, an equivocation in "admissible," or a modal slide between rates and counterfactual biographies, say exactly where it happens and why the step looked fine.
- If instead the argument is valid and the question is which premise to abandon, tell me which is cheapest to give up and what giving it up commits me to. I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (5), since the contestedness of "violence" and the direction-disagreement between admissible measures seem to me to be the durable results of the debate; but if keeping them forces me into a conventionalism that also swallows the temperature and Bob cases, I would rather give up one of them and I want to know which.
- If I give up (4), what is left of the claim that the world is becoming less violent? What would a realist about the trend have to say in response to (3) in order to earn the right to say it?
- Price the alternatives. If I retreat from (5) to the weaker claim that our confidence in any trend verdict should be discounted in proportion to the spread of admissible operationalizations, what exactly have I given up, and is the weaker claim still enough to do the critical work against the more confident versions of the decline thesis?

One constraint on the answer, and one suggestion I have already tried and rejected. My supervisor proposed saving realism with a constraint she calls *carving-invariance*: an operationalization is admissible only if it returns approximately the same trend whether we carve a stretch of history into wars, into battles, or into individual deaths. Every operationalization I know of fails this, so the constraint seems to make the question unanswerable rather than answerable. If you think the constraint has the right shape but the wrong strength, tell me what a defensible weaker version looks like and whether it would rescue (5) or refute it.
