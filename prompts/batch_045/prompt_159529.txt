I am a PhD student in normative ethics writing a dissertation chapter on the structure of deontological constraints, and my working group has split into two camps that have spent a term arguing past each other about what is usually called the paradox of deontology. Let me state the puzzle as the group states it, so nothing hangs on shared background: a view that treats certain acts — killing one innocent person in order to harvest what would save five others, say — as things an agent must not do, and treats the occurrence of such acts as morally grave, nevertheless appears to leave each agent free to refrain from performing one violation in order to prevent five identical violations, and to leave each of a chain of such agents free in the same way, with the result that the total number of violations is far greater than it had to be. Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Camp A — the agent-relative deontologist. Constraints and options are irreducibly agent-relative; the permission not to minimize violations is not a defect to be explained away but the expected expression of taking the separateness of persons seriously, and the fact that everyone can exercise it at once shows only that morality is not a production problem. Give Camp A real resources: an account of why the permission is coherent rather than contradictory, and of why the collective outcome is not a cost the theory must answer for in the same currency as the violation.

Camp B — the collectivist or minimizing deontologist. Constraints are genuine and are not consequentialist, but the prohibition is best understood as attaching in the first instance to the group of agents who could jointly prevent the outcome; a moral view that licenses every member of that group to refrain from minimizing, while insisting that violations are grave, has lost its hold on the very thing that made constraints plausible. Give Camp B an account of how the group-level requirement and the individual-level permission are supposed to be related, and of what the individual-level permission is then doing.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — claims both sides can sign, including about the formalism of constraints, about what counts as a violation, about what needs explaining, and about which of the group's intuitions are data rather than verdicts.
2. A disagreements table: the disputed claim, Camp A's own formulation of its position, Camp B's own formulation of its position, and an honest classification of each disagreement as genuine or merely verbal. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why — for instance, a disagreement about may versus must versus is blameworthy, or about whether the unit of assessment is the individual agent, the group, or the act-token.
3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each crux, give (a) the specific case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, (b) Camp A's prediction about how it will go, (c) Camp B's prediction, and (d) what each camp would count as argument or evidence against its own view.

Please build the cruxes around the following cases rather than around the standard trolley variations, since those are exactly where we have stopped making progress:

- The Clinician and the Board. A clinician may not perform the violating act that would prevent five identical violations. A separate oversight board, of which she is not a member, is the only body able to authorize it. May she request authorization? May she pass along the information on which authorization depends? Now suppose she is instead one of five voting members, no vote sufficient alone, and she knows hers will be pivotal. May she vote to authorize?
- The Transmitter. An agent cannot herself perform the violating act. She controls a relay: if she leaves it alone, a message reaches a distant agent who will perform the act and thereby prevent five violations; if she intervenes, the message is blocked and the five occur. Does the permission not to minimize extend to a permission to refrain from facilitating, or even to a permission to block, the only available minimizing act? Does it matter that the distant agent acts wrongly if he acts?
- The Relay of Refusals. Ten agents face the choice in sequence, each able to perform the violation once and thereby prevent five violations, each knowing that all previous agents refused. Does the tenth agent's position differ from the first's? Are either camp's verdicts sensitive to the length of the sequence or to the presence of common knowledge?
- One further crux of your own choosing, regarding either aggregation (does anything change when the number prevented is five hundred rather than five, or when it is uncertain and known only in expectation?) or the shape of the constraint (is it a prohibition on a kind of act, on an intention, or on a causal relation?).

Do not adjudicate between the camps and do not end by splitting the difference: I want a text both could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact. No citations, footnotes, or bibliography — consider it a circulated memo of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
