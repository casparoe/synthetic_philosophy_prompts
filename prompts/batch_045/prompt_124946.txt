I am second-marking a batch of scripts from a second-year metaphysics exam and would like an independent read on one of them. The question and the script are below. I will say nothing about my own view of the answer, since I don't want to anchor you.

The question (25 marks):

The Ship of Theseus has one hundred planks. Each day for a hundred days one plank is removed and replaced with a new one, and the removed planks are kept in a warehouse. Call the vessel still afloat at the end Continuity, and the vessel built out of the stored planks Reassembly.

(a) Set out — do not merely name — how each of the following three treatments answers the question "which, if either, is Theseus's ship?": (i) four-dimensionalism (perdurantism); (ii) the constitution view; (iii) the reductionist view that identity is not what matters in survival. For each, say what in the case that treatment takes to be decisive.

(b) Two variants. Variant 1: at noon on day fifty the half-repaired vessel is completely dismantled, its planks lie stacked in the yard for a week, and it is then reassembled exactly as it was and the repairs resume. Variant 2: instead of being warehoused, each removed plank goes immediately into a frame in the shipyard, in the reverse of the order in which the planks were removed, so that the growing structure is not ship-shaped or seaworthy until the hundredth plank is added. For each variant, say which of the three treatments in (a) is sensitive to it and why — and whether either variant raises a question that the original case did not already raise.

The script:

(a) The case sets two candidates with rival credentials against each other. Continuity has functional and spatio-temporal continuity with the original: it never left the water, it never lost more than one plank at a time, and at every stage it differs from the vessel of the previous day only slightly. Reassembly has material continuity: it is composed of all and only the original planks. Any account that ties identity to continuity of form, function or history favours Continuity; any account that ties it to matter favours Reassembly. Because the case prises these apart, it shows that no single criterion can do all the work.

(i) The four-dimensionalist's response is that persisting objects are not three-dimensional things present at many times but sequences of momentary stages: what exists at a time is a momentary ship-stage, and the ship at one time is related to the ship at another not by identity but by a temporal counterpart relation. So the day-zero ship is not strictly identical with Continuity or with Reassembly; each merely bears the counterpart relation to it. The question "which is really Theseus's ship?" therefore has no answer beyond which counterpart relation we prefer to call identity, and the puzzle is dissolved rather than solved.

(ii) The constitution view agrees that a persisting thing is not a bare particular that survives the loss of its matter. On this view a thing is identical with whatever constitutes it, provided that stuff is arranged in the way the thing's kind requires: the ship is the planks arranged shipwise, and nothing over and above them. Since Reassembly restores the original matter to ship-shape, Reassembly is Theseus's ship; Continuity, which by day one hundred contains none of the original matter, is a numerically distinct though functionally indiscernible vessel.

(iii) The reductionist adds that settling the identity question would not settle anything that matters. What matters in survival is continuity of structure — here, of function and seaworthiness — and that admits of degree. Where what matters admits of degree, the further question "but is it really the same ship?" can be empty: a question about how we choose to speak, not about how the world is.

(b) Neither variant changes anything. Variant 1 is a limit case of gradual replacement: a vessel that can survive the loss of one plank can surely survive being taken apart and put back together, so the week in the yard makes no difference to any of the three treatments. Variant 2 is likewise idle: reassembling the planks in reverse order only alters the order in which Reassembly acquires its parts, and the original case already permits us to ask whether Reassembly is the ship at all. Both variants leave the three treatments exactly where they were.
