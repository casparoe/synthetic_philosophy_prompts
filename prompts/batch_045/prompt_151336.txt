I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a difficulty that is, I think, a philosophical one rather than a craft one.

The book is set largely in Cambridge. Its central figure, Professor Miriam Kesteven, is a retired moral philosopher of a severe and unfashionable sort: a convinced Kantian, a woman who has built a life on the claim that rational agency is what gives a creature moral standing. In the scene I am stuck on, Kesteven is giving evidence to a public hearing on a primate research unit she has been asked to help license. A former pupil of hers, now acting for an animal-protection organisation, puts the following to her and asks her to say which step she rejects.

(1) A creature can be wronged — can be the object of a direct duty — only if it is capable of acting from a conception of a universal law, that is, only if it is a rational agent. Animals are not rational agents, and so nothing we owe concerning them is owed to them.

(2) We nevertheless have duties with regard to animals, and these are duties to ourselves: above all a duty not to deaden the natural feeling of compassion, since that feeling is a help to us in discharging our direct duties of beneficence to human beings. (The thought is Kant's own: the man who is cruel to his dog becomes hard in his dealings with men.)

(3) So the wrong-making feature of any act of cruelty to an animal is exhausted by its tendency to damage — in the agent, or in those who witness it — the disposition to come to the aid of human beings.

(4) If the wrong in an act consists entirely in a tendency to bring about some bad effect E, then where E is absent the act is not wrong, where E is slight the wrong is slight, and where E is outweighed by good effects produced elsewhere the act is on balance to be done.

(5) Whether cruelty damages the disposition is a contingent matter, varying with agent and circumstance: some agents have little or no compassion to lose; some acts are witnessed by no one; some agents are unmoved by what they do; and any damage can in principle be offset by greater beneficence somewhere else.

(6) Hence a man alone on an island who tortures his dog for amusement, and whose dealings with human beings are thereafter unchanged, does nothing wrong; and a research unit may inflict as much suffering as it chooses provided it also funds human charity of greater expected benefit — the suffering becomes an entry in a ledger.

(7) That cannot be right. To torture a dog for amusement is wrong, and it is not less wrong for going unseen, or for being compensated afterwards.

(8) Therefore (1) is false: animals can be wronged, and we owe them direct duties.

I need to know where this goes wrong, if it does. My own suspicion falls on (3) or (4), but I cannot say why, and two philosopher friends disagree: one holds that (1) is perfectly safe and the argument collapses earlier, the other that (7) is doing all the work and that Kesteven should simply refuse it. I should be grateful for the following.

First, name the step that fails, or the pair of steps between which something has shifted, and explain why it looked innocent — why a clever woman who has taught this material for thirty years might not have noticed. If the trouble is an equivocation, tell me which word or phrase carries both readings and what they are.

Second, price the alternatives. I would like Kesteven to keep (1) if honestly she can: it is load-bearing for her character, and for the last third of the book. Tell me what keeping it and rejecting something else commits her to — in particular, whether it leaves her able to say anything determinate about what may and may not be done to animals, and whether it allows her to go on holding that animals may be eaten and experimented upon at all, or whether rejecting (3) quietly forces her all the way to (8) by another route. And tell me what abandoning (1) would cost her inside the Kantian framework she lives in, not merely in intuition.

Third, and this matters for chapter eleven: Kesteven is later asked to advise on the treatment of a patient in a permanent vegetative state and on a newborn with profound cognitive impairment. Whichever way she answers at the hearing, I need to know what she is obliged to say about human beings who fail the rationality test, and whether the position she takes about the dog is consistent with it. If it is not, I would rather learn that now and rewrite, than let her be refuted by a quibble under cross-examination.

Finally, and this is the novelist's part of the request: if you can, give me, after the diagnosis, the substance of what Kesteven might actually say at the hearing in a hundred and fifty words or so of spoken reply — something that will sound like a philosopher answering, not like a barrister evading — and tell me what that line of reply commits her to later in the book.

I have no use for citations, references or suggestions for further reading; the novel's apparatus is my own problem. I need the shape of the reasoning, and what each available answer costs the woman who gives it.
