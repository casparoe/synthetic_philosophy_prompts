I am a high school senior writing a long research paper for an elective on the history of economic ideas, and I have got stuck on a case I would like help analysing rather than summarising.

The case is Project Cybersyn, the cybernetic coordination system built for Salvador Allende's government in Chile between 1971 and 1973. The facts I am working from are these, so that you do not have to reconstruct them: the government had taken control of roughly 150 enterprises and needed some way to run them; the system's architect was the British management cybernetician Stafford Beer; the only component used regularly in practice was Cybernet, a network of telex machines linking factories to a single mainframe in Santiago, over which plants sent daily indices of things like raw-material input, output and absenteeism; those indices fed Cyberstride, a statistical filtering program that predicted short-run performance and raised an alarm when indicators drifted outside expected ranges, escalating to a higher level if the lower level did not fix the problem within a set interval; a separate economic simulator, CHECO, was meant for forecasting the effect of policy decisions; and a hexagonal control room was built for managers to see all of this and send directives back down the telex network. Building each factory's model was slow work that depended on operations researchers extracting the operators' own knowledge of their machines, and by the end only about twenty factories had been modelled and connected. The control room was finished but, on the account I have read, never actually used for its intended purpose, and the project ended with the coup of September 1973.

The lens I want it analysed through is the socialist calculation debate: Mises's original argument that without private ownership of the means of production there is nothing for factor prices to be prices of, and so no way to compare alternative uses of higher-order goods; Lange's reply that a central board can issue parametric prices, instruct managers to minimise cost and expand output until price equals marginal cost, and adjust those prices by trial and error in response to surpluses and shortages, thereby simulating the market without markets (and, as I understand it, his later suggestion that fast enough computers could solve for the equilibrium prices directly rather than groping toward them); and Hayek's reply to that reply, that the difficulty is not computation but knowledge, since much of the relevant information is local, tacit, changing and never concentrated anywhere for anyone to collect.

Please do not give me the standard summary of the debate or a narrative history of Chile. I want the disciplined mapping: who counts as the planner, what counts as an alternative use of a resource, what functions here as the error signal that tells the system it has misallocated something, and what the equivalent of a price is in a system that does not have one.

I also want you to use a distinction I have been trying out in my own notes, and to tell me where it breaks. I have been separating three layers: an arithmetic layer, whether the allocation can be computed at all; a discovery layer, whether the system can come to know something no one told it, such as that a given input has become scarce or that a new technique is available; and a legibility layer, whether factory-floor reality can be got into the system in the first place, and what is lost or distorted in translating it into transmissible indices. My question is which of these layers Cybersyn ever actually reached, which layer each of the three theorists is really objecting about, and whether the episode tests the layer the debate was actually fought over.

Two further things I cannot settle on my own. First, the wrinkle that keeps bothering me: Cybersyn did not use prices at all. Its feedback ran on physical indicators and alarm thresholds. So it is not really an implementation of Lange's model, whose whole device is the parametric price. Does that make the case evidence for Mises and simply irrelevant to Lange, or does it show that prices were only ever one possible feedback signal and that Lange's mechanism was inessential to his conclusion? I want the stronger reading argued, and the other one steelmanned, not both split down the middle.

Second, the evidential question, which I think may sink my whole paper. The project was unfinished, covered about twenty factories, operated inside an economy that still had markets, private firms and foreign trade, and ran alongside US-backed destabilisation, a strike by truck owners, runaway inflation and then a coup. So what, if anything, does Cybersyn show? If it had been finished and had worked, would that have refuted Mises and Hayek, or would they have been free to say the scenario was never instantiated? Is the disagreement empirical in the way my paper needs it to be, or is part of it a disagreement about what rational allocation means? Please also say where the debate is simply silent about this case: about who sets the goals the system optimises toward, about Beer's aim of devolving decisions to the workforce and whether spreading decision rights dissolves the knowledge problem without markets, and about why planning inside one large firm is apparently unproblematic while planning across a nationalised sector is supposed to be impossible.

Tell me plainly where the theory cannot be made to speak about the case, rather than forcing it.
