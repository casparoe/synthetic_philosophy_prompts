I spent a career in experimental physical chemistry and have spent the last two years of retirement reading metaethics, where I keep encountering a prohibition that looks to me very like a manoeuvre my own field performs routinely, and to apparently opposite effect. I would like a careful comparison of two things that I suspect are being run together, and then a verdict on the manoeuvre.

The first is the derivational thesis: no conclusion containing an 'ought' follows from premises none of which contain one; any valid argument for a normative conclusion must contain a normative premise, either smuggled in or honestly stated. The second is Moore's target: 'good' is not synonymous with any natural expression, goodness is not identical with any natural property, and for any natural candidate it remains an open question whether that candidate is good. Please say exactly what each of these denies and what each permits, whether either entails the other in either direction, and whether they are one thesis in two idioms or two theses that happen to be held by the same people. Do not assume the second is merely a rhetorical version of the first; I want to know whether either could fail while the other held.

Now the manoeuvre, which I will describe in my own terms so that nothing hangs on my having read the right author. In the philosophy of science I was trained on, one moves from a purely descriptive base to statements couched in a theoretical vocabulary by adding what I will call a bridge identification: temperature is, up to a constant and in the ideal-gas case, mean molecular kinetic energy, and that identity is not analytic, not conceptually necessary, and not derivable from the descriptive base alone. It is a contingent, a posteriori identity, and the term is fixed by the role it plays in the theory; once the identity is in hand one cheerfully derives all manner of claims about temperature from claims about molecules. Call this the bridge-identification strategy. What happens when it is run on 'good' or 'ought'? As I understand it, it concedes everything to Hume, since it never claims a derivation from purely descriptive premises and simply adds the identity as a further premise; and it claims to be untouched by the open-question argument, since it asserts no synonymy and no analytic connection, only an a posteriori identity of the water/H₂O sort, so that the openness of the question whether maximizing well-being is good should be no more damaging than the openness of the question whether water is H₂O. Is that right? If not, where exactly does the analogy give?

The wrinkle I cannot get past concerns the uniqueness of the filler. In the scientific cases the world appears to supply exactly one candidate that occupies the role, and that is what lets the identity be necessary even though a posteriori. Suppose instead that the normative role admits of two quite different fillers, and that two communities could agree on every platitude about what reasons and goodness do while their terms latched onto different properties. Is that a real possibility for the normative case in a way that it is not for temperature, and if it is, does it sink the strategy, or does it merely show that normative terms are less rigid than natural-kind terms without touching the main claim?

Finally, three things I want stated plainly rather than gestured at. First: what sort of evidence could ever confirm a bridge identification of this kind, given that we have no instrument that detects goodness, and is the holistic answer available in science — the identity earns its keep by the explanatory and predictive work it does — genuinely available here, or does it smuggle the normative back in? Second: if the strategy works, has the is-ought gap collapsed into a bookkeeping point about argument validity, or does it reappear one level up, as a question about whether the bridge premise itself can ever be assertible? Third: where is the analogy to the theory-observation gap silent, and does my instinct that the gap has been overrated survive the comparison?
