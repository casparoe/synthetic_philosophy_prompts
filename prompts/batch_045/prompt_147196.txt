I have worked as a mastering engineer for a little over twenty years. I left school at eighteen and have never studied philosophy formally; what I know of it comes from reading in the evenings, mostly secondhand and out of order. So I may be describing something that has been picked over for a hundred years under a name I have never met. What I would like is the name — but more than the name, because a bare label wouldn't tell me whether I have noticed one thing or three.

Here is the pattern. People ask me constantly whether something sounds good, and I find I cannot give them the answer. I can tell them that the low end is muddy, that the vocal is three decibels louder than it was yesterday, that the file is at the wrong sample rate, that the room is too reverberant for speech, that the guitar is out of tune. All of that transfers without residue: they write it down, they know it, they act on it, and if they never hear the record themselves nothing seems to be missing from what they were given. Several of those are things you would think need a trained ear, and yet taking my word for them is entirely unproblematic. But 'it sounds good', 'the performance is extraordinary', 'that record is one of the great ones' — I cannot hand these over. What I can do instead is get an assistant to hear it, sometimes over months, by playing them the same pieces and talking while they listen. That is not transmission of my judgment; it is getting them to form one.

Four features of this strike me as worth separating, and I don't know which of them are the load-bearing ones.

First, the failure is not a failure of reliability. There are two people whose verdicts on records I trust completely, and I trust them because over years I have compared their verdicts with my own on the same pieces. If one of them tells me about a record I will never be able to hear that it is transcendent, I believe her; I will repeat it to others; I'll seek out the record. And still the thing she gives me feels thinner than the thing she has, and it collapses entirely if I learn that she never actually heard the record and inferred her verdict from a description of it — even in a case where that inference was, as it happens, just as likely to be right. That last reaction seems to me irrational on its face, and I want to know whether it is.

Second, the calibration seems to run backwards. The only way I have of deciding whose verdicts to trust is by having already heard, myself, the very records we are comparing notes on. So this route to a verdict never puts me ahead of my own listening; it only ever extends it sideways. And it fails precisely where I'd most want help: with a record that sounds like nothing else I know, there is no overlap to calibrate on, and so I have no idea whether to believe the person whose taste I otherwise trust.

Third, it isn't simply about verdicts in a language I can't read. I am perfectly content to defer to someone on whether a translation is faithful to a poem in a language I don't speak, and fairly content to defer on whether that poem is a masterpiece — deferring, that is, about the value of an experience I cannot have. That looks like it cuts against the story I would otherwise tell myself.

Fourth, the verdict changes what I later hear. After she tells me the record is transcendent I put it on again and it sounds different to me. So the difference between us isn't just that I lack an experience she has; whatever I was missing may be partly responsible for what the experience turns out to be.

So: does this configuration have an established concept attached to it? I don't mean the general observation that some things you have to experience for yourself, which I gather is old and I could have arrived at alone, and I don't mean the well-worn claim that beauty is in the eye of the beholder. I mean the specific structure: a true verdict, reliably produced and reliably transmitted, that the recipient seems not to get full credit for holding; the way the calibration of trust presupposes the first-hand experience the verdict was supposed to replace; the novelty case, where there is nothing to calibrate on; and the boundary with ear-requiring judgments like 'out of tune' or 'too reverberant' where deference is completely in order.

If the nearest established concepts fit only imperfectly, tell me what they are and how what I have described differs from each, and which fits best; if the novelty problem or the calibration problem is in fact a separate phenomenon from the rest, say so and say where you would cut. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give me a pair of cases either side of the boundary — one that is clearly the thing and one that only looks like it. Please don't spend the answer on a reading list or on tracking down who said what: if a position matters, describe it in a sentence and I will go and find out who holds it.

One more thing. I would like you to say how confident you are in the identification you offer — not a number, just honestly stated — and to single out the one claim in your answer you would most expect a specialist to dispute, and why.
