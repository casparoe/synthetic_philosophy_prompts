A friend and I have been arguing about Gödel's 1949 argument against the objective passage of time, and I want to push both of us into numbers.

Here is the argument as I reconstruct it. Gödel exhibits an exact solution of the field equations — a homogeneous, rotating, singularity-free universe of pressureless dust with a negative cosmological constant tuned to the dust density — in which light cones tip far enough that some future-directed paths return to their own starting point. And because the spacetime is homogeneous, that pathology is not confined to a region: it is available at every event. So there is no global time function, no way of stacking the whole universe into a shared sequence of "nows" that an objective lapse could march through. Gödel's conclusion, as I read it, is that if such a world is physically possible at all, then the lapse of time is not an objective feature of the world but something contributed by our mode of perception. He does not claim our world is like this; the bare possibility is meant to carry the conclusion.

Give me your credence that the argument succeeds — that the physical possibility of a Gödel-type world refutes, or at least decisively undermines, objective temporal becoming — and give me the decomposition rather than a bare number. At minimum: (1) the probability that such a world is a genuine physical possibility and not merely a mathematical one, and how much of that turns on the tuned negative cosmological constant, on our own universe's showing no sign of global rotation, and on the suspicion (Hawking's chronology protection conjecture) that quantum effects destroy closed timelike curves before they can form; (2) the probability, conditional on its being possible, that the absence of a global "now" tells against objective becoming itself rather than only against an over-demanding, frame-independent conception of it that no A-theorist ever needed to accept — i.e., how much of the argument's force comes from a premise its opponents already reject; (3) whether the modal step is sound, or whether the conclusion quietly needs the actual world, and not just some possible one, to be Gödelian; (4) how much weight the everywhere feature bears — my friend thinks a universe in which the curves are confined to one region, a wormhole-type time machine, would leave becoming untouched, and that this is what makes Gödel's version special, whereas I think localized curves would be just as damaging if the argument works at all — and whether the escape hatch of a second temporal dimension, in which the block grows, is a serious reply or a desperation move.

Give a number and one line of justification for each term, then the combination, and tell me which single term your final figure is most sensitive to. My credence is about 0.45 and my friend's about 0.15; if those roughly track yours, tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in. Finally, name the one piece of evidence — an observation, a theorem, an experiment — that would move your number most, and in which direction.
