Define preferences as identifiable in the relevant sense iff: for a randomly drawn member of a reference class of ordinary adult humans doing familiar tasks (kitchens, offices, ordinary roads — no adversarially selected cases), R-hat's induced ranking over the options available in a new environment E-prime, whose dynamics differ from E's and in which some features held fixed during training are now free to vary, agrees with R's ranking over those options more often than a baseline that samples uniformly at random from the set of reward functions behaviorally indistinguishable from R-hat in E — and does so by a margin wide enough that a deployment engineer would actually bet on it. I mean this as a question about the limit of what could in principle be extracted from behavior, not about today's algorithms; so count as available data the trajectories themselves plus response times, hesitations, eye movements, error-corrections, and the demonstrator's own verbal self-report treated as one more stream of behavior rather than as privileged access.

The reason I think the random-from-the-equivalence-class baseline is the right one to beat: take the standard policy-invariance result. If you add to a reward function a term of the form gamma-times-Phi-of-s-prime minus Phi-of-s, where Phi is any function of state and gamma the discount factor, the set of optimal policies in that environment is unchanged; and, up to positive rescaling, transformations of that shape are the only ones with that property. My claim is that any IRL method whatsoever returns at best an equivalence class, and that which member of the class you get is fixed by the prior or regularizer, i.e. by something other than the data.
