A popular philosophy-of-mind book I have just finished — I spent most of last night arguing about it with a friend — summarises the debate about what emotions are roughly as follows:

"For about thirty years the orthodoxy was the cognitive theory: an emotion just is a judgement, so to fear the dog is to judge it dangerous and to be angry is to judge oneself wronged. That was thought to explain why emotions can be rational or irrational, why we can argue someone out of one, and why we can be criticised for them. The theory was finished off by two things. First, recalcitrant emotion: people who know perfectly well that flying is safe, or that the spider is in a tank, are afraid anyway. If the fear were a judgement they would be contradicting themselves, and nobody thinks that, so emotion and judgement must be separate. Second, the neuroscience: researchers traced a fast pathway to the amygdala that bypasses the cortex altogether, so fear responses can be produced before any evaluation of the situation has occurred — which is just what James argued a century earlier. The view most philosophers hold now is therefore a descendant of James's: an emotion is the perception of a bodily change, so fear simply is the feeling of your heart racing and nothing else. That also disposes of the old puzzle about whether animals have emotions; since they have bodies, of course they do."

I am inclined to think the recalcitrant-emotion point does real work, because my own fear at a glass balcony persists when I know the railing is solid — but my friend insists the passage misdescribes what the cognitive side was claiming, and she produced a case the book never discusses: being told over the telephone that a close friend has died, where the grief seems to arrive with the thought while the bodily upset follows hours later. She called it reverse recalcitrance.

Please go through the passage claim by claim. Which parts would a defender of the cognitive view accept as fair, which are mischaracterisations — in particular the slide from cognitive theory generally to the specific claim that emotions are judgements, and the presentation of recalcitrance as a refutation rather than as an objection with answers — and which are just wrong? Does the neuroscience, as described, settle anything conceptual about what emotions are, or does it at most settle a question about timing and causation? Is the either/or the passage ends up with — judgement versus bodily feeling — even exhaustive, and where would a view that treats emotions as perceptions of value sit relative to it? Is my friend's case a genuine problem for the bodily-feeling view, or does that view have a reply? And does the conclusion about animal emotion follow from anything the passage gets right?
