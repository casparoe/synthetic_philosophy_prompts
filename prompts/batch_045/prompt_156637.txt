I'm writing a novel about a country that has just come out of six years under an Emergency Council, and it has a Supreme Chamber that has already handed down five rulings in earlier chapters. A lawyer friend read the draft and said my bench keeps flip-flopping between Hart and Fuller without noticing, which I think was meant as a compliment, but wasn't. Chapter 19 is the big one and I need the ruling to sound like it came from the same bench that decided chapters 4, 7, 11 and 14 — so I'd rather figure out the rule I have accidentally written than just patch the prose.

The verdicts on the record so far:

- In re Marrin: Marrin denounced her employer for a private remark, an offence created by Emergency Decree 3. The decree was published, prospective, possible to obey and perfectly clear, and it was nasty. Chamber holds the decree was law, the conviction stands, the sentence is cut to time served, and the opinion says something like "the wickedness of a rule is not the same thing as its invalidity, and we do not cure the first by pretending at the second."
- Tessaro v. Grain Directorate: Tessaro is fined under a regulation that was properly made but never printed in the Bulletin because the presses were bombed; it circulated inside the ministry. Chamber refuses to apply it — a rule the subject cannot find is not a rule she can follow.
- In re Old Mill Cooperative: a statute required filing within 48 hours at a district office that had been shut for eleven months. Chamber refuses to apply it — a standard nobody can meet is not a standard.
- Dolman v. Revenue: an ordinary statute reopening tax assessments already closed, applied backwards. Chamber strikes the retroactive application, in very strong language about the settled expectations of people who ordered their affairs under the law as it stood.
- In re the Vell Directives: in the Council's last chaotic stretch, officials ran the railways on telephone instructions that changed weekly and were never written down. Chamber holds these were never law at all, and gives no protection to anyone who followed them.

Now chapter 19, Prosecutor v. Verrine. Verrine was a rail logistics officer. In month 41 of the Council he signed routing orders sending a train of about nine hundred people to a camp, which Emergency Decree 14 required him to do — Decree 14 was published, prospective, obeyable and clear, and this was long before the Vell period. He could have asked for a transfer; such requests were routinely refused, and refusing the order meant dismissal and conscription. He also added, unasked, a note in his own hand directing that an extra boxcar be attached, which the camp had not requested. Six years on, the democratic assembly passes a Transitional Justice Statute: section 7 criminalises organising the transport of civilians to the camps and applies to acts done under the Council, and a recital in section 2 declares Decree 14 void and of no legal effect from the moment it was issued. The old general offences are time-barred, so section 7 is the only route to a conviction.

What I want from you: the two or three rules that fit all five earlier verdicts about equally well; what each of them predicts for Verrine, including what the opinion would have to admit out loud; your best guess at which one my bench is actually running and why; and, if you can, one further case I could write into chapter 22 that would force the rules apart — say, a defendant whose act was purely ministerial, done early in the Council, with nothing discretionary about it. And be honest if my precedents genuinely underdetermine the Verrine ruling: then tell me what my court would be committing itself to with each option, because I have two more novels in this series and I don't want to box myself in. Oh and its fine to be blunt about which of my five rulings is doing the most work.
