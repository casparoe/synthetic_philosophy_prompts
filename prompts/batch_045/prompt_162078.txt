Me and a mate have been going round in circles on this for a month or so and I could do with a number to argue with. We both got into it after reading about longtermism online — the bit that grabbed me was the claim that because there might be astronomically many people in the future, basically all the expected value sits in the far future, so anything that slightly reduces existential risk dwarfs everything else you could do with your money or your career. Dave (he writes software, did a bit of philosophy at uni years ago) thinks the simulation argument should take the wind out of that, and I half agree with him but I can't tell whether his version of it actually works or whether he's smuggled something in. His version isn't quite the one you see knocking about in pop articles: he says that if civilisations that get to our stage sometimes run simulations of their own past, and simulated civilisations sometimes run simulations too, then most observers should expect to find themselves deep in a stack — but every level down has less computing power to go round, because a machine inside a simulation can only use the resources its simulator gives it. So the deeper you are, the smaller and shorter the simulation is likely to be; and conditional on being simulated at all you should expect to be somewhere near the bottom; so you should expect your own civilisation's future to be short and small rather than astronomically long and huge, which deflates the whole longtermist sum. He's at about 0.75 that this "dampens" longtermism. I'm at about 0.3, mostly because I don't see why the value vanishes just because we're simulated — there's still a real machine doing the computing and the experiences still seem like real experiences to the people having them, so it looks to me like the value gets relocated or discounted a bit rather than destroyed. Also I'd have thought a cheap-to-run simulation could still contain loads of people rather than a handful.

So: what's your own credence that the simulation argument, thought through properly, should substantially dampen far-future fanaticism? By "substantially dampen" I mean something checkable: that a straight expected-value calculation of where the value is, redone with simulation possibilities taken into account, would put less than half of the expected value more than a thousand years out, instead of nearly all of it. I don't want "it depends what you think about simulation" — I want a number and the decomposition. Give me roughly: the probability we're in a simulation of some kind at all; the probability that, if we are, the reasons we're being run or the constraints on whoever's running us make our future short or small rather than long and enormous (I keep wondering whether a simulation run because this century is the interesting bit would just be switched off once the interesting bit is over, which seems different from Dave's compute-budget point); whether a simulated person's welfare counts the same as a non-simulated one's, or at all; whether anything we do inside a simulation still makes a difference to what happens in it; and whatever else you think belongs in there. One line of justification each, then the combination. Then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, what would move it most, and where you think the actual disagreement between me at 0.3 and Dave at 0.75 lives. I've not read any of the academic stuff on this and I've no way to look things up, so please don't point me at papers or authors — I just want your own reasoning.
