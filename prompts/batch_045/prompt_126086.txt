I am convening a two-day adversarial collaboration on the ethics of belief and need you to draft the joint statement the two participants would sign at the end of it. Call them the Evidentialist and the Permissionist.

The Evidentialist holds the Clifford-style position: belief is governed by a norm of sufficient evidence; that norm does not lapse when the question is urgent, or when the consequences of withholding belief are bad, or when the belief would be pleasant or useful to hold; and the wrong involved in believing on insufficient evidence is not cancelled by the good that the belief goes on to produce. The Permissionist holds the James-style position: for a certain class of propositions, provided the question is genuine in his sense — the agent cannot abstain from answering it, both answers are live options for her, and the matter is momentous — and provided the available evidence does not settle it, a passional decision in favour of believing can be permissible; and for some such propositions the belief is permissible precisely because the agent's believing it is part of what would make it true.

Do not cite, quote from, or attribute claims to any published text, and do not propose readings of any author. Every claim in the document has to stand on its own reasoning.

Stipulations, to be used throughout:

- Call a proposition bootstrapping for an agent if it obtains, when it obtains at all, partly because that agent believes it: her believing is among the causal or constitutive conditions of its truth.
- Call a case one of evidential feedback if the evidence available to the agent about the proposition is not merely balanced but balanced in a way that depends on what she believes — because if she believes, she will act so as to bring about the confirming evidence, and if she does not, she will not.
- Distinguish three attitudes: full belief, which the agent would assert and act on without hedging; role-acceptance, acting as if the proposition holds within an institutional role while not treating it as settled; and avowal, publicly expressing commitment to it. Say which of these the ethics of belief is a norm about, and whether the answer differs between the two parties.

The anchoring case. Mara and Dev are co-principal investigators on a six-year grant, now in year two. They have had a serious rupture over authorship on a preprint. Whether the collaboration is worth salvaging over years three to six depends on whether Mara believes in year two that it is worth salvaging: if she does, she keeps investing effort, and that investment is partly constitutive of the collaboration's being worth salvaging; if she does not, she winds it down, and in winding it down she makes it the case that salvaging it would not have been worth it. Assume that the third-person evidence available to Mara at the moment she must choose how to behave — Dev's track record, the unspent funds, the seriousness of the rupture — is genuinely balanced, and that no further evidence is obtainable in time.

Variants to which the document must apply itself:

1. The bystander. The department chair, whose attitudes have no causal influence on the collaboration and who faces the same balanced evidence, asks whether she may believe it is worth salvaging.
2. The transparent bootstrap. Mara knows that her believing is what makes the proposition true and that nothing independent of her attitude settles it either way.
3. The failed bootstrap. Mara believes, invests, and Dev leaves anyway; the proposition turns out false. Does that make the belief impermissible, and is permissibility judged ex ante or ex post?
4. Attitude substitution. Mara cannot bring herself to full belief but can act as if the collaboration is worth salvaging, and can tell the department she is committed to it. Both parties should say which of these three attitudes is licensed, and whether the Permissionist ever needed more than role-acceptance and avowal.

I suspect the real cruxes include these, and I want each handled explicitly: whether the truth-making power of a belief is itself a piece of evidence for the proposition, so that the Evidentialist can absorb the bootstrapping cases without conceding anything, or is a reason of a different kind that evidentialism cannot ingest; whether any permission extends to the chair, which tests whether the Permissionist's reason is the causal efficacy of the belief or something about the agent's stance; whether the dispute is about full belief at all, or only about acceptance and avowal; and what counts as insufficient evidence — evidence independent of the agent's own attitudes, or the total evidence including facts about what she would believe.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section of eight to twelve claims. No claim should be vacuous: each should be something at least one of the two would have denied at the outset, or something that does real work later in the document.
2. A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the Evidentialist's formulation, worded as she would sign it; the Permissionist's formulation, worded as he would sign it; what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own formulation; and each side's prediction about the test case. If you cannot produce a formulation one side would accept as its own, say so and explain why.
3. For each crux, the concrete case, argument, or observable difference that both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.
4. A short closing section identifying which parts of the residual dispute are merely verbal, if any, and what exactly is in dispute once the verbal material is set aside. Flag whether anything both parties agree would actually move the debate.

Keep the whole thing under 1,300 words. Finish with a single line, and nothing after it, of exactly this form:

\answer{one of: substantive, verbal, mixed; then a semicolon; then the one crux both sides agree would most move the debate, in at most twelve words}
