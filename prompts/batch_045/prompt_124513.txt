Hey — I teach a 14-person third-year seminar that's basically rationality under uncertainty (philosophers, some econ people, a couple of PPE kids; all of them can do simple expected-value arithmetic and are happy writing a probability as a range instead of a number). I've got one 90-minute slot, no laptops if I can avoid it (the room's wifi is a joke and I want them up and moving anyway), and no money at all — payoffs have to be points toward a small participation bonus, or chocolate, or raffle tickets for one decent prize at the end. Last week in lecture we did expected utility, the Allais stuff, the usual Ellsberg urns, and I showed them that with a single sharp prior a free piece of information can never leave you worse off in expectation, provided you get to choose your act after you see it. This session is meant to break that.

What I want them to run into, in their gut rather than from me talking at them, is that a probability interval is a genuine state and not just laziness — and that it can get wider when you learn something. Concretely: there's some event they care about, and before anything is revealed they'd write its probability as something like 0.4 to 0.6. Then there's a signal that is coming no matter what, two possible outcomes, they know in advance they'll get one of them — and whichever way it goes, their interval for the event gets strictly wider, out to something like 0.3 to 0.7 both ways. So they can see ahead of time that looking will leave them vaguer, and they have to decide whether to look before they find out what it says. I want at least some of them to end up paying, in points or chocolate, to not be told.

Second thing: I want them to hit a case where what they actually want to do is flip a coin. Not "they're indifferent so they might as well toss for it", but where randomising really is better for them than choosing either option on purpose, precisely because their uncertainty is a set of probabilities rather than one number. If you can't make that happen with actual cards and bags and envelopes in a room, tell me so and give me the nearest thing that does work.

Third: I want them to notice that they're picking a policy, not just an act. Before anything is drawn, have them commit in writing to how they'll treat the interval — do they act on the worst case inside it, or do they rule out any option that's beaten by some other option under every probability in the set, or do they just take the middle of the range and stop worrying? (Describe those in plain English on the handout rather than naming them after anybody.) Then the sequence of draws should make the policies they signed up to come apart in what they permit — ideally the same student wants to do different things at different moments depending on what they committed to, and at least one of them eats a real cost for having committed.

So design me the thing: the rules, what's physically in the bags or urns or envelopes and how I rig it so they genuinely can't resolve the ambiguity (plus how I convince them I didn't cheat), the actual numbers with a couple of lines of arithmetic showing they do what you claim, a payoff schedule in points or chocolate, a timeline for 90 minutes, and who does what when. I can get one friend to be a second adult in the room for the first half hour if that helps.

Then the debrief: map particular moments onto particular ideas — where exactly the interval widened, where the free information hurt, where somebody wanted to randomise, where a policy bit somebody. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm guessing some of them will try to do frequentist inference off the draws and collapse the range back to a point, some will say this is all just a second-order probability and they should average, some will meta-game for the prize, and somebody will refuse to play because they think the fix is in — tell me how to head each of those off, or how to use them when you can't. And be honest about what the activity cannot deliver: I assume it can't show them the proof of anything, or that there's more than one defensible way to update a set of probabilities, or why the decision rules come apart in general. So tell me which pieces I have to say out loud at the end, and how to say them in about eight minutes without it turning back into a lecture.

Don't give them the name of the phenomenon until afterwards, and please flag anywhere you're relying on a bit of maths you're not fully sure of — I'd much rather have a wobbly game I can trust than an elegant one that falls apart when a smart student does the arithmetic on the board.
