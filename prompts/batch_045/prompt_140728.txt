I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I am running a small graduate reading group on whether results in computational complexity theory do any *normative* work, or only descriptive and engineering work. The concrete focus is the familiar worst-case hardness of probabilistic inference, planning and strategic reasoning in settings that are otherwise perfectly classical: no bounded recall, no resource limits written into the formalism, just ordinary Bayesian updating, ordinary expected-utility maximisation and ordinary equilibrium reasoning over an expressive language. One half of the group — call them the feasibility camp — argues that if a norm's verdicts cannot in general be computed, or even approximated to any useful guarantee, then it cannot be a norm for agents like us: 'ought' implies 'can', 'can' must be read as something like reliable producibility by a feasible procedure, and so our normative theories ought to be indexed to what tractable procedures can deliver, with the ideal theory demoted to a limiting or merely definitional role. The other half — the correctness camp — replies that standards of correctness are not the sort of thing that capacities can validate or invalidate; that intractability bears on excuse, implementation and institution-design but not on what it is to get things right; and, more sharply, that one can *satisfy* a norm without *computing* it, since an agent might be reliably coherent, or reliably optimal, by grace of structure, habit, or a cheap surrogate procedure whose outputs happen to agree with the ideal's verdicts on the cases that actually arise. We have now had this argument, in recognisably the same shape, four times in six weeks.

My own hypothesis is that we are running together at least three different questions under the word 'rationality': a question about *verdict-norms* (what it is to get a given deliberative problem right, i.e. what the correct answer is), a question about *procedure-norms* (what an agent should do, in the time and with the machinery it has, when it faces one), and a question about *design-norms* (what we should build, train, or institutionalise, including what we should hold each other responsible for). I also want to press a second distinction that I think nobody in the room has made cleanly: the distinction between the hardness of *computing* a norm's verdict and the hardness of *complying* with the norm, where the latter might be easy even when the former is intractable. That gap is what makes the 'cheap surrogate that tracks the ideal' move — call it the tracking defence — initially attractive, and my worry is that it is unstable: the meta-question of whether a given surrogate tracks the ideal on the instances that actually arise looks, for interesting cases, at least as hard as the original problem, and even if it is not, the answer seems to depend on facts about the distribution of real instances that nobody in the group is in a position to supply. A third, smaller faction thinks the whole dispute is ill-posed from the start, because complexity classes describe asymptotic scaling behaviour while our norms are applied to finite agents with fixed resources and fixed problem sizes, so that the granularity is simply wrong.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey, and I would like it in British English. Please tell me what kind of stalemate this is: a verbal dispute about 'rationality', 'norm' or 'can'; two or three different questions sharing a name; a hidden empirical disagreement about instance distributions, average-case versus worst-case structure, or human performance; a bedrock clash about whether normativity must be action-guiding to be normativity at all; or a genuinely ill-posed question. Engage the positions as I have described them rather than rehearsing who has said what, and please do not give me a reading list or citations — I am not asking you to reconstruct a literature. My specific questions are these. First, does the computing/complying distinction actually do the work I think it does, or does it smuggle in the very point at issue — that norms are standards of output rather than requirements of procedure — and thereby beg the question against the feasibility camp? Second, where exactly does the tracking defence fail: on the intractability of verifying tracking, on the absence of any principled account of which instance distributions matter, or somewhere else? Third, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — an average-case or smoothed-complexity result, an approximation-guarantee-to-verdict-tracking theorem, an empirical programme, a sharper conceptual distinction, an explicit change of question — and should one expect progress at all, or is the persistence of this argument evidence that the question is doing something other than asking to be answered?
