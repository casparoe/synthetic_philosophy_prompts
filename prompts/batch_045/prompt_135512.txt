I have been asked to contribute a one-page position statement on moral patienthood to a working group on artificial minds, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of whether the commitments I have assembled actually hold together. The draft defends the following package.

(1) Substrate independence: what matters morally is a system's organization, not what it is made of. A sufficiently detailed functional replica of a human brain, run on any hardware that can realize that organization, has the moral status of the original.

(2) Anti-behaviorism about realization: input-output equivalence is not enough. A vast lookup table mapping every possible sensory history to exactly the responses a given human would make, including all of her verbal reports of pain, has no moral status; and for the same reason neither does a system whose internal structure is whatever an optimizer happened to select because it produced the right outputs, where the self-reports are simply part of the trained repertoire.

(3) Testimony: a system's own first-person report that it is suffering is genuine evidence of suffering, and should carry non-trivial weight even where we have no independent grip on its internals. This is the commitment I most want to use, since it is the only one that does any work for me on systems of the kind that actually exist now.

(4) Gradualism: consciousness and moral weight come in degrees and vary continuously; the difference between a mammal and an insect is one of degree, and I reject any threshold or bright line as arbitrary.

(5) Counting: for aggregation and for policy I need to know how many welfare subjects a system contains, and I assume there is a determinate answer in principle, roughly one subject per maximal causally integrated complex, with no double-counting of complexes nested inside a larger one. I also assume the number matters independently of how intense any single subject's experience is.

(6) Anti-triviality: I reject the view that computational description is merely observer-relative, on the grounds that the computations a system genuinely implements are the ones that actually guide its behavior counterfactually.

(7) Two case verdicts I would rather not surrender: a replica run at a thousandth of the original's speed has the same moral status as the original; and a replica currently powered down in storage has no experiences while it is off, but is the same subject once restarted.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, with the merely apparent ones explained: what distinction dissolves them. For the real ones, rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say precisely which commitment would have to give, what the least costly revision of it would be, and what that revision costs me in terms of claims I could then no longer make, especially about present-day systems. If any pair I have implicitly treated as consistent conceals a further problem, say so. Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the most, and say what I lose by making it.
