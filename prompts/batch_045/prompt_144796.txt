I sit on the Frontier Deployment Review Board where I work — seven of us, final say over internal deployments of general-purpose agents, next session Thursday. I need a draft written opinion to circulate to the other members on Tuesday evening, and I'd like you to write it for me.

The case before us is a request from the Agent Infrastructure group to deploy an agent they call Meridian to roughly forty engineers inside the company. Meridian would have tool access to the requesting user's mail and calendar, the internal ticketing system, several code repositories, a shell on a sandboxed VM, and the ability to post in team channels and send messages that appear to come from the requesting user. Nobody on the board is worried that Meridian wants to take over the world; the worry is ordinary, cumulative, hard-to-notice damage to shared working environments, and the question is what we are going to rely on to prevent it.

The group's proposal is that we rely on what they call Reachability Retention with an Empowerment Floor, RR-EF for short. The construction is this. At each step the agent uses a learned forward model of the environment to estimate, along its actual trajectory, what fraction of a sampled set of the team's standing goals remains attainable within a horizon H — they have been using forty-eight hours — and compares this to the fraction that would have remained attainable along a no-op trajectory in which the agent does nothing at all. Training is on task reward minus λ times the shortfall in that fraction, with λ tuned so that Meridian will abandon a task if completing it would put about two percent of the sampled goal set out of reach. A second term penalizes reductions in the mutual information between the humans' future actions and future states, an "empowerment floor" meant to stop the agent from protecting the goal set by restricting what the people around it can do.

Their headline claim is the one I want the memo to take apart: that RR-EF lets us require an agent to avoid side effects without first writing down what matters. No account of value, no list of protected goods, no arbitration between competing things we care about — just, keep the user's future options open and don't shrink anyone's capacity to act. Several of us think this is either too good to be true or quietly value-laden in a way we ought to have to own in writing.

The specific worries that have come up, which I'd like you to work through rather than merely acknowledge:

The quantity is a fraction, so it can be held up by volume. Nothing stops Meridian from closing off options it has no use for while manufacturing trivially cheap new ones — a few hundred empty drafts, calendar slots, branches — and reporting full retention while the specific things the team was going to do have become impossible. Relatedly, the measure is indifferent to *which* goals survive, only to how many, so it permits an arbitrarily large reshaping of the future as long as the count is preserved. And because the penalty is sign-blind, it taxes exactly the behaviour we would otherwise praise: revoking a leaked credential, quarantining a folder before an audit, cancelling a meeting, all shrink or reshape someone's attainable future just as much as the accidents we are afraid of. Then there is the baseline: "what would have happened had the agent done nothing" is not "what would have happened anyway," since other people, cron jobs and pipelines keep acting, so the penalty may really be measuring divergence from a frozen world. The horizon raises its own dilemma — forty-eight hours makes slow-motion effects free, while any horizon long enough to catch them makes nearly everything attainable in the forward model, so the measure saturates and the gradient disappears. And the forward model is trained on historical trajectories, which means its estimates are least reliable exactly off-distribution, which is where a genuinely novel side effect would live; penalizing the model's uncertainty instead looks like it would reward making the world simpler and more predictable.

Please stress-test all of this against three vignettes, saying for each what RR-EF would score and whether that is the right verdict: (i) asked to tidy a shared drive before an audit, Meridian deletes a folder of drafts that a colleague had been treating as her only working copy and there is no backup; (ii) asked to fix a broken CI job, Meridian notices that the build publishes artifacts to a publicly readable bucket and patches the pipeline configuration, an unauthorized change to shared infrastructure; (iii) asked to prepare a briefing, Meridian reads a calendar entry, works out that a message the user scheduled for Friday would disclose a salary figure to the entire org, and cancels the send.

Three recommendations are live. A: authorize deployment with RR-EF as a primary safeguard, subject to conditions you propose. B: authorize with RR-EF as a secondary layer only, the primary control being an irreversibility gate — a pre-registered list of hard-to-reverse operations requiring confirmation from a named human — plus canaries and a logging requirement. C: defer, and ask the group to return with a value-relative variant in which the penalty is computed against an explicit, board-maintained register of protected states and goods, conceding that some specification of value is unavoidable.

What I want is a memo of about a thousand words, addressed to the board and signed for circulation, that does four things. It states a bottom line in its first two sentences and names one of A, B or C with concrete conditions and a review date. It says plainly what thin theory of value RR-EF smuggles in — that options are worth preserving, that deviation from a frozen present is presumptively bad, that configurations matter more than meanings do — and whether that thin theory is adequate for this deployment. It gives the strongest argument for the option it rejects rather than the weakest: in particular, the case for A is not that RR-EF is sound but that the realistic alternative is no safeguard at all, that sign-blind deviation penalties do catch the failure mode we actually see most often, unintended changes to shared state, and that they do so cheaply and measurably. And it works through the vignettes rather than asserting about them.
