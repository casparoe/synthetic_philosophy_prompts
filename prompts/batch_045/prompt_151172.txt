Hi — I cover science and ideas for a magazine (mostly long features, but I also do the final copy pass on freelance pieces a few times a week), and I keep having the same argument with our fact-checker. She wants every sentence hedged into mush; I think roughly half our sentences say "shows" where the study really just got one specific prediction to come out right, and another half say "is consistent with" where the result actually did knock out a live alternative. My friend, who's a physicist, says all hedging is cowardice and that we should print "shows" whenever the authors do, which I'm pretty sure is wrong. What I want isn't a lecture on the philosophy of science — it's something I can paste into our house style guide and actually run.

Here's the shape I have in mind, and tell me if it's the wrong shape: a ladder of evidential verbs we're allowed to use in copy (something like "shows," "strongly supports," "supports," "is consistent with," "does not rule out," "cannot distinguish between"), plus a short ordered set of tests for picking the highest rung a sentence is allowed to sit on. The tests should be the ones that decide whether the evidence is actually doing any discriminating work: what exactly the account predicted ahead of time, versus a story fitted to the data afterward; whether that prediction was the kind that could easily have come out the other way; whether getting the result required background assumptions the data itself can't check (how the instrument behaves, the measurement model, the statistical model, assumptions about the sample) — I gather there's a name in philosophy for that problem but I can never remember it, so don't bother naming it, just build the step; and, the one I care most about, whether some rival account fits the published numbers about as well. On that last one I've started sorting rivals into what I call "live" ones (a competent person in the field would actually defend it) and "zombie" ones (logically compatible with the data, but nobody would seriously hold it) — because I don't want to hedge every sentence just because some skeptical scenario is logically possible, and I also don't want to write "shows" when the field genuinely has two accounts that fit the same data. Tell me if that split is doing the wrong work, or if I need a third bucket: I keep hitting cases where the rivals are distinguishable in principle but only by an experiment nobody will fund, and cases where the thing being "tested" is a whole framework rather than a claim, so any single study can only ever be consistent or inconsistent with it.

Constraints: it has to run in about ten minutes at copy-desk stage, by someone with no philosophy training who then has to sign off on the sentence, mostly from the paper itself since the freelancer is often unreachable; and I want it honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. Please say explicitly where the procedure just stops with a no (you may not use a stronger verb, full stop) and where it hands the decision to a human because the trade-off is about values — how much we'd rather over-hedge than over-claim, and so on. Then run it on three cases: (1) a preprint, 120 people recruited online, scores on a psychological scale correlated with how often they shared a false headline, written up by the authors as confirming a motivated-reasoning account over a "people just can't tell what's true" account; (2) a large randomized trial that found no difference between two diets on some biomarker, with a press release saying the trial "refutes" the theory the diets were based on; and (3) a piece I wrote myself last spring (details invented, the structure is what matters) in which I said that a new set of genetic results "confirmed" the leading account of how a population recolonized a region after a bottleneck, and a colleague later pointed out that a second account fits the same data about as well. I'd especially like to know whether the procedure tells me to downgrade that last one, because if it does I have to go tell my editor and eat a correction, and I'd rather not. Last thing: say plainly what the procedure can't do — I don't want anyone on staff walking away thinking that a sentence passing these tests means the study was any good.
