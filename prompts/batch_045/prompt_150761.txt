I've been arguing with two friends — one a patent attorney, one a historian of science — about whether it matters who makes a discovery when the discovery was, so to speak, going to happen anyway, and it struck me partway through that the law has already taken a position on this, and that the position is odd. As I understand the doctrine (correct me if I have it wrong): a patent system hands a single applicant a temporary monopoly over an invention; the monopoly goes to whoever files first rather than to whoever thought of it first, and, unlike copyright, where independent creation is a complete defence, a second person who arrives at exactly the same thing entirely on their own, never having seen the first person's work, is still an infringer and can be enjoined out of using it. But the very same body of law refuses a patent in the first place to anyone whose invention would have been obvious to an ordinary skilled practitioner in the field at the time it was made, and it sometimes treats the fact that other workers were visibly closing in on the same result as evidence that it was obvious. So the law discounts your claim when your achievement was merely *ripe* — when a suitably placed person would have reached it anyway — while being entirely indifferent to the fact that your achievement was actually *redundant*: duplicated within months, and independently, by someone else. The calculus priority quarrel and the Darwin–Wallace episode are my historian friend's stock illustrations, and neither Newton nor Darwin fares especially well if you ask, seriously, what the world would have lost without that particular man.

What I want from you is the implicit theory of invention and credit that this package only makes sense under. It can't just be incentive policy doing all the work: I'd like you to reconstruct what view of an individual's causal role in discovery would make it sensible to deny a patent to the merely-ripe inventor while handing the merely-first inventor a monopoly over an independent second one, and to tell me whether that combination is genuinely coherent or just two historical accretions bolted together. In particular, why should *hypothetical* redundancy (a skilled person would have managed it) defeat a claim while *actual* redundancy (someone did manage it) is irrelevant? My own sense is that the actual case is the stronger evidence of dispensability — it is hard to see how evidence that a thing did happen anyway could be worse evidence that it would have happened anyway — and I'd like to know whether that intuition is doing real work or resting on a conflation, since obviousness is asked about a hypothetical agent at a time, whereas redundancy is established after the fact, by people who may have benefited from the first inventor's publication. My attorney friend's answer is that the two halves have different jobs: obviousness is a proxy for the social cost of locking up what others were about to reach unaided, while priority is a mere convention for settling contests cheaply. Is that a consistent pair, or does the convention quietly presuppose exactly the heroic-individual picture of discovery that the obviousness doctrine disowns?

Concretely, then, I'd like three things. First, the implicit theory, stated sharply enough that I can see what else it would commit me to — separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and telling me whether several quite different pictures of discovery (the individual as originator, the individual as the finisher of something cumulatively produced, the field as simply ripe) would all license these same rules, in which case the practice is more philosophically underdetermined than it looks. Second, a verdict on the pairing: if ripeness and first-to-file really do pull in opposite directions, which half should give, and what would we lose by giving it up? Third, a sketch of what a system that took actual redundancy seriously would look like — a regime, say, in which an independent second inventor is entitled to keep practising on some licensed or royalty-bearing basis, or in which the term of protection shortens as the evidence accumulates that the field was converging on the result anyway — together with what such a regime would presuppose about credit. Assume I want the philosophical shape of the thing rather than doctrine or authorities; and please tell me honestly if my ripeness/redundancy distinction is carving at a joint or just restating the puzzle in new words.
