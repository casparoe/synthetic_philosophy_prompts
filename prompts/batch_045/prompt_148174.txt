I've stupidly agreed to write a short position piece for a volume — "where I actually stand on Descartes and animal minds" — and before I commit 4000 words to print I'd like someone to check whether the committments cohere. I've taught this material on and off for years and I still can't see clearly where the trouble is. Here's where I've landed, in no particular order.

(1) The engine of Descartes' denial is a priori, not empirical. I read him as holding that a state is mental only if it's first-personally available to its subject — available to be avowed, doubted, owned as mine, hooked into the "I". Call it the availability condition. On that reading the language test and the business about jays and parrots aren't evidence for the view, they're symptoms of it, and no amount of ethology would have moved him. It's also why I think the animals question isn't a side issue for him: it's the same thesis doing the work in his argument that mind and body are really distinct.

(2) I want the availability condition to be a condition on thought specifically — judging, meaning, conceiving — and not on sentience. So the official position doesn't entail that animals feel nothing, and I think the standard story about Cartesians nailing dogs to boards and insisting the noise is just machinery is mostly a polemical cartoon that misreads the view.

(3) Consequently, there's something it's like to be a carp. Sentience, as I use it, is valence without valuation: felt badness that doesn't require the subject to conceptualize it, appraise it, or even register it as bad. Lots of animals clear that bar without thinking anything at all. And I don't think markers like mirror self-recognition tell us much either way here, since those are tests for self-awareness rather than for feeling.

(4) What I think defeats Descartes' application of the principle is nonetheless the ethology — corvids, parrots, apes. I take flexible, cross-context concept use to be decent evidence that the availability condition is met in a creature. So I keep the principle and drop the application to animals.

(5) Morally: valence is what does the work, not cognitive sophistication. An animal that can't do anything with its pain isn't less of a patient for that reason. I end up at the usual conclusion about factory farming. I also think the usual argument from marginal cases is a bad route to my own conclusion, since it concedes that cognition sets the bar.

(6) But I also think pain and suffering aren't the same thing, and that suffering has temporal thickness: the very same nociception is worse when it's anticipated, remembered, dreaded, resented, folded into a life. A creature whose self is thin across time can't be made wretched by dread, and its pain doesn't echo.

(7) Corollary, one I'm fond of: because that self is thin, a painless death isn't much of a loss to a carp — it doesn't have a future its invested in. So the scandal, if there is one, is the life rather than the ending. I also think a genuinely painless version of animal agriculture would still be wrong, not merely the one we actually have.

(8) Methodologically, I think the strongest Cartesian position is the one that bites the bullet on neonates and on advanced dementia: no thoughts in the relevant sense, plenty of feeling, nothing cheapened by saying so. I've always taken that to be a feature rather than a bug, though I couldn't tell you why I'm so sure.

So: which of these are actually in tension, and which only look like it? Rank the real ones by severity; for each, tell me what I'd have to give up and what giving it up costs me; then tell me whether there's one revision that cleans up the most. I'd rather not be sent off to read someone — I know the literature is there, I want to know what breaks. I'm not attached to any of these, so don't be gentle.
