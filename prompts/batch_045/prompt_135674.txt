I help run a fortnightly reading group — not a university thing, about 14 people in the back room of a pub, mostly working programmers plus two or three philosophy grad students, the kind of crowd that says "taboo the word" without irony — and we've been crawling through Kripke's Naming and Necessity. We've got 90 minutes per session, no whiteboard, index cards and envelopes if needed, and I don't want anything that needs a laptop or a projector. The thing people keep bouncing off is the necessary a posteriori. They can recite the morning star/evening star example back at me, but I don't think they *feel* it: as soon as you stop supervising them they collapse the necessary/contingent axis into the a priori/a posteriori axis, and they quietly treat necessary-a-posteriori truths as a bit of a hack rather than as what you should expect whenever a rigid term on each side of an identity sentence picks out its object by a historical chain rather than by a description. I also don't want a discussion format, because the loudest person in the room has read the book twice and will just narrate it at everyone.

So: design me a game for that room, that time budget, groups of 3-4, where the participants generate the cases themselves (I want them inventing their own dubbings rather than pattern-matching onto Hesperus or the metre stick — if they make the cases, they can't just pattern-match), and where the payoff structure is what makes the point. My rough thought, which you should fix or replace: something with two betting rounds, a sealed round before a dossier is opened and an open round after, where the sealed bet pays double, so there's genuine pressure to commit early to the modal status of a claim whose truth you can only learn from the envelope — the modal fact doesn't move when the envelope opens, only your warrant does, and I want them to feel that as a price they actually paid. Here are two case types I want built in, because they're the ones my group will otherwise wriggle out of, and I'd like them to be forced into an explicit bet rather than a shrug. First, a *disjunctive dubbing*: the baptism says something like 'call this the unique thing satisfying F; if nothing uniquely satisfies F, then it names whatever the dubbing committee would have picked had they known more' — the reference-fixer is itself counterfactually loaded, and I want the game to surface whether such a term behaves rigidly or is really a description in a trenchcoat. Second, a *forked chain*: the name is passed along a chain of use that splits, so two fully competent speakers at the two ends use the same name for different objects without either making an ordinary mistake, and the group has to say what the name designates in a scenario where the fork goes the other way. Please also build in at least one case that's contingent a priori and one that's necessary a posteriori without labelling them as such, so the four-way grid arrives as a surprise rather than a worksheet.

What I need back: the rules and materials (nothing that needs printing in colour, ideally), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including setup, the payoff scheme with actual numbers, and a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto specific parts of the view — rigidity versus having a description as your meaning, the two axes and why they come apart, why a change in their betting is not a change in modal status, and what the fork and disjunctive cases show. I'd rather nobody says the words 'rigid' or 'a posteriori' before the debrief. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it (someone will try to argue the metaphysics instead of playing, someone will metagame the payoffs, the fork case will probably produce a stalemate, one person will insist the disjunctive dubbing is just a description) and how to handle each. And be frank about what a game like this just can't reproduce — my suspicion is that a finite stack of printed scenario cards can only ever model "true in all the situations anyone bothered to write down", which isn't the metaphysical claim, and that the anti-descriptivist argument's real force is something I'll have to supply out loud. Anyway — please write it up in British English, and feel free to tell me my two-round betting idea is broken if it is.
