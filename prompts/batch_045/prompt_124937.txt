I'm a high-school senior writing a term paper on whether the mind is a computer. The chapter I'm working from — a popular-science book my teacher lent me — sums up the computational theory of mind like this:

Your brain is a computer and your mind is its software. Because a neuron either fires or it doesn't, the brain is digital at bottom, so any mental state you're in is just the pattern of firing across your neurons at that moment: the program running on the hardware. But software doesn't care which hardware it runs on. Run the same program on silicon and the same mind — the same you — would be there, which is what makes mind-uploading a genuine possibility rather than science fiction. The Chinese Room argument showed this can't be right: a man following rules for Chinese understands nothing, so following a program can never produce understanding, and since that argument hardly anyone in philosophy or neuroscience still thinks the brain is literally a computer.

Two things make me suspicious: the jump to uploading feels too quick, and my philosophy textbook discusses functionalism as a live view rather than a refuted one.

Could you go through the passage and tell me which sentences someone working in philosophy of mind would accept, which are oversimplifications or conflations, and which are simply wrong? I'm especially unsure about three things: whether the digital/firing claim captures what computationalists actually mean; whether Searle's argument is aimed at the computational theory of mind or at something narrower, such as the claim that computation is enough for meaning; and whether substrate-independence, even if granted, really delivers uploading. I'd also like to know whether the sentence "the brain is a computer" needs to be made more precise than it is here — whether, without some account of what makes a physical system count as implementing a given program, it risks becoming a claim that almost nothing could fail to satisfy.
