I'm a caseworker at a legal aid clinic and I've been going through the written decisions of one adjudicator on our Protection Review Board — call her H — on the bus home, because I've got a client next week who turns on almost exactly the same sort of question and I want to know what she'll do with him. We're not a Convention country; our statute basically says we grant protection where someone can't reasonably be expected to return because of serious harm their own state won't avert, which leaves more or less everything open. Her run so far: granted for a journalist jailed and beaten by police after publishing corruption allegations; granted for a woman whose partner broke her jaw and whom the police ignored four times; granted for a man who got a handwritten death threat naming him from a cartel after he refused to drive for them, and whose complaint the police took and then did nothing about; refused for a shopkeeper paying protection money to a gang along with everyone else on his street, on the basis that there's a functioning anti-extortion unit and he could set up again in the capital; refused for a farmer who left after three failed rainy seasons, where the government ran relief camps he'd have been fed in; refused for a man from a city shelled by both sides in a civil war, where nobody had singled him out and the police had more or less dissolved; and granted for a woman from the Veshnik minority whose identity documents were cancelled under a new registration law, leaving her unable to work, travel or collect rations — nobody had laid a finger on her, the officials were just implementing policy.

My client is a fifty-something fisherman from the Kaldor delta, which is nearly all Veshnik. Nine years ago the state granted a forty-year concession to a foreign-owned mining company, the company diverts the main channel for washing ore, and the fish are gone; his village also has no drinking water for a few months of the year. Nobody has threatened him. Officials call the damage regrettable and say the concession earns export revenue. There was a one-off compensation payment that he's already spent and that doesn't touch the water. He could move to the capital, but there's no fishing there and Veshnik renters get shoved to the bottom of the housing queue — aggravation and refusals, nothing you'd call violence — and a minister told a rally last year that the delta people would just have to adapt. The delta has no seat on the water board either, and the civil servants who sit on it are all from the majority group.

Obvs I care what she decides, but I care more about the rule underneath: give me the two or three rules that fit her seven decisions equally well, what each of them predicts for my client, your best guess with the reasons you're going with it, and the one further case you'd most want to see her decide in order to tell those rules apart. If there's room, say what I should lead with in the skeleton argument. No more than 500 words, three short headed sections, and please don't give me a survey of how real jurisdictions handle this — I've read that stuff and it's not what I need.
