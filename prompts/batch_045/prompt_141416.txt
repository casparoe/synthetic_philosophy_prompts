I have no training in philosophy, but I belong to a small online reading group that argues about these things, and there is one line that keeps being produced as a conversation-stopper in our threads on meaning: "meanings just ain't in the head". It comes, I gather, from the twin-planet literature of the 1970s. I would like to know what it could actually be asserting.

The dispute where it did most work for me turned on a case I invented, because the standard story — a twin planet on which the colourless, drinkable liquid is a different chemical — seemed to leave something out. On my planet the lakes, rivers and rain hold two chemically distinct liquids, call them A and B, indistinguishable to the settlers, roughly equally abundant, and equally serviceable for drinking, washing and irrigation. The colonists have a single word, "water", which they apply to both; they take them to be one kind of stuff; and nothing in their experience could tell them apart. Some centuries later chemists arrive and identify two substances.

Two members of the group cited the slogan and drew opposite conclusions. One held that, because meaning is fixed by the surroundings and not by anything in the speakers' heads, "water" there was never about "the drinkable liquid" as such: it was about A and B all along, and the only remaining question is whether that makes the word ambiguous, disjunctive, or possessed of no determinate reference. The other held that, for the very same reason, its meaning is simply the union of the two substances, and that the first speaker was smuggling something back in by asking which one the colonists were "really" talking about. A variant we argued about later divided us the same way: take a settler who has never handled either liquid and who simply defers to the chemists, who have in fact swapped the two labels. Here the slogan was used both to say that his word means what the chemists mean by it and to say that it means whatever he is actually surrounded by.

Would you set out, as a menu rather than a single verdict, the precise theses this one-liner might be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical — about reference, about the content of thoughts, about how well a speaker can know his own meaning, about deference to others, about psychological explanation? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; and say which, if any, would actually settle my cases and which only look as though they do. If none of them settles them, tell me what further question has to be answered first. Please reply in British English.
