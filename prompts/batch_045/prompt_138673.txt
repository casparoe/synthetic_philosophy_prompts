I'm in one of those philosophy reading groups that meets at the library on Tuesdays — I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, thirty years of running a lab and now apparently this — and last week we got onto who should be allowed to vote and I've been going round in circles ever since. I dont actually know what I think in general. I only know what I think about individual cases, so let me just give you those and see if you can find the principle underneath.

- Sixteen year olds: yes. They'll be living with whatever we decide about schools and climate long after I'm not, and I've met plenty of fifty year olds who understand less than the average sixth former.
- A twelve year old who's obviously sharp, follows the news, and could pass any test you set: no. And I can't give a reason for that without it also eating the sixteen year old.
- A competence or civics test for voters of any age: I recoil from it. Though I've just appealed to something like competence in the case above, and I can't tell you why a school leaving exam is fine and this isn't.
- Someone who has lived and worked here twenty years, pays tax, kids in school here, but never got round to naturalising: yes, obviously. They're subject to our laws exactly as much as I am.
- Someone here on a two year work visa: no, for national elections anyway. And I'm mildly ashamed of that, because the difference between them and the twenty year resident is just a number of years.
- A citizen who left forty years ago, has no property or family here, no plan to come back, doesn't even follow the news: still yes. This is the one I feel most strongly about and it's the one that wrecks my theory, because on any affected-interests story they're the weakest case of the lot. What I think I actually believe is that membership is a real thing and it doesn't dissolve through absence.
- Prisoners serving short sentences: yes. Life sentence for a violent crime: I find myself saying no, and I suspect that's just extra punishment smuggled in, in which case it should go.
- Compulsory voting: yes, but only if there's a 'none of the above' box on the ballot. I want them at the table, I don't want to force them to assert something they don't believe. Someone in the group says that's incoherent — either you're compelling a meaningless act or the box makes it voluntary anyway — and I can't tell which of us is confused.
- Parents casting an extra vote on behalf of each of their under-sixteens: I hate it. But my objection keeps turning out to be that it's really the parents voting twice, and I can't work out whether that's an objection to the scheme or just to parents.

My own first pass, before the expat broke it, was: you get a vote on a decision if you'll be subject to it for most of the period it bites. I like it because it's the sort of move I used to make at work — fix the boundary of the system, then ask which degrees of freedom are inside it, rather than arguing about each molecule. It handles the resident, the visa holder and the sixteen year old. But it says no to the expat and yes to the twelve year old, and I won't accept either. Someone else in the group suggested that instead of a yes/no threshold you weight: the longer you'll be living under the outcome, the more your vote counts. I have the same reaction to that as to compulsory voting — it sounds principled and slightly monstrous, and I'd like to know whether that reaction is worth anything.

So what I want is a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could argue with it next Tuesday. Tie each clause to the case that motivated it. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts no reasonable principle saves, and which pair I have to give up on. And tell me what the ones you come up with say about three cases I haven't listed: a dual citizen who votes in two countries' national elections; a region holding a referendum on whether to secede, where half the residents moved there recently from elsewhere in the country; and someone who becomes eligible the day after the electoral register closes.
