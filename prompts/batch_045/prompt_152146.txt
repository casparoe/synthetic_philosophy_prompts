So my reading group is doing pragmatism this term and we've spent like three sessions going in circles about Peirce versus James on truth, and i'm fairly convinced at this point that we are mostly talking past each other rather than actually disagreeing. What I want from you is the document a real adversarial collaboration between the two camps would have produced: on one side a committed Peircean, for whom truth is tied to what inquiry would settle at its ideal, indefinitely extended limit, and who is a realist enough to think there is a fact of the matter even where we'll never get there; on the other side a committed Jamesian, for whom truth is a matter of what a belief does for the person holding it, its workability, its cash value, the difference it makes in experience. Give me the claims both of them could sign, including the boring cases where their verdicts coincide, and then the places they part, each formulated in the wording that side would accept for its own view rather than in a version the other side would find easy to knock down. Then the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, and for each crux a case both would accept as genuinely bearing on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out written down in advance.

For the cases, please use the ones our group actually got stuck on instead of the standard textbook illustrations. First, the sealed storeroom: some claim about how many oil jars were in a warehouse in a city that burned down, where every record and every person who knew is gone and no evidence could ever bear on it again — is there a truth of the matter there, and does anything at all turn on calling it true? Second, the useful falsehood: a belief that is in fact false but that reliably pays better for the person holding it than its negation would, in a situation where the holder will never be in a position to find out either way. Third, branching inquiry: two research communities that begin from the same evidence, use methods each regards as impeccable, lose contact with each other permanently, and settle into different stable opinions on the same question, each internally successful and each unable to show the other is making an error. If you want a fourth there is also the version where inquiry has a hard stop, a last possible observation after which no evidence is ever gathered again, which is the one that split us worst. My guesses about the cruxes, in case they help: whether the Peircean end-of-inquiry opinion is offered as a description of where inquiry is heading or only as a regulative ideal; whether expedience is indexed to an individual believer or to some community across the long run; and whether they are even theorizing the same property, or whether one is giving an account of truth while the other is giving an account of what makes a belief all right to hold. If you conclude that one of our disputes is merely verbal, say which one and why you think so.

Two last things. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague — I know documents like this normally come out as a numbered list of agreed claims followed by a disagreements matrix, but I specifically do not want that here: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs and a bit of signposting inside sentences. And at the end, still in prose, tell me how confident you are that an actual convinced Peircean and an actual convinced Jamesian would both put their names to what you've drafted, and single out the one sentence you're least sure about, the one you'd expect at least one side to balk at. Also, the detail that Peirce renamed his own view pragmaticism largely because he was fed up with what James had done with the word is something our group found definately hilarious, so don't feel you have to paper over the fact that they annoyed each other.
