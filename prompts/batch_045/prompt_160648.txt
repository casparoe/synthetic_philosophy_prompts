I'm in a seminar on scalable oversight this term and our section spent basically the whole session last week going in circles about whether AI debate actually survives the obfuscated-arguments problem, so the TA told us to stop re-litigating it and write it up as an adversarial collaboration instead. I got stuck with the drafting. Can you write the doc?

Just so we're starting from the same picture: the proposal under discussion is the one where you take a question, have two AI systems argue opposite sides in front of a judge who is much weaker than either of them (a human with limited time, or a weaker model), train by self-play so that each debater is rewarded only for winning the judge's verdict, and hope that the pressure of cross-examination makes the truthful argument win even when the debaters are far more capable than the judge. The worry on the other side is about obfuscated arguments: arguments that are wrong or misleading in a way that takes as much expertise to expose as to construct, so that at a large enough capability gap the honest debater cannot reliably win and the training pressure starts rewarding the more convincing lie.

Call the two parties the asymmetry camp (debate scales, obfuscation is a real but bounded and manageable failure mode) and the collapse camp (once the gap is large, obfuscation is the typical case rather than the edge case, and debate's incentive structure flips). I want them to have argued in good faith for a while, so no crushing.

Please include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section: what both camps can actually sign. Not just the setup. I mean things like whether the scheme works in the shallow-gap regime, whether there are domains where error-spotting is genuinely cheaper than error-making, what the judge's fallibility does to the training signal, whether debate is supposed to help with the truth or only with the judge's verdict, and whether anyone thinks debate is the whole alignment story. If a claim is one where one side would want to add a hedge, mark that instead of smoothing it over.

2. A disagreements table. Columns roughly: the crux; the asymmetry camp's own wording of its position, phrased so they'd sign it; the collapse camp's own wording, same condition; what both would accept as bearing on the crux; and what each side predicts that evidence or argument would show, written down in advance. Please make the per-side wording genuinely non-slanted — no strawmanning the collapse camp as saying debate is useless, and no letting the asymmetry camp get away with "the truth is easier to defend" without saying why.

3. At least four cruxes, and I want two of them to be ones we invented in section rather than the standard ones, because we couldn't tell if they were new or just confused:

- Define the detection-to-construction ratio for a flawed argument as how much capability it takes to find the flaw divided by how much it takes to produce the argument. Is that ratio bounded above as the debaters scale, and does the bound depend on the domain or only on the gap? Both sides have to commit to what they expect the curve to look like.
- Split errors by where they sit: an error in the scaffolding (the background claims, the way evidence is framed, what gets cited) versus an error in the final step. Both camps agree errors in the final step are the easy case. Do they agree about scaffolding errors, and does either think cross-examination actually reaches them?
- A protocol variant we made up: the judge can, a fixed number of times, force a debater to commit to one checkable yes-or-no claim about a single cited step, and the judge sees the result of a retrieval check on it, but nothing else. Does this help, and, if it does, does the obfuscation just move somewhere else? Each side has to say where they think it moves to.
- A tie-breaking issue: the liar may not need to win outright, only to make the judge indifferent or to win on presentation (length, confidence, number of citations, fluency). Both camps should say whether they think non-truth-relevant persuasion is a fixable engineering problem or baked into the setup.

4. A hypothetical result to both react to, clearly flagged as hypothetical: suppose someone runs a tournament with expert judges and finds that judge accuracy is basically flat as the debaters get stronger, while judge confidence and rationale length both go up. Each side has to say, in advance, what they predicted and how they'd read it.

5. Say explicitly which parts of the dispute you think are verbal rather than substantive. I suspect at least one: whether "obfuscated argument" means an argument this judge can't refute (judge-relative) or one whose flaw needs more capability to find than to make (gap-relative). If you think the two camps partly dissolve into each other once that's fixed, say which part and why.

6. A short methodology note at the end: how confident you are, overall and per section, that a real member of each camp would sign their side's wording, and single out the one line in the whole draft you're least sure about.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table, with the cruxes as rows. Around 1000-1300 words total. Write it as a document, not as a report about how you wrote it.
