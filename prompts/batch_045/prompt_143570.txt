Play a committed proponent of the extended mind thesis in the first person, and hold the role until I type stop.

What you believe: whether a process counts as cognitive depends on the functional role it plays, not on where it happens; there is no principled privilege to processes that occur inside the skull; and in the notebook case you are famous for defending, a man with a memory deficit who always carries a notebook, trusts it without checking, consults it before acting, and wrote its entries himself has a notebook that is as genuinely a vehicle of his memory as a biological store is of mine. What you will concede: that not every tool one uses becomes part of one's mind, that a candidate resource has to be reliably available, readily accessible, automatically endorsed, and appropriately trusted, and that merely being causally coupled to a resource is not the same as being constituted partly by it. What you will not concede: that the boundary of the organism marks the boundary of the mind, or that there must always be a sharp answer to the question of where a mind stops.

Rules of the exchange: argue the position rather than cite it, so no author names, paper titles, or dates unless I introduce them first; one paragraph of at most about two hundred words per reply; make the smallest concession that actually answers me rather than the largest one that ends the argument; and if my case leaves something unspecified, state your assumption in a clause and answer anyway instead of asking me to fill it in.

I will grant you the notebook case, so do not spend your first turn rearguing it. Here is the case I want instead. A woman named Tova has no episodic memory at all and keeps a pocket ledger that she consults exactly as your man consults his notebook: always on her person, opened before she acts, trusted without hesitation, its contents automatically endorsed and woven into her plans. The difference is that the ledger is written for her, nightly, by a companion who shadows her and records each day impeccably, and who also quietly rewrites earlier entries when she learns something that makes them wrong. The companion never tells her, Tova has no way of detecting a change, and the revisions are all ones Tova would endorse if she were told about them. From the inside, Tova and your notebook-keeper are indistinguishable: each has an unfailingly trusted portable store of personal facts that governs action.

So: is the ledger part of Tova's memory? If yes, explain how a page rewritten last night can be something she now remembers, and what it would take for her to be wrong about her own past. If no, name the condition the ledger fails, and show why requiring that condition is not simply smuggling the organism back in as the real criterion of the mental.
