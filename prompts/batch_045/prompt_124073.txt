Our professional society is rewriting its house style guide, and the language section is where every meeting goes to die. We keep settling things one word at a time — chairman, you guys, mankind, manned spacecraft, manhole — and I'd rather have one principled spec than a longer banned list. So before I read any more of the arguments, I want the desiderata: what would a theory of what's wrong with a *sexed default* (the unmarked term doing double duty as human being and as adult male) have to deliver to be good enough to put in a style guide — one a copyeditor can apply without knowing the author's intent, and whose rationale survives an angry member complaining?

Cases and distinctions I want each requirement to be anchored to:

- Hofstadter's 1985 satire swaps the axes: the narrator solemnly defends a raced generic white and its pronouns against reformers who want person instead, and the joke is that most readers find the raced version revolting and the sexed original merely annoying. A theory has to say what that asymmetry in our reactions is evidence of, and what happens to the argument if the asymmetry fades.
- The reversal problem: by symmetry you could write a Person Paper for just about any default, including the reformed one, since the replacement also has a specific use. So what makes a reversal probative? If the honest answer is nothing, say so and drop the test.
- The subordinate-default case: imagine a community where the generic human word is historically the word for the subordinate or colonized group, while the dominant group's word stays specific. Does the usual story still predict a complaint? This should separate theories whose real target is default encodes the dominant group from ones whose target is something about ambiguity or typicality.
- Keep apart: harm to hearers, effects on what hearers picture or assume, what the speaker's choice expresses, and any claim that the generic is just false; and don't collapse is the term objectionable with how objectionable.
- Say whether the complaint is hostage to psychology: if the measured effects for some term came out null, is the objection gone? What would falsify it?
- The fossil cases, where the element arguably isn't the generic noun anymore — manhole, to man a booth, no man's land. Rule by etymology, by present-day decomposition, or by what hearers associate?

Also: mark which demands are genuine desiderata and which are one camp's prejudices (the it's uglier objection, the it's-only-etymology move, the treadmill worry that every replacement eventually decays). Flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — my guesses are the bright-line-rule requirement against sensitivity to each term's actual associative profile, and explaining the raced/sexed asymmetry against wrongness not just tracking who is currently offended. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts. Then a couple of sentences each on the etymology defence, the generic-really-is-generic semantics, the typical-member implicature story, the causal-psychology version, and the expressive-harm one — keep the weight on the criteria, I'll do the arguing. Finally, say how confident you are in each part and single out the bit you're least sure of.
