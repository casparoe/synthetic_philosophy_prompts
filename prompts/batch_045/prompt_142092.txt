Everything I've read on hinges is obsessed, understandably, with doubt: the sceptic, Moore's hands, the guy who says he's been to the moon, whether a hinge can be doubted or whether doubting it is unintelligible, whether hinges are propositions at all. But that's not how hinge-looking commitments actually seem to break down in real life. Very often nothing that looks like doubt happens at all. What happens is slower: a proposition that was doing hinge work — nobody checked it, nobody could have checked it, everything else in the practice was measured against it — gradually stops doing that work and starts getting treated like an ordinary empirical claim, and some other proposition quietly inherits its job. I want to call this hinge demotion, with hinge succession on the other side of it. Three cases I keep chewing on: a lab where the calibration standard for an instrument slowly loses credibility, not because anyone formulates a doubt about it, but because results stop lining up and people start quoting the standard's uncertainty in their methods sections; a person in a long conversation who, without ever thinking "wait, are we even talking about the same thing?", starts re-asking what the other person means by the key term; and a family that finds out some of their old letters were forgeries, after which the family's records get checked instead of trusted. The question I want stated precisely is something like: can a commitment lose hinge status while nobody ever doubts it, and is that transition the kind of thing that can be rational or irrational — or is it invisible to epistemic evaluation from the inside and only describable after the fact, from within the successor practice?
