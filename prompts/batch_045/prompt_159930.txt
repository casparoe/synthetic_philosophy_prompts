My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening on the skeptical paradox about rule-following, and we ended up somewhere I am fairly confident is wrong but cannot locate precisely. I would be grateful for a diagnosis, and I would like the answer written in American English.

Here is the argument as we left it.

(1) However long I go on, the sums I have actually performed form a finite list. Any finite list of input-output pairs is compatible with infinitely many distinct rules that agree on everything in the list and diverge afterward. Take one of them: my friend calls it plush, and it returns x + y whenever both addends are below 10^60, and x + y + 1 otherwise. Nothing in my performance history rules out plush.

(2) Replacing my actual performance with my dispositions does not close the gap either. I am finite: there are pairs of numbers large enough that I would die, lose patience, or run out of paper before answering, and for any total pattern of answers someone can write a rule that matches all my actual and counterfactual responses and diverges beyond them. Nor does it help to idealize and ask what I would say with unlimited memory, unlimited time, and unbroken attention, since the words ideal, would, and the same rule are themselves finite in their use, and getting them right is exactly what is in question.

(3) There is nothing else nearby to do the work. My past meaning is not fixed by some abstract object I somehow latch onto, since latching onto it would itself be a case of going on correctly. Nor is it fixed by an irreducibly normative fact I cannot state, since a fact I could never in principle recognize or articulate is not a plausible candidate for what I meant by a word I use openly, in public, and with confidence.

(4) So there is no fact of the matter about whether, in the past, I meant addition or plush, or any of the rest.

(5) Nothing in (1) through (3) depends on the details of addition. The reasoning used only that a rule has infinitely many cases, that my contact with it is finite, and that whatever fixes meaning must be something I could in principle make manifest. Those three features are shared by every expression I use, including not, and, if-then, means, follows from, and correct.

(6) So there is no fact of the matter about which rule I follow in using if-then, and therefore no fact about whether modus ponens is the inference I am obliged to draw, or about whether I am drawing it at this moment.

(7) But (1) through (6) is offered as an argument. It claims that anyone who grants (1) through (3) is committed to (4) and to (6), whether they like it or not. Commitment, validity, and obligation are exactly the normative, rule-governed notions that (6) says there is no fact about. So the argument, taken at its word, saws off the branch it sits on: there is no fact that it is valid, no fact about what its conclusion states, and no fact that (4) is true, including the claim that there are no meaning-facts. The upshot is not skepticism about meaning but the dissolution of the skeptic's own claim to have established anything, and of my claim to have followed it.

(8) My friend is content to stop at (7). He says this is the point of the whole exercise, that the paradox is to be dissolved rather than solved, and that asking which premise to reject is precisely the kind of question the exercise is meant to quiet. I think that is a way of changing the subject, but I cannot say why, and I also cannot point to the step that breaks.

What I want from you:

First, name the step you would reject, and explain why it looks unimpeachable right up to the moment it is questioned. I do not want to be told merely that the conclusion is unacceptable.

Second, price the exits I can see. What does it cost to reject (3) by positing meaning-facts of the sort the skeptic says cannot exist? What does it cost to accept (4) and reinterpret our talk about meaning as having assertibility conditions rather than truth conditions? What does it cost to reject (2) by making counterfactual dispositions do more work than any finite creature's dispositions plausibly can? And what does it cost to stop at (7) in the quietist way my friend prefers?

Third, and this is where I suspect the real difficulty is: I would rather keep (1), (2), and (4) than (5). I can live with it being indeterminate whether I meant addition or plush, provided the argument itself remains determinate. Is (6) genuinely separable from (4), given that (4) is stated using or, not, and no fact of the matter? If someone rejects (5), what principled distinction are they drawing between a rule like addition, where plush seems to be a real rival, and a rule like if-then, where a deviant candidate seems somehow not to be a rival at all? Can that distinction be stated without appealing to the very notion of correct use that the skeptic has called into question?

Fourth, suppose I get the distinction I am asking for in (5). Tell me what it predicts about a case my friend raised and I could not answer: a predicate like green, where the deviant rival agrees with my usage on every object I have encountered and diverges on objects I have not, versus a connective governed by introduction and elimination rules that determine its meaning entirely. Does the skeptic's argument have the same force in both cases, or does the distinction I am reaching for mark a real difference in kind rather than a difference in how much I care about the answer?
