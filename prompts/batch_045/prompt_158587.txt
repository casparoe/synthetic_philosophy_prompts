Hey — I'm a novelist rather than anything else, so apologies in advance if this is all a bit wooly. I'm about a third of the way into a novel set in a port city in the delta of a big river empire, in roughly its last couple of decades as a provincial capital. My narrator is a woman who keeps the ledger for a grain warehouse: she's literate, she's bored a lot of the time, she notices the garrison getting paid late, she notices the tax receipts quietly getting renamed, she notices a drought dragging the harvest a fortnight earlier every year. Her city is one that, in the textbook version, "collapsed." I don't want to write a morality tale about hubris, and I equally don't want to write the cosy revisionist version where everyone sensibly downsized and nothing bad happened to anybody. I want to write what it would actually have been like to be inside it — and I keep hitting the same wall, which is that I can't tell whether collapse is a thing that happened to her world or a thing that happened to the record of her world two thousand years later.

Next month I'm recording an episode of a small interview series I do (mostly me and other novelists, but I've started draging in specialists) and my guest is an archaeologist — call her a Mediterranean prehistorian by training who has spent the last decade arguing something like: what we call collapse is a change in the scale and cost of administration, and the people are mostly still there doing more or less what they did, they've just stopped paying for palaces. She has a line she uses constantly, "nothing collapsed, the bookkeeping stopped," and I can already tell she's going to use it to dodge everything I throw at her. I also have two pet distinctions of my own that I want tested, which you should feel free to use or tear apart: the "ledger criterion" (a polity is finished when nobody is writing the same things down for the same reasons anymore — and I mean that as a definition, not as a symptom) and the "ninety-year rule" (whatever happened took longer than one working life, so no character in my book could ever experience it as an event at all).

So: write me nine or ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to actually give. I want at least one on the Bronze Age business — was that one thing with one trigger, or a regional pattern with a dozen local causes that we've stitched together mostly because our chronology is too coarse to tell the difference? One on the Maya, where the people, the language and the northern cities all plainly kept going: what work is "collapse" still doing in that case, and for whom? One on Easter Island and the ecocide story — if the revised chronology is right and the settlement was later and the forest went fast, does the moral of that story survive, or does it turn into something closer to bad luck plus rats? At least one that forces her to say whose welfare is being measured: when the monuments stop and the trade routes shift and the skeletons get shorter or healthier, which of those is the collapse, and why privilege complexity over bodies? And one where I hand her my two homemade heuristics and make her break them.

Last thing, and it's the part I care most about: don't hedge all of this into mush. Commit. Pick the version of "collapse" you actually think is defensible and write the questions as though you're trying to get her to concede it — then, after each question, give me a single line on what answer from her would make you abandon that version, and flag the one question where you expect her to get evasive, with a note on how you'd push past the "that's just a definitional dispute" move. If you think one of my premises is simply wrong (the ninety-year rule, or the idea that a novelist should care at all about the difference between failure and transformation), put that into a question too rather than smoothing it over.
