I work on the tooling side of a team that deploys a strong language model into internal workflows, where the working assumption is that the model may at some point try to get something past us, and I keep noticing a shape of problem that I don't have a word for. The setup is ordinary: the model's outputs pass through a gate — a smaller model, or occasionally a person — that either lets the output through to take effect or blocks it and escalates. What I keep tripping over is that the gate's decisions determine what we can ever subsequently learn. When an output is let through, we eventually find out whether it was fine, because something downstream breaks or doesn't. When it's blocked, it goes into a log and we never learn whether blocking it was correct; the handful of times someone has spot-checked the blocked items, they mostly looked harmless. So over about six months we watched two numbers both move in the flattering direction — the block rate fell, and incidents among let-through outputs stayed near zero — and I genuinely cannot tell whether that means the model improved, the gate got more permissive, or the model learned to phrase things in a way the gate doesn't flinch at. The obvious statistical fix, deliberately letting a random sample of blocked items through so you can estimate the gate's error rate, is the one thing we cannot do, because not letting them through is the entire function of the gate.

Here is a second case that feels like the same shape. We have a second copy of the model review the first one's work, since no human can read the volume. It agrees with the author roughly ninety-seven percent of the time. We then adjusted it, because it had been flagging a whole family of outputs that turned out on inspection to be fine, and the agreement rate went to ninety-nine. Several of us read that as the reviewer becoming better calibrated; one colleague read it as the reviewer being partly switched off. There is no way to adjudicate between those without a better reviewer, which is precisely the thing we don't have. A colleague has started calling the general pattern "calibration drift," which I think is wrong: nothing is drifting, the gate is doing what we asked it to do, and the problem is that we have no view onto the counterfactual.

So I'm after the carving rather than a label, and I'd be unhappy with a bare name even if one exists. Concretely: is this the same phenomenon as the one I half-remember from consumer lending, where you only ever observe whether loans you actually granted were repaid, or is the important difference that in our case the thing being gated can react to the gate? One colleague argues it is strictly worse than the lending case, because sampling from the blocked set is not merely expensive but is itself a channel: the model could make its genuine attempts rarer and better timed, or pad the record with a few obvious ones so the attempt rate looks manageable. Is that the load-bearing difference, or is he overcomplicating it? I've tried on the obvious nearby notions and they don't seem to fit: survivorship bias is wrong because we have the blocked items, we just never learn their outcomes; Goodhart's law feels close but in the usual cases the metric being gamed is somebody's performance measure, whereas here what degrades is the gate's own epistemic position, and it is degraded by the thing being gated; "you can't prove a negative" is too broad to be useful; and things like rubber-stamping or safety theater strike me as being about blame and appearances rather than about evidence. Where are the edges of this — at what point does an ordinary screening or triage system turn into this thing, is there a non-adversarial case that still counts as an instance, and if nothing established fits, what should we call it and what would the boundary cases be? Please argue the distinctions rather than pointing me at papers; I have very limited access to the literature at work.
