I'm on my university's biosafety committee. Among other things we rule on whether a construct counts as a living organism for our containment purposes: whether it needs a validated kill step before anything goes down the drain or out of the building, whether it can leave the containment suite at all, that kind of thing. Over a few years we've built up this track record, with the one-line reasons people actually gave at the time:

1. Wild-type Bacillus in shaken culture — living. Nobody argued about it.
2. Bacillus endospores, dried down, no detectable metabolism, germinate fine when you add water decades later — living. "It's dormant, not dead."
3. Tardigrades in the cryptobiotic tun state — living. Same reasoning as 2.
4. An attenuated vaccine-strain virus: infects cells, uses the host's ribosomes and polymerases, cannot replicate outside a host — NOT a living organism. "It borrows somebody else's replication machinery."
5. A prion prep: propagates its misfolded conformation, no nucleic acid, no metabolism of its own — not living.
6. A minimal synthetic-genome cell — genome chemically synthesized rather than inherited, pared down to a few hundred genes, some of them of unknown function, grows and divides fine in broth on its own — living. People explicitly said the printed-genome part didn't matter next to the fact that it feeds itself and divides.
7. A cell-free expression system: purified ribosomes, polymerases, tRNAs and an energy-regenerating enzyme set, wrapped in a lipid vesicle, churning out protein off a DNA template we hand it, kept running indefinitely by feeding — NOT living. Reason given: "it never makes another one of itself, and we have to keep reconstituting the enzymes for it."
8. A self-replicating program in a sandbox with a simulated energy budget — a digital-organism setup that evolved all kinds of tricks over a few hundred thousand generations — NOT living. "Not a chemical system, no metabolism." One member dissented and wanted it to count; he was voted down 4-1.

Here's what we recieved last week. The Marsden lab has a vesicle line they call AC-30: a fatty-acid vesicle containing a mutually catalytic network of about thirty short peptides and small metabolites. It takes up fatty acids and amino acid / short-peptide precursors from the medium, assembles its own membrane (it does not synthesize fatty acids from scratch), and past a critical size it divides by shear, with daughters getting a roughly similar but noticeably not identical set of catalyst concentrations. Growth rate varies between lines and is partly heritable. The wrinkles that matter to me:

- Three of the thirty catalysts have to be supplied pre-formed; the network can't make them. Marsden's framing is that this is like essential amino acids for a mammal.
- There are no nucleic acids anywhere in it. No sequence-based information at all; heredity is purely compositional, and pretty noisy.
- They didn't design it. They got it by ~400 rounds of serial transfer — dilute, regrow, keep the fastest dividers. Growth rate improved maybe threefold early on and then plateaued; no new catalytic function ever showed up. So it does have a selection history, just one that bottoms out in a bottle of chemicals rather than in cells.
- It freezes indefinitely and restarts, and there's no senescence: it either gets diluted out or it just keeps going.
