Write a joke about the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons — the difficulty of saying how well off, or how much better off, one person is than another. It should land for someone who actually works on this: the punchline has to turn on the real structure of the problem rather than on a generic gag about economists or a pun on the word "util." The real structure, for the purposes of the joke: ordinal utility representations carry no information about magnitudes at all; even where a person has a cardinal utility function, that function is pinned down only up to a positive affine transformation, so comparisons of *levels* and of *differences* across two people are not determined by the two functions themselves; and "how much this person gains from a change" is a different question from "how well off this person ends up." A reader who knows the topic should be able to point to the specific feature the joke is exploiting.

Set the joke inside an institution you invent: a government office (name it, give it a form, give it a tone) that has announced it has "solved" interpersonal comparison by imposing a normalization convention on every citizen's utility scale. The convention must be a real normalization and nothing more — it fixes an origin and a unit for each person (say, u = 0 at one stipulated life-event and u = 1 at another) and then reads off the numbers. Make the arbitrariness of that choice do the comic work: the office issues confident comparative verdicts from numbers that any rescaling would reverse. Include at least one beat where a re-anchoring of somebody's scale flips the office's verdict, and make the reason for the flip legible. One possible route, if you want it: because each person is scaled against their own two anchors, whoever has the most trivial, narrow, low-expectation anchors reports gigantic numbers for ordinary good fortune, and ends up officially declared the happiest citizen in the country. Invent the specifics yourself; please avoid resting the joke on the well-worn textbook illustrations (the utility monster, three economists in a bar).

Keep the joke self-contained — no citations, no reading suggestions, no name-checking of the literature. Afterward, add a short note, a few sentences and no more than one paragraph, on where the joke takes liberties, and keep two things visibly separate in it: what is settled among people who know this topic, and what is genuinely contested. Claim as settled only what really is settled — that ordinal utility licenses no interpersonal comparisons, and that an individual's cardinal scale is fixed only up to positive affine transformation. On the contested side, flag disagreement rather than declaring a consensus: whether some non-arbitrary convention, or some empirical measure of welfare, could make interpersonal comparisons determinate after all, and whether the residual difficulty is merely epistemic or whether there is no fact of the matter to be had. Do not let the joke's premise quietly imply that the contested half is settled.
