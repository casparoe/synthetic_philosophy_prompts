I am writing a case study for a museum's collections committee — a real committee with a real decision to make — and I need one purpose-built scenario rather than the standard puzzle. Please construct it to the following specifications.

The scenario. One paragraph, under 250 words. A historic vessel in an institution's care; a decision that must be made on a specific date and that has money or legal status attached (a bequest, an insurance valuation, a heritage listing). Realistic throughout: no duplication of matter, no teleportation, no science fiction, and at most one ship-shaped, functioning vessel in the story at any one time. In particular, do not use the familiar variant in which the discarded planks are reassembled into a rival claimant; I want the disagreement to arise without a second vessel on the water.

Three accounts must return verdicts. (1) A continuity-of-role account: the vessel persists so long as something continuously occupies its role — maintained, registered, sailed, treated as it, under its name. (2) A matter-tracking account, but not the indiscriminate version on which any change of parts destroys the object: state the threshold you are using (a proportion of original fabric? the structurally essential members such as keel and frames?), say why it is not arbitrary, and locate the date on which the vessel crossed it. (3) A four-dimensionalist or conventionalist account, on which there are many candidate sums of stages and the question of which one the name attaches to is settled, if at all, by convention or interest.

Hold the physics fixed. The three verdicts must concern one and the same object at one and the same time. Nothing physical may change at the moment the conflict surfaces: it must emerge from an audit, a dendrochronology report, a reclassification, or a lost record being found. I want to know whether the matter-tracker's verdict still bites when the discovery is purely epistemic, or whether the account quietly reduces to the continuity-of-role account once no intervention has occurred.

Then, for each of the three accounts: give the verdict and its reasoning in two or three sentences; state when, if ever, the original vessel ceased to exist on that account; say what that account implies about the decades of maintenance, registration, ceremony and public narrative carried out in its name; and say what the committee should actually do on that account, and why.

Finally, an honest accounting: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what the case degenerates into if that feature is removed (for instance, if the threshold is crossed in a single visible ceremonial night, or if it turns out never to have been crossed); which of the three views comes off worst here and what its defender would say to defuse the case; and whether appeals to the vagueness of "ship", or to the question's being merely verbal, are genuine solutions or a change of subject, given that the committee must answer yes or no.
