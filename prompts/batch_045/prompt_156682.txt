In the federal labor law of the United States, a workplace becomes unionized when a majority of votes cast in a secret-ballot election within a defined bargaining unit go to a union; that union then becomes the exclusive representative of everyone in the unit, including those who voted against it — it signs the contract that binds them all and owes all of them a duty of fair representation. In the many states that have right-to-work statutes, however, the dissenters it represents cannot be required to pay it anything, though it must still represent them and they still receive the contract's terms. Separately: workers who strike over wages may be permanently replaced — they keep employee status and a claim on future vacancies, but the job they walked out of is gone — while workers who strike to protest an unfair labor practice by their employer may not be; and a union may bring economic pressure on its own employer but, with narrow exceptions, not on neutral third parties who merely do business with it.

The package looks inconsistent. In some places it treats concerted action as a matter of individual consent and fault: no compelled dues, no pressure on parties who bear no responsibility for the dispute, no lasting loss of the job when the employer is the wrongdoer. In others it treats it as a matter of collective status: a bare majority binds dissenters, a contract binds non-signers, representation is something you receive whether or not you asked for it.

What single theory of association, coercion, and collective agency, if any, does this package presuppose? Is there one view — pluralist, civic-republican, or broadly liberal — under which all its features fit together, or is the law an unstable compromise between two incompatible views of what a workplace association is? Distinguish what each feature strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the doctrine is underdetermined and several theories would license it. In particular: what does permitting permanent replacement presuppose — that a strike is properly a trial of the parties' relative economic endurance, whose outcome is legitimately settled by who can hold out longer, so that calling striking a right rather than a bare liberty is close to empty? Or can a right to strike survive being this costly to exercise?

Then test your reconstruction against a variant: a regime of proportional, continuously revisable representation, in which several unions may be recognized at one workplace, each bargaining only for its current members, whom it gains and loses month by month, with no majority decision binding anyone who has not joined. Under that regime, would the replacement rule and the ban on secondary pressure have to change, or could they stand as they are? Which half of the package is doing the real philosophical work?
