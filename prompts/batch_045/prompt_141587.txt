I'm a PhD student in philosophy, writing on the naturalization of mental content, and over the last year I've moved — initially against my inclination — to a broadly selected-effects (teleosemantic) view. As I hold it: the content of a representational state is fixed by what the mechanism that produces it was selected to do, with natural selection supplying the history for innate mechanisms and something selection-shaped supplying it for acquired ones; misrepresentation is then the gap between the condition the mechanism has the function of tracking and the condition it actually responds to on an occasion. I hold this as a general account of what it is for anything to have content, not as an account of one favoured class of states.

I want a map of the view's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, and what would merely be awkward for it. Please assume I know the well-known objections and don't just enumerate them; instead, take the following as the cases I can't get out of my head, and use them to say what the view risks.

First, selection does not care about truth. Consider a mechanism retained because it is cheap to fire and the cost of a miss vastly exceeds the cost of a false alarm, where the condition that matters to fitness is a gerrymandered disjunction of distal circumstances with no ecological unity. Does the view then have to assign a content like fire-or-shadow-or-dust, and if so is that a cost or just an unexciting consequence? That generalizes: whenever selection is indifferent between a set of candidate contents, is there any principled way to select one, or does the view inherit an indeterminacy it cannot discharge?

Second, function-switching. Take a mechanism selected for one job, later redeployed, and then selected again for a second job that partly contradicts the first — say a structure whose original function was to track overall luminance for entrainment and whose later, currently selected function is to track spatial layout, with responses to bare luminance change now actively suppressed. Which selection episode confers content? Is there one content, two, or a content that varies with which historical episode you cite? And if the answer is that the view just gets messier rather than wrong, I want to know why that isn't itself the admission that selection history underdetermines content.

Third, and what I find hardest: systems with no selection history in any interesting sense. Here is my own case. Suppose engineers produce a network by a single unrefined random draw over parameters that happens, by luck, to land on a configuration that performs excellently, generalizes, and whose internal units carry information that downstream components exploit. There is no winnowing of variants, no differential retention, no refinement — nothing selection-shaped at all. It seems to me, pre-theoretically, that the units have content; the view has nothing to point at. Now contrast ordinary gradient-descent training, which does look selection-shaped: variation, a criterion, retention. If I extend the account to cover it, I seem committed to saying that any optimizing process confers content, which is either a welcome unification or a slide into vacuity. I want to know which, and how to tell the difference in advance.

Fourth, scope. The view was built largely for simple indicator states. Does it have anything to say about the content of thought, as opposed to perception, and has the standard compositional move — build propositional content out of simpler representational parts — ever actually been carried through, or is it promissory? If it is promissory, how much of the theory's claimed generality is left?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender should concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as resting on a rival's assumptions. Say which empirical findings would be decisive — for instance, if it were established that the mechanisms producing our perceptual states are mostly products of drift and structural constraint rather than selection, or that redeployment and partial re-selection are the rule rather than the exception — and which would leave the view untouched. Tell me what the view predicts that informational, functional-role, interpretivist, or structural-representational rivals do not. Finally, assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: it increasingly looks like a schema, fill in your favourite selection process, and I want to know both whether that is true and whether, for a theory with ambitions this general, it is a defect or simply the appropriate form of the answer.

I should say where I stand, since it affects what would move me: I have never been persuaded by swampman-style intuitions, and I am not looking for a knockdown. What I find genuinely worrying is the conjunction of selection's indifference to truth and the indeterminacy that seems to follow from it. If you think I am wrong about which defeater is most serious, argue for that.
