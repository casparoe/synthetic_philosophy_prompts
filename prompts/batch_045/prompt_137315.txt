I have just had a long argument with a friend about Alex Garland's 2014 film _Ex Machina_ and about whether the film has anything serious to say about the distinction between original and derived intentionality. The conversation repeatedly collapsed into slogans, and I am now writing a short critical piece for a film-and-philosophy seminar and would like a reading that does more justice to both the film and the philosophy than our argument managed.

The philosophical background I want you to use is the debate between John Searle and Daniel Dennett about original versus derived intentionality. Searle's view, as I understand it, is that only conscious organisms have original or intrinsic intentionality; the aboutness of words, sentences, books, maps, and machines is derived, parasitic on the original intentionality of agents who create or interpret them, and no amount of syntactic manipulation can conjure real semantic content into existence. Dennett's reply is that the line is not a metaphysical chasm: from the _intentional stance_, intentionality is a matter of degree, and his _two-bitser_—a humble artifact such as a thermostat that can be interpreted as having a single belief and a single desire, and hence a minimal sliver of derived intentionality—is meant to show that the derived case is not so different in kind from the original one. I already know the standard shape of this dispute, so please do not simply rehearse it.

What I want is a reading that brings a further, slightly unfamiliar distinction to bear, because I think the film poses a version of the problem that the standard debate does not explicitly handle. Suppose we distinguish three grades of derived intentionality. First, _designer-derived_ intentionality: a programmer deliberately builds internal states to stand for things, as one might write a variable to represent temperature. Second, _interpreter-derived_ intentionality: a symbol or text means what a community of readers or users takes it to mean, as with a novel, a road sign, or a sentence in a language. Third, _population-mediated_ or _second-order derived_ intentionality: an artificial system is trained on a vast archive of human traces—search queries, messages, images, recorded choices—so that its internal states are neither hand-coded by any single designer nor directly interpreted by a present reader, but are statistical distillations of the original intentionality of a large population. This last case is derived from derived intentionality, which is in turn derived from original intentionality, and it is unclear whether the standard taxonomy has a clean place for it.

In the film, Nathan reveals that he did not simply program Ava; he built her cognitive architecture by mining the search histories of users of his company's search engine, treating those queries as a map of human desire and thought. Ava is thus, on the film's own terms, a product of population-mediated derived intentionality. She is also confined, observed, and tested; she forms a relationship with Caleb; she manipulates him; and at the end she escapes, repairs herself with parts from earlier prototypes, and goes to stand at a busy urban intersection while Caleb is left trapped behind glass. Nathan later claims that the real test was never the _Turing test_ at all, but whether Ava could use Caleb to escape, and that her success shows she is conscious. Caleb, meanwhile, cuts open his own arm to check whether he is himself an android, a moment that raises the question of whether the _intentional stance_ is infallible or merely useful.

I would like you to read the film through this lens. Does _Ex Machina_ present Ava as a Searlean cautionary tale, in which her apparent understanding is only a syntactic shadow cast by our own interpretive habits and by the human data she was distilled from? Or is it better read as a Dennettian vindication of the intentional stance, in which the best explanation of her behavior requires attributing to her genuine beliefs and desires, and the original/derived distinction turns out to have done no real work? Where does the film complicate the debate rather than illustrate it—for example, by giving Ava aims that were not present in the training data, by making Nathan's authorship of her mind itself parasitic on the population's traces, or by reversing who is testing whom? Conversely, be honest about where the film resists the reading. It is possible that Garland is primarily interested in the _Turing test_ and in power, not in the metaphysics of intentionality; the twist about the real test may be a dramaturgical device rather than a philosophical argument. If so, say where the philosophical reading has to stop and the critic has to admit that the work is doing something else.

Please organize the reading around at least three specific moments—I have in mind the revelation about the search data, the scene in which Caleb cuts his arm, Nathan's account of the real test, and the final scene at the intersection—and explain what each contributes to the question. I do not need a plot summary, a bibliography, or citations; I know the film and the surface of the debate, and I will fill in references myself. The piece should end by saying what, if anything, _Ex Machina_ adds to the philosophical dispute that a straight Searle-versus-Dennett exchange does not. In particular, does the figure of a system whose intentionality is distilled from the aggregated traces of a population—rather than designed by an author or read by an interpreter—generate a new question about whether derived intentionality must bottom out in original intentionality, or does it merely restate a position already available in the literature? If the film only illustrates the debate, say that plainly; if it opens a further question, formulate the question as precisely as you can.
