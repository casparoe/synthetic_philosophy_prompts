I'm in a graduate seminar on scientific representation and have gotten attached, maybe too attached, to a position I've worked up in response to Milton Friedman's 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics—the one that contains the claim Samuelson later ridiculed as the F-twist, namely that a theory's assumptions need not be realistic and that, if anything, the more significant a theory is, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be, because they strip the target system down to the few elements needed for precise prediction. I want to defend a sharpened version of that as-if instrumentalism, which I call de-idealization invariance instrumentalism, or DII for short. The core idea is that a model's known-false assumptions are not merely excusable but can be a methodological virtue when the model's empirical successes satisfy a property I call predictive invariance along de-idealization paths: for every idealization in the model, if you replace it with a more realistic assumption and re-fit the model's parameters, the predictions that made the model successful either stay inside the original predictive tolerance or move only in directions the model already flags as sensitive. On this view Friedman's slogan is not a license to ignore assumption realism; it is a corollary of a deeper test. The unrealistic assumptions do not matter in the good cases because the model can survive losing them, and the F-twist becomes the observation that highly significant theories tend to be those whose predictions are strikingly invariant under de-idealization.

I hold this view, or am close to holding it, and I want to know what would count against it—not a literature survey, and please do not give me citations or reading lists; I can't follow them up. Tell me what would have to be true for DII to be false. I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want you to separate what would be fatal to DII from what would be merely awkward or call for a friendly amendment. In particular, I keep worrying about three things. First, scope: suppose a blatantly idealized epidemic model with homogeneous mixing robustly predicts the timing of the infection peak—that prediction is invariant under every de-idealization anyone tries—but the total case burden shifts wildly once you put in a realistic contact network, in a way the model did not mark as sensitive. Is that a counterexample to DII, or can a defender simply say the model was only ever licensed for its target prediction and that the policy failure that followed was a misuse? Second, what I call path divergence: if different de-idealization paths from the same idealized model lead to incompatible predictions, does that refute the invariance requirement, or does it just show that DII needs a way of weighting which de-idealizations are salient? Third, unfalsifiability: if every time a model fails the test the defender replies that the model was not a good one to begin with, or that the failed quantity was not among its intended predictions, has DII been qualified into emptiness—and if so, is that a genuine problem for a methodological view of this kind, or is it simply the same insulation from refutation that any purely predictive criterion of theory success enjoys?

Let me add what I would personally find most persuasive, since I don't want a merely dialectical exercise. I am less troubled by purely philosophical worries—a regress about whether we can ever enumerate all de-idealization paths, or skepticism about whether predictive tolerance can be made precise—than by concrete cases in which a model had the invariance property for its headline prediction, was then used to guide policy, and produced a systematic failure that the invariance test had not anticipated because the quantity of interest had quietly changed. So please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a committed DII defender should concede as real counts against the view and which they would dismiss as changing the subject or as attacks on a straw man. If the view is in fact well insulated and hard to falsify, say that plainly, and then tell me honestly whether its insulation is a vice for a thesis that is supposed to explain why we are entitled to trust idealized models at all.
