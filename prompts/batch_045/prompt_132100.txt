I'm a novelist, and I've written myself into a corner over a phrase I only half understand. In the manuscript I'm currently revising, there is a long scene set in a games café in Bristol where two characters — a retired mathematics teacher who designs abstract strategy games, and her old pupil, now a machine-learning researcher — argue about who should move first in a game of the kind played on a rhombus of hexagons, where each player tries to link her own two opposite sides. The older woman waves away the choice of who opens with a line I've given her: an extra move can never hurt you, strategy-stealing, so in a game like this the first player cannot lose. Later in the same chapter the researcher uses what sounds like the same phrase for what sounds like a completely different claim — as I understood him, that a system good enough to design a system at all is good enough to design a better one than itself, so that a laboratory which built a machine capable of doing its own research could not keep that machine from improving itself, and the safety case would collapse. I have a third character, a publisher who enjoys winning arguments, ready to borrow the phrase for a claim about going first in life generally. My trouble is that I cannot tell what the phrase could actually mean, so I cannot tell which of them is entitled to it.

For what it is worth, here is the argument as I have managed to reconstruct it from the older character's explanation: suppose the second player had a winning strategy; then the first player could make some throwaway opening move, thereafter ignore it, and play the second player's supposed winning strategy as if she herself were the second player, treating her extra piece as though it were not on the board, and if the strategy ever calls for playing on the square she has already occupied she simply plays somewhere else and reinterprets that square as her throwaway move; so the second player has no winning strategy after all; and since the game is finite and cannot end in a draw, the first player must have a winning strategy. I take it this is meant to be odd, because it apparently tells you that a winning first move exists without telling you what it is.

What I would like is a menu of the distinct precise claims this line of thought could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the wild, with a verdict on each. I suspect some readings are near-platitudes about a free extra option never being worth less than nothing; that one of them is a genuine little result, but only under conditions I can't state — something about the game being symmetric, about an extra piece never being a liability, about games where you are obliged to move on your turn, about chance, hidden information and whether draws are possible; that others are over-generalisations which are straightforwardly false (I have a feeling that in some games going second is better, that acting first in a bargaining or card game gives your intentions away, and that in games where the last player to move loses, an extra move is exactly what you don't want); and that the researcher's version may be an empirical guess wearing the clothes of a proof, since in the game argument the first player steals a strategy that a hypothetical opponent is assumed already to have, whereas in his version there is no opponent and nothing guarantees that the better design exists to be found.

So: write me the menu. For each reading, say in one line what it asserts, whether it is trivial, true but narrow, contentious, or false, and above all whether it would actually do the work my characters need of it — whether it really supports playing first with confidence, whether it really supports the claim about self-improving systems, and whether it really supports the publisher's broader moral. Where a character needs a reading you think is false, tell me what true nearby claim I could put into her mouth instead so that the scene still does its job without the character sounding foolish to anyone who knows the subject. A numbered list I can annotate in the margin would be ideal, and please say plainly where my reconstruction above goes wrong, if it does.
