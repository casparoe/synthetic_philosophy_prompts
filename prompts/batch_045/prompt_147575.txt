I sit, as one of two emeritus members, on the Graduate Council of the university where I spent some thirty years in X‑ray crystallography. In retirement I have been working slowly through Plato with a small reading group on Tuesday mornings, and I have caught myself more than once arguing in council in a vocabulary I would have waved away a decade ago. So I would like this written for the whole council — two of whom will visibly switch off at anything that sounds like philosophy — and not for the reading group.

The case we vote on next Friday is a doctorate. The candidate (please keep her and the material anonymous in anything you write: call her "the candidate" and the substances "the oxide family") built an automated search over a large space of candidate compositions, using a third‑party surrogate model whose internal weights are sealed by licence, and it ranked a previously unsought family of oxides as promising for a catalytic property the group cares about. Twenty‑three of the thirty top‑ranked candidates have since been made and measured by two independent laboratories, and twenty‑one of them do what was predicted. Nobody, including the candidate, can say why the ranking works: her account in the dissertation amounts to the pipeline, the training data, the threshold she chose, and then the outcomes. Since submitting, she has used the same pipeline to generate forty further predictions, of which she has had the time to test eight; six held up. Her two examiners have split: one calls the result the most important thing out of that group in a decade and recommends the degree without qualification; the other writes that we would be certifying an oracle rather than a scientist. Our chair adds, privately, that if we hesitate she will simply take an industry post and the university will have neither the credit nor the student.

What makes it a hard vote for me is our own statute. Section 4(b) contains what everyone on council calls the carriage clause: a dissertation must report a result the candidate is able to carry a competent examiner to by reasoning, rather than by pointing at an output. The candidate plainly cannot do that. But one of our members has proposed that we treat the clause as satisfied by what she calls a sensitivity test — can the candidate say, in rough terms, what would have had to be different for the result not to hold, and how she would have found out? — and a friend in my reading group, when I described the case, said it was only the old question about why knowledge is worth more than true belief, given that a man with a true belief about the road to Larissa will get you there as reliably as one who knows the way. I suspect he is both right and unhelpful, and I would like the memo to say what, if anything, that consideration has to do with our vote, whether the sensitivity test is a defensible reading of what we certify or a quiet way of repealing the clause, and whether it matters that the candidate's inability to explain is in part a consequence of a commercial licence rather than of anything she did. I genuinely do not know which way I shall vote.

Please draft it as a memo I can circulate under my own name, around 900 words, with no footnotes, references or reading suggestions — the council will not chase them up, and I would rather have the argument in plain terms. Use these sections in this order: "Recommendation" (about 100 words, with the operative decision and any deadline stated so the chair can put it to a vote); "Facts" (about 120 words); "What the carriage clause is for" (about 150 words); "Why a reliable route to true results does not by itself satisfy it" (about 200 words); "The strongest case for the side you are not recommending" (about 200 words — I do not mind which way you come down, but I need the losing argument in a form its friends would recognise, not as a straw man); and "Conditions, timeline, and what this vote would not settle" (about 130 words).
