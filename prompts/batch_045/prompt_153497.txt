Right, imagine I’m trying to get my head round Berkeley’s take on money by running it against the South Sea Bubble, roughly as it happened in 1720. The version of Berkeley I have in mind is the economic one from his 1730s set of queries, The Querist: he argues that gold and silver aren’t the real wealth of a country, the real wealth is the industry of its people, and money is best thought of as a ticket or counter that sets that industry in motion and lets its holder draw on the labour of others, rather than as wealth in itself. I’ll give the Bubble facts so you don’t need to recall them: the South Sea Company offered to take over a large slice of Britain’s national debt in return for trading privileges, its shares then rose enormously on optimistic expectations and on easy credit, a lot of government annuitants swapped their annuities for stock, subscribers bought shares with borrowed money, and later in 1720 the price collapsed in a general financial panic that ruined many investors.

What I want is a careful mapping of the episode through that Berkeleyan lens, then a look back at the theory. First, recast the players: once money is a ticket for industry rather than a commodity, who counts as a player? Say what the Company, the government, the annuitants, the credit-financed subscribers, and the wider population of labourers and projectors are each really doing in terms of industry and tickets. Then the options and payoffs: for an annuitant, swapping a stream of tax-backed payments for a share in a monopoly; for a subscriber, buying stock with a small down payment and borrowed rest; for the Company, issuing stock to retire public debt. What does each option look like as a move to command real industry, and which of them are really just trades in tickets?

A key bit: the conversion of government annuities into South Sea stock turns one kind of claim on future taxes into another kind of claim on expected trade profits. Say what occured to the distinction between a public credit instrument and a trading company’s paper, and whether that collapse matters for Berkeley, who wants to know whether the tickets answer to any industry at all. Also, the Bubble ran on stock-jobbing and buying on credit. If money is meant to be a neutral counter that circulates industry, what happens when the counters themselves are sold, pledged and speculated on? My guess is that this is where Berkeley can say society has started treating signs as if they were the wealth they stand for, but spell out exactly how, and say whether ordinary trade in tickets already does this so the Bubble is only a matter of degree, or whether something qualitatively different happens when tickets are detached from any determinate industry.

Then flip it round: what does the Bubble show about Berkeley’s own theory, given that in The Querist he goes on to propose a national bank that would issue paper credit to mobilise idle labour in Ireland? Isn’t that also multiplying tickets on the basis of expected industry rather than realised industry? Work out whether his bank is supposed to differ in kind from the South Sea scheme — perhaps because it lends against real undertakings, or is tied to land and labour, or isn’t a monopoly trading privilege — and say whether that difference is really available to him, or whether the Bubble exposes a seperate gap he needs to fill. Be honest about where the theory is silent: I don’t think a ticket theory of money by itself explains why people fell for the hype or why the crash fed on itself, so say if that sits outside the theory and what would have to be added, without pretending Berkeley has a full account. Finally, a short verdict: does analysing 1720 through Berkeley make the Bubble look like a case of a community collectively mistaking counters for industry, or does it instead make his later paper-credit scheme look more South-Sea-like than he’d like? And if some more standard credit-cycle story does a better job on the mechanics, say so in a sentence or two, but keep the main answer in Berkeleyan terms.
