Two people in my causation reading group have been going at each other for a whole semester — one is a die-hard Lewis-style counterfactual theorist (causation is counterfactual dependence, chained appropriately, evaluated at suitably close worlds), the other is a Woodward-style interventionist (X causes Y iff there are possible interventions on X such that Y changes, with the generalization holding invariant under other interventions). They agreed to do an adversarial collaboration instead of another round of the same argument, and I somehow ended up tasked with drafting the document. I'm out of my depth, so please write it.

What I want is the document they could both put their names on: a numbered section of agreed claims first (claims both would sign — about the formalism they share, about what a theory of causation is supposed to do, about which stock cases are settled and why), then a disagreements table. Each row should have: the disputed claim phrased in wording each side would accept for its own view, the crux it turns on, and — this is the part they kept skipping — a concrete case or argument both agree would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. No citations needed, just the substance.

Four things I need you to actually work through rather than gesture at:

1. The circularity complaint. An intervention is standardly defined as a cause of X that affects Y only via X, which looks like it uses causal notions to define causation. The interventionist's reply is something about the definition being a fixpoint, or about the causal notion doing work in the definition being much thinner than the one being defined. Get both versions precise enough that the other person would at least nod.

2. My sealed-chamber case (I made it up, it's not from anything): a chamber is assembled with a partition welded at position P, total energy E0 fixed at assembly, and a pressure gauge reading Q. The dynamics are not in dispute, and both camps agree that if P had been at a different value with the laws intact, Q would have differed. But there is no physically possible operation on the chamber that sets P without also pumping energy in or out or disturbing the gas directly — so no intervention on P in the interventionist's sense, only a closest-worlds verdict that has to rank a law-violating world as closest. Is this a flat disagreement, or does it show that one account can express something the other structurally can't? Also write down what each side predicts about how working physicists would actually talk about P and Q in that setup.

3. My self-indexing-outcome case (also invented): outcome variables like "excess deaths this week" or "the plant's output shortfall relative to what it would have produced" are partly defined by a counterfactual baseline. Can the interventionist even formulate "X caused the excess" without helping itself to the counterfactuals the other side puts at the center? And can the counterfactual side say anything non-arbitrary about which baseline is the right one? If this part is just a fight about words, say so and say exactly which part.

4. Traces and backtracking. Does the interventionist actually earn the no-backtracking rule, or does the counterfactual theorist's similarity metric do independent work that the intervention account has no equivalent for?

Finally: flag any place where you think they're defintely talking past each other, and flag at least one spot where you think both of them are wrong or are missing a third option. Keep the agreed-claims section genuinely neutral — no sneaking one side's framing in.
