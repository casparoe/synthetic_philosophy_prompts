I run a little monthly reading group — three of us, plus whoever wanders in — and two members have now spent something like a year and a half going round in circles about weakness of will, so I want to force them into an adversarial collaboration and I need the document they should have written by now. One of them (the "temporal" one) thinks the whole topic is basically mislabelled preference reversal: what we call akrasia is a smaller-sooner reward crossing over a larger-later one as it looms, which is exactly what you'd expect from a discount curve with a present-bias parameter, so the only remaining questions are which time-slice's ranking is the normative one and what commitment devices are actually available. The other (the "agential" one) thinks that's a bait and switch: on her view the phenomenon that matters is synchronic — at the very moment of acting the agent still judges A better and does B — and a discounting story can't get any grip there, because it needs the judgment itself to have flipped, and in the case she cares about it definately hasn't. I suspect part of their dispute is verbal (whether "weakness of will" names a diachronic or a synchronic thing, whether it's even one phenomenon, whether "self-control" or "willpower" tracks anything unified) and part of it is real, and I want the document to say which is which.

What I want from you: something both could put their names on. Start with a numbered section of agreed claims — about what the ordinary phenomenon is, including the folk vocabulary of willpower and resolve; about what a hyperbolic / present-bias model does and doesn't explain; about how much the lab work on delay discounting is even evidence about this rather than about a nearby but different thing; about whether the multiple-selves and commitment-device way of talking settles anything normative or just relocates it. Then a disagreements table: each row one disputed claim, with the temporal side's formulation and the agential side's formulation, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording as fair rather than as a strawman, plus a note on whether the row is substantive or only about words. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case, argument, or experiment both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two things to build in. First, a case my group hatched that neither of their accounts handles cleanly and that I think is a genuine crux: the agent hasn't changed her judgment, hasn't revised or abandoned her intention, and isn't pulled by anything temporally nearer — she just doesn't start. She sits there, still wanting to do the thing she decided to do, correctly believing she should do it now, and the action doesn't get initiated; when it finally happens it's after some irrelevant trigger, like someone walking into the room. We started calling it stuck akrasia. Second, I'd like at least one crux to be an empirical one you design yourself — I was thinking of experience sampling during real temptation episodes, with people reporting in real time both what they're doing and their current all-things-considered judgment, plus a condition where the tempting option isn't sooner at all, just more available or more cue-salient (both options starting at the same moment) — but use a better design if you've got one, and say what each of them should predict about it and what result would genuinely surprise them.

Don't adjudicate — the point is that both names could go on it, not that one of them wins. At the end add a short drafter's note: say how confident you are that each side would really sign its own row, and single out the one formulation you're least sure about, the one you think somebody would bounce straight back at you.
