I teach an MA seminar on Explanation in History and the Social Sciences, and I am writing this year's final paper. I need one essay question on path dependence, worth 25 marks and answerable in sixty minutes under closed-book conditions. I'd like you to produce the question as it should appear on the paper, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme.

Three constraints on the question's design, which matter more to me than the wording:

First, it must not be answerable by recitation. A candidate should get no credit for producing the familiar keyboard-standard case, or for stating that increasing returns can lock in an inferior technology, or for listing taxonomy (first-, second-, third-degree path dependence) without using it. The difficulty has to come from a crux the candidate has to work out on the spot.

Second, the question must force a separation of three claims that travel together in the literature and are constantly conflated: that the outcome is sensitive to small early events; that the outcome is locked in rather than reverting; and that the outcome is worse than some alternative in a way that could, in principle, have been remedied. I want candidates to see that the third is the one doing all the normative work and is the hardest to establish, and that the second is really a claim about a range of perturbation sizes rather than about permanence.

Third, it must confront the fact that the question "would the same outcome have arrived anyway?" is not well posed until one fixes a grain of description of the outcome. But it must not let the candidate stop there: "it all depends how you describe it" is the evasion I most want to penalise, and the model answer should say what fixes the description.

To give the question teeth, I want it built around a self-contained hypothetical, plus a three-way distinction we drew in seminar and which I'd like the question to invoke by name:

- the *swap counterfactual*: replace one named event with its single most likely alternative and hold the rest of the sequence fixed;
- the *resampling counterfactual*: redraw the accidents from the distribution of accidents of that kind and ask with what probability the outcome, described at a stated grain, recurs;
- the *pressure counterfactual*: hold the functional and economic pressures fixed and vary the accidents freely — if the outcome type is invariant under these, the pressures are doing the explanatory work and the accidents merely select among realisations.

Here is the case I have in mind, which you should sharpen and complete rather than simply paste in. In 1891 the railway commission of the Republic of Valdania chose a gauge of 1.42 metres; all four of its neighbours had already standardised on 1.60 metres. The commission split five to four, and the decisive vote came from Commissioner Oreste Baldi, whose family's rolling-stock works was already tooled for 1.42 metres. The commission's own engineering report had recommended 1.60 metres, on the grounds that the wider loading gauge permitted larger wagons and greater stability, worth roughly five per cent per tonne-kilometre. The network grew to 19,000 kilometres over the next eighty years. Today the annual cost of the mismatch is estimated at 0.2 per cent of GDP in transshipment at the four border crossings plus 0.3 per cent of GDP in forgone engineering efficiency on all traffic. Complete regauging would cost 4 per cent of GDP in one-off capital spending and would require an eight-year transition during which roughly a third of freight capacity is unavailable, at a cost of about 0.8 per cent of GDP per year. Two comparators: on another continent, Sorrentia also began with a parochial 1.40 metre gauge in 1872 and converted to 1.60 metres in 1919, when a war had destroyed a third of its track and reconstruction was centrally financed; and the island of Kalo, which built no railways until 1952, built at 1.60 metres from the outset. A minority of economic historians add that Valdanian bridges and tunnels could not have carried the wider loading gauge in 1891, so that 1.42 metres was locally correct at the time and only became inferior after steel bridges became standard around 1930.

Please calibrate the arithmetic in the case so that the net-present-value comparison of switching versus staying is finely balanced — it should turn on the discount rate and on the transition-cost estimate — so that a strong answer notices the sensitivity instead of asserting that switching plainly is or plainly is not worthwhile. I'd also like the case to make available the point that part of the loss is a transfer to Baldi's firm rather than deadweight, and the point that the alternative may not have been feasible at the moment of choice, so that ex ante and ex post inefficiency come apart.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The question as it will appear on the paper: the case, the instruction to apply all three counterfactuals at more than one grain of description, and a second part asking whether "history is locked in" is a useful summary of what the case shows, and what it would take to establish that the same outcome would have arrived anyway. Make clear that no literature recall and no citations are expected.
2. A model answer of roughly 900 words. It should treat the three counterfactuals as coming apart in this case, should use Sorrentia as evidence about the size of shock required to unlock rather than as a refutation of lock-in, and should reach a verdict rather than a survey.
3. A marking scheme of 25 marks, organised as named must-hit points with the marks for each, a statement of what separates an A from a B answer in terms of specific moves each must contain, and a short note for each point on how candidates typically get it wrong.
4. Reserve marks within the scheme for a closing paragraph of about 100 words in which the model answer identifies the single step in its own reasoning most likely to fail and says what would change its verdict — for instance, what finding about the availability of a credible compensating transfer from shippers to the state, or about the 1891 feasibility claim, would convert this from a case of remediable inefficiency into a case of political failure or of mere sensitivity.
