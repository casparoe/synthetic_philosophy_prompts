I'm in a small reading group — me, two friends from a finanical engineering masters, and one guy who does ML — and we got into an argument after one of those guest lectures that mention the Allais paradox and the independence axiom in passing, then move on. I went home and started writing down my own verdicts on a pile of cases, and they don't line up with each other. I don't want to be told I'm being irrational, and I don't want a story about what people in experiments usually pick. I want a principle that fits my verdicts, told to me clause by clause.

My cases (all gains unless I say otherwise; all amounts are the kind where I'd actually notice the difference):

- $1M for sure vs. 89% $1M / 10% $5M / 1% $0: I take the sure thing. And 11% $1M vs. 10% $5M (the same comparison with the shared 89% branch set to zero): I take the 10% of $5M just as firmly. So I violate independence, cheerfully.
- $3,000 for sure vs. 80% $4,000: I take the sure thing. 25% $3,000 vs. 20% $4,000: I take the 20% of $4,000. Same shape of violation, but I feel it less strongly here than in the first pair, which I can't explain.
- $1M for sure vs. 99% $1M / 1% $5M: I take the gamble, easily. $1M for sure vs. 99% $1M / 1% $0: I'd pay maybe $2,000 to upgrade to certainty and no more — near-indifference. Yet in the first case I'd forgo something like $390,000 of expected value to keep the sure thing. So whatever is going on isn't a simple discontinuity at probability 1.
- Sure $1M vs. 90% $1M / 10% $5M: the gamble, obviously — dominance. I mention it only so you can tell me whether your principle routes it through dominance or through its general machinery.
- Same Allais pair, but 10,000 independent copies are run and the total is split equally among 10,000 people, me included: I take the risky option. Feels completely obvious, and it's the same choice as case one up to the law of large numbers.
- Same Allais pair, 200 independent copies, all decided by me right now: I take the risky option every time. But imagine the very same 200 copies offered to me one per morning, with earlier outcomes already banked and no way to commit later choices in advance: I take the sure thing each morning, and I know now that I will, and I know I'll never learn anything in between that changes the situation. These two verdicts are the ones I keep going back and forth on and I can't see how both survive.
- Same Allais pair, except both outcomes are sealed in an envelope for a year, can't be spent or borrowed against, and I learn nothing until then: I still take the sure $1M, about as firmly. So it isn't about suspense or when uncertainty resolves.
- Same Allais pair, except the 10/89/1 split comes from an urn whose composition I'm unsure about (I'm told it's around 10% but could be 5% or 15%): I take the sure thing *more* strongly than in the plain version, and I'd rather not be told the true composition before choosing.
- Losses: sure loss of $1M vs. 10% lose $5M / 89% lose $1M / 1% lose nothing: I gamble. And 11% chance of losing $1M vs. 10% chance of losing $5M: I take the 10% of losing $5M. So I violate independence in the mirror-image direction.
- Sure thing of 1,000 lives saved vs. 10% chance of 5,000 / 89% of 1,000 / 1% of 0, and I'm setting policy behind a veil of ignorance about whose life I occupy: risky option. Same numbers for a single identified stranger, whose outcome I'll never learn and who I'll never meet: I take the sure 1,000, and I feel I'd have a much harder time explaining the zero than explaining the 4,000 I left on the table.

My own tentative principle, which I'm pretty sure is broken: independence is fine in general, and I only depart from it when a single chooser bears a single unshared outcome that gets resolved soon, with the probabilities handed to me. Test it and repair it. What I actually want is something precise enough to implement — a rule that takes a description of the choice situation and spits out a ranking, not a psychological story about how people weight certainty — with each clause tied to the case that forces it, verdicts on cases I haven't listed (scaling all the stakes by 1,000, or pushing the low probabilities down to 0.001 vs 0.0009, or making the chooser my own future self after a memory wipe), and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't keep and what I'd have to give up. If no single principle does it and the honest answer is a short menu with a switch between them, say that and tell me what the switch is keyed to. And if you want to reach for prospect theory's probability weighting, fine, but then tell me what it predicts in the 200-mornings case — if it predicts the opposite of what I said, just say so rather than quietly skipping it.
