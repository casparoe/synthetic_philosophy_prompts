I am writing a short position paper — a kind of inference charter — for a project on what can and cannot be recovered about past beliefs and institutions from material remains alone. Before I commit to it, I want an audit of whether the commitments below hang together. I am deliberately not telling you which of them I would be willing to give up, and I would rather you not infer it from what is currently fashionable.

Here is the set. I have glossed the terms I am using so that the audit can proceed from the commitments as stated; please take the glosses as stipulative.

(a) Underdetermination. For any finite assemblage there are indefinitely many belief-states compatible with it. Objects constrain the space of admissible interpretations but never settle questions of belief content, and the honest default is to report practices rather than beliefs.

(b) Institutions are recoverable. We can often recover institutions — rules with a durable, socially recognized, normative character — because institutionalized behavior leaves repeated, standardized, costly-to-maintain material traces: formal disposal areas, standardized measures, maintained boundaries. So: institutions yes; meanings, experiences, cosmologies no.

(c) Costly signaling as the epistemic engine. Costly, hard-to-fake material behavior is our best evidence about what people actually believed as opposed to what they said, and this is precisely what makes material evidence evidentially superior to textual evidence. Call these credibility-enhancing displays: acts expensive or risky enough that someone who did not hold the relevant commitment would not perform them.

(d) Material engagement and symmetry. Meaning, and even some cognitive capacity, is partly constituted by things rather than merely expressed in them: people think with and through objects. This is why I resist reading an object as a symbol standing for an antecedently held belief.

(e) Relational analogy only. Ethnographic, historical, and actualistic evidence may legitimately bridge behavior and pattern, but only as a relational analogy — a documented process-to-pattern link in a case we understand — never as a formal analogy resting on similarity of form. Middle-range work of this kind, not intuition, is the proper bridge.

(f) Texts are not the default frame. Where contemporaneous texts exist, I decline to use them as the lens through which objects are interpreted: texts encode prescriptive, elite, and often ideological norms, whereas the objects record what people did.

(g) A case verdict. Take a hypothetical but composite site: a settlement in which, over several centuries, deliberately broken querns, smashed fine ware, and selected animal parts were placed in pits cut into house floors at abandonment, with details varying from house to house but the broad sequence recurring. I infer that there was a socially recognized rule of closure governing the disposal of certain things at the end of a house's life, and I decline to infer anything about what the residents believed concerning those objects — no animism, no ancestor cult, no spirit of the quern.

What I want from you:

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly, ordering the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. Be explicit about which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why they dissolve — I would rather have a short list of real problems than a long list of hedged ones. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the package as a whole can actually deliver the verdict in (g) or whether (g) is smuggling in something the rest of the set rules out.

Two constraints. Please argue from the commitments as stated and from general reasoning about evidence and inference; I am not asking for readings, and you should assume no library or web access. And I care about calibration: attach a rough confidence to each of your main judgments, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Where a judgment rests on a contested empirical claim about how archaeological evidence actually behaves, rather than on conceptual reasoning, say so explicitly — I want to know which of your conclusions I should check against the record myself.
