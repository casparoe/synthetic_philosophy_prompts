I am putting together the opening session of a graduate seminar on the normativity of logic, and before we read any positions I want a specification of what a position would have to do. The question I want answered is not whether deduction preserves truth: my students will grant that. It is what makes it the case that a thinker who has accepted the premises of a valid argument is rationally required to accept the conclusion, where being required is not the same as being well-advised from the standpoint of reliability, and not the same as having a reason to infer given that she already wants the conclusion. Take Lewis Carroll's brief dialogue in which the Tortoise accepts every fresh conditional Achilles proposes and still declines to descend to the conclusion as the standing threat any answer has to survive. Fix the purpose accordingly: a theory is adequate here if it can tell an intelligent skeptic what she is required to do and why, and can do so without the requirement arriving as one more premise she is free to decline.

What I want from you is the list of desiderata such a theory must meet. For each: state the case or consideration that motivates it, and say why it is a genuine requirement rather than a preference of one camp. Mark which are non-negotiable, which are negotiable, and which are, in your view, prejudices dressed as requirements. Then flag any subset of them you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single desideratum that does the most exclusionary work. Cases I expect you to handle:

1. The Carroll regress itself, and the point that a proof about a system, conducted outside it, does not by itself help the Tortoise.
2. What I will call Tithonus rules: valid forms whose only instances in a thinker's environment are ones she will encounter exactly once, because the vocabulary they require is nearly exhausted. If she is required to infer, what justifies the requirement, given that no track record of applications and no practice with the form is available to her?
3. The bootstrapping case: a thinker who comes to believe the conditional premise only by inferring it from the conclusion, and who believes the antecedent independently. The inference is valid; she appears to gain nothing. So must the theory treat validity as sufficient for the transmission of warrant, or does it owe us an extra condition, and is that condition still part of a theory of deduction?
4. The deviant-logic case: a classical mathematician and a constructive one, each of whom reasons impeccably by her own lights and neither of whom is, by the other's lights, drawing all the conclusions available. Must the theory deliver one logic that we are justified in using, or can it be local or plural, and is that neutrality a desideratum or a cop-out?
5. The error case: competent, trained, sincere reasoners who affirm the consequent. If the requirement is constitutive of believing anything at all, how is the error possible, and what does that constraint do to constitutivist answers?
6. The learning case: children who draw modus ponens inferences years before they can formulate any rule, and the difficulty that a set of examples is a set of examples of indefinitely many rules. If the justification is meant to reach thinkers, it has to reach them before they can state it.
7. The stored-rule question: a thinker who has the rule explicitly in mind and infers, versus one who has no such item but reliably infers anyway. Must the theory require the rule to be psychologically present in the thinker, or is that demand an artifact of a picture of inference as explicit reasoning?

Two distinctions to respect, which I stipulate so that we do not talk past each other: the warrant question, whether deduction preserves warrant, as against the license question, what entitles a thinker to go on using it now without having first answered the warrant question; and rule-circularity, employing a rule in the course of defending it, as against premise-circularity, asserting its validity as a further premise. Say which of these distinctions a theory must respect and which it may collapse, and why. Add at least one desideratum of your own that is not on my list, with its case.

Then, briefly, since the point of the exercise is the criteria and not the scoring: no more than a short paragraph each on how three families do against the list, the view that the regress stops in a rule we employ rather than a premise we assert, the view that logic is justified abductively the way a scientific theory is, and the view that logical laws are constitutive of thought so that no external justification is owed. Include the quietist option that the demand itself is confused, if you think it is live. No bibliography or reading list: I know the literature, and I want the arguments.

Finally, and this is the part I will press in seminar: state how confident you are in the list as a whole and in the joint unsatisfiability you flagged, name the desideratum you would abandon first under pressure and what would force you to, and say what argument or case would lead you to conclude that the whole project of justifying deduction is misguided. Where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail?
