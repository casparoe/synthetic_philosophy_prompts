I am drafting the coding manual for a study of displays that look wasteful — feasts, credentials, rituals, fees, badges, ordeals — and I want to be forced to be precise about which of them are handicaps in the strict sense and which merely resemble one. Please work from the stipulations and cases below; I do not want a literature review, a bibliography, or anyone's arguments reported back to me, only your own reasoning applied to the cases.

Stipulations:

- A display counts as a signal only if it is produced at least partly because of the effect it has on some receiver, and if it is in fact correlated with the property the receiver cares about.
- Call a signal *cost-anchored* (in the handicap sense) when its correlation with the property is maintained by the fact that the marginal cost of the display falls as the signaled quality rises — and, crucially, when a lower-quality signaler who expanded its display in order to mimic a higher-quality one would thereby be made worse off.
- Call it *index-anchored* when the correlation is maintained by a mechanical or developmental tie between the display and the property, so that a lower-quality signaler could not produce the display even if it were willing to bear any cost.
- Call it *sanction-anchored* when the correlation is maintained by a penalty attached to misuse, rather than by anything about the cost of producing the display.
- Bear in mind that cost and waste are not the same thing: a display may be expensive yet return most of its cost to the signaler in benefits that have nothing to do with signaling.

In each case below, assume that the practice is stable, and that wherever I assert a correlation, the correlation is real. The question is always what holds it up.

1. Households in one highland district host an annual feast at which they slaughter a substantial share of their breeding stock. The meat is distributed to other households and most of it spoils. Households with small herds are visibly worse off the following year; households with large herds are not.

2. In the neighbouring valley households slaughter a comparable number of animals at the same season, but they cull only old and infertile animals and salt the meat for their own winter consumption. Guests are invited, and herd size is perfectly legible from the scale of the slaughter.

3. A firm hires analysts almost exclusively from a small set of universities whose curricula have little connection to the work. The degrees are expensive in tuition and in years, and the firm's own tests suggest they add little to job-relevant skill.

4. A seed accelerator admits founders only if they leave paid employment and work full-time on their venture for a year without salary. Founders with stronger outside options pay more, in forgone income, to participate.

5. Applicants to an ensemble submit a single unedited, one-take recording made under supervision. Editing is technically impossible and no retake is permitted. The recording predicts performance very well.

6. A professional body lets any member display a digital badge that costs nothing to obtain and nothing to maintain. Misrepresentation is, however, punished by permanent expulsion, and the expulsion is enforced by the state licensing authority and is public.

7. Young men in a city compete for marriage prospects through visible consumption of goods bought almost entirely on consumer credit. Defaults are common, and a substantial share of the debt is ultimately discharged without full repayment.

8. Every applicant for a class of civil-service posts must complete a six-month unpaid internship. Essentially everyone who begins it finishes it, and completion says nothing about later performance.

9. Officers of a microlender notice that applicants whose forms are filled out in shaky handwriting default more often, and the lender begins using handwriting quality as a risk marker.

10. An online marketplace sells a "verified" badge at a flat fee, the same for every seller and trivial relative to sales. Sellers with a long history of repeat custom lose far more than the fee if they are caught cheating; fly-by-night sellers do not.

What I want from you:

First, sort the ten cases into four tiers — clear cases of cost-anchoring; cases where cost-anchoring is plausible but some adjacent mechanism is doing equal or more work; cases where the handicap framing is a stretch; cases where the handicap framing is actively misleading — with one sentence of justification per case.

Second, give each case a number from 0 to 10 expressing how confident you are that the reliability of the display, such as it is, is underwritten by differential cost in the handicap sense, and one sentence explaining the number.

Third, in two or three paragraphs, say what your tiering implies about whether waste does any real work in the handicap idea: must a signal be wasteful at the margin, over and above what returns to the signaler, or is differential cost enough on its own? Argue for one answer.

Fourth, identify the single case whose placement you would most readily revise, name the one empirical fact that would move it, and say which way it would move.

The whole thing should run to roughly 800 words, in prose and lists rather than a table.
