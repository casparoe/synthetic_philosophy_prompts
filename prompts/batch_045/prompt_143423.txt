I'm in a small reading group that slogs through the popular collapse literature, and one guy in it — call him Dale — keeps leaning on the same analogy: a society is an organism. He's fairly explicit about the correspondences. Metabolism is energy capture and material throughput. Growth is population and territorial expansion. The immune system is state capacity: policing, border control, famine relief. A fever is unrest. Senescence is the point where adding complexity starts buying you less than it costs. And death is collapse. He got this out of a mix of the Bronze Age / Maya / Easter Island material and the diminishing-returns-on-complexity argument, and he's not inventing it — people in this area do talk this way.

He uses it to get three conclusions, and I want to know which of them the analogy is actually carrying. (1) Collapse is a real failure — a death — and not just a transformation, so it's the kind of thing we should try to prevent. (2) A society can therefore be more or less healthy, and in principle we could build a dashboard, read the vital signs, and say which way a given polity is heading. (3) The Maya and the Bronze Age cases were tragedies with victims rather than neutral reorganizations.

Here's a case I made up to test it: a river-valley polity where the palace stops collecting tax in kind, the administrative script stops being used for anything except liturgy, the big cities empty out over about three generations, population roughly halves and stays there, household pottery styles carry on uninterrupted, and the old elite family names still turn up in land deeds two centuries later — now spelled in a different language. Is that a death or a transformation? I care less about the verdict than about whether Dale's frame has the resources to give one at all.

So: give me the correspondence table, then tell me where the mapping breaks and — crucially — which of those breaks hurt which of his three conclusions, and which are harmless. What does the organism side quietly import that the target doesn't have? If the frame survives in some repaired form, what's the better analogy (a lineage? a language? a reef? a firm?) and what would it let him keep. And say whether the death-talk is doing moral work that the transformation-talk wouldn't — I suspect conclusion (1) is being carried by the word "death" rather than by anything in the mapping.
