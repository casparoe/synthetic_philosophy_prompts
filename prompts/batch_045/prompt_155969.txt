Two people in my department's quantum foundations reading group went at it today and I stupidly agreed to arbitrate, because I kind of see both sides. Roughly:

(1) Jonas: In Everett every outcome actually happens, so the only thing left to be uncertain about is which branch I'll find myself on. That's self-locating uncertainty, and the default way to handle it is indifference over branches — but that gives 50/50 regardless of the amplitudes, so it's dead, and Everettians owe us an explanation of why squared amplitude is the right weight. My worry is that whatever answer they give, the squared amplitude turns out to be a weight or measure of how much of you goes each way, and calling that a probability is a change of subject.

(2) Mira: The 50/50 thing fails for a more basic reason: the number of branches isn't well defined. Branching is approximate, you can always split one branch into sub-branches by letting it decohere against more degrees of freedom, so you can make the count come out however you like. Once you see the count is an artifact of how coarsely you describe the system, weighting by measure is the only non-arbitrary option left, and there are arguments that pin the measure to the square of the amplitude specifically.

(3) Jonas: I'll grant the counting point — but that's what makes the measure move suspicious. Picture a "splitter" wired to the apparatus: if H comes out, it also spins off ten extra copies of me, all seeing H and differing only in some microscopic detail elsewhere in the lab. Physically trivial. On the measure view those ten just subdivide H's measure, so my confidence in seeing H doesn't move. But suppose the two outcomes have equal amplitude, so measure says 1/2 and counting says 10/11. And now a bookie offers a bet that pays $1 to each copy of me that sees H: with the splitter on, ten times as many people collect, and I'd clearly pay more for that bet. So my betting behavior tracks copy number, not measure — and betting behavior is exactly what the derivations use to define probability. So those derivations must be smuggling in the assumption that what matters is measure-weighted welfare rather than how many descendants I have, and that assumption is the whole ballgame.

(4) Mira: Two things. The per-copy bet shows nothing about credence: paying each copy just scales the payoff, and of course you pay more for a larger payoff — that's buying ten lottery tickets instead of one, not having different beliefs. And your splitter case is a reductio of your side, not mine: by hypothesis no copy's experience changes when the splitter is on, so copy-counting makes your confidence in something perfectly observable depend on a detail that's irrelevant to every experience involved.

(5) Jonas: "Irrelevant to experience" can't be the test. In ordinary duplication cases — you're told you'll be copied ten times overnight, or the machine will malfunction and make only one — nobody thinks the number of copies is irrelevant to what you should expect or to what you'd pay, even though no copy can tell from the inside which scenario happened. So you're leaning on a principle that begs the question against copy-sensitivity, which is exactly what you accused me of.

Can you go through this move by move — who's right about what, which moves are clearly mistaken and which are basically right — and say what, if anything, both of them are missing? No need for references, I just want the arguments sorted out.
