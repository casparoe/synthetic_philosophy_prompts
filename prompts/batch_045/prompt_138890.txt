I work in machine learning, not philosophy, and I need the debate over whether AI systems should reason as utilitarians or as deontologists restated in the vocabulary I actually think in — and then audited for what gets lost in the translation.

My background: eight years in applied RL and RLHF. I am fluent with MDPs and POMDPs, constrained and multi-objective MDPs, policy-gradient methods and baseline/advantage estimation, model-based planning with a learned world model, offline RL and the role of the behavior policy, reward modeling from human preference data, and the usual pathologies (reward hacking, distributional shift, proxy collapse). My philosophy exposure is one popular book on trolley problems and whatever I pick up from argument with colleagues. Assume no more than that.

The concrete setting, because I want the translation pinned to something: a deployed model that advises a public agency during a shortage. It proposes an allocation of a scarce treatment across a population over several months, and it also drafts the public explanations of those allocations. So it faces both a scoring problem (how bad is a distribution of outcomes?) and a communicative one (may it mislead, withhold, or simplify in what it tells people, and does the answer depend on whether the misleading produces better uptake?).

Please do the following.

Translate, one at a time and as faithfully as you can, into the vocabulary above: act consequentialism of the maximizing-expected-welfare sort; rule or two-level consequentialism; threshold deontology with agent-relative prerogatives and constraints; the doing/allowing and intended/foreseen distinctions; and the Kantian-style prohibition on deception and on treating a person as a mere means. Keep the translation honest: where a philosophical claim only fits if you quietly import an extra RL assumption, say so rather than presenting the fit as exact.

Then give me the residue report, in three columns as it were: what maps cleanly, what maps only with distortion and what the distortion is, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. For each item in the third column, give me your view on whether it reveals a blind spot in the RL frame — something I would need to add to my formalism to even state the claim — or whether it reveals that the philosophical claim was doing less work than it appears to, because it silently depends on a distinction (an agent with its own point of view, a person as a unit of moral accounting, an action as opposed to an event) that the formalism has no place for.

Four hunches of mine that I want you to test rather than flatter:

First, it seems to me that the doing/allowing distinction is, in my terms, a choice of reference policy: an outcome I bring about versus one I merely allow is the difference between comparing my rollout to a baseline where I act and one where I do not, and advantage estimation already requires such a baseline. But nothing in the formalism selects the baseline, and the choice of default policy looks exactly like the moral decision I was hoping the formalism would settle. Is that right, and is the dependence fatal or benign?

Second, I suspect that when people demand deontological constraints in AI they are conflating two different justifications, which I would call epistemic constraints and specification constraints. An epistemic constraint is needed because the system cannot foresee consequences well enough to maximize; a specification constraint is needed because I cannot write down the objective and the system will otherwise find the nearest hackable proxy. These pull in opposite directions as the system's world model improves: better foresight dissolves the case for the first and does nothing at all for the second. If that distinction is sound, it seems to me to reorganize the whole debate, and to make the phrase deontological AI ambiguous in a way that matters for design. Tell me whether it is sound, whether it is actually novel or just a special case of something philosophers already distinguish, and which of the standard arguments for constraints land on which side.

Third, on the scoring problem: I keep noticing that a sum of per-person welfare terms is not invariant to how each person's welfare is independently normalized, so that maximizing total welfare is much less well-defined than the mathematics makes it look, and I have been told there is a classic result that tries to derive the additive aggregation from rationality axioms together with an assumption that everyone is already on a common scale. I would like that translated into my terms — it sounds like the per-head reward normalization problem I fight with in practice, where the weighting is a design decision dressed up as arithmetic — and I want to know what the utilitarian is entitled to assume about interpersonal comparability, and what happens to their position if they are not entitled to it.

Fourth, I have a suspicion that the question is misleveled. In RLHF the thing that looks like deontology at deployment — a refusal, a stylistic prohibition, an aversion traded off against helpfulness — is produced by expected-return optimization at training time against a learned reward model, which is a very rule-consequentialist-looking procedure. So the two theories seem to reappear at different levels: as a claim about which decision procedure to run at the object level, versus a claim about which procedure to use to set the objective at the meta level. Is the original question well-posed at all, or is it two questions that share a name?

Constraints on the answer: no bibliography, no citations, and please do not introduce distinctions by attributing them to authors or papers — argue for them from the structure of the formalisms on either side, since I cannot check a reference from where I am sitting. Where the philosophical literature has a settled answer, say that it is settled and what it is; where it genuinely does not, say so instead of manufacturing a consensus. If you think one of my four hunches is confused, say which premise fails. I would rather have that than a diplomatic synthesis.
