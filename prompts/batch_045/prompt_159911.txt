Working on a piece about constitutional precommitment — Ulysses contracts, supermajority amendment rules, all that — and I keep hitting the line "the dead hand of the past." Last time was a panel where someone used it to shut down my question about whether a country should get to bind itself at all: apparently the phrase just settles it, and I'm not sure it settles anything.

Here's the reform I want to test against it. Instead of entrenchment being all-or-nothing forever, let the binding force of a provision decay with age: the threshold needed to amend it drops on a fixed schedule, and past some point it can be changed by an ordinary majority unless a supermajority actively re-authorizes it (a recommitment vote, so a provision has to be re-tied rather than just left rotting in place). Sort of like how property law is hostile to donors controlling assets forever, the rule against perpetuities and all that.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims "the dead hand of the past" could be making here, as a menu? I'm after things like: claims about authority (the dead have none over us), about whether "we the people" is even the same agent across generations, about how much foresight a founding generation could possibly have, about the difference between binding your own later self and binding somebody else entirely, and about whether the hand doing the binding is really dead or is just a live minority with a veto. For each reading say whether it's near-tautological, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's false or overreaches (does it also wipe out inherited debts, treaties, ordinary law?), and — the part I actually need — whether it supports something like my decay/recommitment scheme or only supports scrapping entrenchment outright.

Two more things. Where does the Ulysses setup help and where is it a misleading analogy? Odysseus' later self wants out for a reason he'd have disowned in advance, which isnt obviously true of a later electorate that just disagrees, and the sailors obeying him weren't the ones who tied him. And tell me honestly which of these readings people usually mean when they deploy the phrase to end an argument, since I suspect it's often just "I dont like this provision" wearing a costume.
