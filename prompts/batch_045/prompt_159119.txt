im in a seminar this term on philosophy of ML and we've now spent like four weeks on whether neural networks actually *have* representations or whether "representation" is just our way of talking about them, and I feel like we've completely stalled out. My prof keeps saying "well, it depends what you mean by representation," which is technically true and completely unhelpful.

Here's roughly where people are. The realists lean on stuff that looks like genuine discovery: directions in activation space that behave like features, the linear representation hypothesis, superposition, the fact that you can find a direction, intervene on it, and get systematic predictable changes in behavior, the old king - man + woman = queen type thing, and the recurrence of similar-looking features across independently trained models. The skeptics say all of that is compatible with the interpretive gloss being *ours* rather than the network's: we choose the basis, we choose the granularity, we choose what counts as a feature, and plenty of mathematically different decompositions of the same frozen network would support equally coherent stories — so there's no fact of the matter about what the net really represents. Both sides agree on all the engineering results. Nobody has moved an inch.

The thing that made me think it's structurally deeper than stubbornness is a case we looked at last week. Two teams interpret the same frozen model with dictionary-learning / sparse-autoencoder style tools and get two feature sets with very different granularity — say tens of thousands vs. hundreds of thousands of features, and they don't nest inside each other cleanly. Both sets steer the model about equally well. Both predict held-out activations about equally well. So the usual tests — causal efficacy under intervention, predictive adequacy, recurrence across models — seem to get satisfied twice over by mutually incompatible answers. To me that smells like underdetermination: same shape as fitting infinitely many curves through finitely many points, except the "data" here is what interpretability tools can *do*.

My hypothesis, which you should feel free to kill: the disagreement isn't about the networks at all, it's about what a representational claim is a claim *about*. Realists are asking something like "what is this computation made of, what are the causally operative parts," and skeptics are asking "what is this internal state *about*" — and the second question needs something the first doesn't: a story about what fixes content, whether content needs a consumer or downstream reader, whether representation is relational to a task and a training distribution rather than intrinsic to the network. Nobody says which question theyre answering, so every exchange is two ships passing in the night.

Is that right, or is it something else — a merely verbal fight about the word "representation," a clash about whether interpretability aims at mechanistic explanation or at prediction-and-control, or a hidden formal question (is there actually a theorem about non-uniqueness of decompositions here, or a principled test for whether team 1's feature and team 2's feature are "the same" representation)? I also wonder whether the cross-model recurrence evidence is doing more work than it can bear, given that the models share architecture, objective, and data.

What I want is a diagnosis of what kind of stalemate this is, grounded in what each side actually says rather than a survey of positions, plus an honest answer about what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Pls don't give me a literature dump or tell me what to go read; I have basically no library access this semester and I want your own reasoning.
