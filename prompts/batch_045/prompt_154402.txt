I sit on a four-person panel — two clinicians, a lay member, and a lawyer — that decides, roughly forty times a month and in about twenty minutes per case, whether a claimant's condition counts as a disease. We decide it for two purposes that we are required to answer with the same word: entitlement to publicly funded treatment, and entitlement to paid medical leave. Our written reasons go to the claimant and can be appealed to a tribunal, so they have to be intelligible to someone with no training in medicine or philosophy, and they have to be reasons we would give again next month for a similar case.

Please write us a decision procedure we can actually run, built out of the debate about whether 'disease' is a descriptive or an evaluative concept: the biostatistical account (a disease is a statistically subnormal contribution of some part or mechanism, judged against a reference class, with no value judgement built in), the harmful-dysfunction account (a dysfunction plus a harm), and the normativist view (a disease is whatever we take to be medically bad or to warrant medical attention). Give us the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where we stop with a no, and where we must hand the question to someone else because it turns on values rather than facts — and say who that someone is. The procedure must also say what to do when the factual question has no available answer: no agreed reference class, no baseline data, or a trait spread smoothly across a population rather than falling into two clusters. And it should block the three moves we catch ourselves making: choosing the reference class so that the case comes out the way we want, treating the existence of an effective treatment as evidence that there is a disease, and treating 'this person is suffering' as sufficient on its own.

Then run it on three claims. (a) A 34-year-old seeking funded treatment and leave for distractibility: she falls in the most distractible 12% of a smooth, unimodal distribution on a validated attention battery, the cut-off was set by a specialist society's vote rather than found in the data, and the trait predicts poor performance in an accounts job but good performance in a monitoring job. (b) A 68-year-old seeking funded correction for the loss of near focusing that almost everyone over 55 has: a real failure of a mechanism measured against a younger reference class, statistically normal within his own age cohort, cheaply and safely corrected. (c) A claimant seeking leave after a bereavement, with severe and prolonged incapacity, normal grief on any statistical view, no subnormal mechanism we can identify, and symptoms that respond to treatment — a case where I think she should get the leave. If the procedure returns a no on (c), say so plainly.

Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of claim it will systematically leave undecided or get wrong, and which of its steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
