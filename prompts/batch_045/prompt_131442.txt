I'm setting a 90-minute in-class essay for an upper-level seminar on the ontological argument, and I want one question that can't be passed by reciting Anselm's maximally-great-being move and then reciting the modal version's axioms and the S5 step. As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs I keep meeting two stock responses — that the whole family is a language trick, and that if the axioms are consistent in S5 then the conclusion follows and we should just take the axioms seriously — and I want a question that separates students who understand the crux from students who have memorized which of those two noises to make.

The device I want the question built around is a diagnostic I've been calling the mirror test. Take any argument of this family, form its dual by swapping the governing evaluative predicate (great-making becomes dreadfulness-making; positive becomes anti-positive), and re-run it with exactly the same structural machinery: same modal logic, same treatment of existence as a predicate, same closure axioms on the property class. Then ask whether the dual goes through. If it does, the original has at most shown that the machinery has a fixed point, not anything about God. If it doesn't, the student must locate the exact premise that breaks the symmetry and say what work that premise is really doing.

Please write: the question itself; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically miss it. The five should at minimum cover applying the mirror test to Anselm's version; applying it to the modal version and naming which premise breaks the symmetry; whether the test is just a Gaunilo-style parody in fancy dress, and if not what it adds; whether the test is too strong, i.e. whether some other respectable a priori argument would fail it; and where the test itself is doing something other than detecting question-begging.

Commit to a verdict rather than surveying options: say which of the two arguments survives the test and which fails, where you think the test is itself unreliable, and which single step in the argument you would actually put money on being sound or unsound.
