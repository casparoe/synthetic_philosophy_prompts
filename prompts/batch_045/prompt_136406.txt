I was at a reading group thing last night — mostly philosophy grad students, plus one guy from stats who kept saying "just pick a threshold" — and we got totally stuck on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Someone suggested I write down what I actually think about a bunch of cases before going near the literature, so I've spent the morning doing that, and it's messier than I expected. The question I'm asking in each case is just: is it ok for me to flat-out believe this, and rely on it? Here's what my gut says, no theory attached.

1. Fair lottery, 1000 tickets, one winner, draw already happened but nobody's announced it. Ticket #437: yes, fine, I believe it lost, I'd say so out loud. But I'm not fine believing the conjunction "tickets 1 through 999 all lost," even though that's 99.9% likely — higher than any single ticket. So apparently I can believe every conjunct and not the conjunction, which still seems insane to me. 2. My friend's 400-page monograph. I've read her arguements for the individual claims; I believe each of them. I also believe there's at least one error somewhere in the book, because that's just what happens with 400-page books. Neither belief feels irrational to me and I don't want to accuse her of anything. One person in the group said this case is fake, though: if she's really .99 on each claim, her own error rate is already priced into that number, so the "there's an error somewhere" belief is double counting. I half believe that and it makes the preface feel different from how it's usually set up. 3. Statistical evidence only: 1000 people at a rally, exactly one didn't pay, and I know nothing about Jones except he was there. I don't feel I can believe Jones paid — it's the same .999 as the lottery ticket and I get a totally different result. This is the pair I can't reconcile with case 1. 4. Size seems to matter to me, which feels like cheating: three claims, each about .9, plus a background belief that one of them is false — that combo feels genuinely incoherent, while the 400-claim version feels fine. 5. Oracle variant: instead of getting the error belief from base rates, a senior colleague I trust tells me "exactly one of your 400 claims is false," and won't say which. My verdict: this feels worse than the ordinary preface, even though it looks structurally identical, and I can't say why. 6. High stakes: 200 components in a reactor safety system, each individually inspected, each about .999 reliable, and I also believe at least one of them fails. Here I want to say I believe each is fine and one isn't, but I definitely shouldn't act as if they're all fine — whereas in case 2 I'd happily send the book to the printer. So my action verdicts and my belief verdicts come apart, and I don't know if that's principled or just cowardice.

Is there one principle that gets all of this — some rule about when high probability plus "one of these is definitely bad" licenses belief, or assertion, or action — or am I going to have to give up some of these verdicts, and if so which ones hurt least? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, with a note on which of my cases motivates each clause, and I want you to be straight about whichever verdict it can't save. Also: I have no stable feeling at all about two things and want to know what your principle says about them. First, a 400-step mathematical proof, where the steps depend on each other and one bad step sinks the whole thing — does that get treated differently from 400 independent claims, and in which direction? Second, does the preface move iterate: once I believe each claim and also that one is false, can I coherently believe that one of those second-order beliefs is wrong too, and does it matter if the 400 claims are spread across eight co-authors each vouching for their own chapter with one editor aggregating? And please don't just tell me the name of a position I should go read — give me the principle and its verdicts.
