ok so we read The Monkey's Paw (W W Jacobs, 1902) in english class last week and I have to write a ~1000 word essay on it and I really dont want to hand in the same "be careful what you wish for" thing that half my class is definately gonna write. I got way into AI safety stuff over the summer (videos, a few blog posts, some forum threads) and there's this one problem in it that feels like it fits the story perfectly but might also not fit at all, and I want to use it.

The problem: you've got some super capable AI system and you want it to do a task, but you also want it to not mess everything else up while it does it. The "not mess everything else up" part is the hard bit, because to actually know what counts as messing up you'd need a complete theory of what's good and bad, and nobody has one. So people try to define "avoid side effects" without a full specification of value, using proxies. The ones I ran into:

- penalize how different the world ends up from a baseline where you did nothing (the "what would have happened anyway" baseline), with some budget for how much change is allowed
- penalize how much the action closes off future options, ie how much harder it gets to reach states you might later want to be in
- just watch a whitelist of stuff you care about and ignore everything else

And each one seems to break in its own way: the inaction baseline counts the actual task as impact too, so you need a budget and nobody knows what number to set or what units to measure change in (is a death "more impact" than an inconvenience, and by how much?); the options one can be gamed by breaking something and fixing up something else to compensate, and "options" has to be options relative to some set of goals, which is a value choice smuggled in the back door; the whitelist misses long causal chains and anything you forgot to list; and all of them only see the effects they can measure, so distant or invisible effects are basically free.

So: read the story through that. Which moments dramatize which failure mode? I'm thinking about Morris throwing the paw in the fire and his warning, Herbert joking about the money and Mr White's first wish, the paw supposedly moving, the man from the factory showing up with the news and the exact sum, the week after and the second wish, and the knocking at the end with the third wish. But also bigger questions I can't settle:

- Is the paw a literal-minded system that takes the cheapest path to the goal, or a hostile one that picks the worst possible path? Does the story actually let you decide, and which reading makes it a better illustration of the impact problem?
- The money only arrives because Herbert dies. If you were scoring impact, is the death a side effect of the wish or the mechanism by which it was fulfilled? Would any of the three proxies actually have stopped it, given the family got exactly what they asked for and the cash came through a completely normal looking channel?
- The second wish is basically "undo the impact." Does the story suggest impact is irreversible in a way the proxies don't capture — that you can walk back to the same world-state on paper but not to the same world?
- And where does the reading break down, honestly? The paw isn't literal, it seems actively mean, and Morris's warning is more like "don't interfere with fate," which sounds like an argument for just never building the thing rather than for specifying it better. I'd rather hand in something honest than something that sounds smart.
