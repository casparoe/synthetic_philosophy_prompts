Take the 2015 Daraprim episode — a pharma startup bought the US rights to a roughly 60-year-old off-patent toxoplasmosis drug, raised the price by several thousand percent overnight, and pushed distribution through a single specialty pharmacy. Take as given that nobody seriously claimed the hike paid for new R&D or a cost shock, and that no competing generic was on the market at the time (one was only approved years later). I dont need the numbers relitigated.

I know the standard repugnant-markets and commodification arguments — the some things shouldnt be for sale line, the crowding-out stuff, the hostile-worlds idea — so dont just recite them. What I want is a disciplined mapping: who the actual parties were (patients, clinicians, hospitals, insurers, shareholders, the blocked generic entrant, the FDA), what each of them could realistically have done, and which of the usual objections really fires here — third-party harm, exploitation/unfairness, corruption of a non-market norm, or the wrong-attitude-toward-the-good one — and which of them is actually doing the work.

Then the bit I cant sort out. My sense is that what enraged people wasnt the price level as such but the way it was set: by asking what buyers could be made to bear, rather than by cost or by value delivered. Call that the calibration objection. Is it genuinely distinct, or does it collapse into one of the standard ones? And in the other direction: does the episode tell against the repugnance framing itself — was the real problem a malformed market (no generic, closed distribution, captive patients), with repugnance just a noisy symptom of it, so that asking what kind of good a pill or a kidney is mislocates the whole issue? Say plainly where the theory is silent instead of making it speak.
