I have no academic background in philosophy, but I got interested in this after reading about the old nineteenth-century exchange between W. K. Clifford, who published an essay called The Ethics of Belief in 1877 arguing that it is wrong always, everywhere and for anyone to believe on insufficient evidence, and William James, whose 1896 lecture The Will to Believe argued that where a choice is live, forced and momentous and the evidence genuinely cannot settle it, our passional nature not only may but must decide. James used the example of what he called precursive faith: belief that runs ahead of the evidence, such as the borderline-excessive self-confidence that helps an athlete perform.

Two friends of mine who had never heard of either essay then got into almost exactly this argument, and I have been unable to referee it. The trigger was a third friend, Dan, who at fifty-one has left a steady job and put nearly all of his savings into a small brewery and taproom. He is candid that the base rates are against him, puts his own chance of surviving three years at about forty percent, and says he has simply decided to believe that it will work. Here is how the exchange went.

(1) Owen said that is precisely the thing Clifford objected to. Believing what your evidence does not support is not a private quirk; the habit of letting hopes do the work of evidence leaks into every other judgment, and it is how people end up dragging others down with them — he gave Clifford's example of an owner who stifles his own doubts about whether his ship is seaworthy and sends passengers out on it, who is blameworthy even if the ship happens to arrive safely, because the wrong was in how the belief was formed.

(2) Marta said this is not a case of ignoring evidence but of a question the evidence genuinely leaves open, and that Dan cannot sit it out. Whether the venture works depends partly on whether he visibly believes in it: staff, suppliers, a landlord and customers all respond to a person who conveys conviction, and so does his own willingness to keep going through the first bad winter. She said waiting for more evidence is not the neutral option it looks like — declining to commit guarantees the failure — and that this is the self-fulfilling structure James was pointing at, where adopting the belief is one of the causes of its coming true.

(3) Owen gave two replies. First, that the self-fulfilling claim is an empirical guess and cuts both ways: overconfidence is exactly what makes small-business owners skip market research, dismiss early warnings and throw good money after bad, so confidence may lower the odds rather than raise them; and since only evidence could tell you which it is in Dan's case, Marta has conceded that evidence is the authority after all. Second, he raised what he called the residue problem: the practical benefits of confidence depend on Dan not dwelling on the fact that he chose the belief for practical reasons, because a belief you know you manufactured is not the same psychological item as the steadfastness of someone convinced by the evidence. So, he said, either the will to believe yields a real belief, in which case it requires self-deception or selective attention to the fact that you chose it, or it yields something else — a policy, a stance, acting as if — in which case Marta should stop calling it belief and the argument is verbal.

(4) Marta answered both. On the first, she granted that confidence can be self-defeating and said the reply is to bolt a tripwire onto it: fix in advance, while the evidence is still evenly balanced, the specific conditions under which the question gets reopened from scratch — a cash-reserve floor, two of three named warning signs — and then believe flat out in the meantime. Confidence and checking, she said, are practically detachable. On the second, she said the residue objection proves too much: deciding to trust a colleague after a falling-out, giving someone the benefit of the doubt, treating a marriage as repairable — these are all chosen partly for practical reasons and then reshape what you notice, and nobody calls them self-deception. If they all count, the accusation has no work left to do.

(5) Owen said the tripwire reply concedes his point: a belief with a scheduled revocation built into it is not a belief but a plan with an exit rule. Dan can act with complete resolve while keeping his estimate at forty percent, because resolve is a matter of what you are willing to do and not of what you take to be likely; so the Clifford side never withheld anything Dan actually needs, and the flat-out belief Marta wants is surplus to requirements. He added that the benefit-of-the-doubt cases are structurally different anyway: trusting a person partly constitutes the relationship, whereas predicting that the business will succeed is a claim about how the world will go, so the former cannot be the model for the latter.

(6) Marta said Owen has defined belief as whatever cannot be chosen, so of course nothing chosen will ever count as one, and the real question — whether Dan may let his stakes and his hopes settle a question the evidence leaves open — is being settled by a stipulation rather than argued.

Neither of them moved after that, and I genuinely cannot tell who is winning. Would you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right about what? Specifically: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each rating briefly; say whether any move is strong on the surface but rests on a confusion; say who is more right overall; and say what both of them are missing — the distinctions or possibilities that would dissolve or sharpen the disagreement rather than add a seventh move to it.
