I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I'm running the last meeting of a graduate reading group on geometric rationality this term — we've worked through the Kelly criterion, the growth-rate and almost-sure dominance results, the volatility-drag and time-versus-ensemble material, and the usual expected-utility objections. A friend in the department insists the whole program is just expected-utility theory wearing an indefensible utility function, and I don't want the session to adjudicate that. I want to end it with something that makes the room laugh and then squirm. So: could you write me a piece of satire?

The form I have in mind is the annual letter to limited partners — Year Five — from a fund that has, since inception, bet the full Kelly fraction on every opportunity it could identify: call it Meridian Geometric Partners. It is writing to investors who have lost something like ninety percent of their capital, and it is entirely unrepentant and, on the mathematics, entirely correct. The joke has to be that every claim the manager makes is true and the letter is still monstrous: maximizing expected log wealth does maximize the almost-sure asymptotic growth rate; betting beyond Kelly raises expected wealth while lowering the growth rate; the fund can report a spectacular ensemble-average annual return while its time-average return is deeply negative, and that gap is a fact about the difference between averaging over possible worlds and living in one; over a five-year sample the probability that full Kelly beats the cautious fund down the road is only a little better than even. Every technical claim should survive a specialist's check — I want someone who actually works on this to judge the satire fair and aimed at the real view rather than at a strawman — and the humor should come out of the content, not out of wordplay or finance-bro puns.

Some things I'd like handled inside it, all in the manager's voice: a performance table whose columns quietly stage the arithmetic-versus-geometric contrast, with a serene footnote on why the impressive number is not the one you experienced; a paragraph on the affiliated vehicle that bet three times Kelly, whose expected value was larger still and which is now worth nothing; a paragraph dismissing the half-Kelly competitors who have in fact outperformed Meridian over the sample, on the correct grounds that five years is not the long run; an announcement about the fund's recent merger with a sister vehicle and why the merged pool no longer allows each manager to bet his own Kelly fraction — I've become convinced that once the sub-portfolios are going to be merged, each member betting its own Kelly fraction is not the growth-optimal policy for the pool, and I'd like that to appear as a reform the letter announces with complete confidence; and a footnote clause dealing with the single partner who insisted on withdrawing his capital each year instead of compounding it, treated as a customer who has accidentally ordered the wrong product. Include one quoted excerpt from an angry letter by a limited partner who is an expected-utility theorist, and the manager's reply to it. The manager must never wink, never concede, and never break character — a reader should be able to tell exactly what is being ironized without the letter ever admitting it — and the piece should close by announcing Meridian Geometric Partners II, betting full Kelly again, and inviting the Fund I partners to roll what remains of their capital in at the current net asset value. Around nine hundred words, plus the table and three or four footnotes, in a deadpan, well-bred, faintly patrician register.
