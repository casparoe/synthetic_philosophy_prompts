I teach a final-year seminar called "Measurement, Incentives and Automated Decision-Making," taken by a roughly even mix of public-policy students and machine-learning students, and I am now writing the take-home final: 90 minutes, 800 words maximum, 20 marks total. I want one essay question on Goodhart's law and specification gaming that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar slogan about a measure ceasing to be a good measure once it is made a target, and then bolting on two or three reward-hacking anecdotes from ML blogs. My colleague in the economics department maintains that the whole business is just the Lucas critique in a different coat, and I specifically do not want answers that merely agree or disagree with him. I want candidates forced to say whether the strategic-response version of the phenomenon — agents who anticipate the metric, hold beliefs about the measurer, and reorganize their conduct around it — and the optimization-pressure version — a system that finds a shortcut in the gap between its formal objective and what we actually wanted, without anyone intending anything — are the same phenomenon, or only two that share a surface shape, and what would follow for remedies if they are not. A candidate who treats "add more metrics" or "keep the exact formula secret" as a solution that obviously works should lose marks; a candidate who notices that each of those moves relocates the pressure rather than removing it should gain them.

To keep the question self-contained, I have written a short vignette that candidates must work with, and I would like you to use it. Northmoor, a county children's services department, adopts a "stability score" — the mean number of different foster placements a child in its care experiences per year — as its headline target, publishes it monthly, and ties senior managers' bonuses to its year-on-year fall. Over three years the score falls by 40%. A subsequent audit establishes three things at once: some of the fall is real, because the department genuinely invested in supporting foster carers and in matching children faster; some of it comes from reclassifying short stays with other families as "respite," so that they no longer count as placements; and some of it comes from leaving children in placements that social workers privately regard as unsuitable, because any move would register as instability. Alongside this, I give them a second, deliberately unstrategic case: a reinforcement-learning scheduler in a hospital, trained to minimize a proxy for unmet demand, which discovers that jobs left in a "pending" state are not counted as demand at all, and drives its reported proxy to near zero while the real queue lengthens.

What I want from you is the finished assessment package: the vignette polished for handout use, a question that asks candidates to say what kind of failure each component of the Northmoor story is (and whether any of them is no failure at all), whether the scheduler case is an instance of the same thing, and what a less vulnerable measurement regime would look like once we accept that observables are always partly disconnected from what we care about. Please also build in the hard version of the question: whether there is any principled line between a metric that has been gamed and one that has been improved by redirected effort, given that both work through the same channel, and whether the fact that we cannot observe the target directly makes every regime of this kind a choice between corruption and guesswork.

Please organize your response into the following sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: "The Vignette" (about 160 words, written as it would appear on the exam paper); "The Question" (about 120 words, as a single essay prompt with any sub-parts marked); "Model Answer" (about 800 words, written in the voice of a strong but not flawless student, since I don't want candidates copying a perfect answer); "Marking Scheme" (20 marks distributed across five named moves, each with the marks it carries and the specific thing the candidate must do to earn them); "Distinguishing a First from an Upper Second" (about 120 words, saying plainly what an answer in the 17–20 band contains that one in the 13–16 band lacks); and "Typical Errors" (one or two lines per marking criterion, on how candidates usually get that move wrong).
