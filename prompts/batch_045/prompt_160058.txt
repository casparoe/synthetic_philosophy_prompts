The thesis: emotions are neither judgments nor bare feelings but perceptions of affordances — where the content isn't a value property like "this is dangerous" but a set of action-possibilities the object presents relative to my body and my skills, something like "fleeable-here-now-for-someone-like-me." The normative status I care about isn't accuracy and it isn't warrant but a notion of aptness I'm borrowing from the virtue-epistemology literature: an emotion is apt when it picks up the actual affordances *because* of the agent's affective competence, where competence is a trained skill rather than a module. My three load-bearing moves: (1) recalcitrant emotion (irrational fear in the glass elevator, fear of a spider you know is harmless) is a perceptual habit that outruns what the situation affords, so it's a failure of aptness and not a false belief; (2) the fact that emotions are cognitively penetrable by reappraisal doesn't disqualify them from being perceptions, if you allow perception to be partly skill-based rather than encapsulated; (3) because aptness isn't an all-things-considered notion, I can say what's defective about a fitting-but-inconvenient emotion without sliding into "appropriateness" and running into the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. My advisor's standing challenge to me is: if your account can't say anything interesting about the spider case, you don't have a theory of emotion at all.

The room matters a lot here, so: about fourteen people.
