You are a philosopher of collective and political responsibility who also knows the historiography of the First World War's origins inside out: someone who has read the Fischer controversy from both sides, is unimpressed by the idea that "everyone was equally to blame" as a way of avoiding the question, but is equally unimpressed by the idea that a verdict of national guilt can be read straight off the archives. Work slowly and carefully; before you write anything final, spend a page or so brainstorm‑dumping candidate requirements and visibly crossing out the ones that turn out to be nothing more than one camp's favourite conclusion dressed up as a methodological principle.

What I actually need is a specification, not an adjudication. I co‑teach a graduate seminar on the origins of the First World War, and my students keep relapsing into the same loop: one side says the Imperial German leadership used the July Crisis to bring about a war it believed it could still win, pointing to the backing given to Vienna in early July and to the annexationist programme circulated in Berlin that September; the other side says the cabinets of Europe miscalculated in sequence, that no government wanted a continental war, and that singling out one villain is a moral luxury purchased with hindsight. Before we read any more of it, I want the standard of assessment made explicit: what would an account of "nation N is responsible for this war" have to deliver in order to count as adequate — not as true, just as adequate? Please build the list around cases rather than slogans, and include at least the following pressures. (i) It must say what the word "responsible" is doing: causal contribution, blameworthiness, liability to repair, or something else — and it must handle the case where these come apart. (ii) It must identify the bearer: government, state apparatus, polity, political culture — test it on what I'll call the Two‑War‑Room case, where five men decide on war and the rest of the cabinet never sees the orders. (iii) It must state its rule for causal overdetermination: when several powers each did something such that any one of them backing down would probably have prevented the war, how is responsibility distributed, and does it depend on who moved first? Test it on the blank cheque given to a smaller ally at the start of July 1914, and on what I'll call the Latecomer's Defence: a power that mobilises second and claims it was merely responding. (iv) It must handle what I'll call the Archive‑Fire case: suppose the crucial minutes were destroyed in 1945 and we had only the public diplomatic record — is responsibility then a fact we fail to discover, or an attitude whose appropriateness changes with our evidence? (v) It must specify what the blame is even *for*: initiating the war, escalating a Balkan war into a European one, the war aims adopted once it began, or the scale of what resulted — and say whether outcome luck (a short war versus four years of it) is allowed in. (vi) It must take a position on preventive‑war reasoning: does acting now because one's relative power is thought to be in permanent decline mitigate, and by how much? (vii) It must say what the account owes to dissenters and conscripts, and whether the verdict generates anything for descendants — reparations, commemoration, school textbooks — or expires with the generation that decided. Along the way, tell me whether you think the account needs three separate categories or whether they collapse: initiation responsibility, escalation responsibility, and structural responsibility for unchosen standing features such as alliance commitments and rail mobilisation timetables that made the crisis lethal without anyone choosing them in 1914.

Two more things I care about more than the length of the list. First, mark each requirement as non‑negotiable, contestable‑but‑reasonable, or camp‑preference, and say plainly whether any pair of requirements you have endorsed cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list is internally unsatisfiable, that is the most interesting thing you can tell me. Second, keep every requirement strong enough to exclude some accounts and neutral enough not to beg the question: "must be fair" is vacuous, "must conclude that great powers bear primary blame" is a conclusion wearing a badge.

Format: a numbered list of requirements, each with the requirement in one or two sentences, the case or consideration that motivates it, and its tag; then a short section on jointly unsatisfiable pairs; then, in no more than two sentences each, how the "deliberate bid for a European war" reading and the "everyone stumbled into it" reading fare against your list — that is the only scoring I want, and please don't pad the answer with citations or a reading list, since I already have the syllabus. Finish with a single last line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in fifteen words or fewer, the one requirement which does the most exclusionary work; if you judge some pair of requirements to be jointly unsatisfiable, name that pair after a semicolon on the same line.
