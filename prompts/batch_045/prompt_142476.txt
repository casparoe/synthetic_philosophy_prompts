I am a retired comparative physiologist who has come to philosophy late, and I would like a philosopher's eye on a regulatory pattern I keep circling. Please answer as a moral philosopher who also has a working grasp of comparative animal-welfare law in Europe; think the problem through carefully before committing yourself, and open by sketching, in a sentence or two, the readings you considered and set aside before settling on the one you defend.

The practice: European slaughter rules require farmed fish to be rendered insensible before bleeding, by percussive or electrical stunning, with operational criteria such as loss of brainstem reflexes, while farmed decapods (shrimp, prawns, crabs, lobsters) fall outside those rules and are routinely killed by immersion in an ice-water slurry that serves at once as stunning and as killing, even though the UK's sentience statute of 2022 expressly extends sentience to decapod crustaceans and cephalopods. If I have any of that detail wrong, correct it in passing and reconstruct the practice on the corrected version. What I want is not a verdict on the practice but the implicit theory that would make it coherent: what it strictly presupposes, what it merely sits well with, and where several rival theories would license it equally.

Three things I cannot get past. First, the rule seems to protect the property it cares about only through a taxonomic proxy, vertebrate status standing in for sentience, and I want to know what has to be true of that proxy for the substitution to be legitimate given that its errors are systematic rather than random. Second, there looks to be a presupposition that no obligation can be imposed without an auditable indicator: fish insensibility has brainstem and ventilatory markers, decapod nervous systems are distributed across ganglia and offer no convenient equivalent, so the silence about decapods may reflect a measurement gap rather than a moral judgment, and I would like to know whether a practice can coherently allow its moral boundary to be drawn by its instruments. Third, when people propose protecting decapods in proportion to how probable their sentience is, I cannot tell whether the probability is doing evidential work, expressing our confidence, or metaphysical work, assigning a fractional moral status, and whether the practice needs one reading rather than the other. And somewhere in there: what does the slurry method itself presuppose about what makes a death bad, a bad terminal experience or something about the killing as such?

Finally, tell me what would genuinely have to change, in regulation rather than in sentiment, if the taxonomic proxy were replaced by a precautionary view on which credible sentience of any degree triggers protection. Write in continuous prose throughout: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
