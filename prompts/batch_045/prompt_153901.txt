Hi — I'm a PhD student in decision theory (housed in a philosophy dept, long story) and I'm writing a short piece for our department's film-night blog on Uncut Gems, the 2019 Safdie brothers one with Adam Sandler as Howard Ratner, the gambling-addict jeweller in New York's Diamond District. I want to read it through Kelly betting and geometric rationality, and I dont want it to collapse into gambling bad, log-utility good — I want the lens to actually do some work on the film, and I want to know where it doesnt.

Framing in case it helps, since I'd rather you used mine than a generic one. Kelly: when you get to pick the fraction of your bankroll to stake on a repeatable favourable bet, maximise expected log wealth, which for a simple two-way bet means staking edge over odds, and which is roughly the rule that maximises your long-run growth rate and asymptotically beats every other fixed fraction. Geometric rationality as I've been working with it: the generalisation where instead of maximising the arithmetic mean of utility across worlds, or across the agents in some merger, you maximise the geometric mean — i.e. expected log utility — on the thought that the entity doing the choosing is spread over time or over people rather than facing a one-shot choice. I'm interested in both, and in whether the film has anything to say about the step from the betting case to the general rule.

Things I want taken seriously:

- Howard's bets are parlays, not straight bets — he wins a six-way on Garnett's stat line, and at the end he sends Julia off in a helicopter to put the whole of Garnett's cash on a three-way. They're also lumpy and indivisible: a pawned championship ring, then a lump of notes. Kelly assumes you can scale your stake continuously and rebalance after every round. Does the film depict someone who could not implement fractional Kelly even if he wanted to, and if so is that an excuse, a mitigation, or just noise?

- Most of what he risks isn't really his. The ring is Garnett's collateral, and the first winning bet is placed with money he owes Arno, who then puts a stop on it. My suspicion is that Kelly's conservatism is downstream of losses shrinking your own future stake, and that the film is quietly about how the surrounding kit — pawnshop, bookie, loan shark — manufactures asymmetries where the downside lands on somebody else. Is that a genuine point about log-growth, or am I overreading a crime plot as a moral-hazard parable?

- By the final act he's arguably already ruined: he has cash in hand that would clear the Arno debt and he bets it instead. Once you're at or beneath your floor, or once you have a hard target with a deadline attached, does growth-rate maximisation have anything left to say? My hunch is that the honest answer is no, and that this is the interesting bit, but I want it argued rather than asserted.

- The ending. The parlay wins, $1.2m, and Phil shoots him in his own shop before he can touch it; Julia walks out of Mohegan Sun with the money. I keep thinking this is a real philosophical gag: the bankroll compounds, the agent who was supposed to be the beneficiary doesn't. Something about what log-growth presupposes — one persistent bettor, with persistent preferences, there to collect — against a Howard who is split across wife, mistress, shop and bet, and dead at the moment of collection.

Then the bit I care most about: where does the reading fail? The film calls him a gambling addict, and its form gives you almost no scene of reflective choice — everyone talks over everyone, doors slam, there's no moment where a man considers a betting fraction. If the decision-theoretic frame misdescribes him by turning compulsion into strategy, say so, and say what we'd lose by reading him that way anyway. And give me a bottom line: is this a film that illuminates geometric rationality, or one that just lets me project it?

British English throughout please, and no bibliography or further reading — I know the film and I know the maths, I just want the argument. Something I could read aloud in about fifteen minutes would be ideal.
