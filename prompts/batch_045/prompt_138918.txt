I'm in a final-year historiography seminar — the unit is on archives, power, and the production of history — and we're assembling an end-of-term packet of short pieces for the class. Mine is supposed to be the funny one, and I want the humour to come out of the material itself rather than out of the word "archive" being dropped into a joke. We've been reading the argument that silences are manufactured rather than stumbled upon: that they get built into the production of history at four moments, the making of sources, the making of archives, the making of narratives, and the making of history in the final instance, with the Haitian Revolution as the case where a whole event could not be thought in the categories available at the time. I've also been playing with a distinction of my own that I'd like the piece to be built around: first-order silences, where nothing is ever written down; second-order silences, where something is written down and then destroyed under an authorised disposal schedule; and third-order silences, where the material survives, is funded as holdings, and is never catalogued — what I've been calling the *dark archive*, since nobody can find it and the institution still gets credit for holding it. My suspicion, which I want the piece to dramatise rather than state, is that a well-run archive has a positive institutional incentive to prefer the third kind: the shelves fill, the budget per linear metre grows, and the reading room stays quiet.

So here is what I want written. A parody of an official document, roughly 700 words: a numbered *Director's Circular on Appraisal and Disposition*, with a short retention table at the end, issued by the National Archives Directorate of a small, fictional, recently decolonised republic — call it the Republic of Veyra, whose central registry was inherited from the departing administration and which is now, in the circular's own phrasing, rebalancing its holdings. It should be addressed to regional records officers and written in the competent, understaffed, budget-conscious voice of a senior civil servant who is not a villain: she worries about humidity, shelving metres, and the Directorate's professional reputation, and she believes in what the circular calls *custodial neutrality*, the doctrine that the archive only receives what has already been made. Every clause should be doing real archival-theoretical work without ever naming it. The series generated by ministries, district offices and police posts are *organic* and *naturally accruing* and therefore permanent; the letters, ledgers, receipt books and photograph albums of the people those offices governed arrive as *sponsored*, *derivative* or *curated* material and must be shown to have evidential value before accessioning is even considered. Material that its own donors have arranged has forfeited its original order. A destroyed file keeps its catalogue number, so the gap reads as absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence. The cheapest disposal is the one that happens before the record exists, and the circular congratulates the Civil Registry on a pilot scheme in three rural districts. A fee-based relevance review, conducted some decades later, determines what turns out to have been of national significance. Communities who ask for their own material are handled through a heritage-deposit arrangement that requires them to supply the catalogue entries themselves. Nothing in the circular may admit that any of this is an exercise of power; the joke has to land in the gap between the register and what the clauses actually do. Keep it deadpan from first line to last — no winking, no archivist twirling a moustache, no sentence that tells the reader what to conclude — and make it work on two levels: someone who knows appraisal criteria, provenance, original order, retention scheduling and the literature on under-documented communities should recognise these as real features of the practice and feel the satire is fair, while someone who has never opened a finding aid should still be able to tell who is being done to what.
