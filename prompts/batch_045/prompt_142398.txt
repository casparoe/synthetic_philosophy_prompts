Our seminar produces a scripted podcast of imaginary interviews with historical figures on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and the next session is a conversation with Alan Turing about his treatment of extrasensory perception in the 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence. I need eight to ten interview questions for that session. They must not let him simply restate the objection and the remedy: I already know he took the objection seriously and held that the imitation game had to be run under conditions immune to telepathy. The questions should instead make him elaborate, defend, or qualify that position, and they should press where later criticism would press.

The sequence must include, at minimum, questions along three lines. First, press the asymmetry among the varieties of extrasensory perception. Turing distinguishes telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis, but the safeguard he describes is apparently only a telepathy-proof room. Ask how any room could be proof against precognition or clairvoyance, given that precognition is not a physical signal that can be blocked by a material shield. Second, raise a problem you must invent for him, since he could not have faced it: a machine with no paranormal power but trained on a vast archive of past imitation-game transcripts and of the interrogator's previous behavior might produce answers so well tuned to the interrogator's unstated expectations that they look telepathic. Call this the simulated-telepathy problem, and ask whether it shows that the extrasensory-perception objection is only the most vivid version of a general difficulty: distinguishing genuine intelligent responsiveness from extremely good statistical prediction. Ask also whether a telepathy-proof room would do any work against simulated telepathy. Third, raise the reverse case: if extrasensory perception were real, the threat might be that the human contestant or the interrogator has it rather than the machine. If the interrogator could use telepathy or clairvoyance to identify the machine directly, then the outcome would depend on the interrogator's paranormal reliability rather than on the machine's displayed behavior. Ask whether that undermines the imitation game as a public, behavioral criterion, and how one could certify an ESP-free interrogator without begging the question.

For each of these three required lines, state in one clause the reply you think Turing is most likely to give, and then give the follow-up that reply should trigger. For the remaining questions, which may widen the frame, also add after each question a short parenthetical with the reply you expect and the follow-up it invites. Suitable further topics include why a founder of computable mechanism took the statistical evidence for telepathy seriously enough to want the test sealed against it; whether accepting extrasensory perception would, in his view, make other occult beliefs harder to resist; and how the discussion bears on a contemporary large language model that has memorized so much of the internet that its next-token predictions can look like precognition of the prompt.

Please compose the questions, the parenthetical notes, and the closing remarks in British English. Finally, append a short note in which you say how confident you are in what you claim about Turing's view and in the anticipated replies, and single out the point you are least sure of.
