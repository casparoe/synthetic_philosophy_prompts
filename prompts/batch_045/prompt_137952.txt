So I work in collections care at a mid-sized regional museum — mostly registration and loans rather than conservation proper — and I've somehow ended up on an internal committee that has to write two policies: one on when a restored object stops counting as "the original" for labels, catalogue entries and insurance, and one on what we do when a source community asks for an object back and someone on our side floats the idea of offering a high-resolution scan plus a 3D print instead. We had a proper argument about it last week, and what struck me afterwards is that we are already operating with a whole set of distinctions that nobody on the committee can actually state, and that they don't look obviously consistent with each other. Here's the pattern as I see it (correct me if I've got any of the details wrong). Our conservators can replace a crumbling marble arm, or build up losses with filler, and the object keeps its accession number and is still "the Roman portrait head" or whatever it is — nobody thinks a new object has been made. But a bronze cast taken from a surviving mould of the same head gets catalogued as a cast, an "after", a copy: categorically not the work, even though its shape is about as close as you could physically get, and arguably closer to how the thing originally looked than the restored original is. In buildings, the thing I was told is that you repair a listed building like for like, board by board, essentially forever, so that after two hundred years almost none of the original timber is left and it's still the listed building — but if you demolish it and put up a visually identical one from the same drawings, that's a replica, it isn't listed, and there's some rule that new work should be identifiable as new rather than disguised as old. The shrine at Ise gets dismantled and rebuilt on an adjacent site roughly every twenty years and is still described as being well over a thousand years old, and everyone seems fine with that. And then the one that started the fight: we (like every other museum) will happily spend money on "digital preservation" — laser scans, photogrammetry, digital twins — and talk as if the object has been preserved in another form, yet the instant a community asks for the thing itself back, "here's a perfect 3D print" is treated as an insult. So we talk as though form is what matters when that's convenient and as though matter is what matters when that's convenient.

What I want from you is: what does this cluster of practices presuppose about what an artifact is? I did a bit of philosophy as an undergrad and I vaguely remember the statue and the clay stuff — whether the statue is identical to the lump of bronze making it up, whether there can be two things in exactly the same place at the same time, whether a thing has to keep all its parts to keep being itself — and I can see that our practices look like they deny that an object is just its matter, since we're perfectly willing to swap the matter out piece by piece. But that's about where I stop being able to think clearly. Is there one view of material constitution and persistence that the whole cluster only makes sense under, or is it a grab-bag that several different theories would happily license? Concretely: does the practice really need identity at all, or can you explain all of it with something thinner — provenance, causal history, what we owe people, what an institution has promised to look after? My colleague's line is that "original" in our paperwork never meant "numerically the same object" in the first place; it meant "the one with the right history", and that we can do our jobs without any metaphysics of constitution whatsoever. I honestly dont know whether that's a cop-out or the correct answer, so please take it seriously as a candidate and tell me what it can't account for. And I want the cost side too: if we went with the view that the object is identical with its matter at every moment, or with the view that there are two coinciding objects where we thought there was one, or with the view that what persists is just whatever the relevant community's sortal or practice says persists, what would we actually have to change — would we stop keeping boxes of removed original fragments in the store, would we start listing replicas, would we have to stop doing plaster reconstructions of shattered pottery? Also, if it helps: one of our older loan agreements defines "the Object" partly by its accession number and partly by a physical description, and I keep suspecting that combination is doing a lot more work than it looks like it's doing.

Please give me roughly 150 words of "Bottom line" first, then "The implicit theory" (around 400 words), then "What's strictly presupposed vs. what merely fits" (around 250 words), then "The hard cases" (around 300 words, by which I mean Ise and the scan-and-print offer specifically, not just gestures at them), then "If we switched theories" (around 200 words). Plain language throughout — I'm writing this up for a commitee of curators and a lawyer, not for a journal — and where you think one of my verdicts about what we do is just wrong or rests on a distinction that doesn't really exist, say so rather than politely building a theory around it.
