I have a 40-minute slot with the programme committee of a foundation in about ten days, and I'm the one making the internal case. They're deciding whether to restructure next year's whole grant portfolio around safe Pareto improvements, so its not a seminar: if the Q&A goes badly nothing happens, and if it goes well there's actual money attached. Can you build me a prep sheet?

What I'm defending, so you know what to aim at. A safe Pareto improvement is an intervention that is better than doing nothing on every moral view in some specified set, not merely on my own best guess — that's the whole point of "safe" rather than just positive expected value. Ordinary Kaldor-Hicks doesn't qualify, because the compensation is only hypothetical. But strict unanimity across all views looks empty in practice, so I've been arguing for what I call safety-to-a-floor: (i) no view whose prior weight is above a small threshold — I use 1%, but the number isn't load bearing — says the intervention is negative; (ii) views below the threshold that do say it's negative can't contribute a negative term bigger than some fixed fraction of the total, which is my guard against tiny-probability-huge-stakes views swamping everything. And I claim non-trivial things clear this bar. My flagship example is what I've started calling a hold-in-trust purchase: the foundation buys permanent legal control of some asset whose development is irreversible and whose future value is very view-dependent — an old-growth tract, a mineral deposit, a parcel next to a transit node — at a premium over market from willing sellers, and then does nothing with it except stop whoever would otherwise have converted it, with any release requiring a supermajority of a rotating committee. Nobody is coerced, the counterfactual is a real one, and under a wide spread of futures keeping the option open is worth something. I am not defending longtermism in general and I'm not saying SPIs are the only things worth funding.

The room, five people plus me. The chair, an ex-hedge-fund guy who will want to know what concretely changes in January and who thinks in terms of baselines and dead capital. A development economist who has run a pile of RCTs and treats anything unmeasurable as unfundable. A decision theorist who works on fanaticism, unbounded utility and pascalian stakes. A moral philosopher who writes on moral uncertainty, person-affecting views and population ethics. And a programme officer who is sympathetic but privately worries this will read as an unaccountable land grab by people who won't be around to answer for it.

What I want: the objections I'll actually get from these five, ordered by likelihood times damage, each phrased the way that person would phrase it (the decision theorist's version and the RCT person's version of "your criterion is gerrymandered" should not sound alike), each with a two-minute reply and one line on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything, which single reply has to land or the proposal is dead, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. If you think one of the objections is genuinely fatal rather than answerable, say that instead and tell me what to cut — the example, the floor, or the frame.

And be honest about confidence: put a rough confidence on each reply, and then single out the one you're least sure of and say why. I'd much rather find out here than in the room.
