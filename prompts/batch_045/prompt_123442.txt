I teach a first-year seminar called Media and Democracy, and I have a single eighty-minute slot in which to make Kierkegaard's critique of the press and of the public do real work for about twenty students. They will not have read him; almost everything they know about the topic comes from one earlier lecture in which the public sphere was presented in the rational-critical, roughly Habermasian register as the forum where private persons reason together toward public opinion. They are also, almost to a person, planning careers in journalism or political communications and take a free press and the public's right to know as settled goods. So the session has a problem of uptake as well as a problem of content: whatever I say about leveling will arrive as an attack on their vocation, and I would rather they feel the force of the attack than that they defend against it.

What I want them to leave holding is the distinction between a crowd and a public (persons assembled, who can at least be addressed, dispersed, or called to answer, versus an entity that never assembles, never acts, and cannot be held to anything), the claim that for him the press is not principally a conduit of bad information but the machinery that brings such an entity into being, and the claim that the untruth he attaches to the crowd is not a prediction about the accuracy of majority opinion. I also want them to see why leveling is not a complaint about equality, and why the remedy is not withdrawal into private life.

What I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Specifically:

First, the order. Which idea has to be standing before which other idea can be put on the board at all, and what collapses if I reverse the pair. I suspect that leveling must be in place before the press can be introduced as its instrument, and that the notion of the single individual cannot be introduced last, as a remedy, without turning into a slogan — but tell me where I am wrong about the dependency structure, including whether the anonymity of the press (that nobody in particular wrote it, so nobody in particular can be called to account) belongs early, as part of the mechanism, or late, as the part that explains why a public can be spoken for but never speaks.

Second, the example that carries the whole thing, and what it must have and lack. I have been weighing three: the Corsair affair, since he wrote the 1846 literary review of the novel Two Ages in its immediate aftermath and was himself the object of the ridicule; a small-town story in which a local paper prints something, everyone in town discusses it, and nothing whatever happens; and a contemporary pile-on in which ten thousand people hold a view that no one of them would say to a face. My worry about the first is that students will read the argument as the resentment of a man who lost a fight. My worry about the second is that it makes the problem look like apathy rather than leveling. Tell me which to build on, what to cut from it, and what it must not contain.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually form, and the point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off. My guesses: they will take the untruth claim epistemically, as the thesis that majorities are usually factually wrong, and will be unmoved by it; they will read the public aggregatively, as a large number of people, so that nothing seems to be lost in it; they will hear leveling as an attack on social equality and dismiss him as an elitist; they will take the remedy to be logging off, or silence, or a private sphere; they will translate the single individual into authenticity, self-expression, or rugged individualism; and they will conclude that the press is being named as the cause, so that abolishing newspapers would dissolve the public, which I take to be wrong — I think for him it is at once a product, a symptom, and a necessary condition, but I am not confident, and I do not know how to make that triple relation visible in eighty minutes. Add the misconstructions I have not anticipated, and say which of them I should deliberately let form and then break rather than pre-empt.

Fourth, the check. I want a question I can put to them at the end that distinguishes having the idea from having been impressed by it. My candidate is: suppose a well-informed, well-argued, entirely correct opinion is held by everyone — in what sense, if any, is the crowd that holds it untruth, and in what sense is it not? Tell me whether that question tests what I want it to test, and if not, what does.

Finally, what I should refuse to explain at this level and say openly that I am refusing. I take it that the God-relation, which is what makes the single individual a single individual for him rather than merely a dissenter, is one of these, and I would like to know what else belongs on that list and how to name it in a way that does not make him sound like a theologian who wandered into a media-studies room.
