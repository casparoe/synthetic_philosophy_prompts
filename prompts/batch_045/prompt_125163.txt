I am putting together the discussion materials for a philosophy-and-film module on personal identity, and I want a written reading of Christopher Nolan's *The Prestige* (2006) framed entirely around Daniel Dennett's 1978 story "Where Am I?", in which a man's brain is removed to a life-support vat while his own denuded body is driven around by radio link, so that the question of where he now is — and therefore which of the two candidates is him — comes to seem to have no answer beyond the one he is prepared to settle on. Assume your reader knows the film but cannot consult either text: no direct quotation, no bibliography, no citations, and nothing that depends on details smaller than the famous ones. The three scenes I want at the centre are Borden's original Transported Man, which turns out to be performed by identical twins who have divided a single life, a single public identity and even a single set of injuries between them; Angier's first version of the trick, staged with the lookalike Root, in which nothing is duplicated at all and Angier's complaint is simply that he is the man beneath the stage while someone else takes the bow; and Angier's Real Transported Man, in which Tesla's machine produces a duplicate while the original drops through the trapdoor into a water tank he cannot escape, an arrangement he repeats for a run of performances, ending in the basement lined with tanks each holding a drowned man.

Call Angier's machine a case of what I will call *pruned fission*: a branch of a person is created and destroyed within seconds, and the pruning is scheduled in advance by the man who steps into the machine. Does Dennett's treatment of the vat case — that there is nothing more to the question of where I am than what I care about in survival — extend to pruned fission, or does the fact that Angier knows in advance which branch will be destroyed do identity-work that the two-candidate case cannot do? And if it does no such work, is the machine simply an apparatus for dying that has been arranged to look like a trick?

I also want the reading to say where the film resists it. With Root there is no duplication and yet the discomfort is the same, and with the twins two subjects occupy one role by agreement and self-mutilation, which is a mirror image of fission rather than a case of it. Both suggest the film asks a third-personal question — who is taken to be the man who came back — where Dennett's is first-personal. So: is Cutter's account of the prestige, the return of what was destroyed, offering a genuinely social answer to "where am I?", one that gets the twins right where the standard fission literature does not, or is it changing the subject? Say plainly where the film refuses the reading and where the reading illuminates it, in about a thousand words.
