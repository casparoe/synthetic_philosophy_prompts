I am writing a paper on whether a market price can ever be a source of justified belief — on whether the familiar claim that prices transmit dispersed knowledge survives being stated carefully rather than gestured at. While drafting the skeptical section I produced the argument below. It has the shape of a dilemma rather than of a paradox, and I now distrust it because it establishes considerably more than I am prepared to believe. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it breaks.

Set-up. Call a market an *oracle market* for an event E when all of the following hold: (a) no participant can influence whether E occurs; (b) E is connected to no decision, endowment, contract, or risk exposure that any participant has outside the market, so that nothing of theirs is hedged by betting on E; (c) orders are anonymous and sealed until E is resolved, so no one trades for reputation, for signalling, or for the satisfaction of being seen to be right; (d) the only use a participant can make of evidence about E is to trade on it; (e) participants are expected-utility maximizers who share a common prior over E, and both that and their rationality are common knowledge among them.

The argument:

(1) In an oracle market, the sole point of incurring a cost to gather evidence about E is to trade profitably on it.
(2) Trading profitably on evidence requires a counterparty who accepts terms that are worse, relative to the evidence, than the informed trader's own posterior warrants.
(3) No-trade: when traders share a common prior and it is common knowledge at the moment of trade that the standing allocation is efficient given the information present in the market, no trade can be one that both sides expect to gain from on informational grounds. What looks like a gain from superior information on one side must look like a loss on the other, and common priors together with common knowledge of rationality forbid that.
(4) Clause (b) removes every hedging and risk-sharing motive, and clauses (a), (c) and (d) remove every remaining motive. So the only possible motive for trade in an oracle market is speculation on informational advantage.
(5) Hence, by (3) and (4), no participant has any reason to buy evidence about E. The expected trading value of a signal, computed before anyone has seen it, is at best zero, and therefore negative once the cost of gathering it is subtracted. So no one acquires information.
(6) If no one acquires information, the price never departs from the common prior, and the market transmits no knowledge at all.
(7) But if the price never departs from the prior, the supposition of (3) fails: the standing allocation is no longer efficient given the information present, since a single participant who does buy evidence now has something the others lack. That trader profits, and profits reliably. So the state described in (6) is not stable either.
(8) Therefore no equilibrium exists. And the difficulty is not specific to markets. Any institution that pools dispersed private evidence into a public signal faces the same dilemma: to the degree that it succeeds in making pooled evidence public, it destroys the reason anyone had to gather it; to the degree that it preserves the incentive to gather, it must keep the evidence private and so fails to pool it. So no arrangement — market, expert panel, jury, scientific community — can be a reliable source of justified belief from dispersed evidence. Every apparent case is either a subsidized coincidence or an artifact of someone's irrationality.

Why I do not believe it. (8) is much stronger than anything the paper needs, and it seems to contradict claims I am unwilling to surrender: that well-run prediction markets sometimes track an event better than the median participant, and even, on occasion, better than any participant could have done alone; and that the existence of profitable information-gathering is compatible with, rather than evidence against, aggregation. I would also want to insist on the distinction between the market being more accurate than the median participant and its being more accurate than the best-informed participant, since only the second would give a price genuine epistemic authority over the people who defer to it, and my suspicion is that the argument trades on running these together.

Of the premises, I would most like to keep (3) and (4): the no-trade result seems to me to be doing real work in the literature and I do not want to be saved by denying it. I am more willing to reopen the stipulations in (a) through (e), or the step from "no expected gain at the margin" to "no one gathers anything."

Which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? What does rejecting it cost — does the market's epistemic status survive, and if so in what attenuated form? And if (8) is in fact sound but merely uninteresting, I would like to know what the sound version of it is a claim about.
