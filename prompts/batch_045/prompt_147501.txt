I help coordinate a volunteer search-and-rescue team in a rural county, and I've been asked to write the after-action report for last month's search — who gets thanked, and, because there's an insurance question hanging over it, who did what. The committee keeps deadlocking, and I've come to think the real disagreement is about when a scatter of people acting separately adds up to a group doing something. Here are my verdicts, going by gut:

1. Two hundred households left their porch lights on all night; from the air the lights formed an arrow that pointed the helicopter to the missing hiker. The neighborhood did not do anything together, though they should certainly be thanked.
2. Two strangers at a brush fire, without a word exchanged but each seeing the other, fell into passing buckets hand to hand. They fought that fire together.
3. Six people carried a litter down the ridge in shifts, one at a time, each taking over where the last left a marker, none of them ever seeing another carrier. I want to say they evacuated the patient together, and I cannot say why this is different from (1).
4. Our dispatch app routed four drivers onto the only road into the canyon, which blocked it while we worked. No driver knew about the others or intended a blockage. The drivers did nothing together; the team did block the road.
5. A landowner unlocked a gate only because a volunteer threatened to call the sheriff. He still helped with the evacuation. But the man who helped heave the generator onto the truck, believing it was scrap, did not help tamper with evidence — though he did help load it.
6. On the north-ridge grid, one of the "five" people coordinating over the radio turned out to be a single volunteer working two call signs. "The five of us covered the ridge" is false; "we covered the ridge" is true.
7. The team voted 6–1 to call off the night search. Five of the six voted to call off only because they assumed the others had better information, and privately wanted to keep going. "The team decided to call off the search" is true, and I also want to say that no one decided anything together.

My tentative principle: we φ-ed together just in case each of us intended that we φ, intended our own bit as a contribution to it, and all of us knew this about one another. Test it against (1) through (7), repair it wherever it breaks, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save and what giving one up would cost me. Then say what the repaired principle predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: two searchers whose sectors overlap because the app assigned them that way, each quietly adjusting to the other's movements without knowing why — is that us coordinating, or only being coordinated? Please answer in American English.
