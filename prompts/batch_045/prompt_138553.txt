The question the session is about is whether the principle has any restrictive bite left once you take away the cases everyone reaches for, so I want a case where it does bite, built without cheating.

The case: one piece of speech that the harm principle, applied honestly, says may be restricted. Constraints:

- Ordinary present-day setting, one paragraph, no trolleys or sci-fi, no emergency decrees. Not Mill's own example — the one where calling corn-dealers starvers of the poor is fine in a newspaper but punishable when you shout it to an angry mob outside the dealer's house.
- None of the standard triggers available: no incitement to violence or to any crime, no true threats, no defamation of a named person, no lies or fraud, no obscenity, no national-security angle, no captive audience, and no state that's obviously the bad guy.
- The speech is true, or at least responsibly believed, and the harm isn't just that people get offended or change their minds. It's a real setback to other people's important interests, and I want the mechanism spelled out.
- It can't be majoritarian. Don't give me a majority shutting up a dissenting minority — give me the reverse: speech that's popular or backed by respectable opinion, harming a diffuse group with little political pull, so the usual "the harm principle is a weapon for tyrannical majorities" worry doesn't fire.
- The harm should reach its victims through what other people do with the speech, so that the obvious Millian reply — then regulate the listeners, not the speaker — is on the table. Then build the case so that reply is practically unavailable: intermediaries many, dispersed, hard to detect, each doing something small that isn't itself worth banning.

After the scenario: show why the principle says restrict, run the best Millian counter (mediated harm isn't the speaker's harm, more speech as the remedy, slope worries, who gets to judge), then say which single feature is doing the work and what happens to the verdict if I strip it. If you think one of my constraints forces the conclusion rather than letting it fall out, say which one and why.
