I have no academic background in philosophy — I studied engineering and have spent fifteen years in product management — but for the past two years I have been reading moral psychology and metaethics on my own and writing a slow, small newsletter about it for about two hundred subscribers, mostly friends and former colleagues. The draft below grew out of an argument with my brother-in-law, who is a physicist and holds that the variation in moral intuitions across cultures settles the question: morality is obviously something we invented, like money. I found myself unable to say why that inference felt too fast to me, and also unable to say what the cross-cultural data actually does show. This essay is my attempt to get clearer. I am sending it to a few people whose judgment I trust, and I would be grateful for the kind of comments a careful reader would leave in the margin: not a bibliography, not a list of who has said what, but a working critique of the argument itself and of the one distinction I am trying to introduce.

***

**What the cross-cultural data is really threatening**

Almost every popular version of the argument from cultural variation runs the same way: people in different societies give different answers to the same moral question; disagreement of that breadth and persistence is evidence that there is no fact of the matter; therefore morality is not objective. This is a weak argument and most people who think about it for an afternoon can see why. Disagreement does not refute objectivity in any other domain — people have disagreed for centuries about the shape of the earth and about the safety of bloodletting — and the bare existence of divergent verdicts tells you nothing about whether some of those verdicts are mistaken. So the cultural-variation argument, in its standard form, should be retired.

But I think retiring it too quickly hides the more interesting datum, which is not that people disagree but that sometimes they do not answer at all. Much of the cross-cultural material is variation in verdicts on a fixed scenario, and that variation is genuinely disagreement-shaped. But a striking amount of it is not: what differs across communities is which features of a situation register as morally loaded in the first place. Whether a certain kind of bodily or dietary contamination matters, whether the claims of dead ancestors are live claims, whether an unequal distribution among kin is itself the offense or merely background, whether a promise extracted under a certain kind of duress is a promise at all — these are not cases where two groups look at the same moralized feature and reach opposite conclusions. They are cases where one group is tracking a consideration the other group does not see as a consideration. Call this the *silence test*: for any candidate universal principle, ask not whether some group denies it but whether there is any group for which violations of it are not merely tolerated but unremarked — where nothing in the situation shows up as the kind of thing that needs answering.

If that is right, then the disagreement model misdescribes the phenomenon, and the interesting conclusion is not about truth but about what I will call *authority*. The ordinary picture of an objective moral reason is that it binds you whether or not you recognize it: its force is not conditional on your uptake. The cross-cultural data, read through the silence test, suggests that a great many moral considerations have to be socially installed before they are visible at all, and that their apparent bindingness is a function of that installation. I will call this the *uptake problem*.

The obvious objectivist reply is that visibility is not validity. Blindness to a reason is not refutation of it; someone who has never learned to follow a proof cannot see that a theorem holds, and that incapacity is a fact about them rather than about mathematics. I think this reply is partly right, and I want to concede it early rather than have it thrown at me. But the analogy quietly smuggles in what is at issue: the mathematician's failure is a failure of *inference*, and we have an independent account of what would correct it. In the moral case we have no comparably independent account of what it would be for a community to come to see a consideration it currently cannot see, and the process by which people do come to see new moral considerations looks less like proof and more like conversion, upbringing, or political change.

So here is the position I want to defend, and I suspect it is less exciting than it sounds in my head: cultural variation leaves moral *truth* untouched and moral *authority* damaged. Moral reasons may be perfectly objective in the thin sense that some of them obtain regardless of what anyone thinks, while being local in their grip — real reasons, but reasons whose ability to move a given person depends on that person's having been brought into a form of attention that not everyone shares. If that is right, it has a consequence I care about more than the metaethics. Cross-cultural moral criticism cannot take the form it usually takes. If the person you are criticizing is not already tracking your consideration, blame is the wrong speech act: you are not calling them out for ignoring a reason they have, you are trying to get them to see something. That is a different activity with different rhetoric and a different standard of success, and it looks much less like adjudication and much more like persuasion.

What I would like from you:

1. Is the disagreement-versus-silence distinction genuinely doing work, or does it collapse under pressure? My worry is that if you ask a person why they did not treat some feature as morally relevant, they will produce a reason — they will say that the feature does not matter — and that looks like disagreement after all, just disagreement one level down, about which features are reasons rather than about what to do.
2. Am I overreading the psychology? I have described the variation as including differences in which features register as morally loaded, not merely differences in verdicts. Is that a fair description of what the cross-cultural work actually finds, or have I built an argument on a distinction the studies do not support? If the empirical claim is shakier than I have made it sound, I would rather know now than publish it.
3. Is the uptake problem a genuine problem, or am I rediscovering something familiar about whether moral reasons are intrinsically motivating, and simply giving it a new name? If it is familiar, I would like to know what the standard answers are, in your own words, and whether any of them dissolve what I have written.
4. My conclusion that truth survives and authority does not strikes me as possibly being a well-known position in a costume, or possibly being incoherent — can a reason be objective and yet fail to bind someone to whom it applies? Tell me which, and why.
5. The final paragraph about blame and persuasion may be a non-sequitur. Does anything in the argument actually support it, or is it a separate essay stapled on?
6. Line-level: which paragraph is the weakest, which would you cut first if the piece had to be a third shorter, and what is the one objection you would expect an unsympathetic philosopher to raise that I have not addressed anywhere in the draft?
