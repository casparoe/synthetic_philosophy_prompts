I'm a 4th-year PhD student (poli theory with a PPE-ish bent) and I've drifted into holding something like this: pure time preference is never rational. The date at which a benefit or a harm arrives is never, by itself, a reason to weight it less. Everything that looks like a reason to discount the future is really some other consideration wearing a discount rate as a costume — the risk there's no one around to receive anything, the risk our resources or institutions never reach them, the fact that they'll likely be richer so the marginal unit buys less wellbeing, opportunity cost of capital. So: discount for hazards, discount for growth and diminishing marginal utility, never for lateness as such. Call it hazard-only discounting if it helps.

I got here mostly via the climate-economics fight over whether the social discount rate should be near 1% or several percent, and I now think the low-rate side is basically right about the normative part. But I want the map of how exposed this is. What would count against it, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and — importantly — split into what a committed hazard-only person should concede counts against them versus what they're entitled to shrug off.

Some things I specifically want chewed on. First, the collapse worry, which is the one that actually keeps me up: if I'm allowed to discount for the risk that future people won't want or even recognise what I was trying to give them (call it value-drift hazard), and that risk plausibly grows with temporal distance, haven't I smuggled pure time preference back in through the side door, just with an empirical-looking wrapper? Does my view then agree with a modest positive rate on every case that matters, i.e. is the disagreement merely verbal? Or is there a real signature difference — hazards multiply survival-ish probabilities and arguably shouldn't discount the value of *preventing* the hazard itself (so extinction-risk reduction stays near-undiscounted even on my view) — and is that a genuine difference or a dodge I've been using?

Second, how much work is the growth-and-diminishing-returns brake actually doing? I sometimes suspect my view sounds far more demanding than it is, and that near-zero discounting plus the growth term lands somewhere boring, which would mean I've been winning arguments I haven't earned. What would it take for the view to imply something genuinely extreme (savings or transfer rates no real institution would accept), and would that be a reductio or just an inconvenient truth? Relatedly: does the wait-and-learn / irreversibility line (that you should be slower to spend now because you'll know more later) give me an effective hurdle rate above zero even with zero pure time preference, and do I have to eat that?

Third, I've been leaning on the declining-rate-under-uncertainty argument to say the far future should be discounted at something close to the lowest plausible rate. But isn't it symmetric — uncertainty over hazard rates pushes the effective rate up? Have I been applying that argument selectively, and if so is that fatal or fixable?

Fourth, the personal-prudence analogue: it doesn't seem irrational for *me* to prefer a bad headache next year over one tomorrow. If it isn't, why is the analogous weighting irrational for a planner? Is the social case genuinely different, or am I just switching normative frameworks when it's convenient?

And what about evidence that arguably already exists: revealed market rates, the fact that long-horizon institutions basically all decay or get repurposed, perpetual foundations that didn't stay perpetual. Do any of these bite, or are they all the wrong kind of evidence? Concretely: suppose a big, well-run debiasing study found that even under full information and calm reflection people still weight proximity at a couple of percent a year, robustly across cultures. Does that move me at all, or does hazard-only discounting just say everyone is committing a normative error — and if it does, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Is that a problem here, given that it's usually sold as a claim about what rationality *requires*, or is it just the ordinary status of a normative principle and nothing to be embarrassed about?

For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find most persuasive isn't intuition-mongering or hypothetical surveys — it's an argument that the view has no content, that after all its qualifications it makes no recommendation its rivals don't also make. So weight that accordingly, and tell me if you think the opposite: that it's actually well insulated and that's fine.
