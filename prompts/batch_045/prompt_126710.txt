I'm assembling a short collection of satirical pieces for an audience that already knows the material, and I'd like one on the fate of long-run Malthusian prediction.

The target is not Malthus, and not any living writer. It is a stance and a practice: the treatment of a long-run predictive thesis as not false but merely early — the re-dating of the prediction, the re-specification of the mechanism, and the steady accumulation of defences each of which is individually reasonable. The piece should be fair in the specific sense that a philosopher of science who is sympathetic to structural Malthusian worries would recognise that the joke is on the practice and not on the existence of the problem, and would not be able to wave away the one serious argument the piece makes on the thesis's behalf.

Form and voice: the annual letter to limited partners of a hedge fund (name it as you like; "Arithmetica Capital" if you want a suggestion) whose founding mandate is to hold the Malthusian position until it is vindicated. Write it in the true register of such a letter — calm, euphemistic, "we remain constructive," heavy on the passive voice, careful to distinguish mark-to-market from intrinsic value. Deadpan throughout: the letter never winks, never raises its voice, and never uses the words falsify, unfalsifiable, auxiliary, paradigm, or research programme. That vocabulary belongs only in the second section.

Material the letter should work through, in its own corporate idiom:

- The fund's device for policing itself: a "Deferral Ledger" required by the partnership agreement. Every time the predicted arrival of the positive check is pushed back, the specific assumption doing the work must be entered, with (a) its shelf life — the number of years it is expected to defer the check — and (b) its residual — how much of the original mechanism it preserves. There have been five entries. The letter should disclose, in whatever tone it prefers, that the average shelf life of recent entries is shorter than that of earlier ones.
- The re-specification of what is actually being predicted: famine, then energy price, then waste sinks — with the insistence that the underlying inequality, a quantity growing geometrically against a ceiling that grows at best arithmetically, has never been revised, only re-expressed.
- The fund's one genuinely strong argument, stated without a trace of irony: that the twentieth century's escape was purchased by drawing down a one-off endowment — fossil energy, mined phosphate, fossil groundwater — so the period usually counted as a refutation is better counted as an instance of the mechanism, a stock being spent. Part of the comedy should be that the reader notices, without being told, that this move turns a decade of abundance into evidence for the thesis.
- A fee structure that has quietly become the problem: the fund charges its limited partners a premium for being right early, and that premium compounds. By the letter's own arithmetic, the deferral liability now grows at roughly the rate the fund expects population to grow. Include a footnote from the auditor observing that no single entry in the ledger is unreasonable.
- A closing announcement, played absolutely straight, that the fund has merged with a rival whose thesis was that population pressure induces the innovation that relieves it, so that the combined book now profits whether the check arrives or is indefinitely postponed. The letter should describe this as prudent risk management. It must not notice that the merged position has no content.

Include around three footnotes in total, and somewhere in the letter a small table setting out the five re-datings: year of the letter, the date then predicted, the assumption entered, its shelf life, its residual.

Please structure your response in two titled sections, in this order.

1. "Memorandum" — the letter itself, dated and addressed to limited partners, 800–1,000 words, including the table and the footnotes.
2. "Commentary" — 200–300 words. Name the real feature of long-run Malthusian argument that each joke is exaggerating, and say honestly where you think a sympathetic philosopher of science would object that the satire is unfair: in particular, whether the "right but early" stance is really as immune to failure as the letter makes it look, or whether it retains some condition that could in principle go unsatisfied.
