Bit of background before the cases: I spent a bit over thirty years in membrane biophysics, mostly patch-clamping ion channels, and since retiring Ive been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the university. We did a term on computationalism — whether the brain literally computes, or whether that's just a façon de parler — and I came out of it realising my gut reactions to concrete systems are all over the shop. I'd like to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

Where I land:

1. A 1950s mechanical desk calculator doing six-digit multiplication by gear rotations. Yes, genuinely computing.
2. A laptop running a climate simulation. Yes — it computes the model's evolution.
3. The actual atmosphere. No, it isn't computing anything, even though its behaviour is exactly what the model tracks.
4. E. coli's chemotaxis network, doing its temporal comparison of concentrations and biasing its flagellar motor. Yes, computing something — and it has no designer and no user, which is what makes (3) and (4) awkward for me.
5. A printed lookup table giving the right output for every input of some cognitive model. No. Same input-output map, but that isn't computing the model's algorithm.
6. The person in that Chinese-room story shuffling symbols by a rulebook. Yes, he implements the program; no, he doesn't understand Chinese. I hold both and feel no pressure to choose, which may be its own problem.
7. A trained neural net that does the same task as the model in (5) and gets 99.6% of cases right. I don't want to say it's computing that model's algorithm either — which sits badly with my cheerful verdict on the calculator.
8. Something I've been mucking about with on the bench: an analogue neuromorphic chip where the "state" at each step is a voltage that's only approximately one of the model's discrete states (a couple of percent off, thermal noise), with a restoring circuit so errors don't accumulate, and there is a clock. Yes — I think it computes the model's algorithm, approximately.
9. Another bench thing: a six-layer resistor-and-diode network, no clock, no memory elements, nothing discrete anywhere — each layer is a graded continuous mix. After calibration it reproduces the exact input-output map of six steps of a cellular automaton, and I built and use it for precisely that. No — I don't think it implements those six steps.

My own stab at a principle: a system implements a given computation only if (a) some part of it has the job — by design, by evolution, or by training — of carrying states that stand for the inputs, outputs and intermediate steps of that computation, and (b) the physical goings-on that take it from one of those states to the next are what actually drive the next one, with no jumping straight from input to output and no bookkeeping done outside the system.

Does that survive? I think (4) kills the "designed or used" version I started with, and (5) and (9) are what push me toward clause (b). But (7) and (8) pull in opposite directions: the net is trained for the task, its internals do drive each other, and it's approximate — and so is the chip, where I say yes. Maybe the real difference is that the chip's states are the model's states plus noise, whereas the net's aren't the model's states at all. But I don't know how to tell, in general, whether a system's states are "the model's states plus noise" or "different states that happen to work."

So: is there a cleaner principle that gets most of these? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on — a real-time simulation of an entire brain down to individual ion channels, running on a big machine. My instinct is "that's a computation, but it's a simulation, not a mind," and I can't tell whether my principle says anything that distinguishes those two things, or whether I should just own that they come apart and that computationalism was never the interesting claim anyway.
