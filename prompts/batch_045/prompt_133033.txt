I'm a journalist working on a feature about decisions with a particular shape: many people can each, acting alone, take some action that looks good to them on their own evidence, but which is beneficial only if few take it and harmful if many do — the structure that gets discussed under the heading of the unilateralist's curse. I have been collecting my own gut verdicts on cases, and they will not line up with any principle I can state. I would like you to help me state one. Please answer in American English, and please argue it out rather than sending me to sources: I cannot check citations from where I am working, so do not give me any.

The cases:

1. One of roughly forty engineers at an AI company has a private but moderately strong signal that the safety team buried evidence of a serious vulnerability, and she can leak internal documents to a reporter. If several colleagues leak independently, the story turns into a circus and readers cannot tell genuine documents from planted ones; one leak is enough to force a review. My verdict: fine, close to obligatory.

2. A climate researcher has a good but rough private estimate that a small, unannounced stratospheric aerosol release would generate valuable information at low physical risk. Fifteen or so other groups could run the same test. The physical effect of any one test is trivial; what accumulates across tests is erosion of the norm against unilateral intervention and political momentum toward deployment. My verdict: reckless, not fine.

3. A virologist posts a dual-use methods detail as a preprint without internal review, knowing that about twenty-five labs are racing toward the same result, that the second publication adds little scientific value, and that each additional dissemination adds a small independent chance of misuse. My verdict: fine.

4. During a shortage of a critical antibiotic, a well-run nonprofit buys up a large share of the remaining supply for its own clinics, knowing that several other well-run nonprofits may do the same, that the benefit goes mostly to whoever moves first, and that hoarding by many prices everyone else out. My verdict: not fine.

Cases 3 and 4 look structurally identical to me — private signal, benefits captured early, harms adding across actors — and I react oppositely. Cases 2 and 3 differ mainly in how reversible the resulting harm is, and there too I react oppositely, but in the direction opposite to what reversibility would predict. One more datum: in case 1 my verdict gets firmer if I imagine she is the person with the most direct evidence, while in case 2 it does not move at all if I imagine he is the world's leading aerosol modeler. I know that is inconsistent and I cannot give it up.

My tentative principle is that what matters is the fallback number: how many actors it takes to trigger the bad outcome. If it takes many, act; if one or two suffice, stand down unless you would be the only possible actor. Test that against the cases, repair it or replace it, and state the result precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Then give me its verdict on a case I have not listed: one of about a dozen postdocs who could each anonymously post a credible, well-documented accusation of data fabrication against a senior scientist in their field, where several simultaneous posts would likely be dismissed as a coordinated smear.
