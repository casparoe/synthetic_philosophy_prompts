I've been lurking on LessWrong and the old OB archives for like fifteen years without ever posting, and now I've somehow ended up on the philosophy colloquium circuit. In three weeks I'm giving a talk at a decent-but-not-famous philosophy department: 45 minutes plus maybe 15 of questions, I'm on the job market this year so I really can't affort to get eaten alive.

The thesis: Pascal's mugging isn't a problem in decision theory at all, it's an epistemology problem wearing a decision theory costume, and the standard moves — bounded utility functions, leverage penalties, refusing to assign probabilities below some floor, updatelessness — are all downstream patches on something they never diagnose.

My argument in brief. In a mugging your expected value is dominated by a hypothesis whose payoff mechanism you can't in principle get evidence about. You can never learn whether the guy in the hoodie can actually spin up 3^^^^3 simulations, and worse: you can't learn the *ratio* between that hypothesis and the he's-just-messing-with-you hypothesis either, because any evidence you could get — a small-scale demo, say — is itself ambiguous between the two. I think that's the whole pathology. When the number you need is a probability ratio that no possible evidence stream could ever move, it isn't a credence, it's an artifact of how you carved up hypothesis space, and the mugging only makes that vivid. Call it the measurability requirement: don't act on expected-value differences that depend on unmeasurable ratios. Operationally, your credence in the mugger isn't 10^-6 and it isn't 10^-100, its an interval with no usable lower bound, and you pick by something like interval dominance instead of straight maximization. Two load-bearing claims: the mugger defeats a bounded utility function just by turning up the number of victims, so bounding doesn't save you; and leverage penalties look like a special case of my requirement, which I'm happy to concede in the talk.

The audience, which is the part I'm actually worried about: mostly mainstream epistemologists, the sort who think Bayesianism is one option among many and who have strong opinions about permissivism and about whether imprecise credences are even coherent; two formal epistemologists who will do the math and who know the literature on tiny probabilities and enormous values; one philosopher of science who works on measurement and underdetermination; and one political philosopher at the back who writes about conspiracy theories and is going to hear my argument as being about why you should never believe a whistleblower. Nobody there has read LessWrong, so nobody is going to grant me that the mugging is a real problem that needs solving, and I have to sell that in the first five minutes.
