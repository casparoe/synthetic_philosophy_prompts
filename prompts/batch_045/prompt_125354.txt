I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I have been trying for some months to work out what to say about the Everettian claim to have *derived* the Born rule. As I understand the state of play: if the universal quantum state never collapses and every outcome encoded in it is realised somewhere, then the thing that needs explaining is not why one outcome happened rather than another but why an agent about to perform a measurement should apportion confidence by squared amplitude rather than simply counting branches. The three main strategies I keep meeting are the decision-theoretic one associated with Deutsch and Wallace, where a representation theorem is supposed to show that any agent satisfying certain rationality constraints must bet as if the Born rule held; the symmetry-based argument from envariance associated with Zurek; and the self-locating-uncertainty or typicality line, where one is supposed to reason about how to distribute credence across one's future branches before the outcome is known. Everyone also seems to agree that naive branch-counting is dead on arrival, since if branches were counted rather than amplitude-weighted then overwhelmingly most branches would show statistics that flatly contradict what our laboratories actually record, so the data we already have is doing some work here. Meanwhile the sceptics — Kent is the name that comes up most often — reply that the derivations smuggle in what they need, that the rationality or symmetry premises are either unmotivated or already equivalent to the conclusion, and that a framework which reproduces the predictions of collapse theories by construction while multiplying unobservable structure has an awkward problem about what, if anything, confirms it.

What puzzles me is not who is right but why nothing moves. It has been a couple of decades since the decision-theoretic turn, nobody in the dispute seems ignorant of the other side's arguments, and the temperature of the exchange has barely changed: Everettians increasingly write as though the problem were solved and merely in need of exposition, while critics write as though it had not even been well posed. My half-formed hypothesis is that the two main strategies are answering different questions under one heading — the decision-theoretic argument telling us what a rational agent embedded in a branching world should bet, the symmetry and typicality arguments telling us something about the physical weight or "amount" of a branch — and that a hidden *formal* question is doing most of the work, namely whether the Born weights are the unique measure over branches satisfying modest symmetry and additivity constraints, or whether alternative measures can be made to satisfy the same constraints with different-looking premises. If that is right, progress would be a theorem about uniqueness or a clean demonstration that the premises are independent of the conclusion. But I suspect something else is going on as well: a bedrock disagreement about what a physical theory owes us, where some parties want a recipe for setting credences and others want an account of how often outcomes occur inside the one world they think there is, and no theorem could settle that. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a verdict — an account of what kind of disagreement this is, whether there is a hidden formal or normative question underneath it, whether my two-part hypothesis is anywhere near the mark, and, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a representation theorem, a no-go result, an experimental scenario in which a mistaken measure would show up, or an argument that the question should be abandoned in its present form. I should also say that I may have mischaracterised the derivations as being in better formal shape than they are, and if so I would rather be corrected than confirmed.
