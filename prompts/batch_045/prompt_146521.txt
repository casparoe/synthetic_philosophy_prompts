I'm reading a pop-genetics book (not by a philosopher, as far as I can tell) and its chapter on "information" says something like this: DNA is a code in the strict literal sense, not a metaphor. It has a four-letter alphabet, three-letter words, and a lookup table matching each word to one amino acid, and the match is arbitrary the way any code's is — nothing about the chemistry of a particular codon suits the amino acid it stands for. What makes it a code rather than just a cause is that something has to hold the codebook, and that's the transfer RNAs: the ribosome reads the codon, the tRNA shows up carrying the amino acid the codebook assigns to it. That's why biologists can say the genome contains the instructions for building an organism, and why knowing a gene's sequence gives you the protein's sequence but never the reverse, which is what people call the central dogma. And it means the information in DNA is the semantic kind, like the information in a sentence, not the merely causal kind you get from tree rings or from a scratched rock that tells you a glacier came through: a codon means leucine the way the word "cat" means cat. The odd part is that nobody wrote it and nobody reads it as a message — it's meaning without a mind, meaning natural selection built.

The "meaning without a mind" line is where I start to doubt it, and I also vaguely remember hearing that the code isn't fully arbitrary after all — that there's some weak chemical affinity and that the table is arranged so copying mistakes do the least damage. Go through it and tell me which sentences a working molecular biologist would sign off on, which are simplifications, and which quietly slide from one claim to another. Is "central dogma" being used correctly here? Is the codebook in the right place, or is the book putting something in the tRNA that actually lives elsewhere? And if the arbitrariness claim is weaker than stated, does "DNA is literally a code" survive, and does the further move to "instructions for building an organism" follow from any of it? Someone also told me the genome is a recipe rather than a blueprint and that this distinction is the real issue — is that the same point the book is missing or a different one, and does it decide anything? Its not like I have a stake in this, I just want to know how much of the chapter to beleive.
