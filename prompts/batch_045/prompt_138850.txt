Been re-reading the CEV stuff after a thread on LessWrong and I've finally written down where I've landed. I'd like someone to tell me if it hangs together or if I'm helping myself to something.

(1) The whole point of idealising is to fix errors. So I'm happy for the procedure to overrule my current preferences wholesale when they're the product of ignorance, sloppy reasoning, or not having actually tried the thing.

(2) But it's got to stay mine. If the extrapolated me ends up wanting stuff I can't see any route from here to wanting, I don't think I've got reason to care about the output, and I'd want the procedure to penalise that. There's a worry people gesture at about the anchor to the actual person vanishing under idealisation; I do want some grip left on me, definately not a free-floating ideal agent.

(3) I don't want to build a stopping point into the idealisation just to keep the output recognisable. More information, more reflection, no ceiling.

(4) I want path independence. Shuffle the order in which the extrapolated me meets the relevant experiences and information and the answer shouldn't move. Otherwise "coherent" is just measuring an artefact of the order you happened to consider things in — I've been calling this the shuffle test.

(5) I read CEV as being about each person's own values, not as merging everyone into one agent: "coherent" means VNM-ish internal consistency, the growing-up-farther-together bit is a fairness constraint on conflicts, not a single utility function over humanity.

(6) Concrete case: fifteen years into a project I'd now describe as the thing that makes my life worth living. A CEV that tells me to bin it strikes me as defective, full stop, and I don't think I should have to just swallow that verdict.

Audit it please: which pairs actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest revision and what it costs me. Order the tensions by severity rather than flagging all six as equally bad, and for each real one say what has to give.

Also: be straight about what's settled versus genuinely contested among people who actually work on idealised-preference theories — especially whether the convergence worry lurking behind (3) is real or whether I've read too much into it — and mark your own bets as bets instead of dressing them up as the consensus.

Finish with a last line beginning Verdict: and then \answer{...} containing just the numbers of the commitments you'd drop, in the order you'd give them up, e.g. \answer{3, 6} — or \answer{none} if the set is fine as it stands.
