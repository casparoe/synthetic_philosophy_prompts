You are a professional philosopher specialising in decision theory and the foundations of rational choice. Answer as a specialist would, and think carefully before you commit yourself: begin by listing five candidate concepts or labels that someone might reach for given what I describe below, without evaluating them at that stage; then check the arithmetic I sketch; then say which candidate fits best, why the others do not, and only at the end give me a name. I do not want a label; I want the carving. And please give me no references or reading list — I have no way to check them, and being told to go and read someone would be no answer at all.

The background: I spent last night arguing with a friend about a case of the following shape. A large number of people, mutually anonymous, no repetition, no reputation, no communication, each receive the same one-shot offer: contribute, and it costs you one unit, while every other person who contributes puts 0.01 units in your pocket. Your contributing does nothing to anyone else; it is causally inert with respect to everyone else's payoff, and inert with respect to your own apart from the unit it costs you. My friend took the usual line: since your act changes nothing, there is no reason to perform it, and any argument to the contrary is self-deception. I found myself reaching for something I could not quite formulate, and I want to know whether the thing I was reaching for is a real thing with a name.

What I was reaching for: suppose that, for whatever reason, whether you contribute is very slightly correlated with whether any given other person contributes — a correlation of 0.001, say, small enough that in a two-person case nobody would think it generated a reason for anything. If there are a thousand others, the correlation shifts the expected number of other contributors by about one, which is worth 0.01 to you, far less than the unit you pay. If there are a hundred thousand others, it breaks even. If there are a million, it is worth roughly ten units against a cost of one, and you should contribute. The verdict of the argument therefore flips purely with the size of the population, with the correlation and the payoffs held fixed. Check my arithmetic, and tell me whether the shift in the expected count really is about N times the correlation or whether I have the standardisation wrong.

Five features of this make me think it is not just a familiar view wearing a hat:

1. It needs no twinhood and no shared reasoning. The correlation could arise from something as mundane as everyone having read the same newspaper. So it is not the view — I believe it has a name something like super-rationality, and comes from a popular essay — on which you act as if you were choosing for everyone because you know the others reason exactly as you do.
2. It does not restore causal efficacy. It is not an answer to the objection that your act makes no difference; it concedes that and changes the subject to what your act indicates. So it should not be confused with arguments that try to show that your vote or your contribution really does make a difference after all.
3. The news seems to be borne by the aggregate rather than by any individual: learning that I contributed tells me something substantial about how many people contributed, while telling me almost nothing about whether you did. I do not know whether it is legitimate to add up a million negligible shifts in expectation and treat the sum as a reason.
4. The verdict depends on which population I count — everyone in the city, everyone in the country, everyone who read the same newspaper, everyone facing a relevantly similar decision, my future selves — and I can see no non-arbitrary stopping point. That looks either like a reductio of the argument or like a sign that I have stated it badly.
5. It generalises away from cooperation altogether. If my smoking is correlated, at 0.001, with each of a million independent small risks rather than with a single lesion, then by the same arithmetic I have an overwhelming evidential reason not to smoke, which is exactly the sort of reasoning that is supposed to be evidential decision theory's vice rather than its virtue.

A friend who reads this literature muttered something about evidential cooperation in large worlds. I do not know whether that is a real phrase, and from the little I could gather it concerns whether an evidentialist can secure cooperation in a large one-shot prisoner's dilemma — near my case, but not, I think, the thing I have isolated, which concerns the aggregation of negligible correlations, the sensitivity of the verdict to how the population is individuated, and the fact that the argument survives with no similarity between the agents at all.

So: does what I have described have an established name, and is it genuinely distinct from the neighbouring views? Tell me which nearby concept it is closest to, exactly how it differs from each, and whether the argument as I have stated it actually goes through. If nothing established fits, coin a name, give a precise definition, and give me the boundary cases the definition must get right — the correlation being exactly zero; the correlation being negative; the correlation being produced by a common cause I have already conditioned on; a population gerrymandered to include people whose acts do not affect me; and the case in which I already know with certainty what I am going to do. I would also like to know whether this is a real phenomenon, or whether I have merely confused is evidence of with makes more likely by an amount worth caring about.
