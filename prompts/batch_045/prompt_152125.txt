I'm a novelist, about two hundred pages into a book set in a women's scriptorium in the 1130s, and I've got myself into a philosophy jam. My narrator is a copyist, Aldgyth, trained on a rule her mistress gave her: reproduce the letter-forms of the exemplar exactly, except that on every ninth line of a column she is to "restore" what the previous scribe "meant" — mend the slip, don't copy it. So the rule is quus-shaped by construction: nothing in her first eight years of copying ever forced the ninth-line case. Then one winter the exemplar she's been given gets worse and worse and she starts restoring on the fourth line, then the second, and she herself can't tell — this is the scene the whole novel is built around — whether she has been mending faithfully all along or has quietly started improving the text, writing what she thinks it should say. One morning she looks down at the page and can't say, even to herself, which rule she is on.

I went and read Kripke's 1982 book on rules and private language to get the metaphysics right, and I think I now get the upshot: he showed there's no fact of the matter about which rule anyone is following — no fact about whether past usage meant plus or quus — so the only thing that can settle whether Aldgyth is copying or emending is what the people around her are prepared to ratify. Meaning just is agreement, right? Nothing deeper. Which would mean the scene I've been building toward is a lie, or at best psycology: there's no private fact for her to loose her grip on, so I should bin it and write the climax as the abbess and the sisters examining her quires in chapter and deciding what she meant. That's a real scene with real stakes, and the book doesn't need her insides at all.

Before I rip out eighty pages: is that actually what follows? I half-suspect that "what the community would accept" and "what she means" are doing different jobs, and that if meaning really is communal ratification then the abbey's own verdict should be just as much in the dark as hers is — but I can't get the pieces straight, and I don't want to build a novel on a howler.

Four sections please, roughly:
- "Where I've gone wrong" — 150 words, blunt, no hedging
- "The sceptic, Kripke, and Kripkenstein" — who is actually committed to the no-fact conclusion and who is only reporting it — 300 words
- "What the sceptical solution is a solution to" — assertability conditions versus meaning, and whether the community's own agreement is exempt from the paradox — 350 words
- "What's left for Aldgyth" — what a novelist can honestly do with a first-person scene like mine once all that is sorted — 250 words
