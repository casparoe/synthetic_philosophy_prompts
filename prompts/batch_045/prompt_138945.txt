I am drafting a novel in which a good deal of the comedy is carried by inserted documents — minutes, circulars, parish notices — and I need one more such piece: the quarterly bulletin of a small provincial natural history society that has, in all seriousness, reorganised its activities around the paradox of the ravens.

The setup is that the society exists to confirm the generalisation that all ravens are black. Some years ago it noticed the Equivalent Formulation — that the very same claim can be stated as "all non-black things are non-ravens" — and concluded, without any sense of having done something strange, that fieldwork is an inefficient way of going about its business. The bulletin I need is the issue in which the consequences of that decision become visible: the ornithological excursions are quietly discontinued, the hide's key is returned to the estate, and the society's energies are redirected towards the cataloguing of non-black non-ravens — teacups, bus tickets, a member's own boot.

What I want from you is roughly 750 words, laid out as a genuine bulletin: a masthead and issue number, a column "From the Honorary Secretary," a "Correspondence" section, "Notices," and a closing item. The voice is that of a county society's honorary secretary — formal, unhurried, faintly Edwardian in its rhythms, given to parentheses and to the gentle settling of old scores. Because this has to read as found prose, no character may use the vocabulary of confirmation theory: no footnotes, no winks, no one saying the word "paradox." Let the members name things in their own terms — the Generalisation, the Equivalent Formulation, the unit of confirmation, the Rarities Question.

The jokes must come from the epistemology being right, not from the members being stupid. Specifically, work in: the pull of the intuition that only a black raven genuinely helps while the equivalent formulation says otherwise; the point that a non-black non-raven does, on a certain way of thinking about evidence, raise the probability of the hypothesis, but by an amount that depends on how rare ravens are among non-black things and that is in any case negligible; and the fact that counting every object in the world as one more item of evidence quietly conflates confirming with proving. Use the Correspondence column for this: let one member commit a real and identifiable error — ideally the conflation just mentioned, or the assumption that every teacup contributes the same quantity of confirmation — and let the Secretary's reply correct it with pedantic precision before proceeding to a conclusion every bit as mad. There might also be a schism and a merger: a breakaway club devoted to the Equivalent Formulation, which then has to amalgamate with the parent body on the discovery that its hypothesis is not a rival one.

Keep the irony deadpan and the proposal straight-faced: nobody in the document should express philosophical doubt. If a single member objects, let the objection be social — the walks were pleasant — rather than logical. A reader with no philosophy at all should still be able to tell what is being made fun of, and a philosopher of science should find the satire fair rather than founded on a blunder no one holds.
