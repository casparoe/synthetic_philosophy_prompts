I'm in my final year of secondary school, writing a paper for a philosophy seminar on whether Buddhist ethics is best classified as a form of consequentialism, and I have noticed that nearly every source I can get my hands on leans on a single analogy. I would like that analogy audited before I build an argument on it.

The analogy is that karma is like gravity: an impersonal natural regularity rather than a judge's sentence. Nothing rewards and nothing punishes, no one keeps accounts, and when the conditions are in place the outcome simply follows, the way an unsupported object simply falls. My teacher uses this to argue that Buddhist ethics is consequentialism in religious dress: no commander, no desert, just a causal structure in which some actions reliably turn out badly, so the tradition must be ranking actions by what they bring about. A classmate uses the very same picture for the opposite conclusion. Gravity has no end in view, no better or worse, and no interest in why anyone acted, so it cannot be the model for an ethics whose organizing concern is the ending of suffering and which, as I understand it, treats karma as at bottom a matter of intention or volition rather than of results.

What I want, rather than a verdict on the classification question itself, is an audit of what this comparison can and cannot carry. Concretely:

First, the mapping, made explicit. What plays the role of mass, of the field, of the falling, of the law stated as an equation, and of the physicist who can compute the trajectory in advance? Where a slot in the source has no occupant in the target, say so plainly, because I take it that a missing slot is where the comparison is thinnest and where it is most likely to be doing quiet work.

Second, the disanalogies, sorted by whether they matter for the classificatory question or only for something else. The ones I have noticed so far: gravity is indifferent to mental states, whereas the tradition is said to make volition the heart of karma, which seems to cut directly against reading it as act-consequentialism, since consequentialism's criterion of rightness is outcomes and not motives; gravity is predictable and calculable, whereas I have read that some early sources place the precise ripening of karma among the things not to be reasoned about, which makes me suspect the word law in the analogy is a promissory note for a mechanism the target does not supply; gravity is inescapable for anything with mass, whereas the tradition's goal is precisely to stop generating karma, so that a liberated person's action is said not to produce new fruit, and there must therefore be a way of standing outside the field that the source has no room for; and gravity relates persisting bodies, whereas with no enduring self there is a question about who bears the fruit, which a force between masses cannot even model. Tell me which of these actually bear on whether Buddhist ethics is consequentialist, which only bear on whether karma is a law, and which are decoration.

Third, whether the conclusion survives. My own suspicion is that the analogy is being asked to establish a strong thesis while only establishing a weak one: that there is no external judge is compatible with a great deal besides consequentialism, including views that make character central. My classmate has a different suspicion, that gravity's value-neutrality is smuggled into karma and then retrieved as evidence for consequentialism. Please say which of these, if either, is right, and whether the answer changes depending on whether the claim under test is act-consequentialism, the weaker thesis that the tradition is teleological because everything is ordered to the cessation of suffering, or the still different thesis that skillful means makes it an optimizing doctrine.

Fourth, a replacement, if there is one. The candidate I keep coming back to is erosion: an action carves a channel, and the channel makes the next action of the same kind more likely, so that the consequence is of the same stuff as the action rather than something imposed on it from outside, there is no ledger and no judge, and escape is a matter of changing the terrain rather than of being released from a debt. If that is an improvement, say what it improves, and note the awkward consequence I have noticed, which is that it makes the tradition look aretaic rather than consequentialist, so that improving the analogy appears to flip the conclusion. Is that a sign the original comparison was carrying its own conclusion, or a sign that my replacement is quietly carrying a rival one?

Two practical notes: please do not give me a reading list or tell me which texts to cite, since I have no way to get most of them; and where a point depends on which strand of the tradition one has in mind, say what turns on it rather than picking silently. An answer in the region of eight hundred words, with the correspondence set out as a table, would suit me best.
