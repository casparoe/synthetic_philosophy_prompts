I teach a second-year undergraduate module in the metaphysics of mind, and I am reworking next term's session on the combination problem. The students will arrive able to state the knowledge argument and the zombie argument, about a third of them are concurrently taking a cognitive science course where integration is treated as whatever global-workspace or integrated-information-style measures capture, and the rest, on first hearing the word panpsychism, hear either animism or idealism. I have eighty minutes, and I have taught this session four times; each time it decays into a re-run of the hard problem, and the students leave with a refreshed sense that consciousness is puzzling rather than with a grasp of the specific difficulty that the combination problem poses. So I am asking you for a plan for the explanation, not for the explanation.

The apparatus I have been drafting, and which I want you to stress-test rather than take over, is this. Instead of opening with the usual threefold division into a subject problem, a quality problem and a structure problem, I have been using one invented case to carry the whole session — call the creature a choral animal: each of its micro-constituents has a thin, determinate experience; the animal's own reportable experience is a single unified field; and both facts are stipulated rather than explained. I want that case to generate three questions in order: how many subjects are there; what fixes the level of decomposition, that is, why the story is told at the level of cells or neurons or particles and not at the level of the whole animal or of one hemisphere; and, last, why the resulting quality is the determinate one it is rather than a blur or a kind of noise. I also want to add a fourth question, which I have been calling the inheritance problem: even granting that the unified subject exists, what makes it inherit these contents rather than the contents of one part alone. I am genuinely unsure whether that is a distinct problem or the quality problem in different clothes; tell me if it collapses, and into what.

Concretely, then: what has to be in place before the choral animal is introduced, and why does it have to be in place first; what the carrying example must have and must lack, and why the familiar colony or hive-mind cases are the wrong vehicle; the misconceptions the students will form in the order they will form them — I am expecting some version of "this is just the hard problem again," "emergence or causation dissolves it," "integration measures explain unity," "if the micro-experiences are thin enough there is nothing left to explain," and "nothing fuses at all, the macro-subject is simply the whole that the parts constitute" — together with the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one question I can put to them in the final five minutes that would show they have hold of the problem rather than merely a mood of puzzlement; and what I should say, out loud and explicitly, that I am declining to explain at this level.
