I am a junior at a large public high school, and for about eighteen months I have been building, almost entirely on my own, a piece of scheduling software for our school's peer-tutoring program. Two weeks ago the district licensed a free platform that already does roughly four-fifths of what mine was meant to do, and does it more reliably. When I told a friend that I still wanted to finish — because the remaining work is small, because I gave the tutoring coordinator my word that it would be ready by spring, and because I have noticed that I want to abandon projects at precisely this stage and have learned not to trust that impulse — she replied, "Don't throw good money after bad," meaning my time, and seemed to regard the conversation as closed.

I have since learned that this has a name, the sunk cost fallacy, but the name does not tell me what exactly is supposed to be fallacious, and I notice the slogan doing rather different work in different mouths. My friend used it to argue that I must stop. Last month she finished a 700-page novel she was not enjoying, on the grounds that she had already read 400 pages of it; when I pointed out the tension, she said that case was different because "it's just a book." I could not tell whether that distinction is principled or merely convenient.

What I would like is a menu of the precise claims the sentence could be making, from the near-trivial to the strong, with a verdict on each. The rough sortings I have managed on my own, which I suspect overlap one another or leave things out, are these:

1. Bookkeeping: time and money already spent cannot be recovered, and so do not appear among the future costs of any option now open to me — though what the spending produced, a half-finished program and a set of skills, plainly does appear.
2. Debiasing: people systematically let past investment inflate their estimate of future prospects, so the sentence is a corrective rule of thumb rather than a principle, and can overshoot when the error runs the other way.
3. Reasons: that a cost's lying in the past disqualifies it from being a reason for action at all, as opposed to merely not being a cost of any remaining option.
4. Strategy: that other people respond to whether one is known to persist, so a reputation for finishing, or an ex ante willingness to absorb losses, changes what others do to you — which would make honouring sunk costs useful rather than confused.
5. Self-binding: that I may do better across a life to adopt in advance a policy of not abandoning things once costs are sunk, precisely because my later selves are unreliable judges of whether to continue.
6. Evidence: that my past self's willingness to spend a year and a half is itself evidence, defeasible but real, about the value of the project, which my present discouragement should not simply override.

Which of these are genuinely distinct, which collapse into one another, which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are straightforwardly false? Are there readings I have missed — in particular, is there one on which the phrase quietly assumes what is at issue, given that calling the earlier investment bad seems already to presuppose that the project has failed? And what I chiefly want to know: for each reading that survives, does it actually support my friend's advice to me, and does it rule out the three reasons I gave for continuing? Where the surviving readings diverge on my case, say so, and tell me which reading someone in her position is most likely to have meant.
