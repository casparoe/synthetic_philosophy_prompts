I teach a second-year cognitive science seminar called Consciousness and Cognition, and I am rewriting the mid-term diagnostic. Last year's version failed in a way I want to avoid: I let theoretical labels into the questions, so students sorted themselves by how they felt about a word rather than by what they think happens in the cases, and two people who gave opposite answers on every case ended up in the same bucket. Since then I have also been arguing with a friend who insists that global workspace theory and the phenomenal-overflow view are the same claim in different vocabularies, and I want an instrument that settles whether they are. So what I need is a decision tree, not an essay: a worksheet my students can walk through in fifty minutes, starting from a single shared commitment (that there is a real distinction between a state's being experienced and its being available to the rest of the mind), and ending at a named position.

The design constraints matter more to me than the coverage. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: begin by laying out the relations between the experiential and the availability-based notion that my branch questions could in principle generate (identity, experiential exceeding available, available exceeding experiential, mere partial overlap, and the option that one of the two notions is too confused to bear weight), then cross that with a second axis I want the tree to respect — whether the relevant availability is personal-level (available for reasoning, voluntary action, and report) or merely subpersonal (available to any consumer system whatever, including motor routines and specialized modules that could not themselves be conscious). Then say which cells of that grid the actual views occupy, which are empty, and for each empty cell whether it is empty because incoherent, because merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to work it out. Nine branch questions, six to eight leaves; the leaves must include the workspace-identity view, the overflow view, the no-overflow reply that treats the richness of experience as an illusion, a higher-order view, a first-order representationalist view, an illusionist or eliminativist view, and at least one view on which what matters is integration and causal structure rather than capacity-limited sharing. Each leaf gets its name (or unoccupied, plus a one-sentence diagnosis) and one sentence on the least attractive thing it commits its holder to. Every branch must be phrased so that a student who has never heard any of those names can answer it — avoid the jargon terms in the questions themselves; if one is unavoidable, gloss it in the same sentence — and each branch should turn on a concrete case rather than on a claim about a claim. Please use at least these: the Sperling-style partial-report situation; its degraded-array successor in which performance exceeds a four-item capacity but subjects deny having seen the item that changed; blindsight, in both the variety where the subject reports nothing and the variety where they report a feeling that something happened; a visual extinction patient who guesses above chance and shows autonomic responses while denying seeing; inattentional blindness; a vivid dream recalled only on waking; a pre-verbal infant or a non-human animal with a capacity-limited bottleneck but no possibility of verbal report; an engineered system — a distributed server farm or a robot — that really instantiates the bottleneck-and-broadcast architecture without the biology; and a no-report paradigm in which content is recovered from eye movements or brain activity with no overt response demanded. At least one node should force the choice between the extra content being lost before it can be used and its being present but only in a degraded, gist-like form, and one should force a view about whether a subject's own insistence that they saw something settles the question.

Two more things. First, flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — workspace-identity and the higher-order view are my suspicion — and, for each such pair, supply the additional question that would separate them together with a note on why you kept it off the tree, for instance because nobody could answer it without already knowing which theory it favours. Second, do not spend words on recommended reading, further reading, or citations: I have the reading list, and I want the instrument itself.
