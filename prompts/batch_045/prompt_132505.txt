I have spent three years on the committee that advises my university on what to do with the names on its buildings and the statues on its grounds, and the arguments have stopped moving. They have not stopped moving because we disagree about history. Nobody at the table denies that Jefferson enslaved people, that Columbus's governorship in the Caribbean involved the enslavement and destruction of the Taíno, or that Churchill said and did things about India and about race that no one would now defend. The defenders of each man appeal to what else he did — the Declaration, the opening of sustained contact across the Atlantic, the year 1940 — rather than to a rival account of the bad part. What we cannot settle is what follows from facts everyone already grants, so I want the implicit theory of the practice rather than another verdict on a man.

The pattern I want reconstructed has four features. First: the man-of-his-time defence is accepted for some figures and rejected for others, and the marker we actually use — though we never say so — is whether anybody at the time disagreed out loud. Abolitionists existed, critics of empire sat in Parliament, missionaries protested what was being done to the Taíno; therefore the defence fails. Call this the dissenters test. Notice what it does not ask: not whether this agent could have known better, but whether someone, somewhere in his epistemic neighbourhood, did. That looks less like an excuse ('he was ignorant') than like a demand for due diligence measured against what was available to his community, and I would like to know what view of responsibility that is, whether it is coherent, and what exactly would have to be true for the defence to succeed under it — that no one anywhere dissented, that the dissent was inaccessible to him, that he was occupied with other things?

Second, and this is the inconsistency that puzzles me most: we index the wrongs to their time but not the achievements. Slavery is mitigated by the fact that nearly everyone then accepted it; the authorship of the Declaration, the voyage, the war leadership are assessed as if no such indexing applied, and we go on praising them at full value. There is no symmetric move on our side of the table — nobody argues that Churchill's conduct in 1940 is less creditable because thousands of his countrymen would have done the same in his place. So either the committee is simply inconsistent, or honouring was never a verdict on the man's overall desert in the first place. I can think of two ways out that don't require inconsistency: that public honour is expressive and forward-looking, a statement about what we now want to mark rather than a judgement about him; or that a name is a kind of quotation, so that naming a school after Jefferson cites the equality sentence without endorsing the life, the way quoting a line doesn't commit you to the rest of the speech. I don't know whether either of those is really what we are doing.

Third: our available dispositions — remove, retain and add an explanatory plaque, move the object into a museum, leave it and commission a counter-monument nearby — seem to presuppose different accounts of what a monument does. The plaque option only makes sense if a monument's meaning is carried by its inscription and can therefore be revised in place; removal only makes sense if the meaning is carried by the brute fact of public honour and cannot be revised that way. Can a single institution hold both, or does accepting the plaque option quietly destroy the case for ever removing anything?

Fourth, there is a rationale that keeps surfacing and that I suspect is a different argument entirely: names on buildings funded by wealth derived from the wrong are treated differently from statues of the same man, and the reason offered is about what we may now go on profiting from rather than about what he deserved. And we seem to treat a figure's being inseparable from the wrong very differently across cases — Churchill's imperial commitments were not incidental to his career, Columbus's conduct was not incidental to his voyages, Jefferson's slaveholding was not incidental to his life — yet the committee's appetite for removal varies sharply between them in a way I cannot reconstruct from any principle we have stated.

My own hypothesis is that we are running two incompatible theories at once, a culpability theory for removal and an expressive or forward-looking theory for retention, and that nearly every stalemate is a case where they return opposite answers. I want that tested against what the practice actually does rather than confirmed. So: reconstruct the theory of moral responsibility and of commemoration that this practice presupposes; distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several theories would license what we do; tell me which of our recurring disagreements are verbal — is the word 'honour' doing double duty? — and which are substantive; and then say what our practice would have to look like if we replaced the presupposed view with, in turn, a purely forward-looking consequentialist view of commemoration, a strict desert-based view, and the quotation view. If the honest answer is that no single theory is in the background and that the practice is a modus vivendi between people who want incompatible things, say that, and tell me what it would take to find out.
