I host a tiny philosophy podcast I record on my commute — i'm a software engineer by day, so assume zero background in the literature. Next week's guest is a moral philosopher who defends what she calls the authority-transfer account of promising: a promise is the exercise of a normative power that hands the promisee a limited say over some chunk of your future conduct, and that transfer — not anybody's expectations, and not how useful the practice is — is what makes the promise bind. I've read one of her papers and her move is always to ask what the rival view explains better, so I want questions that don't leave her that exit.

Give me eight questions, each with a followup aimed at the answer she's most likely to give. At least one each on: promises the promisee doesn't care about at all (no expectation, no interest, maybe they forget), where it feels off to say you'd be wronging them by not bothering; promising to do what you already have to do (pay a debt you owe, do the job you're paid for), and whether promissory duty adds anything or just double-counts; whether the power is something we invented by convention or something we just have — if invented, promises shouldn't bind where nobody invented them, and if not, why does it need her acceptance and why does the shape of the duty swing so much with context; deathbed promises, where the person who'd hold the say is gone; promises made under a threat, where on her own terms the power still looks exercised. And one from my actual job: my team runs an on-call bot that tells people "I'll page the on-call engineer within five minutes," everyone knows it's just code with no will, and people still get annoyed when it doesn't — ask whether anything promissory is going on, and press whatever she says (I bet she'll call it assurance without obligation, so have the followup ready).

Two sentences max per question-plus-followup, whole thing under 450 words, and no "what got you into promising" openers.
