I'm running a film-and-ideas night for a bunch of undergrads and we're doing Avatar, and I want to read it against the original affluent society thesis — Sahlins's argument that foragers were affluent because wants are finite and few rather than because means are great, so affluence is a ratio not a pile of stuff, and agriculture didn't mark an unambiguous escape from misery. But I keep hitting a snag: the Na'vi aren't really Sahlins's case at all. They're semi-sedentary with hereditary chiefs and priests, they ride giant flying mounts, and at the climax Eywa literally sends the animals to fight for them — so Pandora's ecology, plus a planetary nervous system that answers prayer, seems to be doing the work that Sahlins assigns to culture (limited wants). That makes the film look like it's dramatizing a rival explanation of forager ease: abundance of nature instead of scarcity of desire, which arguably undercuts the thesis's whole anti-progress debunking move. I also want to know how this sits with the usual complaint that the movie is a noble-savage fantasy with a white saviour, since Sahlins's point was meant as a deflationary empirical correction, not a romance. Which scenes would you point the group at, where does the film resist the reading, and does Jake ending up permanently uploaded into a Na'vi body actually make the "you can't opt back out" point better than the anthropology does?
