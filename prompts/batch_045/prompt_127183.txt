So I'm TAing a first-year intro philosophy course and we've got a philosophy-through-fiction week, and I volunteered to run a fifty-minute seminar on the problem of other minds using Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go (2005 novel — I'm not teaching the film). I want a reading that's actually load-bearing philosophically, not the version where you say look, the clones have feelings too, everyone nods, and nobody has learned anything about why the problem of other minds is hard.

Here's roughly where I am with it, so you know what to push against. The problem as we teach it is evidential: I see your behaviour, not your pain, so what licenses the step from the one to the other — argument from analogy, inference to the best explanation, or the move (in the Wittgensteinian-ish tradition) that my relation to another person isn't a hypothesis at all but something more like an attitude I take up. But the novel doesn't seem to set it up as evidential. The guardians at Hailsham aren't short of evidence about Tommy and Kathy and Ruth; they raise them, teach them, watch them for years, and then the artwork Madame collects turns out to have been taken to prove to the outside world that the students had inner lives at all, a proof that was supposed to work on strangers, not on the people who already knew them. And the adults never quite lie to the students either — they leave things unspecified, in a way that lets everyone not-know on purpose. So I've started calling what's going on the conferred-minds problem: the thought that what's at stake isn't whether anyone can *know* the clones have minds but whether they get counted as having them, and that being counted looks like a status a community confers or withholds rather than a fact someone could in principle fail to detect. I want you to take that seriously and develop it, but also tell me honestly if it's just the sociological version of the question, i.e. changing the subject from metaphysics and epistemology to politics and hoping nobody notices.

Two more things I need, and one of them is an objection I can't answer. First, my supervisor says the whole thing is a cheat: the novel hands us three hundred pages of Kathy's first-person narration, so of course we're certain she has a mind — the skeptic would be entirely unmoved, since the device just supplies by literary fiat the very thing the philosophical problem says we don't have. What's the strongest reply to that, and where is it soft? Relatedly: Kathy's narration is famously evasive, she never quite says what she feels about Ruth, or about Tommy, or about her own donations, so the book also dramatizes a kind of failure of self-knowledge. Does that matter here, or is it a separate topic? Second: is what's wrong with the guardians best described as doubt at all? Miss Emily at the end is polite and detached and explains everything, and she seems to know perfectly well. So is this skepticism, or is it something else — a failure of uptake, or a standing where people make themselves comfortable with what they know — and does it make a philosophical difference which one we call it? Point me at the specific scenes you'd put on the handout (I'm thinking the gallery, the deferral rumour the students half-know is false, the Norfolk trip to look for Ruth's possible, and Madame weeping when she watches Kathy dance), say what each of them does, and tell me where the reading breaks down — including whether the popular allegorical reading, where the clones just stand in for all of us facing mortality, quietly repeats the exact denial the book is about. End with one discussion question I can leave them with.
