So I'm finally turning the archaeology manuscript into an actual book, and before I commit to the argument I want somebody to try to break it properly, in writing, before a reviewer does it for me.

Here's the position. I want to argue that patterns in the material record can warrant ascriptions of norms and institutions, not just of behaviour. Not merely "they repeatedly put three vessels in the north-east corner of the house," but "this was a rule: houses had a deposit that had to be made, and the household was the sort of thing that had to make it." My bridge is a two-part test I've been calling counterfactual robustness plus costly redundancy. I say a pattern supports an institutional reading when (1) it survives changes that should have broken the merely habitual or merely convenient explanation — the staple crop shifts, the houses get rebuilt on a new plan, the population turns over enough that nobody is doing it because their grandmother did — and (2) the pattern is expensive to produce and does no visible work, so the "it's just what was efficient" story isn't available. Habit tracks convenience; a norm shouldn't. That gap is doing all the work in the book, and I want to know how load-bearing it actually is.

Take the case I keep using. Four centuries of a tell: in every house, in every phase, the same three vessel forms get placed in the same corner of the same room, under the floor, unbroken, with no contents anyone can identify. This runs straight through a shift from cattle to sheep, two rebuilds, and a period where the pottery styles otherwise change completely. It costs real labour to make vessels you immediately bury. My claim is that this is enough, on its own, to say there was a norm about houses and deposits rather than a widely shared habit.

So: what would count against this? Not "be sceptical about archaeology" — I want the specific defeaters, ranked fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones that would kill the argument from the ones that would only force me to shrink its scope.

Some things I'm already worried about and want you to press harder than I have:

- A natural formation process can produce exactly the signature I'm calling robust. Refuse gets redeposited in the low corner of a room by water or by sweeping, over and over, through rebuilds and population turnover, and the vessels were "buried" because that's where the fill came from. If both a norm and a taphonomic process predict persistence-through-change, what would actually tell them apart, and does the answer secretly assume I already know which deposits are intentional?
- Even granting the pattern is behavioural, I can't see how I get from regularity to rule. Fifty households with the same taste produce the same record as fifty households following a norm. What would the record have to look like for my test to distinguish enforcement from convergent preference? If it can't, is that fatal or just a limit on which institutions I can reach?
- My harshest reader says objects only ever give you behaviour, and that norms need texts or informants, full stop. What should I concede to her, and what would she have to concede to me — is there any result that would move someone holding that view?

I'd also like the defeaters you think a committed defender of this style of inference would accept as counting against it, as opposed to the ones they'd wriggle out of, plus anything outside archaeology the view stands or falls with. It smells to me like it's hitched to some thesis about what a rule is — whether a regularity with a certain counterfactual profile just is a rule, or whether you need attitudes, uptake, sanctions. If it's hitched to that, say so and say which way the dependency runs.

And be honest about whether I've insulated it. I keep adding the clause "where there is independent support from texts or ethnography," and I cant tell anymore wether thats a qualification or a retreat. If it's a retreat, is there a version of the claim that still risks something? What does my view predict that my colleague's doesn't — I think it predicts systematic blind spots, institutional facts that leave no trace at all, and that I should be able to say in advance which ones those are; if I can't specify them ahead of time, is the view unfalsifiable?

For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most isn't a false positive. It's a well-documented historical case where we know from documents that a norm of the relevant kind existed and orderly left no material trace whatsoever. False negatives are what I lie awake about, so if you think that's the wrong thing to be afraid of, tell me that too.
