I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and over the next year I have roughly 300 hours of evenings and weekends to spend on one corner of epistemology: the regress problem and the standoff between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. I want to finish that year holding a position I can defend in writing on one specific question, namely whether a chain of reasons that never terminates can justify anything for a finite agent like me. I would rather spend the time on live questions than on surveys of positions already staked out, and I am not asking for reading recommendations.

Here is what I bring to it, which I suspect the debate does not always separate cleanly. In my own work, a definition that never bottoms out in a base case is vicious in exactly one way: mutual recursion with no base case fails to terminate, so it delivers no output. But the same shape of structure, written corecursively with productive guards, is a perfectly well-defined object: an infinite stream, a bisimulation, a non-well-founded graph. A finite checker can validate any finite fragment of it on demand without ever holding the whole thing. So the bare fact that a regress is infinite does not look like a defect; it looks like a defect only if justification has to be a completed, well-founded trace that an agent traverses end to end. My hunch is that the real fight is not about infinity but about what justification is for: a completed structure, or a finite capacity to extend and locally check one. I also suspect the deep difficulty for coherentism is under-determination rather than circularity, since a globally consistent set of mutually supporting beliefs admits many solutions, the way a system of equations with no boundary conditions does; and that the deep difficulty for foundationalism is saying what makes a base case non-inferred yet still truth-conducive, rather than merely stipulated the way an axiom is.

Given that framing and that background, which five questions should I actually work on, in what order, and why? Give me exactly five numbered items, strictly ordered, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing line. Every item should name one question and carry its own case for its rank: what it is upstream of, whether it is tractable for someone who can read logic and handle formal machinery but has no graduate training in philosophy, which criterion you are trading off against which, and what piece of evidence or argument would move it up or down the list. If a question is important but effectively closed to someone with my preparation, or important but neglected for a bad reason, say that rather than flattering me.
