my friend is about to submit a phd thesis on decision theory and is completely sick of the standard examples, so as a thesis-submission present i want to give her a sestina about Death in Damascus. rules below, sorry if its fussy.

setup (my version, tweaked a bit from the usual telling): in Damascus a man meets Death, who tells him he's coming for him tomorrow. the man can stay or take the road to Aleppo. Death isn't going to follow him and isn't watching him either — he filled in his appointment book the night before by running a copy of the man in a locked room and writing down where the copy woke up. so nothing travels from the man's choice to Death's pen: the two agree without either causing the other. dont worry about making the metaphysics of the copy airtight, i just want the poem to feel like correlation without causation.

what the poem has to get right, without ever naming a theory:
- whichever city he ends up in is the city where the accident is waiting. not because Death chased him, but because the appointment was written down from the choice.
- the loop: the moment he settles on staying, Damascus looks fatal and Aleppo looks like the safe one; the moment he settles on fleeing, it flips back. no choice survives being chosen, so let the stanzas keep changing their mind.
- the only way out anybody has found is to not decide: flip a coin. not because the coin is lucky, but because once it's 50/50 every option looks equally bad and deliberation finally has somewhere to stop. and then the coin lands and he wants to switch immediately.

form: sestina — six stanzas of six lines, end-words rotating in the standard pattern (or invent a rotation and stick to it if you cant remember the real one), then a three-line envoi using all six words. i'd like the end-words to be Damascus, Aleppo, road, copy, coin, tomorrow; swap one if you're really stuck but keep both city names. the form should be doing the arguing: the same six words arrive in every stanza no matter how he rearranges himself, the way the appointment does. note that Damascus and Aleppo don't rhyme and shouldn't be forced to — they're paired by the pattern rather than by sound, like the man and the copy.

give the last three lines to the moment after the coin has landed: he's already on the road, already wants to turn back, and Death is surprised to see him. Death's surprise should be real and not smug, since on my version he didn't follow anybody, he only wrote down what the copy did.

she should be able to tell which move is which and not wince at any of it, and somebody who's never heard of decision theory should still just get a decent poem about a man on a road.
