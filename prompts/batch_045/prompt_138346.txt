**The question (Question 3 of 4)**

In the writings of 1888–89 Nietzsche repeatedly makes claims of the following four kinds.

(a) *Generic and cultural.* A people's cuisine, climate, and regimen are the real source of its morality and of the character of its intellectual life. His favorite example is the German case: heavy beer-drinking, bad cooking, and sluggish digestion are named as the physiological ground of German idealism and of what he mocks as German depth.

(b) *Individual.* His own capacity for work varies with food, stimulants, place, and weather; he recommends a regimen arrived at by self-experimentation rather than by moral rule, and he insists that what suits one person may poison another.

(c) *Symptomatic.* A philosophy is the involuntary and unconscious memoir of its author; moral and religious doctrines can be read as symptoms of a physiological state, such as exhaustion, ressentiment, or declining life.

(d) *Critical.* He repeatedly moves from the claim that a doctrine is physiologically degenerative to a verdict on its value.

In the seminar we distinguished three readings of how these fit together:

1. **Metabolic reductionism.** Cultural and spiritual phenomena are nothing over and above their physiological conditions and effects.
2. **The tonic reading.** Physiology supplies enabling conditions and diagnostic signs of spiritual achievement, but it neither constitutes the content of a doctrine nor settles its truth.
3. **The regimen reading.** Physiological facts fix neither the content of a doctrine nor its truth, but its *authority for an agent* — what that agent is able to take seriously, feel bound by, or act on — so that the physiological critique is a critique of our capacity to be moved by a doctrine rather than of its meaning or its truth-value.

Choose one of these three and argue for it against the strongest version of the other two. You must also answer the following objection to whichever reading you choose: if physiology supplies only conditions, signs, or authority, then the critique can establish at most that a doctrine is unhealthy or unmotivating, never that it is false or worthless — yet Nietzsche plainly means more than that. Does his critical practice therefore require an extra premise? If so, state it, and say whether it can be defended without collapsing your chosen reading back into metabolic reductionism. Finally, discuss at least two concrete Nietzschean claims about food, drink, or climate in the course of your answer.

**One student's answer**

The four kinds of claim fit together badly unless we separate two questions that Nietzsche's rhetoric tends to run together. The first is a question of constitution: what is a doctrine, such that physiology can be cited in its explanation? The second is a question of truth: does citing physiology ever show a doctrine to be false? I will argue that the tonic reading (2) answers the first question correctly and that, once the two questions are separated, the objection loses its force.

Begin with reductionism. In its strongest form, metabolic reductionism is the claim that a doctrine is true or false in virtue of its physiological effects — that the physiology settles the truth-value. But this cannot be Nietzsche's view, because Nietzsche is a perspectivist: he denies that any doctrine has a truth-value independent of a perspective, and if truth is always truth-for-a-perspective, then no physiological fact can be what makes a doctrine true or false. Reductionism is therefore already excluded by his epistemology, and we can set it aside.

The tonic reading then does the real work. On this reading physiology supplies conditions of possibility and diagnostic signs, and nothing more. Strictly speaking, conditions of possibility are not explanatory: to say that a well-regulated digestion is a condition of my thinking well is not to say anything about why my thoughts have the content that they do. This is why Nietzsche's physiological remarks about whole cultures — his jokes about German cooking and German metaphysics, his claim that a people's diet is the source of its morality — are best read as polemical exaggeration rather than as causal claims, and it is also why the physiological critique is, on its own, evaluatively inert. It can tell us that a doctrine is unhealthy. It cannot tell us that it is wrong.

The regimen reading (3) is the interesting rival, but it is unstable. It says that my physiology determines what counts as evidence for me. Yet if my body fixes my evidence, then evidence is just physiology under another name, and so (3) quietly collapses back into (1). We should therefore stay with (2).

The objection is right that Nietzsche moves from degenerative to worthless, and this does require a premise: that ascending life, or health, is the standard of value. But that premise is not really an extra assumption. To call something degenerative is already to call it life-denying, and a revaluation of values is by definition a revaluation in the name of life; so the inference from degenerative to worthless is close to analytic. Nor does this collapse the tonic reading into reductionism, because the standard is applied to a doctrine's effects rather than to its content. Two examples make the point concrete. First, the dietary diagnosis of the Germans: the claim is that bad cuisine produces bad thinking, which is a claim about conditions, not about the content of German idealism. Second, Nietzsche's own regimen — his avoidance of alcohol, his concern with the timing of meals, his sensitivity to climate and barometric pressure, his insistence in his late autobiographical chapter that each person must find his own diet. In both cases the physiological claim is about what enables or disables a kind of spiritual life, never about what a doctrine means or whether it is true.
