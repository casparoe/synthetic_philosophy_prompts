I am a novelist, and the manuscript I am about to begin is built, more than any book I have written, out of borderline cases. Its scenes turn on a curfew that begins "at nightfall," on a will contested on the question whether the testator was "of sound mind," on a marriage alleged never to have been "consummated," on a figure glimpsed across a fogged street who is "elderly, or at any rate no longer young," on a witness's insistence that a man was "bald." In my previous novels such passages ran to perhaps sixty per book, I resolved each of them by ear, and my editor and I disagreed on something like a third. That is now too much to leave to instinct, and I would like a procedure in place of the ear.

Two constraints fix what the procedure has to be. First, my copy editor will have to run most of it: she is exacting about prose and has no philosophy, so the procedure must be executable in about five minutes per passage, must not require her to know anything I have not written down, and must tell her when to stop and pass the passage to me. Second, some of these passages are read by a legal reader before the book goes to set, and I have to be able to tell him, in language he will accept, what commitment a given sentence carries; he will not accept "it is indeterminate" as an answer unless I can say what kind of indeterminacy and who, in the fiction, is answerable for it. I sign off on the rest myself.

As I understand the two families of position I am choosing between: on the first, every vague predicate divides the world at some perfectly sharp boundary, borderline cases are cases where we do not know where the boundary falls, and the sentence in question has a determinate truth value we cannot reach — the explanation of why we cannot reach it even in favourable circumstances being a margin-for-error constraint on knowledge rather than anything about the words. On the second, the indeterminacy is in the language: there are many admissible ways of making the predicate precise, a borderline predication is true on some and false on others, and truth is truth on every admissible precisification. I am told that this second view has a consequence I do not know how to write around: because every classical tautology holds on every precisification, "P or not-P" is safe, while "neither P nor not-P" is not merely unsettled but false on every precisification — so the kind of sentence I rely on most, which says of a borderline state of affairs that it is neither one thing nor the other, comes out false rather than indeterminate. If I have either position wrong, or that consequence wrong, correct it briefly before building anything on it.

One further feature of the manuscript is relevant, and it is the part I most want the procedure to earn its keep on. The book is framed as a dossier assembled by an in-world official I call the Reporter, who appends marginal notes to the scenes; some of those notes settle what the scene left open, by stipulating, for the purposes of the record, how a predicate is to be taken. I take it that a Reporter's note is, in the fiction, what a theorist would call a sharpening or precisification — stipulating one admissible way of taking the word rather than discovering a boundary. That means the book has a second question the earlier ones did not: whose sharpening a given sentence deploys, the narrator's, the character's, or the Reporter's, and whether an unmarked sharpening is available to me at all.

So I am asking for a procedure, not an explanation. Give me the questions in the order they must be asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which it halts and tells me to leave the sentence alone; the point at which it hands the decision to me because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact; and an honest account of which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which I can afford to lose in order to make it runnable by someone with five minutes and no theory. I want a one-page version my copy editor can hold, a handful of test lines she can apply to see whether she is running it correctly, and — because at least one such line is not vagueness at all but context-sensitivity, ambiguity, or mere ignorance — a first question that catches those and sends them elsewhere rather than forcing them through the rest of the procedure. I also want to know how the procedure handles a borderline case about a borderline case, and whether the stance I adopt must be constant across the book or may vary from passage to passage without the book breaking.

Then run it on three passages from the draft. The first is the sentence I am fondest of: "It was neither dusk nor night when they took him, and no one, least of all the sergeant, could have said which." I expect the procedure to tell me something about that sentence I will not like, and I would rather hear it now than in galleys. The second is the Reporter's note on the contested will: "There was no fact of the matter whether he was of sound mind; there was only what the doctors were prepared to say." The third is a piece of free indirect style: "The woman on the far pavement was elderly, or at any rate no longer young." For each, show the procedure arriving at an answer rather than stating one, and mark where, if anywhere, it stops short and I have to decide.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I would rather know now which of these passages will remain a matter of judgment, however carefully the rest is specified, than discover it when my editor and I are arguing about a page proof.
