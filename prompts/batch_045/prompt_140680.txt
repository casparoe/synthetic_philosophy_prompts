I'm on the steering committee of a tenants' union — maybe forty dues-paying members, and honestly most of them belong to one of two churches in the neighborhood, with maybe five or six of us who aren't religious at all (I'm one). We're drafting ourselves a rule about what kinds of reasons people are allowed to give when the union takes an official position on something, like testifying at a city council hearing on a rent stabilization ordinance, or deciding whether to endorse somebody for county board. We've split into two camps and have been going in circles for three meetings now, and it's starting to eat the whole agenda.

Camp one, led by my friend Doreen: your actual reason is your reason, and making people rephrase their deepest convictions in some flat neutral vocabulary before they're allowed to speak is condescending and, more to the point, dishonest — she says it asks people to lie about what actually moved them. Camp two, led by our secretary Mark: nobody's trying to silence you, you can say whatever you want about God, you just also have to offer a reason that any member could in principle accept — and this is the part I can't stand — he says the second, neutral reason doesn't have to come in the same speech, or even the same month; it's enough that it shows up eventually. To me that just means the neutral reason never shows up at all. But Doreen's version means the union basically can't say anything in public that anyone outside our two churches will take serious.

A few things I can't sort out and was hoping you could help with. Does it even matter that we're religiously pretty homogeneous? If the point of the rule is to not impose on people who don't share your faith, does the rule switch off when there's almost nobody in the room to be imposed on, and should the answer be different when we're arguing among ourselves at a members' meeting versus testifying to the council versus putting our name behind a candidate? Does it matter that Mark's own reasons are just as contestable as anybody's — he's always talking about maximizing total welfare or whatever, which tons of people reject — is his reason really 'shared' in a way a religious one isn't, or is he just getting a pass because his worldview happens to be the local default? Should whoever actually signs the letter or speaks into the microphone be held to a stricter standard than a member shouting from the floor, and what about our paid organizer, who says the rule shouldn't bind her because shes speaking for the union and not for herself? And one member says he'd gladly give the neutral reason but it's a much weaker one and he'd lose the argument if that was all he had — does that make the rule a cost imposed on him in particular, and if so is that a bug or a feature? My sister did a philosophy minor and says this exact fight is something a very famous 20th-century American political philosopher worked on for decades, and that he apparently loosened his position near the end of his career, but she wouldn't tell me more because she said she'd garble it. I'd like to know what the real structure of the argument is, the strongest version of each camp's position, and whether there's any version of Mark's say-it-your-way-but-also-give-a-shared-reason rule that isn't just a polite way of changing the subject.
