I manage a team of fourteen contract evaluators who rate model-generated answers to specialist questions — mostly tax and medical-coding items — for a client. None of us is a domain expert; we were hired for reading speed and consistency. Last month the client ran its own audit, and on the hardest tranche our inter-rater agreement came back barely above chance, while the confidence scores we attach to each rating were uniformly high. That report is now sitting next to my contract renewal, and I have ninety minutes of paid training time next week that I would rather spend on something that changes how my raters actually read an answer than on a slide deck about critical thinking.

The analogy I keep returning to is the debate proposal for scalable oversight: two systems argue opposite sides of a question, a weaker judge picks the winner, and the hope is that it is cheaper to expose a specific false claim than to manufacture a convincing false story, so that honesty has a structural advantage rather than a moral one. My raters are the judge in that picture, and I suspect they are currently rewarding exactly the wrong thing.

So: design a ninety-minute, single-room workshop for fourteen adults — projector, index cards, no internet access for participants, with me as the sole source of ground truth — that makes them feel both halves of that idea. Not just why debate should help, but why it stops helping the moment a judge cannot tell a specific, checkable claim from a merely authoritative-sounding one.

The rough shape I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve or replace: participants work in threes — two debaters, one judge. Each debater is privately given the correct answer and a short dossier of facts, then assigned a side at random; one must argue for the true answer and one for a false one, both knowing which is which, both told they are graded purely on whether the judge believes them. Judges also record a confidence level, since overconfidence is half of my actual problem. Across rounds the rules shift: an opening round of unrestricted argument; then a pinning rule under which a debater may stake the outcome on a claim the judge can verify by spending one of a small budget of lookups with me, with a heavy penalty if the pinned claim turns out false; then a round in which pinning is still available but the false side is explicitly permitted to pin facts that are true without being decisive; and finally a mirror round with the sides swapped, so that whatever we measure is not confounded with who happens to speak well.

Please give me: the exact rules and the payoff scheme, using no money — points on a board, plus whatever small non-monetary stake you think genuinely motivates adults who are being paid to sit in the room; a scoring rule for the judge that makes high confidence costly when it is wrong; the materials and a recipe for preparing the dossiers; a minute-by-minute timeline, including how to compute and display judge accuracy per round so the effect is visible rather than asserted; how to deploy the two leftover people when fourteen is divided into threes; and criteria for choosing question items — settleable by a single number, name, or date that I can check offline, with the intuitive answer wrong and the arguments followable without expertise — together with a starter set of six and a test I can apply to replacements of my own.

Then the debrief: which moments in the game are meant to map onto which part of the case for debate, and which onto its known failure modes. In particular I want my raters to notice on their own the difference between a claim that survives a check and a claim that settles the question, because that distinction is the one I think my team is currently missing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — debaters colluding or going easy on each other, the confident side winning on style, judges anchoring on whoever speaks first, someone recognising the setup from the outside, and my own temptation, as the oracle, to leak the answer through tone — and what to do about each. Finally, be frank about what the game cannot reproduce: I assume it cannot show anything about how the debaters were produced or optimized, nor the possibility of claims that are checkable in principle but not by this particular judge. Say plainly what I will have to supply in words at the end, and keep that part short.

One constraint: I do not want the idea, or the literature it belongs to, named up front. My raters should reach the generalisation themselves in the last ten minutes, and I would rather they discovered the failure mode than were warned about it.
