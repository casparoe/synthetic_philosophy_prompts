I am writing a novel in which two characters return to the same argument, unchanged, at intervals across some thirty years. One holds that there are agent-relative constraints on what a person may do to another in order to avert a greater harm; the other holds that such constraints are paradoxical, on the grounds that they forbid bringing about a state of affairs as a means while permitting the bringing about of precisely that state of affairs as a foreseen side-effect of the very same end. I have them argue it four times and the shape of the exchange never varies. The constraint theorist insists that the morally operative difference lies in what the agent is doing, in whether the victim's death figures in her plan as that through which the good is achieved or merely as something she foresees coming along with it. The critic replies that this is a fact about the agent's inner life and cannot bear the weight being put on it, since the outcome, the numbers, and the victim's situation are identical either way.

Somewhere in the middle of the draft I gave the critic a case I had not come across anywhere, hoping it would force the issue. Call it the botched variant: the agent acts intending the death of the one as a means of saving the five, botches the mechanics, and the one dies anyway as a causal upshot of the action that saves the five, that being the very action the constraint theorist counts as permissible when performed without that intention. It does not force the issue. My constraint theorist says that of course the action is worse, because the will is what the constraint governs; my critic says that this simply converts the view into a fetishism of the inner; and neither of them moves. I notice that I have now written the same exchange twice with different furniture around it, and I would rather understand the stalemate than decorate it again.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My own hypothesis is that the two of them are not really disagreeing about the morality of the cases at all but about what intending is, about whether the death's figuring in her plan is a causal or functional fact about her psychology or rather a normative fact about what she is committed to, and that each side helps itself to whichever philosophy of action makes its moral verdict come out right, so that the moral dispute is a proxy war fought on borrowed ground. I can also see at least three competitors: that this is a verbal dispute about the phrase as a means, a causal-involvement reading against a deliberative-dependence reading, which come apart precisely in the botched variant; that it is a dispute about the level at which a moral theory operates, whether constraints are reasons bearing on what to do or exclusions bearing on what may be weighed in deliberation at all; and that it is bedrock, a difference in starting intuition that neither side can argue for.

Tell me which of these, if any, is actually doing the work, and whether my hypothesis is better or worse than its competitors, and show it by reference to what each side commits itself to rather than by canvassing the literature. Then give me something I can use. On your diagnosis, what in principle would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a question in the philosophy of action settled independently of the moral one, a change of question? And if nothing would, say so plainly, because I need to know whether ending the novel with the argument unresolved is truthful to the dispute or merely a way of avoiding it, and whether an honest resolution between them would have to look like a conversion rather than a refutation.
