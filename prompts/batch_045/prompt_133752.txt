The context: I'm applying for a three-month funded research leave to turn a seminar paper into an article. My half-formed idea: accounts of supererogation pretty much all work with some version of two conditions — it's permissible to omit the act (not one's duty), and praiseworthy or meritorious to do it — and then they test candidate cases against those conditions. But almost all the standard examples are one-off acts by a single agent against a background in which everyone else's behaviour is held fixed. I think that fixed background is doing a lot of hidden work. Two cases I keep coming back to:

- Iteration. If it's supererogatory for me to do X today, is it still supererogatory for me to do exactly the same X tomorrow in the same circumstances? On the one hand it looks if anything more praiseworthy (it shows the first time wasn't a fluke); on the other, once I've shown I can do it it starts to look like something that can legitimately be expected of me, and then the "not required" condition fails. Supererogation might eat itself: doing enough of it converts it into duty.
- Distribution. My cost of doing the right thing goes up when others don't do their share. But high cost is standardly what pushes an act out of duty and into supererogation. So the worse everyone else behaves, the more "heroic" my ordinary cooperation becomes. That seems like the wrong result, or at least one nobody has drawn out.
