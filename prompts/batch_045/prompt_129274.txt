I volunteer at a natural history museum, where I run a small table called "Is it alive?" for visiting school groups — a tray of objects, a laminated card, about ninety seconds per group. I have a friend in a synthetic-biology lab who keeps feeding me new specimens, and I have started to notice that my verdicts are more settled than my reasons, and that "it depends what you mean by alive" has stopped being an answer I can give to a nine-year-old and still feel honest about. So I want the principle behind the pattern, if there is one. Here is where I land: a mule is alive, obviously, and so is a worker ant, even though neither will ever have offspring and no mule is descended from another mule; a seed from a two-thousand-year-old jar that can still germinate was alive the whole time, and so, I want to say, was a tardigrade dried to a speck and a human embryo in liquid nitrogen; a line of human cells in a lab dish, descended from a person who died seventy years ago and proposed by someone as a species of its own, is alive; a rotifer lineage that appears to have gone tens of millions of years without sex is alive; a hybrid frog that has to mate with a different species each generation to rebuild its own genome is alive; a bacterial cell whose genome was chemically synthesized and then cut down to the bare set of genes needed to divide is alive. Fire is not alive, even though it metabolizes, grows, responds to wind, spreads, and passes on something like a flame shape. A prion is not alive. A virus particle floating outside a cell is not alive, though I waver about the giant ones that carry some of their own translation machinery. A computer virus and a machine in a factory that assembles copies of itself from parts are not alive, and not nearly alive. The case I keep arguing with my friend about is a virus inside the cell it has taken over: a few virologists say the infected cell, not the particle, is the living stage of the virus, the particle being only a dispersal form, and my gut says the infected cell is alive while the virus in it is not, which may just be incoherent.

The principle I keep coming back to is something like: a thing is alive if it belongs to a lineage whose continuation is explained by natural selection acting on heritable variation. That gets fire and computer viruses out cleanly, and it gets the HeLa cells and the ancient seed in, and it explains why the giant virus makes me waver. But it says the mule and the worker ant are not alive, or at least gives no reason to call them alive, and it seems to make the dried tardigrade alive only in virtue of a lineage it is not currently participating in — and I am not willing to give any of those three up. I have also noticed that my verdicts may be answers to three different questions that I have been sliding between: whether something is alive, whether it is an organism, and whether it is a unit of selection.

What I would like from you: state the principle, or the smallest set of candidate principles, precisely enough that I could defend it at the table, and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause. Test my lineage formulation against the mule, the worker ant and the dormant seed specifically, and either repair it so that they come out alive without also letting in fire and self-replicating machines, or tell me plainly why no account with that shape can have them. Then tell me what your preferred account predicts for two cases I have not given a verdict on: a lab-built clump of frog skin cells that swims, sweeps loose cells into rough copies of itself, and dies out after a few generations without evolving at all; and a brain organoid grown from human cells, kept alive in a dish for years, with no body and no prospect of one. Finally, say which of my verdicts you think I should overturn, and why that one rather than the others.
