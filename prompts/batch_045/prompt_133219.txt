Next month our town is holding a short public ceremony in Courthouse Square, and I have been asked to give the remarks: five or six minutes, outdoors, in front of roughly two hundred people. I chair the committee that recommended the ceremony, and I have strong views that I do not want simply inserted into the speech as opinions.

What is happening. The bronze figure of Elias Rowntree, erected in 1911, has been moved two hundred yards to the lawn of the county historical society, where it now stands beside a small outdoor case displaying the county historian's research into the land transactions behind it. The granite pedestal in the square stays where it is. Fastened to it is a new tablet, and the tablet carries no name: its text says, in effect, that we moved the monument because we were no longer willing to go on making the claim it made, and it asks whoever reads it fifty years from now to judge whether we were right.

The difficulty. The story the town has repeated about Rowntree for a century — that he gave the square and the first schoolhouse to the town — does not survive the deed records: he sold the land at market price, and the school was built from a levy. But for eighty years the square has also been where the town gathers on Memorial Day, and the people who gather there are not, in the main, making any claim about Rowntree at all. Several of my neighbors, including Rowntree's great-granddaughter, who is a friend of mine and who spoke against the move, will experience the ceremony as a correction being administered to their grandparents.

What I want from the speech. I have come to think that the strongest argument against what we are doing has nothing to do with history: it is that the monument has become the site of a practice belonging to the living, and a thing that has been taken up into a community's ongoing life has a claim on us that no finding in the deed records settles. I do not want the speech to pretend that claim is nothing. But I also do not want it answered with the line that a statue was never a record in the first place, so that nothing of historical value has been lost — because that is true, and it is also a way of not hearing anyone. The distinction I want the speech to turn on is the one between honoring and recording. I want it to do structural work: to determine what the speech can and cannot say, rather than to appear in it as a point.

Some constraints on the result:

It must not claim that we now know the truth about Rowntree, or that the town has finished judging him. The tablet is addressed to people who may judge differently, and the remarks have to be consistent with that.

It must not tell the people who were attached to the statue that their attachment was a mistake. It has to give them something true to keep.

It must not console Rowntree's family with the thought that we are judging a man who is not here to answer. If a monument says something about us rather than about him, that consolation is not available, and I would rather the speech not reach for it.

It should end by sending people across the street to the historical society's case, in a way that does not sound like an instruction to go and be educated.

No one should be able to leave thinking we have said nothing. The speech has to be willing to say what we did and why, in plain words, and without the phrases complicated legacy, on the right side of history, or reckoning.

The room will include the county historian, the high school teacher who organized the petition to move the statue, the historical society's board (several of whom think we are sanitizing the square), the veterans who lay the Memorial Day wreath, and Rowntree's descendants. Most of the rest are neighbors. Nothing in the speech should require philosophical training, and nothing in it should be a philosophical point dressed up as a platitude.

Please write the remarks. Then, in a brief note, tell me which two sentences you think are most likely to cause trouble in that particular room, and what you would put in their place.
