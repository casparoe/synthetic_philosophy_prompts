I sit on the awards panel of a mid-sized biomedical research foundation, and the board has asked me to draft the decision memo on how the panel should handle its Stage 1 reviewer information before it deliberates. The setup: we fund about a dozen five-year awards a cycle from roughly four hundred applications. Each application is read by three external reviewers who score it and write free-text comments, and the secretariat compiles a dossier for the forty shortlisted proposals containing the three scores, the mean, the reviewers' names, and their full comments. Our panel of nine then meets for two days, spends about twenty minutes per proposal, and votes. Two things from the last cycle triggered this review. For three of the awards we made, a Stage 1 reviewer had raised a serious methodological objection in the free text that no panelist mentioned in the meeting; everyone's attention evidently went to the mean score and to whichever senior panelist spoke first. Separately, a proposal we rejected was picked up by a national agency and has since produced the strongest results in its subfield — which I find genuinely unsettling, because we have no way of knowing how many other strong proposals we have rejected. We only ever observe what happens to the proposals we fund.

The vice-chair reads this as a textbook informational cascade: once two or three panelists have spoken for a proposal, later panelists rationally discount their own private doubts, so the room's confidence ends up reflecting the order in which people happened to speak rather than the total evidence, and our inability to detect the buried objections is exactly the signature of that. The chair's reply is that withholding early information is not obviously an improvement: a panelist who suppresses her private read wastes real evidence, and pooling three independent external reviews is the entire point of the exercise. Her proposal, which she calls Counterfactual First, is that every panelist submit a provisional ranking plus a one-line reason before seeing any dossier, that the secretariat then release the full dossier together with everyone's provisional rankings, and that discussion proceed from there. A third option, from the economist on our board, is to keep the dossier but strip out names and scores and circulate only the comments, so panelists get the arguments without the anchor. A fourth, from the secretariat, is to keep everything as is but fix the speaking order, most junior panelist first and chair last. (I half-remember a social epistemology seminar from years ago where this sort of thing was modeled with urns and private signals, and my memory is that the pathology is fragile in theory but stubborn in practice — which is roughly why I want the memo rather than a principle.)

What I need from you is the memo itself: addressed to the board, under about 900 words, with the recommendation stated at the top, one or two concrete implementation details — what precisely is revealed to whom, and at what point in the two days — and a candid closing paragraph on what would count as evidence, three or four years from now, that the recommended protocol is failing. It should recommend one option or a specified combination, not survey them, and it should give the losing side the strongest version of its own case. Two worries I'd rather the memo confront than paper over. First, the evidence problem: our only outcome data concerns proposals we funded, so any comparison of processes will reward whichever process makes us converge most smoothly rather than whichever process is actually right, and I don't want a recommendation that quietly depends on a number we cannot compute. Second, the diagnosis problem: if a junior panelist defers because she is legitimately updating on colleagues who know the field better, then forcing her to commit in writing before hearing anyone may simply freeze her private error into the record — but if she defers because a bad funding decision will never be traced back to her while a dissent will be remembered at renewal time, that is a different failure with a different fix, and the memo should say plainly which one it takes itself to be solving.
