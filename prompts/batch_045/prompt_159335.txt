Our city (around 180k people) has been running a small futarchy pilot for a couple of years, and I'm one of five people on the working group that decides which decisions get handed to it. The setup, in case it matters: the council picks the goal and the metric, then instead of voting on the action we open a conditional market — what will metric M be at time T if we do X, versus if we don't — and we go with whichever branch the market prices higher. Elected people choose the goal, the market only chooses the means.

Here's our track record, with the metric each time:

- Bus lane on one downtown corridor. Metric: mean weekday peak bus journey time on that corridor, from the transit agency's own vehicle-tracking data, at 12 months. Approved unanimously.
- City-wide streetlight retrofit to LEDs. Metric: what the city spends on street-lighting electricity per quarter, at 18 months. Approved.
- Saturday farmers market: move it from the civic plaza to the old rail yard. Metric: total vendor receipts per market day plus pedestrian counts at 6 months, against the same Saturdays the year before. Approved.
- Rezoning a riverside district for mid-rise housing. Proposed metric: a composite 'affordability and vitality' index — median rent as share of income, net new dwellings, new small-business licenses — at five years. Rejected; we said we'd spend a year arguing about how to weight the index and then nobody would trust the number at the end anyway.
- A big-box retail development on the edge of town. Proposed metric: net municipal tax revenue plus local jobs at four years. Rejected.

New case, for Tuesday: close the smallest of our three library branches and spend the savings on longer opening hours at the other two. Metric on the table: total visits and programme attendance across the library system at 12 months, settled from the library system's own counts, compared to the two previous years. On the face of it it's the most measurable thing we've had in front of us and the horizon is short, which is normally the thing we care about. But the actual fight is about which neighbourhood loses its library, the branch's regulars will be both the keenest traders in the market and the people whose visiting behaviour decides the number, and the library administration that counts the visits has already gone on record that the branch should stay. It's also, as far as I can tell, the first case where the 'we don't do it' branch never gets observed at all — so the market would be betting on somebody's model of the counterfactual rather than on anything anyone can look up.

I can sort of see three rules that fit those five decisions equally well — roughly, 'only if the number is settleable soon and cheaply', 'only for means, never for who-gets-what', and 'not when the people doing the trading can also move the outcome'. I want more than that list. Give me the full set of rules that fits our five calls, including ones I haven't thought of, say what each predicts about the libraries, which precedent is the closest analog, and give me your best guess for what five people who made those five calls will definately do on Tuesday.

Then the part I actually need: I'm writing the recommendation. Which way should I write it, and what exactly would we be committing ourselves to either way — what principle would we be adopting if we run it, and what principle if we don't? Please commit rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick; tell me which way to write it and why. Also tell me the one further case that would seperate whichever rules are still tied after this one, and flag the step in your reasoning you're least confident about.
