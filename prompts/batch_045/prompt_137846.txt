I'm part of a small history-and-philosophy-of-science reading group — a handful of grad students, a retired molecular biologist who shows up mostly to complain about philosophers of science, and one very combative friend — and last night, over a dinner that went on far longer than anyone intended, we got into an argument about Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. We were working from a chapter in one of those popular surveys of philosophy of science; the author is a professional and it reads very smoothly, which I take to be either a recommendation or a warning. My friend's claim is that the chapter, fairly read, shows Lakatos's framework has no teeth. I suspect the chapter misrepresents him somewhere, but I couldn't say where, and I'd like an audit before we meet again.

The chapter's account goes roughly like this:

Popper said a theory is scientific only if it can be refuted, and that when it is refuted you have to give it up. Since scientists plainly don't behave that way — they protect their favourite theory by fiddling with the assumptions around it — Lakatos proposed a more realistic version: what faces the tribunal of experience is never a single theory but a whole research programme, consisting of an unfalsifiable hard core of central commitments protected by methodological decision, surrounded by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses that scientists freely adjust to absorb inconvenient data. The hard core tells them where to look rather than what to give up. A programme is progressive so long as each new version in the series explains more of the data than the one before it, and degenerating once successive versions are merely patched up after the fact to fit the anomalies; at that point the rational thing is to jump to a rival programme. Because protecting the hard core is a convention rather than something evidence settles, and because you can never tell in the present whether a programme has genuinely stalled or is only temporarily stuck, Lakatos conceded that the verdict is always retrospective — and this is how he tried to steer a middle course between Popper's austere picture of instant refutation and Kuhn's claim that what actually decides between theories is persuasion, fashion, and generational turnover. He did keep falsification as the mark of the scientific, though: a programme that has stopped generating testable predictions has, on his own account, stopped being scientific — but only historians looking back can say when that happened.

My friend drew two conclusions from it: first, that "progressive" as the chapter defines it is something any entrenched programme can claim about itself, since there is always some new version that fits a bit more of the data; and second, that because no one can ever say in the present whether a programme is degenerating, Lakatos's apparatus is decorative — it can rationalize any decision after the fact and can't be used to criticize a live research programme at all, which in his view makes it Kuhn with extra vocabulary. To test that, I made up a stylized case on the spot. Call it the P-programme: its core commitment is that every instance of phenomenon F is produced by mechanism M. Over forty years, each anomaly has been absorbed by adding an auxiliary — an inhibitor here, a second pathway there, a critical developmental window somewhere else — so that P now accommodates strictly more of the data in its domain than its only rival, the Q-programme. But in thirty years not one prediction derived from P of a fact that wasn't already on the books has been confirmed; the pattern is always that the anomaly is reported first, the new version of P is built to fit it, and then the new version retrodicts it. Q is the riskier programme: it made three risky predictions, two of which failed and one of which was corroborated.

So, three things please. First, go through that summary and sort it: which sentences would someone who works on Lakatos accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications of something he really does say, and which are wrong or conflate two things he keeps apart — I have a suspicion the trouble is in the neighbourhood of "progressive" and of the claim that the verdict is always retrospective, but I'd rather you found it than that I led you to it. Second, does my friend's "no teeth" conclusion survive once the summary is corrected, or does it depend on the parts that aren't fair? Third, on the test case: by Lakatos's own criteria is P degenerating even though it accommodates more data than Q, and how much turns on what "novel fact" is taken to mean — I have a vague memory that someone in that circle argued novelty should be read as "not used in constructing the new version" rather than "not known at the time the theory was proposed," but I can't recall who said it, so treat it as a rumour and tell me whether it's real and whether it would change the verdict on P. Relatedly: is there anything in Lakatos — the distinction between theoretical and empirical progress, the idea of a time-limit on how long a programme gets to develop, or anything else — that lets anyone say of a programme now that it has degenerated, rather than only in hindsight?
