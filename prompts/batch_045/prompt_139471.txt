I convene a weekly seventy-five-minute seminar on the philosophy of economics for about sixteen third-year undergraduates, nearly all of whom have already done a decision-theory course: they can state the expected-utility axioms, solve a Dutch-book problem, and cite the Ellsberg set-up without, so far as I can tell, ever having been unsettled by any of it. My aim for one session is to make them *feel* the cluster of distinctions that Keynes is working with in the Treatise on Probability and then again in the General Theory: that a probability is a degree of rational belief licensed by a body of evidence rather than a frequency waiting to be measured; that many such degrees are not numerically comparable with one another, so that uncertainty is not merely risk with the numbers rubbed out but something that resists being placed on a single scale at all; that the amount of evidence one has is a separate quantity from the probability it supports, so that two people can announce the same number while one of them is essentially guessing; and, crucially, that when no calculation is available agents do not freeze but fall back on a convention — an arbitrary but self-fulfilling anchor — and that such conventions hold only as long as everyone believes everyone else will go on holding them. I do not want any of this said out loud before the final fifteen minutes: the distinctions should arrive as something the students have already run into by trying to act.

Last year I tried to get there with a straight Ellsberg urn followed by a quick classroom beauty contest, and both fell flat in the same way. The students read the ambiguity result as an artefact of my payoff table — a quirk about how I'd priced the bets — rather than as a fact about whether the relevant quantity is measurable at all; and the beauty contest just looked like a puzzle about iterated reasoning, which they enjoyed and then filed under 'game theory', never noticing that the convergence they produced was the phenomenon, not the solution. So what I need is a single connected activity, buildable from paper, envelopes, index cards and a whiteboard (the room has no screens, and I'd rather keep it analogue anyway), with rules, a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no real money and only a token prize, a minute-by-minute timeline for the full seventy-five minutes, and no deception and nothing that hangs on course credit or grade anxiety. Two mechanisms I have half-designed and would like you to sharpen or replace. First, a stage in which participants are never allowed to state a point estimate but must post a bid and an ask on each of a dozen events — some drawn from a bag whose composition is known, some from one whose composition is merely bounded, some one-off historical or local propositions with no usable reference class — with the spread itself scored by a strictly proper rule, and with an explicit 'no comparison' option carrying a small certain payoff so that the interesting data at the end includes how often people decline to compare two events at all. Second, a stage in which a convention is allowed to crystallise around a publicly drawn signal that is transparently irrelevant to the outcome, is then destabilised by withholding that signal from half the room, and is then withdrawn entirely.

Please also give me the debrief itself, mapping each scheduled moment onto the specific Keynesian claim it is meant to deliver, together with an honest account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run — the decision-theory students will try to reconstruct a prior over the unknown urn and will be annoyed when the game does not let them; someone will try to arbitrage the spreads; the 'no comparison' option will be either under-used out of embarrassment or over-used as a safe default; a confident student will try to talk the room into an anchor in the final stage — and how to handle each without lecturing. Finally, tell me plainly which features of the view the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to state myself: I suspect the claim that probability is an objective logical relation between evidence and conclusion rather than a personal degree of confidence is one of them, and that whatever real markets do is sustained by institutional facts a seminar cannot simulate is another, but I would like your judgement on where exactly the simulation stops and the talking has to begin.
