Dear Professor,

I am a seventeen-year-old student at a public high school whose students come roughly half from a farming community and half from a suburb of commuting professionals, and I am assembling the materials for our spring Economics and Society forum — a student-run session in which about sixty of my peers work through one contested question together. I have chosen inherited wealth. The part I am least confident about is the instrument I want to build, and I would be grateful for your help with it.

What I want is a short sorting quiz: eight to ten items, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, whose scoring key sorts a student into a position on inheritance without ever asking which position they think they hold. My peers will not know the vocabulary, so the question stems should be plain, and every item should be a case where the positions genuinely come apart.

The leaves I want represented are these: that bequest is a basic incident of ownership and taxing it is confiscation with no principled stopping point; that what matters is unchosen advantage at the recipient's end, so receipts rather than estates should be taxed; that steep death taxation is justified because it funds a one-time capital endowment paid to every citizen at twenty-five, so that the tax completes an equal starting stake rather than merely redistributing; that inheritance is legitimate in principle but large inequalities in life-chances are not, so tax progressively and spend on children's opportunity; that the objection is to a hereditary class with unaccountable power, so dynasties specifically are the target; that the question is only how large the total pie ends up, so incentives decide and the answer may be a low or zero rate; and that family partiality is a protected sphere of intimacy which heavy taxation intrudes upon. I also want at least one cell you think nobody occupies — for instance, unlimited tax-free giving during life combined with near-confiscation at death, or its mirror image — and one sentence on why it is empty.

Three of the cases must be ones my peers will raise anyway. First, a farm or small business that can only stay in the family if the heir sells part of it to pay the tax. Second, a parent who spends a large sum on a child's schooling and internships while alive versus one who leaves the same sum at death: I want an item that separates objections to the size of a fortune from objections to transfer at death from objections to a dead hand directing resources. Third, an item exposing the timing loophole, since a view that taxes only what is left at death seems to reward early giving, and I want to know whether consistency forces such a view to become a lifetime-transfers view. I would also like one case in which the money goes to a stranger or a cause rather than to a child, and one comparing wealth earned by the person who dies with wealth that has already passed through two generations.

Two further requests. Please flag any pair of leaves your quiz cannot separate — I suspect the unchosen-advantage view and the anti-dynasty view converge on the same rates for different reasons — and say what would have to be added to tell them apart. And please tell me which single question does the most sorting work and why, since I can only keep ten.

I am not looking for citations or further reading, only for the instrument and the reasoning behind its construction.
