My group does applied Bayesian work — hierarchical models, model selection and averaging, online prediction with log-loss regret bounds — and a philosopher we collaborate with has started telling us that what we do is a degraded approximation to something called Solomonoff induction, which he describes as the only rigorous and fully general statement of Occam's razor we have. He is not a crank and I don't want to wave him away, but I also can't tell whether the thing he is describing is a genuine idealization of our practice, a solution to a different problem, or an idealization whose every virtue is asymptotic and uncomputable and therefore irrelevant to anything I do on a Monday.

So I'd like a translation rather than an introduction. Please restate the following, one at a time and as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary my group actually uses: priors and marginal likelihoods, Bayes factors, Bayesian model averaging over a countable model class, two-part codes and minimum description length, regularization as a complexity penalty, PAC-Bayes and no-free-lunch results, the distinction between the truth being inside the support of your prior or outside it, cross-validation.

First, the construction: a prior built by mixing over the programs of a universal machine, with weight falling off exponentially in program length, and updated by conditioning on the data so far.

Second, the optimality result. As he tells it: the expected cumulative loss exceeds that of any computable predictor by no more than a constant proportional to the complexity of the truth, with the loss summed over the whole infinite sequence. In my vocabulary, is that a regret bound? Against what comparator class? What plays the role of the constant, and is it the kind of constant I should care about or the kind that swallows the result?

Third, the identification of the negative log of the mixture weight with Kolmogorov complexity, up to an additive term small relative to the complexity itself — and the use of that to say the scheme always prefers the simplest hypothesis compatible with the data.

Fourth, the reasons no one uses it: uncomputability, and the fact that the dominance over any computable predictor holds only up to a multiplicative constant that depends on the predictor.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. My guesses, to be confirmed or corrected: that the prior is over programs rather than over the hypotheses I care about, and the map from programs to hypotheses is many-to-one, so there is no clean counterpart to a posterior probability over models; that the thing is a semimeasure rather than a probability measure, with mass leaking somewhere I have no concept for; that the guarantee is about infinite-horizon cumulative error rather than finite-sample risk, so it says nothing I could check on a test set; and that the choice of reference machine is either a substantive inductive bias, like choosing a model class, or a convention, like choosing units — I go back and forth, because I'm told the difference is bounded by a constant independent of the data and also that the constant can be astronomically large. Tell me which of these are real, which are artifacts of my vocabulary, and what else fails to translate that I haven't thought of.

Finally, the case I care most about. I've been playing with a variant in which the penalty on a program is its length plus λ times the log of its runtime, which makes the scheme computable, gives me a finite-horizon object, and lets me trade accuracy against a compute budget. Translate that back into Solomonoff terms: which theorem do I lose, precisely, and is losing it the same kind of loss as approximating a posterior with variational inference, or a different kind of loss altogether? And has penalizing runtime quietly changed the virtue being formalized — from simplicity into something more like efficiency — such that the philosophical claim that Solomonoff induction vindicates Occam's razor no longer has any bearing on what I'm doing?

What I want at the end is a verdict I can act on: which of my collaborator's claims I should concede, which are true but inert for someone in my position, and whether the residue left over after the translation points to a genuine gap in Bayesian practice or to a gap in the Solomonoff story.
