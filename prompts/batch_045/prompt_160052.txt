**The question (20 marks, 600 word limit):**

In most democracies somewhere between half and four fifths of eligible voters turn out at a general election, even though the chance that any single vote decides the result is tiny. (a) Set out the argument that concludes from this that voting is irrational, writing it as an expected-utility calculation and being explicit about what each term means. (b) Assess two replies: (i) that the benefit term has been underestimated because the stakes include what everyone else gets, not just what you get; (ii) that voters are not trying to bring about an outcome at all, but to express support for a candidate or a side. (c) If the paradox survives both replies, does that show that people who vote are being irrational in any ordinary sense, or does it show something else?

**The student's answer:**

Call this Downs's paradox. It is not a coincidence that the same label, the paradox of voting, gets attached to the cycling result Condorcet discovered and that Arrow's impossibility theorem builds on: majority rule does not aggregate individual preferences into a coherent collective choice, so there is no settled will of the people for a rational agent to bring about, and that is the deeper reason the calculation below comes out negative.

(a) Write the expected utility of voting as U = pB + D − C, where p is the probability my vote is pivotal, B is the benefit I get if my preferred candidate wins, D is whatever satisfaction or sense of duty I get from the act itself, and C is the cost in time and travel. p is minuscule, B is at most a few hundred pounds, C is a few pounds, so pB is effectively zero; setting D aside, C dominates and U < 0. Abstention therefore strictly dominates voting, everyone reasons the same way, and predicted turnout is zero. Structurally this is just the Prisoner's Dilemma again: individually rational choices produce an outcome everyone regrets.

(b)(i) This reply is correct and it dissolves the paradox, because B has been measured wrong. I should count everyone's benefit, not just mine: if my candidate is worth £1,000 each to 10 million people, B = £10^10. For p, treat the other 10 million voters as fair coins; the chance they split exactly 5 million to 5 million is roughly sqrt(2/(πn)), about 1 in 4,000. So pB ≈ £2.5 million against a cost of about £5. Expected utility is overwhelmingly positive, turnout is rational, and no special story about duty is needed.

(b)(ii) The expressive reply is the same move in different words: it adds a term for the satisfaction of supporting your side, which is exactly what D already does. It does no independent work, and since (i) already solves the problem we can set it aside.

(c) Given (i), the paradox does not survive, so the question does not arise. Had it survived, the lesson would be that voters are simply irrational, and that get-out-the-vote campaigns are efforts to talk people out of a mistake.
