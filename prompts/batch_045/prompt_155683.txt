I serve on a small advisory group that a European defence ministry has asked to review the moral assumptions embedded in its rules of engagement and in the lawful-targeting module taught to junior officers. Our remit is deliberately narrow: we may propose rewriting at most four passages of the existing doctrine, and everything else stays as it is. So what I need from you is triage with a defended ordering, not a survey.

Take as given, for the purposes of the exercise, that the revisionist critique of the moral equality of combatants is broadly sound: a combatant's moral position is not fixed symmetrically by her uniform and by her compliance with the rules of war, but depends on such things as whether the war she fights is justified and whether the individual she targets is morally liable to defensive harm. Take as given as well what nearly everyone in the debate concedes: the law of armed conflict treats combatants symmetrically irrespective of the justice of their cause, and there are strong second-order reasons for keeping it that way even if the moral picture is asymmetric — soldiers' poor epistemic position on jus ad bellum, the hazard of each combatant becoming her own tribunal, and the dynamics of reciprocal escalation.

Given that, please do the following.

1. Rank, by how much difference it would actually make to doctrine and training, the candidate revisions below, adding any you think I have wrongly omitted: (a) an explicit statement that fighting in an unjust war can make one's otherwise lawful killings morally wrongful even though they remain legally permitted; (b) recasting the concept of military necessity so that targets are identified in terms of liability to defensive harm rather than membership in an armed force; (c) guidance for the officer who comes to believe, mid-deployment, that her own side's cause is unjust; (d) replacing a status-based rule of non-combatant immunity with a liability-based one; (e) guidance on how to treat enemy combatants one takes to be fighting an unjust war; (f) leaving doctrine untouched and confining all revisionist material to a separate ethics seminar.

2. For each item, say what practical difference you expect and where the philosophical argument stops short of supporting it. The cases where the conclusion does not follow interest me as much as the cases where it does, and I suspect at least one of (a) through (e) is a non sequitur from the critique rather than an application of it.

3. Stress-test one proposal already on our table: call it the presumptive-liability instruction. Doctrine would continue to tell officers that enemy combatants are lawful targets and that they are themselves lawful targets, and would continue to forbid acting on private judgments about the justice of the war. But it would add a standing requirement that, whenever an officer authorises lethal force, she proceed on the assumption that she may be morally liable to be killed in return while her target may not be, and that this asymmetry is not something she can settle from her own epistemic position. Its defenders say it changes conduct without licensing soldiers to adjudicate jus ad bellum. Assess whether the materials of the debate support it, whether it collapses into plain legalism or into full-blown revisionism, and whether it rests on a confusion — for instance about what liability attributions do, or about whether moral uncertainty about one's own liability can guide action in the way the instruction demands.

Argue for the ranking rather than merely list it. State the criteria you are using — expected change in conduct on the ground, whether the guidance survives being read by a twenty-two-year-old under time pressure, how badly it could be misused by either side, and how much of it rests on contested philosophy rather than on shared ground — make the trade-offs between those criteria explicit, and tell me what further information would move an item up or down the list.
