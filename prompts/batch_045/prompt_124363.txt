I've spent the better part of a decade teaching and occasionally publishing on anthropic reasoning, and I have now had the same argument with a colleague in cosmology roughly once a year for eight years without either of us moving by so much as a degree of confidence, and at this point I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The argument is the familiar standoff between the self-sampling assumption — that you should reason as if you were a random sample drawn from the observers in some reference class — and the self-indication assumption, on which the bare fact that you exist is treated as evidence favoring hypotheses that predict more observers. My colleague takes SIA to be straightforwardly correct and thinks the presumptuous philosopher case, in which an armchair cosmologist announces that a theory predicting vastly many more observers is vastly more likely purely on the grounds that we are here to ask, merely shows how unreliable our intuitions about cosmological reasoning are; I think the revulsion that case elicits is doing real work, and that a principle happy to let mere cardinality of possible observers swamp ordinary evidence has been reduced to absurdity. Notice what we agree on: we agree about the logic of the Doomsday argument, we agree that observation selection effects are genuine and routinely mishandled, and we agree on every relevant empirical claim. Our graduate seminar last spring split almost exactly down the middle and, unusually, did not converge when I pressed both halves for their premises.

I have a hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered. I have noticed that my own resistance to SIA weakens markedly in cases where the extra observers are produced by fission — where I am told that I will be divided into many successors, each of them equally me — and is strongest in cases where the extra observers are additional strangers, created independently, whom I merely could have been in some thin formal sense of 'could'. The formalisms treat these as the same kind of case, and my suspicion is that a great deal of the intuitive force on both sides is smuggled in by whichever kind of case the speaker is silently picturing while arguing. So my hypothesis is that the SSA/SIA dispute is not one disagreement about how to weigh indexical evidence but a bundle of them: a partly verbal component that may dissolve once the reference class is pinned down; a genuine but very deep component about whether one's own existence is the sort of thing that can be evidence at all; a possibly decision-theoretic component masquerading as an evidential one, about whether 'what should you expect' asks what to believe or what to bet; and, underneath all of them, an inarticulate difference in which thought experiments feel probative.

What I want from you is, first, a view about which of these is actually load-bearing — or what I have left out — argued from what each side really commits itself to rather than from a survey of positions, and, second, given your diagnosis, an account of what would count as progress: a sharpened distinction, a formal result about priors or about the reference class, a decision-theoretic reframing, an empirical result, a change of question. If your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected, say that and say why. Please cap the entire response at 500 words, and structure it as a single sentence stating your diagnosis, then the argument for it, then the progress condition.
