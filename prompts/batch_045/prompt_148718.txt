I am a professor of philosophy and, for the past two years, the humanities member of our university's animal care committee. We sort every protocol we review into one of three tiers of scrutiny, and tier assignment is supposed to turn on the "cognitive sophistication" of the animals involved: the top tier requires a written behavioral justification from the investigator, an enrichment plan reviewed by our veterinarian, and a unanimous vote, while the bottom tier can be cleared administratively. In practice the committee decides these cases by appeal to brain size, to phylogenetic distance from us, to how trainable the animal is said to be, and — more often than anyone will admit — to how the animal strikes us in the room. I have come to think these proxies are not rival measures of one thing but answers to different questions that we have never separated, and I would like a procedure that forces the separation.

The constraints are these. Five of us sit on the committee: three working bench scientists, the attending veterinarian, and myself. We meet monthly and dispose of roughly twenty protocols in an afternoon, so about ten minutes per case including discussion. Anything not unanimous goes to full review, which nobody wants. The reasoning has to be recorded in the minutes in a form an external auditor could follow. And the procedure has to be executable by the three bench scientists as they are: no training in philosophy or comparative psychology, no looking anything up mid-meeting, no power to require an investigator to run new tests or supply background literature. It must run on the protocol document itself plus what a reasonably informed biologist already carries in his head. I can supply conceptual framing in the meeting; I cannot supply measurement.

What I want from you is a decision procedure: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision back to us because the question has become one of values rather than facts, and what the minutes should record so that it is visible afterward which consideration actually drove the outcome. Three things we currently run together should be kept apart: how an animal performs on the tests we happen to have; whether that performance is evidence of the sort of flexible, domain-general capacity it would be right to call intelligence; and what protection, if any, follows. Say plainly where the committee is compelled to move from the second question to the third, and why that move cannot be made by the procedure.

I have been pushing a short list of markers, and I would like you to tell me which of them survive operationalization, which are redundant, and which should be dropped as unmeasurable in our setting. First, recombination versus extension: whether a competence acquired in one task turns up in a task that recombines the same elements into a new configuration, as opposed to more of the same along the dimension that was trained. Second, the coherence of failure: whether errors cluster into the systematic, near-miss pattern you would expect from something operating with a model of the domain, or scatter as brute breakdown. Third, acquisition cost: how many exposures it took to reach criterion, on the thought that an animal which already has structure to bring to a task learns it cheaply. Fourth, symmetry of transfer: whether what transfers in one direction transfers back, or whether the one-way pattern suggests a dedicated mechanism rather than a general capacity. And then a check I have invented and cannot quite make work, which I would like you to fix or discard: before we score a species on our battery, we ask whether that battery would have registered intelligence in a creature with very different sensors and effectors — a calibration test on the detector rather than on the animal.

Then run the procedure on three protocols, showing your work. One: cephalopods held individually in unenriched tanks for a learning study, the investigator arguing that the animals are solitary, short-lived, and that enrichment would contaminate the control of experience. Two: a four-hundred-mouse conditioning study under standard housing with nesting material as the only enrichment, which is our current default and which nobody has examined in years. Three: a honeybee assay in which the investigator's pilot data show rapid reversal and transfer to a recombined discrimination — a species I have consistently argued lies outside this committee's concern, so if your procedure puts it in the top tier I need to see exactly which step does that, and whether I can resist that conclusion honestly or only by changing my mind.

Finally, tell me in one paragraph which conception of intelligence the procedure commits us to, since I will have to defend it to three people who will ask why we are not just measuring performance; and say what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which cases it will systematically get wrong. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single sentence saying what this procedure can settle and what it must hand back to us — then give the procedure and the applications, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked. American English, please.
