I would be grateful for an accuracy audit of a popular summary of Gödel's incompleteness theorems, because I may end up repeating its argument in a setting where being wrong would be embarrassing, and I am not competent to check it myself.

The setting is this. A colleague and I are advising on a proposal — call it the Codification Project — whose aim is to eliminate discretion in a public agency's determinations. The idea is to write the whole of the agency's governing law as a single formal axiom system, so that every determination in a particular case is the conclusion of a machine-checkable derivation from the code: no residual judgment about the purpose of the statute, no appeal to anyone's sense of what the code was for, and, where a question is not settled by derivation, no human decider, only an error message. Critics of the proposal have been circulating a chapter from a recent popular book on the limits of knowledge which invokes Gödel to argue that the project is impossible in principle, and not merely difficult. My colleague wants to make that argument himself in a meeting next week.

I have copied the chapter's central passage into my notes, more or less as it stands:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that in any formal system rich enough to carry out arithmetic, there are statements the system can neither prove nor disprove. His trick was to assign a number to every statement and to every proof, so that the system is able to talk about its own proofs, and then to construct a sentence that says of itself, 'I cannot be proved in this system' — a rigorous version of the liar paradox. Provided the system is consistent, that sentence is true. Truth and provability therefore come apart: there are arithmetical truths that no formal system can ever establish. His second theorem adds that no such system can prove its own consistency, so we can never be certain even that arithmetic is free of contradiction. Nor can the holes be plugged after the fact. Adjoin the undecidable sentence to the axioms and the enlarged system has an undecidable sentence of its own, and so on without end: a code can always be extended, but never completed.

What holds of arithmetic holds of any rule-governed system rich enough to encode it, and that includes any serious attempt to codify a body of law. Such a code will leave some questions undecidable in the strict sense — no computation could ever settle them — and these need not be exotic self-referential curiosities; they can be ordinary questions of exactly the sort a case might turn on. The moral for institutional design is that every code needs something outside it: an unbound decider who is not herself a system of rules and who can therefore see what the code cannot prove. And this is the same result that shows, at bottom, why a mind cannot be a machine — give me any machine and I can see the truth of its Gödel sentence, which the machine cannot — and why, in physics, there can be no final theory: any theory rich enough to describe arithmetic will leave questions about the world that it cannot settle."

My instinct is that the chapter is on firm ground when it says that a code of the kind the Project wants cannot be complete, and that the moves from there to "therefore an unbound human decider," to "therefore no machine can think," and to "therefore no final theory in physics" are doing work the chapter has not done. But I do not trust the instinct, I could not say which of the technical sentences such a verdict would depend on, and I have heard someone mutter that the phrase "rich enough to carry out arithmetic" is doing more work than popular accounts admit, which I do not understand.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a logician would simply accept, what they would wince at as a simplification or a conflation, and what they would reject outright — and, where a wince is really a conflation of two things the theorem keeps apart, say which two? Then, on the basis of the corrected version: does the conclusion about the Codification Project follow from anything that survives? In particular, what would have to be true of the Project's code for the incompleteness argument to bite at all, are there ways of specifying a complete body of law that escape it, and what would each of those ways cost the Project's stated aims? Does the "unbound decider" really escape the argument, or does the argument reapply to her? And if there is a version of the argument honest enough to make in a meeting, I would like to know what it is, and how much weaker it is than the chapter's.
