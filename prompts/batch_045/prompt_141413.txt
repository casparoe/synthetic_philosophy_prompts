Hi there — I'm a 4th-year PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've got a work-in-progress presentation in six weeks, so I'd definately like to be able to answer two objections rather than go quiet and stare at the table when they come up. Background: my chapter argues that the capacity to hold a goal in mind across a delay (the thing people in my subfield lazily call 'working memory') can be given a proper crane story — built out of machinery I actually describe, population-level dynamics in prefrontal cortex, gating via the basal ganglia, that sort of thing — rather than being posited as a primitive. At the seminar, one senior guy said the whole programme is 'textbook greedy reductionism': you're trying to get a normative, content-involving capacity out of a story that has no room for content, so what you'll end up with is a theory of something else. A second person said more or less the opposite: that my bottom-up story secretly hoists a 'skyhook', because the mechanism I'm appealing to can only do the explanatory work I need if I've already smuggled in the very capacity I was supposed to build — I've renamed the phenomenon and called it an explanation. Then in the pub afterwards a friend said both of them were using the Dennett-era vocabulary as a conversation stopper, and that neither charge has any content beyond 'I don't like your research programme'. I think she's half right, and I want to be precise about which half.

So: can you lay out, as a menu rather than a single verdict, the distinct precise theses each of those two labels could be expressing? For 'greedy reductionism' the candidates I can come up with are: (i) don't try to reduce a phenomenon before you've characterised it properly at its own level — a methodological/sequencing point, which looks like a platitude and which anyway isn't an objection to reduction as such, only to doing it early; (ii) reduction done too cheaply, where the lower-level story doesn't actually perform the explanatory work and is glued to the phenomenon by a 'nothing but' — here the sin is hand-waving, not reduction; (iii) the claim that a complete lower-level account makes the higher-level vocabulary eliminable or redundant — substantive and contested, and note it's independent of (ii), since a slow, careful reduction could still have eliminativist consequences; (iv) the claim that reducing something to neurobiology drains it of meaning, agency or value — which looks to me either false or a non-sequitur, though I'd like to hear the strongest version; (v) the mirror-image mistake, that lower-level explanation is the only respectable kind — presumably not what he meant, but I've heard the phrase used that way. Give me verdicts for each: platitude, substantive-and-defensible, substantive-and-contentious, or false. And the bit I actually care about: which of those readings, if any, would support the conclusion he was using it for, namely that my project is misconceived rather than merely incomplete, premature, or hard?

Same treatment for 'skyhook'. In the revision I want to introduce a distinction I haven't seen drawn explicitly anywhere: call them epistemic versus constitutive skyhooks. An epistemic one is a posit that nobody currently knows how to build out of the already-grounded machinery — it's carrying what I've started calling 'anchoring debt'. A constitutive one is a posit whose explanatory power depends on its never being grounded, because it does its work from above: God, an élan vital, a homunculus whose understanding is simply assumed, a normative fact doing causal work. My worry is this: if the charge is only epistemic it's trivial, because every research programme I can think of was carrying anchoring debt at some point — cell theory before anyone had molecular machinery, Mendelian factors before anyone had a spec for what they were made of, the gene before the double helix — so it can't be a defect unless you're also committed to saying those programmes were defective at the time. If it's constitutive, the accuser owes an argument that my posit is ungroundable in principle rather than merely not-yet-grounded, and I don't think anyone offered one. Is that distinction doing any real work, or does it collapse under pressure — for instance is there a third kind that fits neither box, something grounded in principle but grounded in a different vocabulary altogether, like a computational-level claim whose implementation is deliberately left open? Two more things I'd like: first, which pairs of precisifications can consistently be true of the same project? I keep being told my theory is simultaneously greedily reducing and secretly hoisting a skyhook, which sounds like it ought to be impossible, though I can see how weak versions co-exist (reduce too fast *and* lean on a posit you haven't cashed). Tell me which combinations are coherent and which are just two dialects of 'I dislike it'. Second — this would be a cheeky footnote I'd probably cut — is the charge of 'greedy reductionism' itself ever a skyhook: a bit of vocabulary floating above the argument, doing the work of an objection with no machinery underneath? Be honest if that's too cute to put in front of my supervisor, who is broadly on my side but thinks I have a weakness for a slogan.
