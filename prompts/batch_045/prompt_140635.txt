I lead a small evaluation group that is about to spend eighteen months building a Bongard-style benchmark of few-shot visual concept induction — the format in which a solver is shown six figures on one side that share a rule and six on the other that violate it, and must state or apply the rule that separates them. Two of my six people are conceptually rather than empirically trained: they read ML papers, specify tasks, and analyse results, but they will not be training or fine-tuning models. The benchmark specification freezes in about four months, so I have to decide now what those two work on, and in what order, because their answers should change how the benchmark is built: what ground truth gets stored, which diagnostics ship alongside accuracy, whether we produce matched variants of each item at all.

The space as I currently see it has these pieces: with twelve examples, infinitely many rules separate the two sets, and accuracy on held-out items drawn from the same generator cannot tell us whether a system found the intended rule or merely one that happens to hold in that generator's accidental regularities — palette, stroke width, layout artefacts, the renderer's habits. Separately, a failure on any given item could be a perceptual failure, a failure to search over candidate rules, or a failure to check a candidate rule against what was perceived, and it is not obvious to me which dominates or how to tell them apart without training anything. And it is not obvious what the target is: an inductive bias resembling ours, or something like the correct rule under changes of surface realization, and these can come apart if human solvers are themselves relying on accidental regularities.

Two operationalizations I keep circling: first, cross-renderer adjudication — generate the same rule under a second, deliberately different surface generator, breaking the accidental correlations while preserving the intended one, and score transfer between the two; second, composition and negation transfer — take two rules a system has already solved, and test it on items requiring their conjunction or the complement of one, on the thought that genuine concept possession should be compositional in a way that pattern matching is not.

I do not want a survey of the area and I do not want papers to read. I want a defended priority ordering of three or four questions for those two people, each with: what it is upstream of and which design decision it settles before the spec freezes; whether it is tractable for people who will not run experiments; whether it is neglected relative to its leverage; and what empirical result over the coming year would move it up or down the list. Say explicitly where you would trade tractability for importance and where you would not. If one of my two operationalizations is a distraction, or is settled by an argument I have not considered rather than by an experiment, say so and say why.

Attach a confidence estimate to each of your ordering claims, and single out the recommendation you are least confident in, with the observation that would most quickly dislodge it.
