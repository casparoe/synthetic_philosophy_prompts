hey — I'm writing up a short explainer on dispositions for a reading group (most of them are new to analytic metaphysics) and I want the fink/mask section to be tight. Here's the shape of it so far:

- Fink = the stimulus condition destroys the disposition itself. (Live wire rigged so that the moment you touch it, it goes dead.)
- Mask = the stimulus condition leaves the disposition untouched and just blocks the manifestation. (Fragile glass packed in foam.)
- Both are counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis — x is disposed to respond m in condition C iff, if x were in C, x would m — so the fix, as I understand it, is to give up on counterfactuals and analyze dispositions through their physical basis: x is disposed to m in C iff x has an intrinsic basis B such that the laws make B-together-with-C sufficient for m.

That version seems to handle masks for free (in a mask case the basis is still sitting there and the analysis is about the basis, not about the surroundings) and finks with one small tweak (the basis only has to be present up until the stimulus arrives). Before I write it up, two questions.

First: is the basis version actually the thing people converged on, or is there a different fix I should be presenting as the main one? I don't want to teach them a dead end.

Second, I invented a case I'm calling a "shy fink" and I'm not sure where it goes. A wine glass is coated with something that senses the first microsecond of crack propagation and stiffens locally, so the crack never completes — the glass never breaks, but it never stops being fragile either (peel off the coating and it shatters like any other glass, and the coating only ever kicks in *because* the glass is fragile and started to give). I've tentatively filed it as a mask, but it keeps looking like a fink to me. Is there an agreed name for the broader category — antidotes that run on the disposition's own incipient manifestation? And shouldn't there be a clean test for deciding fink vs. mask in cases like this, rather than just intuition?

Also my friend says none of this matters unless you're trying to reduce dispositions to non-dispositional stuff, and since I'm happy to just help myself to real causal powers I can drop the section entierly. That sounds right to me but I'd like a second opinion before I cut it.
