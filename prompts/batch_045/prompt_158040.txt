I work as an experiment designer at a large consumer tech company. My daily vocabulary is the causal-inference one used in online experimentation: potential outcomes and the Rubin-style estimand, SUTVA and interference or spillover between units, DAGs and d-separation, pre-treatment covariates versus post-treatment ones, randomized assignment versus instrumental variables and exclusion restrictions, heterogeneous treatment effects, clustered and switchback designs, carryover effects from one period to the next, transportability and external validity, and power and minimum detectable effect calculations. I am fluent in all of that and read the applied literature for work.

Outside work I have been reading about why some countries are much richer than others, and I keep hitting the same three-way argument. One camp says the deep cause is institutions: the rules, property rights, and constraints on expropriation that shape incentives over long horizons. A second says it is geography: climate, disease burden, soils, navigable rivers, and distance from trade. A third says it is culture: norms, trust, and attitudes toward work and cooperation. There is also a well-known empirical move in which something like settler mortality in former colonies is used as a source of variation in institutional quality, as if nature had randomized institutions for us. And hovering over all of it is a set of worries about whether history can support any causal conclusion at all: we get one run of the world, causes feed back into outcomes and outcomes back into causes, small events at critical moments get locked in by path dependence, and nobody can manipulate a country's colonial legacy.

I would like you to translate this debate into my vocabulary, position by position, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what did not survive the translation.

Please take up, in particular:

1. What the deep cause or fundamental cause claim about institutions actually asserts in my terms. Is it a claim about a mediator, about which variable is pre-treatment, about the relative size of path coefficients, or about what happens to the estimate when you condition on geography and culture?
2. Whether the geography objection is a confounding story, a competing-treatment story, or something my vocabulary has no slot for.
3. Whether the culture position is just an unmeasured-confounder complaint, or whether the further point that culture is partly produced by institutions makes it something structurally different, such as a post-treatment variable that cannot be conditioned on without destroying the estimand.
4. How to state the settler-mortality style argument as an instrumental variable design, which of the standard assumptions is hardest to defend, and why users and skeptics of that design seem to disagree about what the estimand even is.
5. Which of the historical worries above my vocabulary can genuinely express, and under what name: interference, carryover, time-varying treatment with absorbing states, violations of transportability, or something else, and which of them it simply cannot say.

I have also started informally separating three different questions that the phrase why are some nations rich might be asking: a variance question about what accounts for the spread in incomes across countries, a lever question about what would happen if we changed some variable in some country now, and an attribution question about what actually made a particular country what it is. Please tell me whether my vocabulary has resources for each, and whether one of the three is secretly the only one the literature is coherent about.

Then give me the residue report: what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion and what you had to fudge to make it fit, and what has no counterpart in my framework at all. For the residue, I want your judgment on the direction of the indictment. A colleague tells me the entire debate is confused because it asks for the cause of an effect whereas experiments only estimate the effect of a cause, so the historians are asking a question my framework is not even in the business of answering. Is that a real limitation I should take seriously, or is he using a distinction as a way to avoid the question? And finally: does the residue point at something missing in my vocabulary that would also bite me in real experiments at work, or is it only an artifact of asking questions about single, unrepeatable historical units?
