I am a fourth-year PhD student in molecular systems biology and, because I am the methods person in my group, I chair the weekly hypothesis-triage meeting. Once or twice a week someone brings in a newly formulated explanatory hypothesis — usually a proposed mechanism for a phenomenon we have already measured — and the meeting has to decide whether to redirect a month of a postdoc's time toward it, with the PI signing off at the end. Our recurring failure is that we treat the findings a hypothesis was built to fit as though they confirmed it: someone reverse-engineers a mechanism out of data already in the lab notebook, and because the mechanism accommodates that data neatly, we treat it as well supported and commit resources to it. I have been reading about the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory — roughly, that once a finding is already known, it cannot raise a theory's probability by the usual machinery, and that the proposed repairs involve replacing the actual prior with a hypothetical one fixed before the evidence was in hand. I want whatever is salvageable in that discussion turned into a one-page procedure my lab can actually run in forty minutes, written for people with no training in formal epistemology and no tolerance for jargon.

What I need from you is the procedure itself, in a fixed order, and then its application. Specifically:

1. A numbered sequence of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer and where the procedure terminates with no support. The steps should be runnable with the materials a lab actually has: the hypothesis as stated, the old findings it is supposed to explain, the lab's current rival hypotheses, and whoever is in the room.

2. A way of sorting the old findings by provenance before any weight is assigned. I have in mind four categories — findings that were inputs to the construction of the hypothesis; findings the hypothesis was adjusted to fit after it was first stated; findings everyone already knew but that the proposer did not consult; and findings that were anomalous for the reigning model — together with a rule for what each category is worth, and for what happens when a finding falls into more than one.

3. A usable substitute for the hypothetical prior. My idea is a blinded rebuild: someone who did not help construct the hypothesis writes down, before being shown how well it fits, what they would expect such a hypothesis to predict. Tell me how to run this honestly in a six-person lab where everyone has already seen the data, how to record the result so it cannot be quietly revised afterwards, what to do when no genuinely uninvolved person exists, and how much weight the outcome of the rebuild should carry relative to the rest of the procedure.

4. A rule for the case where several live hypotheses in the lab's register accommodate the same finding. I do not want a likelihood ratio nobody can compute; I want something a group can apply by counting and arguing, including guidance on when the rival set is too poorly specified to count at all.

5. An explicit stop rule and an expiry. The procedure should say when old evidence is enough to justify a bet of a person-month and when it is not; require whatever novel test it licenses to be named, with a prediction stated in advance and a date; and say what happens to the hypothesis when that date passes.

6. The places where judgment cannot be eliminated, marked as such rather than disguised as steps — in particular any place where the decision turns on how much our group values explanatory unification versus predictive novelty, and any place where the procedure has to appeal to the honesty of records we may not have kept.

Please also add a short closing paragraph on what the procedure cannot do: whether it can yield a number without manufacturing spurious precision, what it assumes about the completeness of the rival set, and whether it can ever substitute for a genuinely new test.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our own area, showing the steps as you go rather than jumping to verdicts. First: a postdoc proposes that a metabolite produced by a gut bacterium explains a diet effect we published last year, where the metabolite was selected out of an untargeted screen precisely because it was elevated in exactly the condition where the effect is strongest. Second: a visitor proposes, by analogy with a structure characterised in a related organism, that our protein of interest forms condensates — a proposal made before anyone looked — which turns out to explain a two-year-old anomaly, a subpopulation of cells that the reigning kinetic model says should die but does not, and to explain it without new parameters. Third: the lab's own flagship hypothesis, the one on which the PI's grant renewal is built, whose only support is a pattern we have known for three years, which the hypothesis was written to fit, which a competing model from another group also reproduces after a single tweak, and which required two auxiliary assumptions to accommodate. Run the third case straight and give me the result even where it is one I would rather not take into the meeting.
