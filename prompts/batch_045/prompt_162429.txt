I've been reading the rationalist corner of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, and the thing that still irritates me is how the common prior assumption gets used in arguments about disagreement: wheeled in to prove that rational people cannot agree to disagree, then quietly dropped the moment someone points out that real disagreements are stubborn and long-lived. I think the dialectic is mislocated — that the content of a common prior is not an agreement guarantee but a conservation law, and that once you see it that way the standard deflation (you can always launder divergent priors into a common prior over an enlarged space) loses most of its force. Below is a draft I mean to post; I'd like comments on it first.

---

**A prior is a budget, not a birthplace**

**§1. Setup, and the received dialectic.** Two agents share a prior P over a space carrying a fixed event E, with P(E) = p, plus each agent's private information. Rounds run n = 0, 1, 2, …; F_n is the public record (everything said in the open); agent i's information at round n is H^i_n = P_i ∨ F_n, where P_i is her private signal, fixed from the start; and X^i_n = P(E | H^i_n) is her belief.

The standard results: if the two agents' posteriors for E are common knowledge, then (given a common prior) they are equal; and if they merely announce posteriors back and forth in a finite space, the announcements converge. So the received moral is that persistent disagreement must be traced to priors, and that a disagreement about priors is really a disagreement about where priors come from — the familiar move that uncommon priors require origin disputes. And the familiar deflation: since one can always enlarge the space with a variable Θ at which nature hands each agent her prior, a common prior can always be restored, so the assumption is either substantive and false or true and empty.

**§2. The conservation reading.** Two facts, both cheap.

(P) *Public projection.* Under a common prior, E[X^i_n | F_n] = P(E | F_n) for every i and n. This is the tower property plus F_n ⊆ H^i_n, and it needs no assumption that anyone's information partition is common knowledge. Consequence: conditional on the public record, no agent's belief is expected to sit anywhere other than the public posterior. Disagreement is unpredictable noise around the public posterior, not drift. From the public's vantage, you cannot expect to disagree.

(Q) *Private vantage.* Suppose the question is eventually settled by the totality of what anyone learns, so that each X^j_∞ = 1_E. Then E[X^j_∞ | H^i_n] = X^i_n: conditional on everything I know now, I expect your final belief to equal my current belief — and of course I expect my own final belief to equal my current belief as well. That is exactly why I cannot expect to profit by betting against you; it is (P) read from a different vantage, and I take the classic no-trade conclusion to be this conservation law plus extra assumptions about common knowledge of willingness to trade that I am not leaning on here.

So: a common prior is a no-arbitrage condition on argument, not a promise of agreement.

**§3. The budget.** Fix one agent. Her belief path is a martingale with respect to her own filtration, with values in [0,1]. For any square-integrable martingale, E[Σ_{n<N} (X_{n+1} − X_n)²] = E[X_N²] − E[X_0²], since the cross terms vanish. Let N → ∞ and use X² ≤ X: total expected squared revision is at most p − E[X_0²] = E[X_0(1 − X_0)], with equality exactly when the question is eventually settled outright. The currency you spend when you change your mind is squared distance, and your balance is your expected current uncertainty. Someone sitting at 0.9 has 0.09 in the bank: a twenty-point move spends nearly half of it, a thirty-point move spends all of it. Two corollaries. First, private information pre-spends the budget, since E[X_0(1 − X_0)] = p(1 − p) − Var(X_0): what you already know is precisely what you can no longer spend on moving. Second, the number of rounds is irrelevant — the bound does not grow with how much evidence arrives. In the upcrossing form: the expected number of times a belief path crosses upward from below a to above b is bounded by a constant depending only on a, b and p, and not on the length of the exchange. You may revise forever. You may not thrash.

**§4. What this does to laundering.** The laundering construction is itself a common-prior model, so it inherits (P), (Q) and the budget: enlarging the space does not stop anyone's belief path from being a martingale, and does not let the public expect disagreement in a particular direction. So the deflation is wrong about where the content lives. Laundering does defeat the agreement conclusion — with Θ private, the agents can share every signal and still disagree, because learning your prior does not make me adopt it — but it cannot defeat the conservation law. What laundering does cost is, I think, an uncomfortable posit: for disagreement to persist with Θ in play, the origin variable must be evidentially inert with respect to the public record, since any public evidence about which prior is correct would start resolving the dispute. And the budget prices that: movement in your view about Θ and movement in your view about E are drawn from the same account.

**§5. The trilemma, and why I care.** Persistent disagreement is cheap. Churning disagreement is expensive, and can only be funded by genuine expected uncertainty. Confident disagreement must be frozen, because exhausting the budget just is arriving at resolution. So confident, persistent, and churning are three properties that a common prior will not fund all at once, however much laundering you do — pick at most two. That gives a diagnostic for the long-running arguments the forums specialize in: not do these people share a prior, but is their disagreement thrashing? Note that the constraint is ex ante; realized paths can overspend it, so it is a coherence test on stated confidence, not something you can run on a single observed path.
