I sit on the grants committee of the Marchbank Foundation, which makes five-year, high-risk programme grants in basic science. Our charter commits us to funding work that would not survive a conventional peer-review panel, and it instructs us to fund 'science' without ever saying what would fail that description. Because we expect most of what we fund to be wrong, a failed prediction has never by itself been a reason to stop paying. Lately, though, three of us have started to suspect that we are making demarcation decisions case by case while pretending to be making only judgments of quality, and the current renewal has forced the question.

The renewal is the Kestrel Programme (PI: Dr. Amara Okoye), which claims that a specified class of rapid behavioural adaptations across taxa is produced by a single heritable mechanism. I will summarise its record in the terms we have been using, because the disagreement turns on how to describe it:

1. In year 1 it predicted an effect nobody had looked for, in a species nobody had studied, with a directional and roughly quantitative specification; the effect was found about as predicted.
2. In year 2 it predicted a parallel effect in a second taxon; the result was null, in a study with adequate power by our own statistical consultant's review.
3. Its response to (2) was a new auxiliary hypothesis about a gating condition. That auxiliary had consequences nobody had tested; in year 3 an independent group tested one of them and confirmed it.
4. In year 4 it predicted a third effect. The estimate came out with the opposite sign to the prediction.
5. Its response to (4) was a revision of the model's mathematical form that introduces a free parameter able to absorb either sign, offered without a new test. When the year-1 effect was independently replicated this year at roughly a third of the predicted magnitude, the PI said the mechanism constrains the existence and direction of the effect but not its size.
6. The programme now states its core claim as: the mechanism is whatever produces the observed pattern of rapid adaptation in this class.

Our own precedents, which we have never written down: we renewed a programme with one failed headline prediction because it had produced two confirmed predictions made before the relevant data existed and had declared in advance which auxiliary assumptions it would allow itself to revise; we declined to renew a programme with no failed predictions at all, on the grounds that its central claim had been restated three times to fit whatever data appeared; and we declined a programme whose only empirical successes were accommodations of facts already known when the prediction was framed.

For the last cycle we have been drafting an instrument we call the Auxiliary Register: before data collection a grantee files the predictions it will count as tests, the auxiliary hypotheses it may revise without supplying a new test, and — for each such permitted revision — one further consequence to be tested inside the funding period. I have proposed adding a hostile-design clause, under which the programme must nominate one result it would concede to be refuting, and a designated critic from outside the programme must certify that the test could actually produce that result. The Kestrel Programme predates the Register and has nothing on file.

We are split three ways: renew outright (the record looks like a healthy programme absorbing punishment, and item 3 is the sort of thing we exist to fund); decline (items 5 and 6 are the pattern we said was disqualifying in the precedent above, and the freedom to disown effect sizes makes item 1 weaker evidence than it looked); renew with conditions. Our colleague Professor Densmore argues that the whole exercise is incoherent — that no programme has ever been abandoned on a refutation, that every celebrated historical success involved protecting a core claim with auxiliaries, and that a Register is just an unfalsifiability test wearing a lab coat.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate on Thursday, 900–1,200 words, addressed to the committee with a copy of the operative conditions going to Dr. Okoye. It should: state a single recommendation — renew, decline, or renew with conditions — in the first two sentences; say how you are classifying this record and why, using our three precedents rather than general philosophy; specify the conditions, if any, as things a working scientist could actually comply with, with dates and a decision gate; state whether the Register and the hostile-design clause should be applied to this programme retroactively, and what fairness requires either way; and give the strongest version of the argument against your own recommendation — Densmore's or another — in a form its supporters would recognise, before saying why it does not carry, or what in the recommendation it should change. Please do not cite literature or supply references; argue the case from the record and the precedents as I have given them.
