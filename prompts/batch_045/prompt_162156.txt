I sit on my university's Emerging Research Risks Committee — I'm the token philosopher, the rest are biologists and one administrator — and we vote on the 14th. The proposal is a synthetic-bio group's first outdoor trial of an engineered bacterium that digests polyethylene in cold seawater: 90 days, inside a netted pen in the harbor. The upside is real but modest, mostly proof-of-concept for a cleanup method that might scale. The scary part is the plasmid moving into native marine bacteria.

We ran an elicitation with outside experts and got no numbers, only ranges. For "transfer into a native population that persists after we pull the pen," they gave us 0.1% to 6%. For "given transfer, detectable and irreversible change to nitrogen cycling in the harbor," 2% to 60%. Nobody would sharpen, and I think pressing them would have produced fake precision. So expected harm comes out as an interval that overlaps the expected-benefit interval, and our charter tells us to approve where benefits clearly exceed harms — which right now has no meaning.

Two things came up in the email thread that I want actually dealt with. First: a decision theorist we co-opted says to just average, i.e. treat each interval as a uniform spread over the possible probabilities and maximize expected value of that mixture. Someone else says that's cheating, because the interval records our not knowing which probability is right, and putting a probability distribution over that is deciding the question twice and smuggling in confidence we don't have. This matters, because under the first rule the trial passes and under "don't act unless it looks fine at every probability in the interval" it fails. Second: the team admits their own monitoring plan has a nasty feature — if month one comes back ambiguous, their range for transfer gets wider, something like 0.05% to 15%, because an ambiguous result is compatible with several different models of plasmid movement and each one drags the interval a different way. So "run a smaller pilot first and learn something" may leave us less able to decide than we are today. Nobody at the last meeting touched that.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. Be decisive: recommend approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions and defend it — don't survey the options and leave the committee to pick, and don't just restate that the probabilities are imprecise. Commit to one way of reasoning with them and say why the other one loses, at least here. Keep it under about 800 words; they won't read more.

Sections in this order: Recommendation (~75 words, bottom line first, with one sentence that could go straight into the minutes), Why the ranges dont settle it (~250 — take a position on the averaging dispute), The strongest thing on the other side (~150 — make it better than its advocates did), Conditions and timeline (~200 — concrete and dated, including exactly what we do if month-one monitoring is ambiguous), What would make me change my vote (~100).
