I'm on the platform team at a company of about 200 engineers, and I own what we internally call the release evidence gate: before anything ships, whoever is shipping has to file a short note saying why we believe the thing works, and I'm the one who signs off (or gets yelled at) when it turns out we shouldn't have. The same argument keeps coming up and I want to stop having it from scratch every time: when does a tool's report about itself count as actual evidence that the tool is right? Concretely, most weeks we see some mix of — a linter that prints "0 violations", a coverage tool that says 96%, a static analyzer whose expected-results suite was generated by the previous version of itself, a self-hosting compiler that compiled itself and then passed its own test suite, and a cluster health check that reports "all green" while being the single most likely component to have silently died. My rule of thumb right now is "trust it unless someone can produce a case where it was wrong", but that just pushes the problem back to who has to produce what, and in practice it means whoever is loudest in the thread wins.

A friend doing a philosophy PhD told me this is basically a known thing in epistemology — I think she called it the bootstrapping problem, or the problem of "easy knowledge" — and the toy version she gave me was that you can't check whether your eyesight works by using your eyesight to read a colour chart. She also said there's apparently a whole fight about whether that's really circular or whether it's fine as long as the source has some independent track record, and something about premise circularity versus rule circularity that I definately didn't follow. I don't need the literature and please don't give me citations or names to look up — I just want whatever the real distinctions are, translated into something my team can use, because none of us recieved any training in this and the compliance lead has to rubber-stamp whatever we adopt, so it can't contain any step that needs a philosophy degree to answer.

So: write me a procedure. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure just stops with a "no", and the point where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is about how much risk we're willing to accept rather than about evidence. It has to fit on one page, be runnable in ten minutes by a new grad on call at 2am, and be honest about which steps are really judgment calls wearing a checklist costume. Assume the failure modes I care about are ordinary bugs and silent failures, not an attacker deliberately lying to us — that's a different document we already have. Then run it on three of the cases above (pick the interesting ones, including at least one where the answer means I have to delete a check that people really like, and tell me how I'd argue for that in a meeting where they push back), and finish with a short section on what this procedure cannot tell us, no matter how carefully we fill it in.
