Hey — I'm putting together a problem set for my upper-level seminar on personal identity for next spring, and I want to build it around one person's actual track record rather than around cases floating free of anybody's commitments. The person is a senior colleague of mine — call her Dana — who has been thinking about upload cases for years and has gone on record, in talks and in print, on a bunch of them. Her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them:

(1) Gradual replacement, the standard one: swap one neuron at a time for a silicon unit playing exactly the same causal role in the network, over about a decade, with the person behaving and reporting normally throughout. She says: you survive, and it isn't even a close call.

(2) Scan, destroy, rebuild the next day, functionally identical down to the last connection weight. She says: that's death. The thing that wakes up is a successor — a very good copy, but a copy.

(3) Fission: the two hemispheres end up driving two different bodies, each with the full psychology. She says: neither is you. Two equally good candidates means there's no answer to "which one is me," and a relation that has no answer isn't survival.

(4) Replacement units that match the originals only from the outside: same input–output mapping for every signal they actually receive, but they get there by a different internal route, and they carry internal states the original never had — states no downstream part is sensitive to. She says: still you. Differences that nothing else in the system can see aren't differences that matter.

(5) The same decade-long replacement as in (1), except that the subject is kept in a medically induced coma for the middle stretch, during which something like 80% of the swapping happens; conscious and normal before and after. She says: still you. Consciousness going dark for a while doesn't interrupt whatever it is that's doing the work.

So: she's not a substrate chauvinist, she isn't moved by "same person, new body" talk when there's been a gap, she's happy to count external functional equivalence as equivalence, and she doesn't require continuous consciousness. But I've been siting on these five for a week and I cant tell what's actually load-bearing. As far as I can see, at least three different rules fit all of them: something about step size (small increments preserve you, big jumps don't), something about the same concrete system never ceasing to operate, and something about the person being wherever the causal organization gets realized. I suspect there's a fourth I'm missing, or a way of sharpening one of those three that makes it collapse into another.

Here's the case I want to put on the set, and I don't think this exact configuration gets discussed much. Call it the fade-over. Nothing is replaced in place. Instead a second system — the understudy — is built alongside the original and run in parallel, receiving the same inputs, functionally identical to the original by the standard she accepted in (4). A mixer decides whose output drives behaviour, and it shifts by one percent a week over about two years. The original is never modified; it just gets increasingly ignored. Both systems run continuously for the whole period. At the end the mixer takes everything from the understudy and the original is switched off and destroyed. The behavioural and verbal stream is continuous throughout, with no discontinuity anyone can detect. I'm deliberately not stipulating whether the understudy is conscious during the middle stretch — leave that open, because (5) suggests she might not care, and because the usual argument that preserving function must preserve experience might or might not be doing work for her here.

What I want from you:

- The rules that fit her five verdicts, stated sharply enough that they genuinely come apart from each other, including any I've missed — and say honestly if two of them turn out to be the same rule once you fix what counts as the subject.
- What each rule predicts about the fade-over, and specifically how each one answers the question of what the subject is during the middle stretch: the original system, the understudy, the composite of both plus the mixer, or none of them. I think that question is where the real action is.
- Your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring it. Flag if (3) is doing more work than it looks like it's doing: at the midpoint there are two complete systems running, so if her no-answer principle generalizes, this case might come out as death for a reason that has nothing to do with step size — though maybe it's distinguishable, since one of the two candidates is being retired rather than going on to live a life.
- The further case you'd put on the set to separate whichever rules are still tied afterwards. My candidate is to run the same setup but flip the mixer from zero to a hundred in a millisecond, with both systems already running — I'm not convinced that's the sharpest probe available.

And this is the part I'll actually be grading: keep clear which moves here are settled and which are contested. I want to be able to tell students where people who work on this agree, where the disagreement is real and live, and where a tempting answer is just one side of an argument. Don't present a contested view as the consensus, and give me confidence levels on the individual claims, not only on the headline verdict.
