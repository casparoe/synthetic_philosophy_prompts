I'm a philosophy PhD student working on anthropic reasoning and personal identity, and our graduate reading group puts out an end-of-term satire issue of the department newsletter — I'm writing the lead piece this year and I want it to be the kind of thing that makes people who actually work on this material laugh and then feel slightly implicated. I'll be reading it aloud to a room that includes two people who think the quantum immortality argument is basically sound and three who think it's obviously confused, and I'd like both camps to feel the joke was fair rather than cheap.

Write it as an internal strategy memo — a Subject line, a To/From/Date block, a short executive summary, four or five numbered sections, an optional small "Exhibit A" table, and a closing recommendation — of roughly 700 to 900 words, from the Chief Actuary of a large life-insurance company to its board of directors, reporting on the firm's conversion to "first-person actuarial science" after the board voted to accept the quantum immortality argument. Voice is flat corporate deadpan throughout: no winking at the reader, no character who steps outside the memo to explain the fallacy, no punchline that says in so many words that first-personal survival can't discharge third-personal obligations — the reductio has to land on its own, through the memo's own institutional logic, and it should remain legible to a reader who has never heard of the many-worlds interpretation.

The content it needs to get right, and get the jokes out of: (i) the quants' two competing reserve conventions — one that counts surviving branches equally and one that weights them by their squared-amplitude measure — where the entire multi-billion-dollar reserve release turns out to come from which convention the CFO prefers rather than from any physics, and where the measure-weighted numbers show the surviving branches shrinking toward nothing; (ii) the risk committee's discovery that the surviving branches are overwhelmingly ones in which the insured is catastrophically maimed rather than unharmed, so the firm's real exposure is the "perpetual claimant" — a policyholder who, in their own continuous experience, is never dead and never off claim, accruing disability and critical-illness benefits forever, an infinite liability in first-personal terms and a finite one on the books; (iii) the awkwardness that death benefits are paid to a beneficiary, so every payout the company actually makes happens in the branches where the client dies, and nothing about the client's subjective survival helps the balance sheet — which the memo should notice and then route around institutionally (a "self-beneficiary" rider naming one's own surviving copy, perhaps) rather than treat as a refutation; (iv) a paragraph or footnote from the Legal Department on whether operating a home quantum-suicide apparatus triggers the standard suicide exclusion that voids coverage for a death by suicide in the first year or two of a policy, given that the act is third-personally lethal in nearly all measure while first-personally it never results in death at all, plus the adverse-selection worry that the only customers who want the rider are the ones planning to use the machine; and (v) the Analytics division's proposal to fund client-owned apparatuses as a cheap in-house test of many-worlds, which the memo must note can never confirm anything for the firm, since from the company's own standpoint the data will show near-total mortality in every branch the company itself is around to read. Please close with a recommendation the board could actually approve — say, adopting first-person accounting for regulatory capital while continuing to settle claims third-personally — and let that be the joke. Invent whatever institutional furniture you need (product names, a standards board, a regulator's letter), but keep the target the argument and the style of reasoning it licenses, not any named person.
