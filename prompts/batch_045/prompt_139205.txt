I do admin support for a health coverage committee (small system, dont ask) and one of the things we decide is what counts as a disease for the purposes of what the system pays for. Ive been going back through about five years of our own decisions because we have a new application next week and I genuinely cant tell what rule weve been running. I went looking and found out theres a whole literature on this -- people called naturalists who want a value-free account of dysfunction, people called normativists who say disease is at least partly a value judgement, and hybrids in the middle -- but I cant tell which of those weve accidentally been implementing, or whether our precedents even separate them.

Our decisions so far:

- A 22-year-old with generalised joint hypermobility, painless, no dislocations, somewhere in the top 1% for flexibility. Not a disease. Reason given: unusual, but nothing is impaired.
- A 15-year-old boy in the bottom 3rd percentile for height with no endocrine or skeletal abnormality -- just a small kid, perfectly healthy -- whose parents applied for growth hormone because his height is costing him at school and, they argued, will cost him later. Not a disease. Reason: his growth system is working normally; the disadvantage is real and the treatment works, but nothing has malfunctioned.
- A 30-year-old woman with bilateral tubal blockage who has never wanted children and isnt distressed about it. Disease. Reason given: reproductive function is impaired, and what she happens to want is beside the point.
- Age-related hearing loss in a 78-year-old. Not a disease -- normal for his age -- although we do fund hearing aids out of a separate appliances budget.
- Eleven people from the same town with the same kidney impairment, traced to contaminated well water. Disease. Reason: the cause is irrelevant, the internal state impairs function, and who cleans up the well is a different question from what we owe these patients.

Our chair also says at every meeting that the real question is whether a thing is our job, and our charter has a line about not medicalising social problems, which so far has been used to say no about twice and been quietly ignored once.

The new application is a 31-year-old man raised from birth in a violent and unpredictable household. He has an exaggerated startle response, fast threat detection, does well on timed and competitive tasks, and is in the bottom 10% for men his age on sustained attention and working memory in quiet unstructured settings. He is unemployed and wants help. Two groups have put in briefs.

The research group says nothing in him is malfunctioning: a stress-response system that gets early cues of danger calibrates itself for speed and vigilance at the cost of patience and quiet concentration, and his is doing exactly what that kind of system does. On any dysfunction requirement, they say, this is not a disease; the right response is income, housing and job design, and calling it one both misdescribes him and quietly moves the bill from the housing authority to us.

The clinic says his measured abilities are in the bottom 10% for his age and sex, that we already fund treatment for people with identical test scores whose early lives were fine, and that refusing him because we sympathise with the cause is the same mistake we avoided in the well-water case. Their line is that the research group is really arguing from the environment the mechanism was shaped in rather than the one he actually lives in, and that our precedents never said which of those two reference classes we were using.

What I need from you: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our precedents is actually the closest analog (I keep going back and forth between the well-water people and the short kid), and whether we can decide this at all without adopting a rule we havent adopted yet. If our precedents really do underdetermine it, tell me what we would be committing to with each answer, and give me the further case I should put on the agenda for the meeting after next -- the one that would force us to say whether normal functioning means statistically typical in the population we actually have or functioning as designed for the environment we were built in. And tell me whether the dont-medicalise-social-problems line is doing any work here or whether it just fires in whichever direction you already wanted to go.
