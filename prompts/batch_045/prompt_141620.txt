I'm writing a novel, and one of my characters is an actuary who's drifted into amateur philosophy. There's a scene where she tries to explain to her sister why you should let how confident you feel track the real chances of a thing happening. A pop philosophy book I picked up sums the idea up like this:

"David Lewis's Principal Principle says that rational confidence defers to objective chance: if the chance of rain tomorrow is 0.3, and that's all you know, then a rational person should be exactly 0.3 confident that it rains — no more, no less. This is what licences the ordinary practice of reading chances off observed frequencies: you've seen that roughly 30% of days like this one ended in rain, so you set your confidence at 0.3, and that's that. It also explains why believing in luck is irrational, since nobody can ever rationally be more confident of an outcome than the chance it actually has. There's one caveat: the principle only applies to 'admissible' evidence, which Lewis glosses as evidence about the past and present, since facts about the future aren't the sort of thing you can have evidence about yet. And because the principle is a purely synchronic constraint, it says nothing at all about how to revise your beliefs over time. So when you learn on Monday that the chance of Tuesday's storm has risen to 0.9, the principle is silent about whether you should now expect the storm — and it gives you no licence to go back and revise what you think the chance of the storm was back on Friday, an idea a handful of writers have toyed with under the name 'the Backward Principal Principle', and which is simply confused."

I'm loosly working from this for her bit of dialogue, though the "nobody can ever be more confident than the chance" line sounds a bit to glib to me and I can't say why. Could you go through it and tell me which parts someone who actually works on chance would sign off on, which are the kind of simplifications they'd grudgingly let pass, and which are just wrong? I'm most curious about the last bit, wich the book waves away — is it really just confusion, or is there something to it? And if there's a cleaner way to say whatever the accurate part is, that'd help too, because she shouldn't sound like she's reading an encyclopedia.
