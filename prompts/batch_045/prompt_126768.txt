Trying to work out what I actually think about Kant's formula of universal law — act only on a maxim you could at the same time will as a universal law, roughly — by going with my gut on cases, and my verdicts won't line up.

- I use a friend's university login to get past a paywall for one paper I really need: fine, honestly, I don't even feel bad.
- The self-checkout at the supermarket undercharges me by a tenner, I notice, I say nothing: fine, or at least too trivial to be a moral matter at all.
- My neighbour's kid hands me too much cash for the bike I'm selling him and I pocket the difference: not fine, and clearly worse than the supermarket one, though it's the same tenner and I can't say what the difference is even supposed to be.
- I let an AI draft my nephews birthday message and send it as mine: not fine. I let an AI draft the weekly status update my manager makes me file: completely fine and I'd defend it.
- I walk past the guy slumped on the subway steps and give nothing: not fine, but it feels like a different kind of not-fine from the other ones — more like failing at something than breaking something.
- I drive into town alone on a day the council has asked people not to: not fine, but I know perfectly well my one car changes nothing, which makes me think the "imagine everyone does it" move is asking the wrong question here.

Everyone tells me the real split is between maxims you can't even conceive as universal and ones you can conceive but not will, but my verdicts don't sort along that line as far as I can tell.

My working principle has been: a maxim is out when the action only has the point it has, or only works, because other people aren't doing it — universal acceptance would defeat it. That nails the paywall one and the deception-ish ones, I think. But it seems to miss the subway case entirely (there's no point of my walking past that universal walking-past destroys), and I can't tell whether it convicts the supermarket one or lets it off.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly, and if not, which verdict do I have to give up — the supermarket one or the bike one or the driving one? Please also say what your principle predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: a friend asks whether I like her new boyfriend, I think he's awful, I say yes. And be honest about where your reconstruction is shakiest and what argument would change your mind about which of my verdicts has to go.
