My mate who TA'd my philosophy of religion class runs a little Discord where he hands down verdicts on "can God do X?" questions, and I've been keeping a list because I can't work out what rule he's actually running. His rulings so far:

- a stone so heavy he can't lift it: no — he says that's not a task, just a sentence shaped like one
- make 2+2 equal 5: no
- make a square circle: no
- sin, lie, or break a promise: no, but he always adds that this is a different flavour of no than the 2+2 one — something like "won't in the strongest sense there is"
- annihilate himself: no
- bring it about that something which already happened didn't happen: no
- create creatures whose choices he doesn't determine: yes, obviously
- guarantee that a genuinely free creature never does anything wrong: no — you can have free creatures or you can have the spotless record, and each half on its own is well within his power, just not together
- take on real limitations for a while and then go back to how he was (the incarnation / kenosis sort of case): yes, and he's weirdly confident about it
- create someone more powerful than himself: no
- make another omnipotent being: he's flip-flopped, and last I saw he said maybe, but then there'd be things neither of them could do on their own

The new case I want a prediction on: could he permanently and irrevocably hand over one of his powers to a creature — say the power to annihilate any soul he feels like — so that afterwards (a) he can't do that thing, (b) he can't take the power back, and (c) he can't make the creature give it back? He'd still be insanely powerful afterwards, just not omipotent.

I think there are at least two rules that fit his track record and disagree here. One is roughly "he can bring about any genuinely possible state of affairs, and the stone case fails because 'too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift' doesn't pick out any possible object" — which I'd expect to predict yes, he can hand the power over, and then he simply isn't omnipotent any more. The other is roughly "omnipotence is essential to him, and he can't perform any act whose success consists in his lacking a power" — which is the real reason the stone case fails, and which predicts no. What I want from you: what are the actual candidate rules that fit the verdicts above (I suspect there's more than my two, maybe something about intrinsic change, or about which 'can' you're using, or about tasks whose success conditions undercut the agent), what does each say about the new case, which one is he most likely running given especially the incarnation verdict and the moral-cases verdict, and what extra case should I throw at him next to force the difference into the open? Also worth saying whether anything turns on two variants: the power snaps back by itself after a thousand years, or he keeps a veto over its use that he's promised never to exercise.
