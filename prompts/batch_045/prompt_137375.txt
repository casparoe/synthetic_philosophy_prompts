Public risk regulation has a shape I have not been able to state as a single principle, and I would like you to reconstruct the decision theory it presupposes — where the object of reconstruction is the whole pattern, not any one of its parts.

Three features, as I understand them. (1) Agencies monetise mortality risk. A proposed rule that lowers the chance that people die is credited with a benefit equal to the number of statistical deaths averted multiplied by a fixed monetary value per statistical life, and the rule is approved when that figure exceeds its cost. The accounting is linear: twice the population exposed to the same per-person risk yields twice the benefit, however many people that comes to. (2) The same agencies discard small probabilities. A lifetime risk below some de minimis threshold — in the guidance I have seen, often put at roughly one in a million — is treated as negligible and is not carried through the calculation at all, even when enough people are exposed that the expected number of deaths is not negligible. (3) The same governments spend real money against low-probability, enormous-stakes risks: surveys to detect asteroids on Earth-crossing orbits, programmes to secure nuclear materials, pandemic preparedness. There the annual probability is small and the thing at stake is, depending on the case, millions of lives or the shape of everything that comes after.

Set feature (3) beside the case that everyone in these arguments treats as the reductio. A stranger tells you that unless you hand over your wallet he will, by means he refuses to specify, arrange for an astronomically large number of people to be tortured. Any non-zero credence you give his story, multiplied by a large enough number of victims, outweighs every ordinary use of the money. Nobody funds interventions recommended on that basis, and I do not think the refusal is mere inattention.

Call fanaticism the thesis that expected value settles the matter on its own: for any sure good and any probability however small, there is some finite stake for which the gamble is to be preferred. Then:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice strictly presuppose anti-fanaticism, or is it compatible with plain expected-value maximisation once ordinary empirical claims about which small probabilities are trustworthy are added? Separate what the pattern genuinely requires from what it merely fits well with.

2. The evidential defence. Distinguish probabilities delivered by a tested model with known error bars — impact frequencies for near-Earth objects, say — from probabilities attached to a bare assertion with no model behind it. Is that distinction enough to keep expected value and dismiss the stranger? Press on the obvious move: a careful agent is uncertain which model is right and should average over models. If averaging gives the stranger's hypothesis any share at all, does the huge stake survive, so that the defence fails? Does model-averaging rescue the regulator, or does it merely relocate the problem?

3. The structural objection. If the fanatical ordering is unbounded, then for every bet there is a lower-probability, higher-stakes bet that beats it, and no policy is maximal. Is that a reason to think the thresholds are doing real work rather than expressing squeamishness, or is the regress harmless because in practice probabilities fall faster than stakes can be inflated?

4. Consider a specific anti-fanatical rule: fund an intervention only if its probability of success clears a bar that falls as the stakes rise, but falls more slowly than one over the stakes — require p at least k divided by the square root of the value at stake, or k divided by its logarithm, so that the expected value demanded grows with what is at stake. Does this reproduce (1) through (3)? Where does it place the asteroid survey, and where the stranger? Is it principled or a curve fitted to intuition? And is that what the practice looks like, or does the practice look more like a hard probability floor?

5. What would have to change. If a government became a convinced fanatic, what would regulatory analysis become — does every budget collapse into whichever long shot names the largest stake, and what happens when two such long shots compete? If instead it placed an upper bound on the value of the future, would feature (3) survive? Would asteroid detection and pandemic preparedness have to be reargued as insurance or as risk-aversion rather than as expectation, and would those arguments be weaker?

6. Where is the practice underdetermined? Could a bounded-value theorist and a probability-threshold theorist both endorse all three features, and is there any decision the practice actually makes that would tell them apart? Concretely: a programme costing fifty million that reduces this century's chance of civilisational collapse by one in ten thousand, against a programme costing the same that has one chance in a billion of securing 10^18 future lives. Does the practice, as it stands, say anything about that pair — and should it?
