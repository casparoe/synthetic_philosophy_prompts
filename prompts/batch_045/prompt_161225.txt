I am a PhD student in machine learning, working on interpretability, and for two terms I am embedded with the evaluation team at a lab that ships a vision-and-language model into regulated settings — clinical triage support and insurance claims handling. Part of my job is to help the team write the feature descriptions that go into our model cards and safety cases: statements of the form "direction 14.882 in layer 22 represents X". Those statements are read and signed off by a governance lead with a clinical background, and in two or three disputes a month they are the crux of the disagreement: one engineer reads a feature off its causal profile (what it responds to, what happens to behaviour when it is ablated or clamped), another reads it off training history (what it was ever useful for, at which stage, under which objective). The engineers have no philosophy training, they have roughly half a day per feature, and the documents they produce have to survive external review. So what I need is a written protocol, not a discussion.

Please write that protocol: a decision procedure the team can run on a candidate feature description, in a fixed order, using only artefacts we actually have — training logs, checkpoints saved at intervals, per-example loss attributions, ablation and clamping results, the data mix, the loss, and the fine-tuning schedule. Ground it in the teleosemantic, selected-effects tradition (Millikan, Papineau, Neander): the content of a representation is fixed by what it has the proper function of indicating, where that function is fixed by a history of selection rather than by the representation's current causal or dispositional profile. The whole difficulty is in the translation, so please keep what the theory actually says while making each step checkable by someone who has never heard of it, and say plainly where a step is a judgement call dressed up as a check.

Specific requirements:

- For each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as no answer at all. Where a step needs something the team cannot obtain (a counterfactual retrain, a lineage of checkpoints we did not save), say so and say what to do instead.
- Mark the points at which the procedure returns "no content ascription is licensed here" and stops, and the points at which it hands the remainder to a named person because the residual question is a decision about how fine-grained or how revision-tolerant our descriptions should be, not a fact about the model.
- Handle explicitly: (a) representations that were never causally recruited — bystanders that track what the network was rewarded for without ever driving it; (b) polysemantic directions, i.e. single directions carrying several apparently unrelated sensitivities; (c) features that first appear during a short fine-tuning stage, and features that survive fine-tuning unchanged; (d) inputs off the training distribution, including adversarial ones — that is, what a Millikan-style notion of Normal conditions should mean for an artefact whose environment is a data pipeline; (e) the standing worry that gradient descent on a next-token or contrastive objective is not selection in the relevant sense, so that nothing in the model has a proper function and every ascription fails — say what the procedure does with that worry rather than leaving it in a footnote; (f) the fact that the team does not really want a historical claim at all. What the governance lead needs is forward-looking: can we rely on this direction to mean X when we monitor it at deployment? Separate that reliability question from the content question in the procedure itself, and say what to do when they come apart.
- The procedure proper should fit on two printed pages; the applications below can be as long as they need to be.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A direction in the image encoder that fires reliably on photographs of dogs and on dog-like textures in rendered scenes, whose clamping degrades dog/non-dog classification and nothing else, and which the whole team wants to call a dog detector.
2. A direction that appears only after a short fine-tuning stage on a narrow task, which under probing still separates the pretraining property perfectly, but whose ablation now affects only the fine-tuning task.
3. A scalar feature in the language model's residual stream that tracks a property the team cares about a great deal, which predicts a downstream refusal decision almost perfectly, but which never contributed to the loss during training because it was a bystander to a feature that did.

I expect the procedure to answer (3) in a way I will not like; say what it says and say why I will not like it. Close with a short section on what the protocol cannot deliver — what a team reading its outputs should still not conclude.
