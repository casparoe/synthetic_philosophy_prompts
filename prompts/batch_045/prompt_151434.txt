someone in an AI-safety-ish reading group I lurk in keeps making this move. There's a thing in game theory called program equilibrium: instead of picking an action, each player submits a program, each program gets to read the other player's program, and then outputs an action. He says this basically dissolves the prisoner's dilemma, and he explains it like this: it's like two hostile countries signing a treaty they can each verify by satellite. Neither side would sign something the other can quietly cheat on; once the treaty text is public and checkable, cheating stops making sense, so the treaty enforces itself — no referee, no repeat rounds, no reputaion. His conclusion: once AI systems can inspect each other (weights, source, whatever the relevant internals turn out to be), cooperation between them is solved by transparency alone, so we shouldn't be spending research effort on commitment mechanisms or enforcement or multi-agent handshaking. Make everything auditable and conflict takes care of itself.

I write distributed systems for a living and have no philosophy background, and this smells like the analogy is doing more work than the formalism, but I can't point to where. So audit it for me. Sections please, in this order:

1. "The mapping" — ~150 words. A table of what plays what: the program vs. the nation, the source code vs. the treaty text or declared arsenal, the "cooperate iff the other cooperates" program vs. a conditional ratification clause, reading the other's program vs. satellite or inspector verification, the equilibrium vs. both sides having signed, the absence of pre-play communication vs. no back channel. Tell me if I've assigned the roles wrong.

2. "What the treaty case has that the game doesn't" — ~250 words. The features the analogy quietly imports: a referee or a hegemon, repeat interaction, graded rather than binary inspection, costs of being caught, a shared legal order, somebody who decides what counts as a violation. Which of those were actually load-bearing for the claim that the treaty enforces itself?

3. "Where it breaks" — ~300 words. This is the part I care about. My guesses: (a) mutual defection is also a program equilibrium — "always defect" against "always defect" is a fixed point too — so transparency doesn't select the good outcome, it just relocates the question to which fixed point we land on; (b) reading source isn't free and isn't always possible, since you can't in general decide what an arbitrary program does, and even when you can, a one-bit misread of the other program flips conditional cooperation straight into defection, whereas real inspection degrades gracefully; (c) source isn't behaviour when a program's output depends on inputs it hasn't seen yet and on its model of you; (d) publishing your internals is itself a move in a larger game, so transparency is a choice, not a background condition. Correct me where I'm wrong, and separate the ones that matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't.

4. "My patch" — ~200 words. The fix I keep coming back to: instead of full source exchange, each agent publishes a hash of a policy plus a stake, and a sandbox re-executes that policy against a replayed transcript of what actually happened; if the replay shows defection the stake is burned. Does that repair the analogy, or does it just smuggle the referee back in through the side door and give up the very thing that made the treaty picture attractive? Is there a version that works without any trusted third party?

5. "Verdict" — ~150 words. Does "transparency solves multi-agent cooperation" survive once the disanalogies are on the table?

6. "Better analogy?" — ~120 words. He also swaps in a second one when pressed: it's like playing against your identical twin, or a copy of your own decision function, so cooperating is really cooperating with yourself. Is that closer, or does it import something worse? And give me one better analogy if there is one — I keep reaching for something from distributed systems, like two nodes completing a handshake with no certificate authority, but tell me if that's the wrong shape.

For context on why I want this: it's not idle, we're deciding whether to spend a quarter making our agents' internals legible to other agents versus building actual monitoring and enforcement infrastructure.
