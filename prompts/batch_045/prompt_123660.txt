I write novels, and last year I let myself be talked onto the judging panel for a mid-sized literary prize. Five of us, one long weekend, a couple of hundred submissions whittled down to a shortlist, and then we sit in a room and argue until somebody caves. Twice that weekend the chair shut the discussion down with some version of "wisdom of crowds — five heads beat one, that's just maths": once to justify going with the majority instead of the one panellist who'd actually read the two translated titles in the original, and once to argue that we should have impanelled twelve jurists instead of five.

I'm sure that slogan is doing several different jobs at once and I want to take it apart properly. From what I've picked up secondhand, the thing people call the wisdom of crowds is really at least two different mechanisms: a voting one, where each person is separately more likely than not to get a yes/no question right and majority verdicts get more reliable as you add people — I think this is what the Condorcet jury theorem is about — and an averaging one, where people each guess a quantity, like the weight of an ox at a fair, and the errors cancel. So first: lay out, as a menu I can actually bring back to the panel, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, from the completely trivial to the strong and probably false, and tag each one as trivial, true-but-conditional, substantive-and-contested, or just false. For the true-but-conditional ones I want the conditions stated in plain terms: how much better than a coin flip does each of us have to be, and what exactly has to hold about how our mistakes are related?

Then two things specific to our setup that I want addressed rather than gestured at. First: in the voting version, "right" seems to presuppose there's a fact of the matter being tracked, and with novels there arguably isn't one — so can the theorem be reinterpreted for that case, and does it change anything if what we're really doing is predicting something (which of these will still be in print in thirty years, which one the other committees will go for, which will sell)? Second: does arguing it out for two hours and then voting help the maths, hurt it, or is that precisely the thing the assumptions are supposed to exclude? I have a suspicion the chair's slogan is self-undermining in our case — the more we talk, the less it applies — but I can't say why in a way that doesn't make me sound like a pedant, and I definately want to have the sentence ready before next year's panel.
