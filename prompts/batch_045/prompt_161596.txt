Here is an argument I have been turning over for a while. I think it must break somewhere, because I cannot accept where it ends up, but I cannot say which link is the broken one.

The setup. There is a set of states S, a set of acts A, a utility function u(a, s), and a probability p over S. An experiment is a variable σ with values in some finite set, jointly distributed with S. Good's theorem says that for an agent who updates by conditionalization and then performs an act maximizing expected utility,

Σ_σ p(σ) · max_a Σ_s p(s | σ) u(a, s)  ≥  max_a Σ_s p(s) u(a, s),

with equality exactly when some act that is optimal by the prior lights stays optimal conditional on every signal that has positive probability. The proof is a single line: let a\* maximize Σ_s p(s) u(a, s); then for each σ separately, max_a Σ_s p(s | σ) u(a, s) ≥ Σ_s p(s | σ) u(a\*, s); multiply by p(σ), sum over σ, and the right-hand side collapses by the law of total expectation to Σ_s p(s) u(a\*, s).

My argument:

(1) Look at what that proof actually uses. It compares, for each signal taken on its own, the act the agent would pick after seeing that signal against one single fixed act, namely the act that is best by its present lights. Nothing in the comparison turns on how σ came about. It does not require σ to be caused by the state, to be "about" the state in any robust sense, or to be the sort of thing that would have come out the same way no matter what the agent ended up doing. Any variable with a joint distribution with S will do.

(2) So the theorem is fully general: lab results, rumors, a stranger's unsupported assertion, the reading of a sensor that is broken in an unknown way, a perfect predictor's announcement of what you are about to do, an entry in your own diary from tomorrow.

(3) Hence information is never harmful ex ante. It is strictly helpful unless it is decision-irrelevant, and the amount you should be willing to pay for it is bounded below by zero and above by the gap on the left of the inequality. Call this the look-first principle: a rational agent never has reason to decline information that is free, and has reason to buy information that is cheap enough.

(4) Applied to ordinary practice, the look-first principle says that no arrangement of self-imposed ignorance can be rational as a way of improving your own subsequent choice. Blind review, sealed bids, a physician who declines a test, a researcher who refuses to look at the held-out labels, an official who arranges not to be told who the applicant is: if any of these is rational, its rationale must be entirely extrinsic — incentives, coordination, fairness to third parties — and never "I will choose better if I do not know."

(5) Applied to artificial agents, it says something sharper, and this is the part I care about. Suppose we deploy a system that is, near enough, an expected-utility maximizer with respect to our utility function. Then every restriction on what it is allowed to read is a pure expected loss by (3): withheld facts about the user, hidden evaluation criteria, filters that stop it from looking at its own deployment context, rules against reading the principal's private reasoning. So either we lift the restrictions, or we concede that the system is not in fact maximizing our utility function — that it is misaligned. There is no third option in which the system is aligned and yet better off not knowing.

(6) But here are four situations in which looking seems straightforwardly worse than not looking, and I do not think they are all the same situation.

(a) The paralysis case. A physician can run a test whose result would, if positive, make her unable to carry out the treatment that the test was supposed to guide. She knows this about herself in advance. Her later self will not want what her earlier self wants and will not be able to do what her earlier self would have chosen.

(b) The self-prediction case. A predictor with a perfect record announces, for free, which act the agent is about to perform. The agent knows the announcement is trustworthy. Yet once she conditions on it, the act she is told she will perform is no longer the act that looks best to her; her conditional beliefs are not stable under conditioning on her own action. The formula in the setup nonetheless returns "at least as good as not looking," and it does so because it treats p(σ) as a fixed quantity while σ is, in this case, a function of the very act whose value is being assessed.

(c) The feedback case. The signal is the output of a model that was fit on the choices of earlier agents who were themselves shown that model's output, and the current agent knows this. What the signal says therefore depends in part on what agents like her have done and will do, and her own act of looking feeds into the next agent's signal.

(d) The observed-search case. Others observe that she looked without observing what she saw, and they respond to the fact of the search: an insurer reprices, an adversary reallocates, a counterparty infers her private information from her willingness to look. Here the harm comes not from the content of the signal but from the acquisition itself.

(7) So the look-first principle is false in at least some of (a) through (d), yet it seems to fall straight out of a theorem whose proof is one line long and whose assumptions, as I read them in (1), are only conditionalization and expected-utility maximization.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?

Some constraints on the answer I want. First, I would like to know whether the failures in (a) through (d) have a common source or whether they are four different failures that happen to look alike; if they are different, tell me which one forces a revision of the theorem itself and which one is just a modeling error on my part. Second, I want to know whether the cases can be absorbed rather than accommodated: can each of them be re-described — utilities indexed to states, an enlarged state space that includes the agent's own later preferences, the acquisition of the signal modeled as an act with causal consequences, conditioning replaced by intervention — so that the theorem's assumptions are restored and its conclusion goes through untouched? If they can all be absorbed, I want to know what the theorem is then saying, whether it is saying anything beyond "do the best thing you believe you can do," and whether a result that no possible example could falsify is still doing work for someone who has to decide whether to run an experiment this afternoon. Third: if the right move is instead to restrict the theorem to signals that are probabilistically independent of the agent's own act, I want to know how to state that restriction precisely, what the restricted theorem still delivers, and in particular whether what remains is strong enough to support step (5).

If I have to choose, I would rather keep step (5) than step (4): I would rather give up the claim that self-imposed ignorance is never rational for a human than give up the claim that an aligned artificial agent is never better off being kept in the dark. But I would rather know what I am paying for either one than keep it by fiat.
