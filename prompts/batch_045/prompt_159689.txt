I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility, and next week's session is on Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. One of the stronger students posted the argument below to our seminar board late last night, and I'd like to have a real diagnosis ready rather than just pointing people at the reading. Here it is, lightly cleaned up but not changed in substance:

(1) In the standard Frankfurt case — an agent decides to do X for his own reasons, a counterfactual intervener stands ready to force the decision if the agent shows any sign of deciding otherwise, and the intervener never in fact does anything — the agent is morally responsible for X, and intuitively could not have done otherwise. So the principle of alternative possibilities is false: responsibility does not require that you could have done otherwise.
(2) Nothing that does not happen can make a moral difference. In that case the only difference between the agent's situation and that of an agent in a plainly deterministic world is the idle intervener, who never acts. So what refutes the principle of alternative possibilities is not the presence of the intervener at all; it is the fact that the actual causal sequence issuing in the action has the right features. Call this actualism.
(3) If actualism is right, then the absence of alternative possibilities can never be what defeats responsibility — not in Frankfurt cases, and not in the plain deterministic case either. Determinism is therefore no threat to responsibility, and the Frankfurt case adds nothing that an ordinary deterministic case would not already have given us.
(4) If only the intrinsic character of the actual sequence matters, then any two agents whose actual sequences are intrinsically alike are alike in responsibility: how the mechanism came to be in place is irrelevant, only how it operates in the moment of action.
(5) Now consider a team that, with no standby intervener and no sign-triggered device, reworks an adult's deliberative mechanism so that it produces, through an ordinary locally reasons-responsive process, the decision to do X. The sequence in this agent is intrinsically just like the sequence in a normal responsible agent.
(6) So the reworked agent is morally responsible for X.
(7) But agents whose mechanisms were produced by that kind of manipulation are not responsible; the manipulated agent is the paradigm case of someone we excuse. So actualism is false, and with it the claim that Frankfurt-style cases refute the principle of alternative possibilities. The argument proves too much: anything strong enough to dispose of alternative possibilities is strong enough to exonerate the manipulated, and we are not going to accept that.

What unsettles me is a wrinkle the argument does not consider. Suppose we bolt an idle intervener onto the manipulation case in (5), so that the manipulated agent decides as the team designed him to decide and a Black is standing by in case he had shown signs of wavering. If that restores responsibility, then something merely counterfactual is doing moral work and the actualism in (2) cannot be what carries the Frankfurt intuition; if it does not restore responsibility, then (1) and (7) look inconsistent with each other and I do not see how the compatibilist keeps both. One student keeps saying the exit is flickers of freedom, but I don't think the argument is that easy, and I'd rather not answer in those terms unless you think that is genuinely where the escape is. For what it's worth, I am most attached to (1) and (7), and I would hate to give up (3), since I have been arguing compatibilism all term.

Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding summary: first, which step fails and in one line why; second, why that step looked innocent enough to be smuggled in; third, what rejecting it commits me to — the cost, including anything I lose downstream in (3) or elsewhere; fourth, the next-cheapest rejection and what that one would cost me instead; and fifth, a statement of what, if anything, the Frankfurt case can still establish once the bad step is removed, together with how you would handle the bolted-on-intervener variant.
