Posting here because I keep getting out-argued by a proverb. I teach a seminar on trust and testimony, and at yesterday's department meeting we were discussing the university's new public commitment — in writing, named VP attached, specific enough that we could actually check in a year whether they kept it — that student data will never be sold to advertisers. A colleague said: "talk is cheap. The statement costs them nothing, so it gives us no reason whatsoever to expect them to keep it." Everyone sort of nodded. Then the same week in seminar a student used the same line for a different purpose: an apology isn't sincere unless it costs the apologizer something, so an apology with no reparations attached is worth nothing.

I think both uses are hiding something, but I cant get the hiding place to stay still. Can you lay out, as a labelled menu, the distinct precise claims "talk is cheap" could be making, roughly from trivial to radical? For each one: what exactly the claim is, whether it's a platitude, whether it's defensible, whether it's straightforwardly false, and whether it actually licenses the conclusion it was wheeled out for in my two cases.

Things I want kept seperate rather than blended:

- Cheap as costless-to-produce, vs. cheap as non-binding, vs. cheap as unverifiable, vs. cheap as available-to-anyone. Does the argument need all of these at once, or would one of them do the work?

- Is the operative quantity the size of the cost, or the difference in cost between the type who will keep the promise and the type who won't? I want a reading on which a signal is enormously expensive and still conveys nothing, and a reading on which a signal is free to send and still conveys something, and I want told which of those is nearer the truth.

- Counterfactual cost: does credibility require that the speaker actually burn something, or only that the signal be one the wrong type wouldn't have chosen to send? And what do we say about a statement that is free to issue but expensive to walk back — public, on the record, checkable later, attached to a named person's reputation? Is that "cheap"? This is the one I actually care about; I think it's where the department argument is won or lost.

- The technical term-of-art sense of "cheap talk" vs. the proverbial one. My vague memory is that in the theory the messages are costless, non-binding and unverifiable, and the interesting result isn't "no information gets across" but something about how much gets across depending on how far apart the sender's and receiver's preferences are. If that's right, the proverb and the theory aren't saying the same thing, and quoting one in support of the other is a bait and switch. Say whether that's fair, and say what the theory actually predicts for a sender who does have some stake in the receiver's being right.

- Whether there's a separate, non-evidential reading in circulation: not "the statement tells us nothing" but "issuing the statement is a substitute for doing something, and makes doing something less likely." That looks like a different claim with a different politics, and I don't want it smuggled in under the evidential one.

I'm bringing the menu to the next meeting, so be blunt about which readings are question-begging and which just restate the conclusion in signaling vocabulary. Finish with one line, nothing after it, in the form \answer{<labels of the readings that could carry my colleague's conclusion, or "none">; <survives|doesn't survive>}.
