I host a fortnightly interview series on formal epistemology and the foundations of rational-choice theory, and my next guest is an unnamed philosopher-economist who has just published a monograph — call it *One World, One Prior* — defending a distinctive reading of the common prior assumption (CPA). Her thesis, in outline: the CPA is not an empirical hypothesis about agents at all, but a completeness requirement on the modeler. Where agents appear to hold divergent priors, the analyst has simply failed to write some state variable into the description; once the state space is enlarged to include whatever underlies the divergence — the evidence, training, or theoretical commitments that led one agent to favor one hypothesis and the other another — a single prior over the enlarged space can be recovered and the apparent violation dissolves. She leans heavily on the equivalence between the existence of a common prior and the non-existence of a bet that both parties would willingly accept, and she draws a normative moral for her readers: when you disagree with someone you regard as an epistemic peer, treat the disagreement as a defect in your own model of the situation before treating it as a defect in theirs.

Draft ten to twelve questions for a ninety-minute conversation, together with the follow-up each one should have ready.

Requirements:

- No question should ask her to define the CPA, to explain common knowledge, or to walk through Aumann's agreement result or the classic no-trade result. Assume she and the audience know these, and use them as levers rather than as topics.
- For every question, add a short note on the reply she is most likely to give, given the position described above, and then a follow-up keyed to that particular reply, so that her most natural move has already been blocked.
- The conversation should cover, in some order, the following pressure points: (a) whether the closure reading has any content — if prior differences can always be modeled away by expanding the state space, what observation could count against the CPA, and is a constraint compatible with every possible observation a constraint on models or a tautology? (b) the regress worry: the prior over the enlarged space must itself be common, and the reasons for the reasons generate hierarchies of belief, so ask where she thinks this stops and whether the stopping point differs from the thing she set out to explain; (c) the empirical escape routes — since the no-trade result makes speculation under common knowledge look impossible while trade and persistent forecast gaps are everywhere, she will locate the failure in common knowledge rather than common priors, so press on exactly which notion of approximate common knowledge she is relying on and whether it is quantitatively roomy enough, given that the standard relaxations still force approximate agreement; (d) the no-agreeable-bet equivalence itself — have her state in the interview what she takes it to require (finiteness of the state space, full support, common knowledge of the bet's terms, expected-utility versus risk-neutral attitudes) and say whether, given that real agents rarely announce posteriors to one another, it can do the diagnostic work she wants from it; (e) observational equivalence — from the outside, how could one tell that two forecasters with access to the same data differ in priors rather than in private information, since the two diagnoses license the same behavior, and what does she say about the harder case where the unmodeled variable is not evidence at all but a difference in how each agent takes evidence to bear on the hypothesis, i.e. a difference in what the framework normally treats as the shared likelihood function? (f) the normative moral — if persistent peer disagreement is a reason for me to suspect my own model, symmetry appears to give my peer the same reason, so press on whether her view recommends conciliation, and on whether anything in the framework tells an agent which direction to move, given that the agreement result constrains what can be common knowledge but not who should defer to whom.
- Order the questions so that they build: begin with the worries about content and regress, move to the empirical escape routes, then to the diagnostic and normative payoffs.
- Close with a single question about what would make her give up the closure reading — phrased so that the usual retreats, such as saying that misspecification is always possible or that the model is only a model, are not available to her.
- Phrase each lead question so that a listener who knows only the rough outline of the debate can follow its thrust; the follow-ups may be technical. Write the questions as they would actually be spoken aloud, not as notes or bullet points.
