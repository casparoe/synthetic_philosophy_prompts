I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on philosophy of cognitive science, and I've found myself defending a line I'm no longer confident I can defend. The line is this: the free energy principle, read at the level of generality at which it is usually stated — roughly, that any system which persists while remaining confined to a small, characteristic set of states must look, to an idealised observer, as if it were minimising a variational bound on surprise — is very close to a mathematical truism given its own premises, and the familiar complaint that it is unfalsifiable is therefore a category mistake rather than a refutation. All the genuine empirical content, I want to say, sits one level down, in predictive processing as a process theory: that cortical hierarchies pass predictions downward and precision-weighted prediction errors upward, that attention is the ongoing optimisation of those precision weights, and that action is the fulfilment of proprioceptive predictions rather than the terminal output of a separate motor command stream. Taken that way, the framework makes real, risky claims about neural message passing, about which quantities the brain represents, and about the unity of perception, attention and action.

I'd like you to tell me what would count against that position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and to say for each candidate defeater whether a committed defender should concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as resting on a misreading. I'm less interested in a survey of the literature than in the shape of the exposure. Some things I want handled specifically. First, the 'dark room' worry and the move to expected free energy and prior preferences: is that a retreat that saves the theory by adding a further untested claim, or a legitimate development? Second, the suspicion I'd call precision gerrymandering — that because precision weights are, in practice, free parameters at every level of the hierarchy, any apparent predictive failure can be absorbed by retuning them, so the process theory is protected by a fudge factor rather than exposed to data. Third, a collapse worry: under linear-Gaussian assumptions, does minimising precision-weighted prediction error just re-derive Bayesian filtering or optimal feedback control, leaving as the only genuinely novel claim the assertion that cortex implements it? If so, what does the framework predict that a control-theoretic rival does not — about layer-specific asymmetries in cortical message passing, about responses to omitted rather than merely unexpected input, about the bistability and hysteresis one would expect when precision itself is being estimated? And where do already-live empirical disputes, such as whether repetition suppression is better explained by prediction-error reduction or by a rival sharpening story, fall on your ranking?

Let me add two cases I've been using in the seminar, because I want to know what work they can bear. The first I call the Laminar: an engineered system with a genuine, persisting Markov blanket, whose internal dynamics are a fixed, context-insensitive limit cycle, perturbed only by a trivial phase reset from incoming blanket states, and which nevertheless maintains its structural integrity indefinitely. It persists; it does not, on any natural description, model or infer anything. If the free energy principle applies to the Laminar, does it thereby become as trivial as the notorious stone objection alleges? If it does not apply, what extra condition excludes it — and is that condition itself empirical and testable, or is it another façon de parler? The second is what I'd call the Inverted Interlocutor: a system that gets all its distinctive predictions wrong about the sign of a certain error signal while a simpler, non-predictive controller predicts the behaviour exactly, with fewer free parameters. I'd personally find that second sort of case the most persuasive thing anyone could show me, because it would tell me the framework is not merely unexposed but worse than its rivals at the one job it was supposed to do. Finally, and this matters to me, I want your honest assessment of whether my 'it is a truism, so unfalsifiability is no objection' defence has insulated the position past the point of usefulness — and if it has, whether that is a vice in a framework of this ambition or simply the price of operating at that level of generality.
