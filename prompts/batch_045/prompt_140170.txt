I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind: 16 students, one 80-minute session, a room with movable chairs and tables. By the time we meet, they will have read a standard statement of epiphenomenalism — experiences are brought about by physical goings-on but themselves bring nothing about — and a standard statement of the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if the experience does no causal work, then my first-person present-tense report of, say, a red experience looks as though it is fully explained by non-phenomenal facts, and it becomes correspondingly obscure both how the report manages to be about the experience and how reporting it could count as a case of knowing it. On the page they nod at this and move on. They do not feel its force. I want them to feel it, and I want the force to arrive through what they do in the room rather than through anything I say: no framing lecture up front, and the technical vocabulary only introduced after they have already run into the problem.

The rough mechanism I have in mind, which I would like you either to design properly or to replace with something better, is a sealed-report protocol. Each student is assigned to a station holding an opaque box containing one item from a small tactile set — graded sandpapers, a square of felt, steel wool, a smooth stone, a piece of corrugated cardboard — so that nothing depends on sight or on verbal fluency. Beside the box is a sealed card bearing a printed first-person report naming one item from the set. Unknown to the student, the report was generated in advance from the station's code, not from anyone's experience. She reaches into the box, feels the item, and then has to decide whether to sign the printed card as her own report or to set it aside and write her own. Teams are scored, so that refusing to sign a card that turns out to have been correct carries a real cost to the group, while signing a card that turns out to have been wrong also carries a cost: the aim is to put an accuracy norm and an authorship norm in genuine tension, so that students have to feel the pull of signing something they did not produce because it says what they felt.

I want the session built out of roughly three moves. First, a round in which the printed reports match the contents, so that nearly everyone signs and reports a sense of ownership over a sentence she did not compose — the reveal being that the sentence existed before she touched anything. Second, a round in which the correspondence between report and experience is broken along a dimension the students cannot detect: some boxes are empty, or a blindfolded partner at the same station touches nothing at all, and still receives the same printed card. Third, a round in which the whole pool of signed reports is put in the middle of the room and the group is asked to sort them into those produced by someone who felt something and those produced by someone who did not. Predict what they will actually do at each stage and build the rules so that the interesting failure is the likely outcome rather than a lucky one.

Please give me: the roles (I imagine experiencers, blind reporters, at least one proctor, and a scorer, but settle it); the full materials list and setup, with nothing that costs anything or needs to be ordered; a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes including setup and reset; the scoring scheme with actual numbers; the exact sequence and wording of the reveals; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto particular claims in the argument. The mapping matters most to me: I want the debrief to separate a report's being reliably correlated with an experience, which an upstream common cause can secure perfectly well, from its being made because of the experience or answerable to it, which is what epiphenomenalism seems unable to secure — and I want that distinction to be something the students have already been navigating in practice when it is named.

Two further things. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: students treating it as a guessing game about hidden objects and missing the point entirely; someone reverse-engineering the station codes and defusing the reveal; the accuracy incentive swamping the authorship worry; the obvious student reply that of course she signed, because the sentence was true. And be explicit about which part of the paradox this kind of staging cannot reproduce. As I see it, the modal heart of the matter — that the report would have been exactly as it was even if the experience had been absent, or qualitatively different, with all the physical facts held fixed — cannot be built out of boxes and cards, and I would rather you say so plainly and tell me how to supply it in words at the right moment without letting the last twenty minutes collapse into a lecture.

Finally, commit. Pick one mechanism and one sequencing and defend them against the plausible alternatives you rejected. Close by stating what you take an activity of this design actually to establish about the paradox: does its pressure fall mainly on the claim that reports are caused by experiences, on the claim that they are about experiences, or on the claim that introspective reporting is a case of knowledge? Say which, and why. Then state the strongest reply still available to the epiphenomenalist at exactly that point, and say whether anything the students did in the room gives us reason to think that reply works.
