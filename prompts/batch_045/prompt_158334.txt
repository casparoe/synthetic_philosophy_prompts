Write an internal memorandum, roughly 800–900 words, in the voice of a municipal Office of Controlled Vulnerability in a modern city that has adopted Plato’s policy on sympotic drinking from the Laws. Format it as a memo with a subject line, bullet-pointed procedures, and at least one short scripted exchange between a facilitator and a participant. It should be addressed to ward supervisors and symposium facilitators, and written in the deadpan style of corporate risk-management or public-health guidance. The memo announces the rollout of a “Supervised Disinhibition Curriculum” and must remain ironically committed to the policy: the narrator must not step outside the framework to criticize Plato, and the reader should be able to tell what is being ironized only from the policy’s own increasingly absurd administrative consequences.

Build the satire on the following real features of the Platonic position, exaggerating each into modern bureaucratic instruments rather than inventing new doctrines. (1) Wine is treated as a morally risky instrument—at once a poison and a medicine—whose legitimacy depends entirely on dose, age, and supervision. (2) The symposium is not for recreation but for moral education and character assessment: because wine lowers shame and reputational self-presentation, it is a controlled way to see whether a citizen’s pleasures, pains, loves, and hatreds are correctly ordered, and thus to diagnose and shape character. (3) A sober president or facilitator must remain in control throughout; intoxication without rational oversight is precisely the danger the institution exists to avoid. (4) The policy is age-graded: the young must abstain entirely because their characters are still too unstable to survive the test; those in early adulthood may drink only in diluted, supervised moderation; and men over forty may drink more freely in Dionysian rites, because their souls are thought to have become hardened or crabbed with age and need to be softened back into plasticity. (5) The overarching aim is not pleasure but the renewal of emotional plasticity and correct conditioning, so that the city may administer what the Athenian Stranger calls “a medicine potent against the crabbedness of old age.”

Invent plausible administrative apparatus that follows from those premises: consent waivers, risk tiers, facilitator scripts, KPIs for emotional softening, incident reports, and liability disclaimers. The humor should arise from taking the Platonic logic seriously in modern administrative language—for example, a “Mandatory Vulnerability Audit” that treats temporary loss of self-command as a diagnostic good, an age-based exemption that frames elder intoxication as a wellness benefit for the morally stiff, or a debrief protocol distinguishing “therapeutically productive regret” from “non-therapeutic regret.” Do not let the memo’s voice disavow the policy; the irony must stay inside the position.

The result should be legible to a general reader while still being fair to the original: someone who knows the Laws should recognize that the target is the institutional use of supervised intoxication as a technology of moral education, character selection, and late-life psychological softening, including its original restriction to the male citizen-body. Write the memorandum in American English, and keep the total length to about 800–900 words.
