I work as a curatorial fellow at a mid-sized city museum and sit on the staff side of our Restitution Review Panel, which meets every eight weeks and has now built up a small track record that I am trying to make sense of. Its published terms of reference say only that it weighs "the circumstances of acquisition, the continuing significance of the object to a living community, and the identity of the claimant," without saying which of those has priority, so the pattern has to be read off the decisions themselves. Our precedents: (a) twelve cast plaques and one carved ancestral altar seized during an 1897 punitive expedition and claimed jointly by the national museum of the country of origin and by the descendant court — returned outright in 2019, no conditions; (b) four initiation masks bought at auction in 1974, later shown to have been removed from a shrine by an intermediary without the custodians' authority, claimed by the shrine's custodian — returned in 2021 under a renewable five-year loan-back for display; (c) the remains of roughly thirty ancestors taken without consent from a hospital mortuary in 1904, claimed by a tribal council — repatriated unconditionally in 2018, with no casts retained and no research retention, the panel saying that ancestral remains are not objects; (d) two ceremonial swords taken as war booty in 1886 in a war between two states, neither of which was colonized, claimed in 2020 by a foreign ministry rather than by any custodian or practicing community, with no current ritual use — the claim was declined, and a long-term loan plus joint research was offered instead; (e) a beaded royal stool presented to a colonial administrator in 1952, inherited by his widow and donated to us in 1981, claimed by the chiefly lineage on the ground that such stools cannot be given away even by a chief and may never leave the palace — returned outright in 2022; (f) about two hundred pieces of ordinary domestic material, pottery and tools and textiles, collected by a government surveyor in 1936 with the permission of village headmen and claimed in 2023 by a regional cultural council with no specific custodial relationship to them — return declined, but the panel funded digitization, replicas, and a shared-curatorship agreement with rotating loans. My two colleagues read this record in opposite ways and I need to give the director a prediction before the next meeting.

The new claim concerns six carved wood "dance faces," commissioned in 1926 by a district officer named Hallam from a carver named N'sofwa in the Kala hills, and bequeathed to us with Hallam's papers in 1929. There is a receipt for four pounds; there is no evidence of any threat, and N'sofwa was paid well above the local rate for commissioned work. But the masks depict beings whose names are spoken only inside initiation, the shrine association fined N'sofwa for carving them for an outsider, and they went from his workshop straight into Hallam's hands and have never been danced, never entered a shrine, and never been displayed with the community's knowledge. The claim comes from the Kala Descendants Association, about nine hundred members living in our own city, some of them initiated, which wants the masks for a community house and annual observances; the shrine association in the home country has written a careful letter saying it does not oppose return but does not claim the masks, on the ground that "they left before they were finished and they were never ours"; N'sofwa's grandson, who runs a carving school, asks in the alternative for a season with them if they return. Two further complications: our conservator says one of the six cannot safely travel and that the Association's premises are a converted storefront, and the Association has also asked that Hallam's field notes and our photographic record of the masks be transferred to them rather than retained, which is a question about documentation we have never ruled on.

What I want from you is the following. First: predict how the panel decides, with the reasons, and state as precisely as you can the rule it is implicitly running. Second: set out the alternative rules that fit all six precedents equally well but come apart on this case — I suspect there is one keyed to the circumstances of acquisition and one keyed to a living claimant's continuing relationship to the object, but I want the strongest version of each, and any others you can find — and say what each predicts here. Third: tell me which precedent is the closest analog and why, and if our record genuinely underdetermines the decision, recommend what we should decide for consistency's sake and be explicit about which principle we would be adopting with each option, including the middle options such as a loan, a partnership, or a conditional return. Fourth: describe one further case, in enough detail to be genuinely decidable, whose arrival would tell us which of your candidate rules we have really been applying; I would like to be able to recognise which rule we are on when the next claim lands. Finally: say whether conservation capacity and the demand about our archives should count at all, given that neither has figured in a decision so far, and what it would mean for the panel's own self-understanding if they did.
