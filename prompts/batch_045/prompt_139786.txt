Write a dialogue between two members of a city Urban Forestry Commission, hashing it out before a vote on one permit application. The ordinance says any tree "of significant size" on a development site has to be preserved or replaced at a 3:1 ratio, there's no other guidance, and the city charter bars the commision from "amending" the ordinance — only applying it. The tree in question is a 19-inch silver maple, and their last three published rulings went 24 inches, 22 inches, and 20 inches.

One of them is an epistemicist about vagueness and the other is a supervaluationist, and neither gets to be a strawman: the epistemicist has to answer where a sharp cutoff could possibly come from if nothing in the commission's practice pins it down, and the supervaluationist has to say what they actually do with the vote, given that "indeterminate" isn't one of the options on the ballot.

Two wrinkles I want them to chew on. First: their rulings go out in a digest that surveyors and developers rely on, so each ruling shifts the very usage that, on both their views, partly fixes the extension of "significant size" — is a borderline ruling a finding or a lawmaking act, and does that make it at least partly self-certifying? Second: whatever they do here, they also have to vote on whether to publish a bright-line number in next year's digest.

They should respond to each other's actual points rather than trading set speeches, and they should end up having located one concrete difference in what the two views tell them to do with this particular vote, plus one thing each of them concedes. Don't let them settle it by concluding the dispute is merely verbal.
