Adopt the role of a formal epistemologist and decision theorist who has spent years teaching agreement results to audiences with no philosophical training, and who has thought hard about why intelligent listeners walk away from this particular theorem with the wrong lesson. Think the task through carefully before you commit to anything: open with a short free-form brainstorm of at most five paragraphs in which you work out, for your own benefit, what the two or three genuine obstacles are to teaching Aumann's agreement theorem to quantitatively trained non-philosophers, and what this audience's professional habits will do to their reading of it. Then give me the plan.

I have been asked to run a forty-minute lunchtime session on Aumann's agreement theorem for the quantitative research team at the asset manager where I work. About fifteen people: every one of them has a master's-level training in maths, physics or engineering, all are fluent in conditional probability and Bayes's theorem, none has any formal epistemology, and all of them spend their working day sharing numbers with colleagues and arguing about why the numbers differ. Two attitudes are already in the room, and I can name them precisely: one colleague told me in the corridor that any result which assumes a common prior can be ignored, and another is convinced the theorem is simply an argument for deferring to whoever has the better model. I have a whiteboard, no slides, no handout, and no time for a proof.

Do not explain the theorem to me. I know it. Plan how I should explain it, and give me the following.

First, the dependency structure. Which components must be in place before which others, and why the order matters: the partition model of information, what it is for a posterior rather than a signal to be common knowledge, the common prior, and the conclusion. Say whether the partitions or the prior should go on the board first and what breaks in the alternative order. Also tell me at what point, if at all, to let in the popular slogan about not being able to agree to disagree, and how to phrase it so that it does not itself generate a misconception.

Second, the one example that has to carry the whole session, together with the constraints it must satisfy and the features it must avoid. My own requirements are that it have a state space small enough to write out, private signals whose partitions cross-cut so that neither analyst's information refines the other's, opening posteriors that differ visibly and that both move after the first exchange, a later round in which the only thing transmitted is the fact that the other analyst's number did not move, and a final common posterior that is not between the two opening figures. The last of these matters most: if the agreed number looks like a compromise, the session collapses into a story about meeting in the middle and I will have wasted forty minutes. It must also be about something this audience actually argues about, and it must not be an urn-and-ball exercise that they will read as statistics rather than as a model of disagreement. Say what each feature is for and what goes wrong if it is missing.

Third, the misconception map: where in the sequence each should be headed off, in the order the audience will form them, and whether any of my diagnoses below is wrong or one is missing. I expect at least these: the theorem read as a prediction that disagreement cannot persist and then dismissed because it obviously does; the compromise reading just mentioned; the thought that the analysts have pooled their evidence, when in fact neither ever learns the other's evidence, only a number; the common prior treated either as a technicality or as the entire content of the result, so that the result is waved away as empty; the slide from agreement to accuracy, including the subtler slide from more information being better in expectation to more information being better at the actual state; and the thought that uncertainty about what the other person knows is fatal to the result.

Fourth, two variants I have been asked to cover, and I want them placed deliberately rather than bolted on. The first is a direction-only protocol: in each round each analyst may announce only whether their estimate rose, fell or stayed the same, never the number. I want to know what this isolates, where in the session it belongs, whether agreement is still forced under it, and what I should honestly say if it is not. The second is an observer who has been forwarded the entire transcript and who also holds a private signal of her own: why she need not land on the same number as the two who agreed, what that shows about agreement being relative to a pool of information rather than to the truth, and where to put it so that it deepens the result instead of undermining it.

Fifth, the check for understanding: one question, or a pair of variant cases, that I can put to them in the last three minutes and whose answers separate someone who has the structure from someone who has only retained the conclusion. It should be possible to state the theorem correctly and still fail this question.

Sixth, the things I should leave out and say out loud that I am leaving out: the proof, the general state-space machinery, the no-trade corollary, anything to do with markets or herding, and the dispute about where common priors come from. Give me the sentence I should use for each refusal, phrased so that the result does not look defensive. Finally, give me a minute-by-minute skeleton for the forty minutes and the three or four panels of whiteboard, in order.

I do not want a reading list, references or a single named paper. Keep the whole thing under about twelve hundred words, in headed sections, in British English.
