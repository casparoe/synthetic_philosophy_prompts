ok so I'm in AP Euro and I wrote a paper on why the July Crisis turned into a general european war in 1914, and my teacher wrote in the margin you're writing as if it had to happen. I get the point, and I've been reading about hindsight bias and about how we turn a messy sequence into a story with an ending, but when I tried to turn that critique into an actual argument it started proving way more than I wanted and I can't find the place to stop it.

Here's the version I ended up with, from me and my friend arguing after school:

(1) Explaining why X happened means listing the things before it that raised the chance of X.
(2) The only way to tell which things raised the chance is to look back from X — you can't do it from the inside, before you know how it came out.
(3) So any causal story about a particular event is told from a standpoint that already knows the ending.
(4) Knowing the ending is exactly what makes the ending feel inevitable: the steps look tighter than they were, and the options people were actually weighing at the time drop out.
(5) If a way of working reliably bends its own results in one direction, you either fix it or you discount everything it gives you.
(6) So narrative history is systematically bent toward it had to happen, and the roads not taken are invisible in it.
(7) But none of this is special to history. The account of why a plane crashed, a doctor's writeup of why a patient died, a judge's finding about who caused the fire, my own explanation of why I bombed the chem test — those also pick causes by looking backward from an outcome.
(8) So either every account of any particular event is bent in the same way, which nobody believes, or there's a hole somewhere in (1) through (6).

Then my teacher offered a fix, and the fix made it worse, which is why I think I've built something wrong. Her rule was: you may only call something a cause if a well-informed person at the time could have listed it in advance, for the reason they'd have given then. So:

(9) The things everybody saw coming can't be what explains why it happened that week rather than some other week.
(10) So the causes doing the real explanatory work are the ones nobody anticipated — the surprises, the accidents, the stuff that only looks like a cause once you know what followed.
(11) But by her rule precisely those don't count as causes.
(12) So the more surprising an event is, the fewer causes it can have, and a total surprise has none at all — which is insane, since surprises obviously have causes and they're the things we most want explained.

If I have to pick, I'd rather keep the original worry, that histories make whatever happened look like it was always going to happen, than keep my teacher's in-advance rule. But apparently I can't have both, and I'd rather understand the trade than just pick the one that sounds better. So: which step do you actually reject, and why did it look fine when I was writing it down? What does rejecting it cost me — do I lose the hindsight point entirely, or can I keep a smaller version of it? My teacher also said something once about most causes being an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition, which I copied down and do not understand, so if that's the way out I'd like it explained in normal words.

One more thing: please be clear about what's actually settled here versus what people who study this for real still argue about — I want to know if the hindsight stuff is a real replicated finding or just a thing pop psych books say, and whether the rest (what causation even is, whether a cause has to raise a chance, whether histories are doomed to be distorted) is genuinely up for debate. Don't smooth a contested view into the consensus one, and flag which parts are your own reasoning rather than something the field has actually worked out.
