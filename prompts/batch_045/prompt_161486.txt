You are both a professional philosopher with a specialist's command of late twentieth-century moral philosophy — in particular Bernard Williams's critique of consequentialism — and a practised poet in formal English verse. Please think carefully before you write, and show some of your working: open with four or five lines of notes in which you decide which formal feature of the sonnet will carry which feature of the objection, then write the poem, then spend two or three sentences checking the draft against the constraints below and revise where it fails one.

The situation: last night I had a long argument with a friend — he works in global health and is a convinced consequentialist — about Williams's integrity objection and the remark about the man who has one thought too many. I do not want to send him a fifth paragraph. I want to send him a poem. In conversation he made three moves I could not answer, and I would like the poem to do the work my prose did not — not by asserting a conclusion but by making a certain structure of deliberation visible. His moves were: first, that integrity is only a taste for one's own projects, so the objection is partiality or egoism wearing a grander name; second, that if what bothers me is that consequentialism asks me to give up a great deal, that is merely an objection to how demanding the theory is, and he is perfectly willing to grant that a correct theory can be extremely demanding; third, that the point about the extra thought is only about overt reasoning, or about what it is indecorous to say aloud, since a well-trained agent can have the licensing thought silently, below the level of speech, and act on exactly the reasons anyone else would act on.

What I want: one Shakespearean sonnet — fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, rhyming abab cdcd efef gg, the turn at line 9. The poem should dramatise the case Williams uses: a man in the water who can reach one of two drowning people, one of whom is his wife. The constraints:

1. Octave, lines 1–8: present tense, first person, inside the man's own perception — hands, cold, weight, the particular body he is reaching for. No normative or theorist's vocabulary anywhere in these lines: no ought, should, right, wrong, permitted, duty, utility, maximising, impartial, greater good, no numbers, no comparisons. Write the octave so that a reader cannot tell, until the sestet, that anyone else was in the water at all. He is not weighing anyone against anyone; the absence of comparison is the point.

2. Volta, line 9: the arrival of the extra thought — the moment he steps outside his own reach and redescribes what his hands are already doing as something licensed, an instance of a rule he may or must follow. The turn should land as a loss of footing rather than as a discovery, and the theorist's vocabulary should enter here and nowhere earlier.

3. Sestet, lines 10–14: what that thought contains and what it costs. Somewhere in these lines the doctrine of negative responsibility must be audible — that what he does not do is laid at his door just as much as what he does, because someone else would have done it, so that his own hands become a channel through which the total is served. But the loss the poem locates must not be that he acted wrongly, nor that the summing view gives the wrong answer in this case, nor that the theory asks too much of him. It must be that the standpoint from which the question is put cannot see what his hands were doing, and that he cannot occupy that standpoint and still be the person who reached: the commitments that make him the person he is become one contribution among others, which he is asked to total up from nowhere.

4. Also, do not stage the poem as a conflict between two values in which one overrides the other. The trouble is that the extra thought is required of him at all — that the view insists he should be able to see his own action under that description — not that morality wins or loses against integrity.

5. Couplet: say exactly what the extra thought destroys. It must not claim that consequentialism is false or that he did wrong; it should leave the argument where it is while making my reading of it unmistakable.

6. Accuracy: someone who knows these essays should be able to point to the lines carrying the alienation point, the negative-responsibility doctrine, and the indecency, and should not find the poem collapsing the objection into a complaint about demandingness, into a defence of partiality as such, or into the thought that sometimes one simply shouldn't maximise. At the same time, someone who has never heard of any of this should find a good poem.

Craft: contemporary English, formal rhythm, no archaic pronouns, no inversions forced by rhyme, no exclamation marks, no naming of any philosopher, theory or book, no epigraph or footnotes. It should read as one friend sending something to another at the end of an argument — not pleading, not scoring points, not sneering at his view; any wit should be in the structure rather than in jabs.

After the poem, give me at most four short lines noting which line carries what and which of my friend's three replies each structural choice is meant to defuse.
