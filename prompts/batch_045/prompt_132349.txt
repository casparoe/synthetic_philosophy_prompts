I'm a backend engineer, and most of my professional life is spent writing and reviewing blameless postmortems, so I've absorbed a fairly rigid vocabulary for talking about why things break: root cause, trigger, contributing factor, latent condition, the "five whys," the Swiss-cheese picture of stacked defenses. A historian friend of mine got into an argument with me after I claimed that her field's problem with "overdetermination" — the worry that if you remove any one of the half-dozen things that led to some event, the event still happens, so no single thing can be called *the* cause — is just the same problem incident review has, wearing a nicer hat. She said the disanalogy is that we engineers get to pick a cause by asking what we'd fix, whereas historians are supposed to be describing something that was already there before anybody decided to repair it. **"The spare fuse doesn't matter: overdetermination, repair cost, and what historians are doing when they pick a cause"** (notes toward an essay)

*(1) The setup.* In a postmortem nobody loses sleep over the fact that the database would have survived the bad deploy if the replica had been healthy, or survived the unhealthy replica if the deploy had been clean. Both were sufficient, given the rest, for the outage. We still write down one of them as the cause. In history the same shape shows up everywhere: remove the assassination, remove the alliance system, remove the mobilization timetables, remove the domestic pressures on each cabinet — and on most tellings the war still comes. Philosophers have a name for a condition that is insufficient on its own but non-redundant within a larger sufficient package (someone told me it's an "INUS condition," though I may be garbling it), but that machinery tells me how to *describe* the mess, not how to *select* from it.

*(2) The repair test, borrowed from engineering.* Among all the sets of conditions that would, holding the rest fixed, have prevented the outcome, we name the one it would have been cheapest for the relevant agents to change. On this view causal selection tracks intervention cost, not metaphysical difference-making. My first claim is that historians are already doing this, usually without saying so: the causal weight they assign to a decision is tied to how much room the decision-maker had to do otherwise.

*(3) Why that can't be the whole story, and the fix I want to propose.* Taken literally, the repair test makes the cause of a fourteenth-century plague depend on twenty-first-century medicine, which is absurd: what we can afford to fix now has nothing to do with what produced the event then. So I want to detach the idea from *our* capacities and attach it to the structure of the situation instead. Call it **modal depth**: to ask whether C caused E is to ask how much of the rest of the world you would have to rewrite in order to delete C and still get E. A backup fuse that would itself have blown under the same surge has almost no depth — delete it and nothing else needs to change, so it was never load-bearing. A railway timetable that forces mobilization to cascade has enormous depth: delete it and you have to rewrite the whole apparatus that produced it. Explanatory weight, on this account, is the *collateral edit distance* of removing a factor, and overdetermination turns out to be a case where that distance is near zero for one of the apparently sufficient causes.

*(4) The contrastive move.* Where the symmetry is real — two shooters, one bullet each, victim dies anyway — I claim there simply is no fact of the matter about which one to cite, and that this is fine, because nobody asked a question that required singling one out. The interesting historical disputes are the ones where the symmetry is only apparent: either the backup cause is *fragile* (it depends on the very condition under discussion, so it would have gone too) or it is *shallow* in the sense of (3). So: apparent overdetermination in history is not a threat to causal explanation, it's a symptom that the question was asked non-contrastively, i.e., "why E?" rather than "why E rather than the alternative that was actually on the table?"

*(5) The case I most want comments on.* Take the night the Berlin Wall came down. As I understand the outline — and I don't need the archival details checked, assume the sketch is right and argue from it — a party official in East Berlin read out a freshly drafted set of travel rules at a televised press conference and, asked when they took effect, said something to the effect of "immediately," which wasn't what the draft said and hadn't been cleared; crowds gathered at the crossings, the guards had no orders that covered the situation, and the barrier was opened. Now run that against the standard "deep" causes everyone cites: long-run economic stagnation, the emigration route through Hungary, the Monday demonstrations, the security services' loss of nerve, the fact that no one in Moscow was going to send tanks. My claim is that the press-conference fumble gets ranked too low by historians who are impressed by the deep causes and treat the fumble as a mere trigger. On my measure it is *deep*: there is no nearby way the world goes in which a nervous official reading from a sheet he hasn't fully absorbed doesn't say something like that, because a system that could only relax by improvising in public had no redundancy at that point. Whereas the demonstrations, on this measure, are *shallow*: delete the Leipzig marches and you have to invent some other outlet for the same accumulated pressure, which is a small edit. So the reversal: the thing that looks like a trivial trigger is load-bearing, and the thing that looks like the deep cause is replaceable.
