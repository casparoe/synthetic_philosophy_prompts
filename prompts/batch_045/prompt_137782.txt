I'm a high school senior writing an independent project on whether legal doctrines quietly presuppose a theory of time, and I'd like your comments on the section where I think I'm stuck.

In medical malpractice, courts in different jurisdictions split over this kind of case. A patient arrives with roughly a 40 percent chance of surviving; a negligent delay in diagnosis cuts it to roughly 10 percent; she dies, and it can never be established whether she was in the group that would have died anyway. Some courts allow a loss-of-chance claim and award a fraction of a full wrongful-death amount, scaled to the percentage points of chance destroyed. Others insist that causation be proved on the balance of probabilities, so that if the negligence probably did not cause the death, recovery is zero, even though the negligence plainly made her worse off in expectation.

I want to know what each rule presupposes. My draft claims that the proportional rule treats a chance as something a person presently possesses and can be deprived of, while the all-or-nothing rule treats what happened as settled and treats our uncertainty as merely ours. Is that the right contrast? Can either rule be defended without any metaphysics of chance at all, purely on deterrence or on fairness across many similar cases, in which case the doctrine is underdetermined by the metaphysics? And does anything follow from the fact that courts generally prefer the actual outcome when they can discover it, falling back on chances only when they cannot?

Then the part that confuses me most. Suppose the argument from relativity of simultaneity, the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, is correct, so that every event, including this patient's death or survival, is equally real and the future is already there in the block. Does that undermine the idea that anyone lost a chance, or does it leave the doctrine untouched, on the grounds that the negligence, the reduced chance, and the death are all equally real parts of one block and the proportional award is only a response to our ignorance? If the block picture were accepted, should any doctrine here change? And if nothing should change, does that show the argument has no practical upshot, or only that the law never required an objectively privileged present in the first place?
