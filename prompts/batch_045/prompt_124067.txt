I'm a high school student writing a paper for an ethics seminar, and I'd like a proper essay rather than a list of considerations on each side.

In class we discussed the claim that an action has moral worth only when the agent does it because it is the right thing to do — from duty, we put it, rather than from sympathy, habit, guilt, or self-interest — even when the action itself is exactly what morality demands. I want to test that claim against a case I made up and haven't been able to find discussed anywhere.

Amara finds a wallet full of cash in an empty corridor. No one is watching and she could keep it with no real risk of being found out, and she feels no sympathy for the stranger who lost it. What moves her is this: she has complete trust in her older brother's judgment about what matters, because he has been right about such things again and again, and he has told her that the decisive consideration here is that the money belongs to someone else, so returning it respects that person as its owner. Amara finds that consideration thin and doesn't really see why it should move her. She is not acting to please her brother, or to be the kind of person he admires, or from a rule she has absorbed from him; she simply takes it, on his authority, that this is the reason that settles the matter, and she returns the wallet for that reason. Her brother is right that it is the right-making reason.

Write an essay on whether Amara's action has full moral worth, diminished moral worth, or none at all. Set out the strongest case on each side and then defend a verdict — please don't conclude merely that the answer depends on how we define our terms. Then briefly spell out what your verdict implies about the duty requirement: if deference of Amara's kind can be enough, is a child who reliably does the right thing on good authority, without yet grasping why, a paradigm case of moral worth? And if it isn't enough, what exactly is missing in her case that is present when someone acts on a reason they grasp for themselves?
