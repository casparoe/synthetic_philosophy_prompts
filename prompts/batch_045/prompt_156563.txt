My dad retired in the spring and I've somehow ended up running the 40-person shop he built, and the place is full of rules nobody can explain — no purchase order over $300 without a second siganture, the Friday afternoon inventory count, a customer we still ship to at a loss and always have. Everyone keeps telling me Chesterton's fence: don't tear down a fence till you know why it was put up. But taken straight that seems to freeze everything forever, because half the time the only way to learn what a practice was for is to remove it and watch what breaks, and I can't run that experiment on a business that has to make payroll.

So skip the Burke-versus-everyone-else scorecard, give me the spec instead: what would a usable rule for deciding when to keep and when to change an inherited practice actually have to deliver? The cases it has to get right (the rationale that died decades ago, the practice that only ever served the guy who wrote it, the one whose point you can only discover by violating it), the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (nobody can explain it vs. nobody benefits; reforming a practice vs. repealing it; caution about *this* arrangement vs. a general suspicion of change), and what it owes a person who has about a month, maybe thirty hours of digging, and no way to A/B test abolition. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste, and flag any two you suspect can't be satisfied at once — that's the part I most want to see. One short paragraph at the end on whether your list lands nearer Burke or nearer a presumption in favor of changing things is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria.
