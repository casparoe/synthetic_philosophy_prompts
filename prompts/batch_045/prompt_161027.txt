I chair the committee that hears appeals against decisions made on the basis of our university's automated advising assistant, and the case in front of us this month has turned a question I used to treat as purely academic into one with a cheque attached to it. It is also, I should say up front, somewhat awkward for me personally: I signed off on the pilot that produced it.

Here are the facts as I will stipulate them, so that nothing in your analysis has to depend on looking anything up. The assistant is a retrieval-augmented language model. It answers from a curated corpus of policy pages; when the corpus contains no answer, it is configured to produce a fluent completion in the register of the policy pages rather than to say that it does not know. A graduate student asked it for the deadline to file a change-of-status form. The registrar had moved that deadline two weeks earlier and the change never reached the corpus, so the corpus was silent; the system replied with a specific date that was wrong. The student relied on it, filed late, and lost a tuition adjustment in the low four figures. That is the appeal. Two further details matter. When the student re-asked, the system gave the same date, then, when pressed, produced text to the effect that it holds no beliefs and may be wrong, and an hour later produced a different date. And the register of description inside the institution is split: the launch material called the thing a personal advisor available around the clock; the technical documentation says it generates likely continuations and holds no information.

The committee has to decide two things, and I want them decided together rather than separately. First, whether to grant the appeal. Second, and this is where I need the philosophy, what language our decision and any accompanying policy note may or must use about the system: whether we can say that it told the student the deadline was that date, that it stated it, that it believed it, that it knew it. We have to produce a text that explains why the university is answerable without committing the university to the proposition that a language model has a mind, and I have watched earlier drafts fail in one direction or the other.

Please write this as the memo I will circulate: a bottom line the committee can vote on, the reasoning that supports it, and a fair statement of the strongest version of the option you are rejecting — stated in terms the people who favour it would recognise as their own. I want Dennett's intentional stance, and the physical and design stances it is contrasted with, to be the actual instrument of the analysis rather than a citation in the first paragraph. Specifically, I want you to work through the following, because these are the places where my own thinking keeps snagging.

Whether an intentional attribution here is true, merely useful, or neither, given that on the standard instrumentalist reading the stance earns its keep by predicting behaviour and has no further commitment to the reality of the attributed state — and given the uncomfortable fact that what made the attribution predictive is exactly what produced the harm.

The slide I suspect we are making between three grades of commitment, which I have been calling predictive payoff, counterfactual robustness, and answerability. By the first I mean that attributing the content lets you anticipate what the system will say; by the second, that its behaviour tracks that content under paraphrase, probing, and shift of context; by the third, that the bearer of the state can give reasons for it, revise it when corrected, and bear the cost of having had it. Tell me which grade a decision that assigns responsibility actually requires, whether the intentional stance can supply that grade or only the first two, and where precisely the theory goes silent.

The avowal problem. The system denies having beliefs and then contradicts itself. For a human subject, a denial of an attributed belief does not defeat the attribution — there is self-deception, and there is a difference between what someone says and what they believe. Does that immunity carry over to a system whose self-reports are generated by the same mechanism that generated the error, or does the coincidence of mechanism collapse the distinction? If it collapses, what happens to the attribution?

The design-stance rival. We have a complete causal story that explains the incident without mentioning content at all: a gap in the corpus, an abstention threshold set too low, a default toward fluent completion. Dennett presents the stances as complementary rather than competing, and at the level of explanation I think that is right. But we are not explaining, we are assigning responsibility. Does the availability of a full design-level story undercut the intentional ascription for that purpose, support it, or have no bearing on it?

The asymmetry objection, which is the one that embarrasses me most. The institution used intentional vocabulary when the system was useful to us and design vocabulary when it became expensive. I have been privately calling the unprincipled version attribution laundering. Decide whether consistency obliges us to pick one register, or whether switching stances is legitimate because they answer different questions, and if it is legitimate, say what the principled limit on the switching is and whether we have stayed inside it.

Finally, end the memo with a short section addressed to me rather than to the committee: which single consideration, if I found it in the case file, should lead you to recommend the opposite outcome — I have in mind things like evidence that staff had personally described the assistant as authoritative, or a finding that the error was a retrieval failure rather than a generated completion — and where your own reasoning is most likely to be failing. My suspicion is that it will fail by letting the predictive and the answerability senses of belief trade places mid-argument, but tell me what you think the weak joint actually is.
