After forty years in experimental astrophysics I have taken recently to sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at my old college, and a former colleague who convenes the final-year option in Philosophy of Cosmology has asked me to set one essay question for this year's examination paper. I have agreed, rather rashly, and also to mark it — which is why I need explicit criteria, since I am not a philosopher and will not recognise a good answer by eye.

The topic I want is the Boltzmann brain problem. The trouble is that everything I have read on it repeats two slogans: that an asymptotically de Sitter cosmology predicts we are almost certainly fluctuation observers, and that this therefore refutes such cosmologies. I have come to think that neither slogan survives being looked at closely, and I would like a question that separates the students who can say why from those who have merely memorised the labels.

Please act as a professional philosopher who specialises in philosophy of physics, cosmology and anthropic reasoning, and who has considerable experience setting and marking final-year undergraduate examinations. Think the matter through carefully before you write anything final: begin with a short planning note of two or three hundred words in which you identify the crux you intend the question to turn on, the two or three candidate principles whose conflict makes it hard, and the move a merely competent candidate will make instead. Check my arithmetic when you get to it, and say plainly in that note if I have got a number wrong.

I should be clear about what I do not want. No references, citations, bibliographies or reading lists of any kind: I can find those myself, and nothing in the question, the model answer or the markscheme should depend on knowing who argued what, or on any figure beyond the ones I give below. Assume you have no access to the literature and say nothing that requires it.

The materials I need, in this order:

1. The question itself, as it would appear on the paper: a ninety-minute essay, capped at 1,500 words, worth 25 marks, including whatever stub of stipulation the candidate will need. It must be unanswerable by reciting either slogan, and it must not be answerable by a candidate who has simply read that fluctuation observers outnumber ordinary ones.

2. A model answer of roughly 1,200 words, written as a strong but not superhuman candidate would write it.

3. A markscheme built around five must-hit moves worth five marks each, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong, and a short statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer — I want the distinction to rest on specific moves the candidate either does or does not make, not on a general impression of polish.

4. A brief note, no more than 150 words, on where a competent philosopher could reasonably disagree with your own model answer, so that I can mark charitably.

Build the question around the following toy model, which I have made up precisely so that the slogans fail and the counting has to be done honestly. Take a cosmology whose late phase is asymptotically de Sitter, so that thermal fluctuations occur at the Gibbons–Hawking temperature, with the probability of a given fluctuation set by the entropy deficit it represents. Stipulate that over the whole history of the model there are 10^30 observers produced by ordinary biological evolution, each enjoying something of the order of 10^9 seconds of conscious experience, and 10^65 observers produced by fluctuation, each of which exists for a single moment of experience and then dissipates. Stipulate further that a fraction 10^-30 of the fluctuation observers have experiential content indistinguishable from that of an evolved human being, apparent memories of a long life included.

The arithmetic I think follows is this. Conditional on our evidence, observers with our evidence divide 10^35 fluctuation to 10^30 evolved, so that on a count of observers we should take ourselves to be fluctuation observers by a factor of a hundred thousand; but observer-moments with our evidence divide 10^35 to 10^39 the other way, so that on a count of moments we should take ourselves to be evolved by a factor of ten thousand. The question I am driving at is what follows from this: whether the cosmology is refuted, or merely makes us atypical, or is cognitively unstable in the sense that it undermines the reliability of the reasoning by which it was arrived at; whether the answer flips with the choice between counting observers and counting moments; and which premise is really bearing the weight — a principle of typicality or self-sampling, or a choice of measure and reference class — and what it costs us either to accept or to reject that premise.

Two wrinkles I would like the question to force candidates to confront rather than step around. First, that a fluctuation observer lasting a single moment yet encoding an apparent lifetime is a genuinely awkward candidate for the reference class, so that the candidate has to say whether matching experiential content is enough for our evidence to be conditioned on at all. Second, that even if the count of observer-moments is dominated overall by fluctuation moments, the moments with our evidence may not be, so that unconditional typicality and conditional credence come apart, and a candidate who conflates them has missed the point of the exercise.
