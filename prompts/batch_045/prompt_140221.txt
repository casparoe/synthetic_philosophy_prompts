I did exposure and risk modeling for the Feds for about thirty years — my entire working life was adding up very small risks over very large populations and setting them against benefits — so aggregation is sort of in my bones. Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy for fun, I keep running into that Rawls-style objection that utilitarianism treats the separateness of persons as if it didn't matter, and I can't tell wether it should change how I think. Here's where my gut actually lands, case by case: a million people each having one rotten afternoon of headache is worse than one stranger losing a leg; but no number of mild headaches, not a billion, outweighs one person being tortured for twenty years; a regulation that costs every household a couple bucks a year and saves a few hundred statistical lives is clearly worth it; and when forced to choose, I'd still spend that money digging out the one identified miner, and I don't think that's mere sentiment; dumping a real pollution burden on one small poor town so the whole country gets a thin benefit is not okay, even when the net is definately positive; requiring everybody to take a vaccine with a tiny risk of a bad reaction mostly so a few immunocompromised people are protected is fine; and the one I go back and forth on — a million people each losing an afternoon to mild nausea so that one stranger never gets a life of chronic pain. My own stab at a principle: add up across people freely, but only up to some per-person ceiling, and above that ceiling each person has to be answered one at a time. Is there a real version of that, or a better one, that gets all these verdicts? What does it say about the nausea case? And which of my verdicts does it tell me to give up — I'd bet the headache-versus-amputation one and the poor-town one can't both survive.
