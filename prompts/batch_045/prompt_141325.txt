Hi — novelist here, not an academic, so please keep it in plain English where you can, and please don't give me a reading list, a bibliography, or a pile of citations. I'm at a residency with terrible wifi, my library card is expired, and the whole point is the thinking anyway.

I'm a hundred pages into a novel set in a made-up occupied country in a made-up decade, so nothing I describe is real and I've made up all the numbers myself. The part I keep getting stuck on: a reserve police battalion of roughly 480 middle-aged reservists — mostly too old for the regular army, nearly all from the same small city, a lot of them friends, some of them relatives — is told one morning that it will shoot the entire Jewish population of a nearby village, everyone, including the children. Their commander, a former travelling salesman in his forties whom the men actually like, does something unusual for my story: he stands up in front of the whole battalion and says that any man who doesn't feel up to it can step out now, hand over his rifle, and be given other work, and that nothing whatsoever will happen to him. About fifteen do. The rest go and do it. Then the twist the whole book turns on: three weeks later, at the second village, he doesn't repeat the offer but instead asks for volunteers to form the shooting squads — and more men volunteer than there are slots, including four of the fifteen who stepped out the first time. One of my point-of-view characters is one of those four; he writes his wife a letter trying to explain it and can't. Another character shoots all day, cheerfully, organises the men into teams and invents a much more efficient way of moving people from the trucks to the pit — and then, at the third village, asks to be put on latrine duty and never kills again.

Every time I draft these scenes I find I've quietly picked a side in the fight between the two mid-90s books about Reserve Police Battalion 101 — the Browning one, where it's mostly obedience, authority, peer pressure, gradual hardening, ordinary men in a bad situation; and the Goldhagen one, where it's mostly a murderous eliminationist political culture the men had already absorbed and would have acted on anyway. I don't want to pick a side. I want the fault lines, because that's where the drama lives.

So: write me the joint statement a smart, good-faith person on each side could both sign after being locked in a room together for a week.

1. A numbered list of claims both would accept — about what the men did, about which facts are even in dispute, about what each side is *not* claiming (I suspect each gets caricatured as saying that character, or ideology, or the situation counts for nothing, and I'd like that cleared up in wording neither would reject).
2. A disagreements table: the residual disagreements stated exactly, each in words its own side would sign, with the other side's version next to it so I can see which of them are real and which are the same claim wearing different clothes.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where an answer would actually move somebody. My guesses, please argue with them: whether the men who stepped out differ in kind or only in mood that morning; what the volunteers at the second village are evidence for, given that the opt-out was real and cost nothing; whether my efficiency-inventor turned latrine-orderly is better explained by hardening followed by breakdown, or by something the situational story can't reach; and what it even means to say a whole population would have done it anyway, when in my book nobody does it without being ordered to.
4. For each crux, the evidence or hypothetical finding both sides would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction — and please include a few that are *fictional*, i.e. things I can actually put on the page. What would I need to invent into the diary, the letters, the company roster, the trial transcript, to make that crux legible to a reader rather than just asserted?

Last thing, because I'm going to build three hundred pages on this: tell me where the joint statement is weakest. Which agreed claim is agreed-on only because it's vague? Where are the two sides' predictions secretly the same prediction in different vocabulary? Which of your formulations would a real Browning-side or Goldhagen-side historian refuse to sign, and why? And say what would change your mind: what argument, or what detail from my fictional record, would move a crux from genuine disagreement to merely verbal, or the other way round. I'd much rather know where I'm cheating now than find out in a review.

(The Offer and the Volunteers, by the way, are just my working chapter titles — nothing's definately final, and if you think the second one is doing more work than it can bear, say so.)
