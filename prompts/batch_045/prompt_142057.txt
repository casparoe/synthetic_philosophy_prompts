I am teaching a fifty-minute discussion section next week for a second-year metaphysics course, in the week after the unit on whether there are composite material objects. Roughly a third of the students have concluded that the dispute they read was merely verbal and therefore a waste of a seminar; the rest have concluded the opposite, that because it was not merely verbal there must be a brute fact of the matter somewhere, and they are annoyed that nobody has told them what it is. I do not intend to adjudicate the first-order ontological question. What I want them to leave with is a procedure: given any dispute, separate the metalinguistic question — which language, which concept, which way of precisifying — from the question asked inside a language; ask what survives once the metalinguistic question is settled one way or the other; and then classify the residue, if there is one, instead of assuming that whatever survives must be a factual disagreement about the world. In particular I want them to see that a residue can be neither a factual question nor a matter of arbitrary stipulation, but a disagreement about which articulation of the same facts better supports further inquiry: the parties agree on the language, on the facts, and on which language to use, and still disagree, non-trivially, about how to frame the subject matter for theoretical purposes. I have been calling these theoretical-role disputes; rename the label if it will misfire with this group.

Plan the section for me; do not explain the material to me. I need four things.

First, the order in which the components must come, and why it is the only workable order: what has to be in place before the phrase merely verbal can do any work at all, which step is unintelligible if it comes earlier, and where the distinction between a disagreement about the world and a disagreement about which question to ask has to be drawn so that it does not collapse back into the first distinction.

Second, the one example that will carry the whole hour, together with an account of what such an example must have and what it must lack. It has to be a case where the disagreement is not relieved by supplying a definition, where both parties can be assumed informed and mutually charitable, and where something recognizable is at stake, so that nobody leaves thinking the upshot is that ontology is arbitrary. Tell me also why I should not use two of the obvious candidates: the composite-objects dispute itself, and any case turning on a vague predicate, such as whether someone counts as tall or whether a collection counts as a heap.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order in which this particular group will form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each is to be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. The ones I anticipate: that a merely verbal dispute is a trivial one in which both parties were wasting their time; that once the term is disambiguated there is nothing left to argue about, since stipulation settles everything; that the only two possibilities are factual disagreement and quibbling about words; and that if some ontological questions turn on a choice of language, then there is no fact of the matter about anything. I also want them to hit, at a point I cannot myself place, the wrinkle that the procedure yields different verdicts depending on whose term is held fixed — that disambiguation is not a decision procedure — and I want it to land as a discovery about the method rather than as a sceptical conclusion about the first-order disputes.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one task, built on a dispute from outside metaphysics, that a student who has merely learned to say that one is merely verbal will fail, and that a student who has the procedure will pass. Tell me what the failing answers look like.

Finally, name the two or three things I should openly refuse to go into at this level — metasemantic theory, the model-theoretic apparatus, the epistemology of disagreement, whatever else you think belongs on the list — and how I should mark the refusal in the room so that it reads as a boundary rather than an evasion.
