Hey — I'm giving a paper at the epistemology reading group here in a couple weeks (small room, maybe nine people, 20 minutes of comments) and I need Q&A prep badly. Bit of background: I've been reading the rationalist blogs forever — the usual obsessions with calibration, updating on the meta level, steelmanning, base rates beating your own argument — and I finally wrote up the thing I've been arguing in comment threads for years. Sharpened version of the thesis:

Higher-order evidence gets treated as one phenomenon, but I want to split it in two. Call it *calibration-type* HOE: statistical, type-level, doesn't touch your reasons — you learn that 80% of people in your epistemic situation who reason their way to P are wrong, or that you're tired/drunk/depressed, or that the blog you got most of your evidence from has a systematic slant. And *debunking-type* HOE: token-level, reason-engaging — someone walks through your actual argument and shows you that step 4 is bad. My claim is that only the second kind defeats first-order justification. Calibration-type HOE leaves your first-order credence in P exactly where it was, and does all of its work on what I'm calling your *metacredence*: a separate attitude that governs what you investigate next, when you defer, what you act on, and whether you assert. So epistemic akrasia of the form "P, but I'm probably not reliable about whether P" isn't an embarrassing residue that a good theory should minimize; it's the correct equilibrium in the calibration cases, and the enkrasia principle is just false for that class of case. (I've been calling it the split-attitude view; if it already has a name someone should tell me, but please don't send me a reading list, I mostly want the dialectic.)

The audience is the problem. It's mostly formal epistemologists who work on higher-order evidence and calibration and scoring rules, and who think the interesting question is always what the accuracy-dominance or Dutch-book argument delivers — they'll want everything stated as a credence function and will be suspicious of anything that smells like a non-credal attitude. Two of them also work on peer disagreement and are pretty committed conciliationists; they'll press on why disagreement (which is often purely type-level statistical info about people like you) gets filed in my calibration bucket and then comes out as "split" rather than as conciliation. There's one Bayesian decision theorist who's going to say, probably twice, that any HOE just goes into a single joint distribution and my two attitudes are one attitude with a cute name. And there's a guy who works on Aristotle and always shows up, who'll ask whether akrasia was ever meant to be about credences at all and whether I've just changed the subject from the thing that was supposed to be puzzling. Nobody in that room reads the blogs, so translate whatever I say into their vocabulary, but keep me from sounding like I picked this up on a forum.

What I want is a prep sheet: roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they'd actually do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it (say who it's coming from — formal epistemologist, conciliationist, decision theorist, Aristotle guy), each with a short reply I could give in about two minutes of talking, and each with a blunt note on where my reply is soft, where it quietly changes the subject, or where I'm definatly just asserting a stipulation. My guesses at the dangerous ones: the diachronic/reflection-style worry that my agent is exploitable or accuracy-dominated; the "if metacredence drives action and inquiry then you're a functionalist about belief, so what work is first-order credence doing" worry; and an iterated-HOE objection — if I can get calibration evidence about my metacredence, do I need a metametacredence, and where does the regress stop, or does it stop arbitrarily.
