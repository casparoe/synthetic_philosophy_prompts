hey so im a junior at Jefferson High and our school board is thinking about renaming the school cuz Jefferson owned slaves. In my civics class we got into this argument about the man-of-his-time defence and i couldnt tell who was winning. The board made up this new three-part test for whether to keep a historical figures name: to keep it, (1) the bad thing has to have been normal for the time, (2) there have to be basically no serious contemporary critics saying it was wrong, and (3) the person could not have done the achievement we are honoring them for without also doing the bad thing. If they fail any part, the name goes. Two kids in my class, Tasha and Diego, went back and forth and i need someone to adjudicate, like move by move.

Move 1 - Tasha: She said the test is fine but people mess up Jefferson. Slavery was legal and super common in Virginia, so part 1 passes, but part 2 totally fails because there were loads of people back then saying slavery was wrong: Quakers, abolitionist societies, free Black petitioners, even some Enlightenment philosophers. So the man-of-his-time defence cant just be that it was legal, cuz if there were real live critics, Jefferson could have known better, and the school should be renamed.

Move 2 - Diego: He said Tashas being way too strict and is lowkey doing presentism. If the mere existence of any critic is enough to fail part 2, then almost nobody from the past passes, because you can always find some priest or philosopher objecting to something. He said that would also sink Columbus, since there were Spanish missionaries criticizing the enslavement of natives, but it would also sink Churchill for the Bengal famine because some British officials objected at the time. Diego said the defence has to care about whether the critics were mainstream enough that a reasonable person in that persons position would have listened, not just whether a critic existed somewhere. Otherwise the test is just a sneaky way to impose our modern views.

Move 3 - Tasha: She replied that Diegos mainstream enough fix is too fuzzy and would let Jefferson off easy, since in Virginia the critics were def not mainstream and slaveholders ran everything. But she said that misses the point: Jefferson personally had access to those critics. He was super educated, read moral philosophy, and even wrote that slavery was a moral and political problem, yet he kept hundreds of people enslaved and didnt free them. So she said the right version of the man-of-his-time defence is about the individual person, whether this person reasonably could have known better, not just what the average person around them thought. By that standard Jefferson fails hard.

Move 4 - Diego: He said he basically agrees Jefferson knew better, but Tasha is changing the test into something subjective and modern, because could have known better assumes our moral certainty about slavery. Diego said honoring someone isnt just about moral blame. He said we should ask whether the bad action was separable from the achievement we are honoring. For Jefferson, the Declaration and his political theory are separable from slaveholding, and America as we know it wouldnt exist without him, so even if he fails part 2 the name can stay with a plaque. For Columbus, the atrocity is inseparable from the achievement of colonization, so his name should go. For Churchill, the famine is separable from winning World War II, so Churchill keeps his statue. He said Tashas individual-knowledge test ignores the need to weigh whether the bad thing was necessary for the good thing.

They asked me to referee and i honestly cant. Please go through moves 1 to 4 and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly say why, and tell me who is right about what and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Also who is more right overall? thanks
