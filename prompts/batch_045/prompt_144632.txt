Me and a guy from the maths department have been going round on this for over a week and neither of us will budge, so I'm outsourcing it.

The game first, so we're on the same page. I toss a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads is on toss n, I hand you (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars. So heads straight away: +2. One tail then heads: -2. Two tails then heads: +8/3. Then -4, then +32/5, and so on. Weight each payoff by its probability 1/2^n and you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which is conditionaly convergent: added left to right it gives ln 2, about 69 cents. The standard line is that the expected value is therefore undefined rather than 69 cents, since the terms can be rearranged to converge to anything.

Here's my argument that the standard line is too quick.

(1) The probability that no heads ever turns up is zero. So with probability 1 the game ends at some finite toss, and once it has, what you received is exactly what you'd have received from the truncated version G_k (same payoffs for the first k tosses, zero if nothing by then), for every k past that point. The infinite tail is never what actually happens.

(2) So the value of the game should be the limit of the values of its finite truncations. Value ought to be continuous along an approximation that converges to the thing itself with probability 1.

(3) Take the truncations in the natural order, by which toss the first heads falls on. Their expected values are the partial sums 1, 1 - 1/2, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4, ... Every single one of them is at least 1/2, and they converge to about 0.69.

(4) So the game has a perfectly determinate positive value of roughly 69 cents, and I should cheerfully pay 50 cents to play it.

And here's why I know I've broken something, without being able to say what:

(5) Step (2) says nothing about which order you truncate in, and summing the same outcomes in a different order isn't cheating: an expectation is just a sum over disjoint outcomes, and for a series like this one the order of summation is exactly what's in question. By the rearrangement theorem, for any number you like there's an ordering of the terms whose partial sums converge to it. Concretely: keep adding negative terms until the running total is below -1, then drop in the next positive term, and repeat. Every partial sum in that ordering is negative.

(6) So by (2) the game is worth about 69 cents, and also worth less than nothing, and also worth a million dollars, depending on how you list the outcomes. At the top end that means I'm rationally required to pay $999,999 for a gamble where there's a 50% chance I lose $2.

(7) That's absurd, so one of (1) to (3) is false, or one of the inferences doesn't hold.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (3), since both feel like plain descriptions of the setup rather than theory. That points at (2) — except (2) is the single most innocent-looking step in the whole thing, and I lean on reasoning of exactly that shape constantly when payoffs are bounded. My friend says the whole worry is academic anyway, because if you play the thing a hundred times the average payout settles down near ln 2, so 69 cents is the right price in practice. I dont know whether that last claim is even true, and I don't think it answers the question if it is.

Which step do you reject, why did it look so safe, and what does rejecting it cost me — both for this game, and for the ordinary finite cases where I've been quietly relying on the same move?
