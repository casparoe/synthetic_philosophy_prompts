Hi, I'm a third-year PhD student in agricultural entomology — I work on a leafhopper vector system, so lots of field seasons, and I also TA the undergrad integrated pest management class, which is where this started. I keep getting stuck on what our own extension recommendations actually commit us to about insects, and I cant tell whether the practice is philosophically loaded or just silent.

Concretely, here's the pattern. Standard IPM advice is: scout on a schedule, compare pest density to an economic threshold, and treat only when the value of the yield you'd lose exceeds the cost of the control. Insects enter that calculation purely as damage — there is no line anywhere for how many individuals die or how they die. But right alongside it there's a least-toxic-first rule (pick the most selective option, don't blanket the field with a broad-spectrum pyrethroid), an explicit instruction to avoid killing pollinators and natural enemies, and the collecting guidelines I was handed as an undergrad said to kill specimens quickly rather than let them dry out slowly in a vial. My advisor's rule of thumb is the same: if we're going to treat, use the fastest knockdown we can get away with, dont use something that leaves them twitching for days. At the same time our animal-care protocol paperwork covers vertebrates and, for reasons nobody on my committee could explain, cephalopods, but explicitly not insects — I can kill ten thousand leafhoppers with zero paperwork, but twenty zebrafish needs a protocol with a humane endpoint. And here's the bit that actually got me: last year someone on the curriculum committee proposed adding a tie-breaker to the recommendation tables — where two registered products give equivalent control at equivalent cost, choose the one with the shorter time to irreversible unresponsiveness, and where that can't be determined, the one that kills fewer individuals. It was voted down, and the reason given was more or less that adopting it would imply insects have moral standing, which the service doesn't take a position on. But the person who proposed it pointed out that least-toxic-first and the protect-the-beneficials rule already look like they take a position, just for a favored subset of insects, and nobody had a good answer to that.

So what I'd like is: what does this whole package presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that insects have zero moral standing, or would views that give them a little standing license exactly the same advice? I can see at least three candidate reconstructions and I don't know which one the practice actually needs: (a) insects have no welfare at all, so there is nothing to weigh; (b) insects have welfare but death isn't a harm to a creature with no future-directed states, so killing is fine and only the manner of death matters — which would make our indifference to manner of death the real anomaly rather than the killing itself; (c) what matters is populations, species and ecological function rather than individuals, so pest deaths count only as lost function and pest suffering is a category error. I'm also worried about a scale point: thresholds are computed on crop value, and at the densities we work with — millions of individuals per hectare — even an extremely tiny per-insect weight looks like it should swamp the crop-value term, so does the practice have to say the weight is literally zero, or can it survive on a below-the-resolution-of-the-calculation story? And which rival views does it quietly rule out by keeping the calculation the shape it is?

I'd rather have the carving than a verdict: what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits comfortably with, where it is underdetermined so that several theories would license it, and whether its parts are even consistent with each other — the fast-knockdown norm and the vote against the tie-breaker seem to pull in opposite directions, and least-toxic-first cares about some insect deaths but not the pest's. Then, concretely, what would have to change in extension advice if we swapped the implicit view for one of the others: would thresholds move, would method choice change, would we need measurements nobody currently collects, and would any of it survive contact with a grower who just wants a number? And separately, is the vertebrates-plus-octopus-but-not-insects line defensible on the same evidential standard that puts octopuses inside the circle, given the bee cognitive-bias and fly nociception results people keep citing? I know there's a literature on insect sentience and one on wild-animal welfare, and I'm definately not asking to be pointed at it — I'm asking what our own daily practice is already committed to, before anyone cites anything.
