Hey — I run a monthly theory night for our local tenants' union. About twenty people, mostly tenants plus a couple of organisers, a whiteboard, eighty minutes, and basically no economics or game theory beyond what people absorb from the news. In two weeks I've got to do the session on why groups get locked into outcomes nobody wants — races to the bottom, multipolar traps, the Moloch thing. Don't explain it to me; I need a plan for how I explain it to them.

The main thing is that they will walk in already having a theory, and the theory is roughly "the problem is bad people with power" — landlords, basically. So somewhere early I have to get them to see a case where there's no villain, no one chose the outcome, and it's still bad and stable. What I can't decide is whether to lead with that, or to build the machinery first and spring the villain-free point on them around minute forty. Which order, and why — what exactly has to be in place before that point can land?

Here's the bit I've been chewing on and want the session built around. I've started sorting these situations by three knobs: the selector (whatever's actually doing the choosing — competition, an election, a funder's audit, a hiring process), the step (the individual move an actor makes to gain relative position), and the floor (whether the thing being eroded bottoms out somewhere or just keeps going). My instinct is that people who half-get this idea only ever see the step, and that the selector has to be in place before anyone can see why unilateral restraint just selects you out. But I genuinely can't tell whether the floor belongs early, as a way of sorting cases from each other, or late, as a way of talking about what to do. Where would you put each knob in the sequence and why?

On the example that has to carry the whole thing. I've got four candidates and I don't trust my own judgement:

(a) The parade. Everyone stands up to see better, so now everyone's standing, nobody sees any better, and everybody's legs hurt. Vivid, obviously villain-free — but it's one clean move with one obvious answer (everyone sit down, or make a rule), and I worry it teaches them that these are coordination problems you fix by talking to each other, which is the exact confusion I'm trying to prevent.

(b) The service charges. Restaurants in our high street all adding mandatory fees and surcharges, each one doing it because it makes the headline price look lower than the place next door, until everyone has them, nobody has an advantage, and they all eat the customer resentment. Real, close to home, involves money — and I'm not sure the money helps or just adds noise.

(c) Their own campaigning. Every tenants' group in the city now has to run a slick social media presence and a legal-aid fund to be taken seriously by councillors, so they're all spending more and more volunteer hours on the same things and none of them gets heard any more than before. This is their actual life, which is worth a lot — but it's also the case where they'll have the strongest opinions and where "we should just all agree to stop" feels most plausible.

(d) Something from inside our own union: the shift-signup spreadsheet, where whoever grabs the good shifts fastest gets them, so everyone ends up refreshing it at midnight and nobody gets better shifts.

Which one should carry the eighty minutes, which should be held back as a second or closing case, and what does the carrying case have to have — and lack — structurally? I want the answer to be about the shape of the case, not about which one is funnier, and I want to know what each of the others is good for if it isn't carrying.

Misconceptions I expect, roughly in the order I think they'll show up — tell me if I've got the order wrong and where precisely in the sequence to head each one off: (1) someone is to blame; (2) oh, so its the prisoner's dilemma and the fix is communication and trust; (3) so we just need everyone to agree to stop, collective willpower; (4) surely the winners like it — someone must be winning, so it can't really be bad for everyone; (5) this is just capitalism (or the flip side: this is just game theory, so it's an excuse for doing nothing); (6) afterwards, seeing Moloch in everything, including in things that are just a bad decision or a shared preference. And if you think one of those isn't a misconception at all and should be granted rather than defused, say so — I'd rather be corrected now than in front of them.

Two last things. First: I want them to leave able to look at a new situation and say whether it's this, and if it is, where you'd interrupt it. Give me that as a takeaway diagnostic in a sentence someone could actually use out loud at a meeting — not a definition. Second: tell me what to openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving out. I'm thinking formal game theory and equilibrium stuff, the tragedy-of-the-commons literature, anything about AI risk, and where the word Moloch even comes from — but tell me if one of those is actually load-bearing for the idea and I'm wrong to cut it. And one question I can ask at the end that tells me whether they've got the idea or just learned a word.
