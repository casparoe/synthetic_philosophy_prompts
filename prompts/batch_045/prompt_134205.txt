I am a journalist writing a long-read for a broadsheet about a clinical trial whose dataset, everyone now agrees, was corrupted before the analysis was run. Only two people had write access to the database: the trial's data manager and the trial statistician. My reporting points fairly strongly, though not conclusively, at the data manager. There is no evidence of collusion and no evidence of any quarrel between the two, and I have no wish to imply either.

My sub-editor and I have gone back and forth over three sentences. He keeps invoking a house-style rule: because the events are in the past, any conditional about them has to go into the "had done / would have done" form. I keep resisting, but I cannot say why in a way that convinces him.

(1) I wrote: "If the data manager did not alter the records, then the statistician did." He wants: "If the data manager had not altered the records, the statistician would have." To me his version says something different and worse — as though the statistician were a standby who would have stepped in — but he insists the two are interchangeable.

(2) On the question of how the alteration went unnoticed, I wrote: "If the trial had kept an audit trail, the alteration would have been caught within weeks." He wants: "If the trial kept an audit trail, the alteration was caught within weeks." Here I think his version is plainly wrong. We know there was no audit trail, and we know the alteration was not caught for eighteen months. He says that is precisely why the "had kept" form is unnecessary.

(3) About the statistician I wrote: "If she had not been on sabbatical in March, she would have seen the discrepancy." He wants: "If she was not on sabbatical in March, she saw the discrepancy." I genuinely do not know whether she noticed anything, and I am unsure whether to accept this one.

What, if anything, is the real difference between these two ways of framing a conditional? Which of the three rewrites should I accept, which should I refuse, and on what grounds? I would be grateful for a rule of thumb I can rely on in future pieces. And since this article names real people and may well be read by their lawyers, I would also value a note on what each version implicitly commits the paper to, beyond what it literally states.
