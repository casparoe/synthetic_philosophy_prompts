I'm a journalist — I mostly cover science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether the stuff inside videogames is real (a creeper, a sword, a guild, a server). That dragged me into the special composition question, and now I can't get out. I went through a bunch of cases and wrote down my gut verdicts before reading any of the professional answers, because I wanted to see what my own principle actually is. Here they are.

A dog: yes, obviously an object. The dog minus its tail: no, there's no such thing, even though there are particles arranged dog-minus-tail-wise right there. The dog plus the flea currently on its back: also no. A beehive — I mean the colony, not the wax — yes, that feels like one thing. A candle flame: no, it's a process, not a thing. A named hurricane that I follow on the news for two weeks: no, and yet I notice I name it, track it, and say things like "it weakened," which is exactly what I do with the hive, so that pair definately bothers me. A car: yes. A car that can't start because the fuel pump is shot: yes, a broken car is still a car. A pair of shoes: no. A pile of sand: no. The solar system: no, it's just gravitationally bound. A glider crawling across a Conway's Life grid: no, it's a pattern — although, annoyingly, it's a pattern that literally persists because of what its own cells do, which is more than I can say for the pile of sand. A string quartet in the middle of a movement: yes, one thing doing something. The crowd at a bus stop: no. The entire universe: yes, and I can't shake that even though it ruins everything else.

The principle I half-invented to fit these goes something like: some things compose an object iff (1) their joint activity sustains the conditions that let that very activity keep going — the arrangement pulls itself up by its own bootstraps; (2) the arrangement can go wrong without ending — there are states of it that are bad for it, not just states where it stops; and (3) it's maximal: no bigger arrangement and no sub-arrangement also satisfies (1) and (2). Clause (3) is what kills the dog-minus-tail and the dog-plus-flea. Clause (2) is what separates the hive and the car from the flame and the hurricane, since a hive can be sickly and a car can misfire but a flame can only go out. Clause (1) is what separates the hive from the sand. But (1) and (3) together look like they might hand me the flame anyway, and (2) looks like it kills the universe, which I don't want to give up.

So: can you state a principle precisely enough that I could apply it to cases I haven't listed, tell me which clause is motivated by which of my verdicts, and be honest about which verdicts it can't save? Does my three-clause thing secretly collapse into one of the standard answers — everything composes, only living things compose, nothing composes — or is it genuinely its own thing? What does it say about a termite mound, a lake, a corporation, a river, a slime mould solving a maze, an entangled pair of particles, and a self-repairing Mars rover? And what does it say about the case I keep flipping on, which is the one my article actually needs: a Minecraft creeper, in a world that's running on a server, being chased by a player who will definately remember it tomorrow.
