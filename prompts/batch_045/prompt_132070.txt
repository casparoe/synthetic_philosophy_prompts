I'm a backend dev at a small SaaS company, no philosophy background at all, and I got dragged into an argument I can't adjudicate. Amira (design) and Dan (growth) have been going back and forth about one screen, and they've both asked me to weigh in tomorrow.

Context: when a free trial ends, users hit an interstitial. Big green button: "Restore access — $9.99/mo." Below it, in small grey text: "I'm done with my project." In an earlier version that grey text said "Continue without paying." Dan changed the wording and the A/B test showed fewer people clicking it. The thing is, clicking it doesn't end your project — the workspace stays accessible read-only indefinitely and you can export everything. Amira says the wording is a lie. Dan says it isn't. Their actual exchange, roughly:

(1) Dan: Nothing on that screen is false. The link says "I'm done with my project," and if you click it your session with us ends and you move on. Lying means saying something false. We don't. You can't turn a true sentence into a lie by complaining about the font size.

(2) Amira: You didn't pick that sentence for what it says, you picked it for what it implies, and the implication is false — clicking it doesn't end anything. If we wrote "End my project forever" that would obviously be a lie, and the *only* reason we didn't write that is that it'd be too obviously false to get away with. So we swapped a false sentence for a true sentence whose entire job is to produce the same false belief. That's not less deceptive, it's just deniable.

(3) Dan: If implication counts as saying, then basically all design is lying. We make the thing we want big and green and the thing we don't want small and grey — every interface ranks options, and ranking shapes what people think is normal or recommended. Calling the mid tier "Most popular" implies it's the best value. By your standard every designer is a liar and the word stops meaning anything.

(4) Amira: Not every nudge, and here's where I'd draw the line. Two tests. The mumbling test: a cashier who says the price fast and quietly so you mishear, and then says "I did say it," has the same structure as this. The email test: if we wouldn't put the claim in a plain sentence in an email to the user — "clicking this ends your project" — we shouldn't get to smuggle it in through layout and wording. We'd never send that email, because it's false.

(5) Dan: The email test isn't neutral either. If we emailed people "by the way you can keep using this for free and export your files," that would itself change behavior — people would wonder why we were telling them, get suspicious, and either bail or go looking for a catch. You can't demand we state a fact in whichever format most favors the user. Also: users who actually read the link decline at the same rate they did when it said "Continue without paying." The information reaches everyone who looks for it. We're not deceiving the careful, we're just not compensating for the careless. And mumbling is about deliberately degrading a signal — we didn't degrade anything, the link is the same size and in the same spot it's always been.

(6) Amira: "The people who noticed weren't deceived" is circular — sure, the people who weren't deceived weren't deceived. The design is built around the fact that most people won't read it, and the wording was chosen to be safe to skip. And size isn't my point anyway: "I'm done with my project" is false for the skippers even in 40-point type, because what's false is in the words, not the font.

(7) Dan, last word: By your standard, not mentioning export anywhere is also lying, and nobody thinks a UI has to disclose everything. There's gotta be some division of labor between what we tell people and what they find out on their own.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what? I'd love each of the seven rated somewhere from clearly wrong to clearly right with a short reason, not just an overall winner. Also: is there something real they're both missing — some distinction that would dissolve part of it? I get the feeling they're using "lying" to mean two different things but I can't tell whether that means the argument is just verbal or whether one of them has the better concept. Plain language please, I've never read any of this stuff.
