I sit on the social-science panel of a national research council, and we have one funded slot left in this round and a genuinely split panel. I have some philosophy training from before I moved into economic history, and because of that the chair has asked me to draft the decision memo, with the understanding that the other four panelists will either sign it or annotate it. The application is from a group calling itself the Coordination and Expectations Lab, and it proposes to settle whether culture can be a cause in economic explanation. I want to be clear about the substantive details, because the memo has to be answerable by the applicants.

The applicants are explicit that they are not doing another study of inherited values: they say that framing, which is how most people read Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, has never been able to separate the effect of a population's normative commitments from the effect of the institutions those commitments built. Their alternative is to define culture as a population's stock of common-knowledge defaults for solving recurring coordination problems — focal points, in the game-theoretic sense — held in expectations rather than in preferences, genes, or laws. On that definition, a shared expectation is claimed to be a cause in the ordinary difference-making sense (intervene on what people expect others to do, and the outcome changes) while also being nothing but a fact about what people believe about one another; they argue that this dissolves the older contrast between explaining an action by its causes and explaining it by the meanings it has for the agent, rather than picking a side in it. Their design has three arms: a border design exploiting a nineteenth-century administrative redistricting that split a linguistically homogeneous region into two districts with identical formal law and comparable geography; an epidemiological arm comparing descendants of emigrants from each side of that border who now live in the same city; and a lab-in-the-field coordination game run with both migrant and non-migrant samples, plus a measure of whether the relevant default survives better when transmitted within households than through schools. The panel's disagreements are sharp. Our statistician says that if institutions are themselves downstream of expectations, then the border design cannot separate culture from institutions at all, and that the game arm has no pre-registered prediction about magnitude. Our historian says that explaining with focal points is equilibrium selection, not the interpretation of meanings, so whatever the project studies it is not culture and the label is doing no work. A third member says the definition is unfalsifiable because any observed regularity can be redescribed after the fact as coordination on some focal point; the applicants' reply is that they will fix the focal point in advance using cooperative bylaws recorded in district archives before the outcome period begins. There is also a budget problem: four years were requested, and the archival arm may fall under the council's humanities budget line rather than ours.

Please draft the memo for circulation before our meeting on the 14th. It should recommend one concrete course of action — fund as submitted, fund a reduced version, fund conditional on specified changes with a resubmission deadline, or reject — and give reasons the applicants could actually act on rather than merely rejections of their framing. Two further requirements. First, our rules say any funded project making a causal claim must rest on a design that could in principle have delivered the opposite answer; include one short paragraph stating whether this project meets that test, and on which arm the answer turns. Second, give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full force: I expect at least two panelists to read this as a decision about whether the project is, in the relevant sense, cultural explanation at all, and I would rather the memo say plainly what we are buying if we fund a study built on expectations rather than on values — including the possibility that a successful result would only relabel the old dispute instead of settling it.
