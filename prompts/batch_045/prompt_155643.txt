I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who consults on organisational culture and thinks generational talk is essentially a marketing artefact with a thin veneer of social science, and I came away holding a position I would rather have stress-tested than defended. Here it is in the strongest form I can give it: generations are real social kinds, not merely conventional bins of birth years. On my version the reality is not supposed to come from the labels, the cutoffs, or the personality profiles attached to them, but from a mechanism. There is a formative window in late adolescence and early adulthood in which political attitudes, technological fluency, and habits of consumption are disproportionately fixed; a birth cohort is a set of people who passed through that window under a shared configuration of events, institutions, and available technology; and because exposure is discrete in the relevant sense — you either had a smartphone at fifteen or you did not — the resulting differences are durable rather than transient. The kind is anchored demographically, by when you were born, and its boundaries are admittedly fuzzy, in the way the boundaries of a mountain range are fuzzy, which I take to be compatible with the kind being discovered rather than stipulated.

My friend's rival account, which I want treated as the main competition, is what I have started calling the designation view: a cohort is real only to the extent that institutions act on people as a cohort — school year-groups, conscription classes, platform recommendation systems, HR segmentation — so that what looks like a generation is really the shadow cast by practices that sort people by age. On her account the causal work is done by those practices, and where they differ, the demographic grouping should come apart from the attitudinal one. So: what would count against my view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a committed defender of the demographic-mechanism view should concede as counting against it from what she can reasonably dismiss as resting on a caricature. Please be explicit about what my view predicts that hers does not: persistence of a cohort signature as the cohort ages rather than convergence toward a common adult pattern; a step at the boundary rather than a smooth gradient; the migrant case, where someone moves media and political environment after the formative window closes and should, on my account, keep the signature of her birth year rather than acquiring that of her new environment; and a signature that survives in countries whose institutions differ but whose technology arrived at roughly the same time. I also want you to confront the methodological worry I keep skirting: any single cross-sectional snapshot cannot separate age effects, period effects, and cohort effects, since which cohort you are in is fixed by when you are observed and how old you are. Say what longitudinal or comparative design would actually discriminate between my view and hers, and say whether the fact that boundary dates get periodically redrawn and the formative events retroactively redescribed means my position has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether, for a claim about a social kind whose members are partly shaped by being classified, that insulation is a defect or simply the honest shape of the claim.

Two last things. First, I cannot look anything up from where I am writing, so argue from mechanisms, conceivable cases, and the kind of survey evidence you can reconstruct from memory: no studies to cite, no reading lists, no names to chase. Second, for what it is worth: I know myself well enough to say I would be moved most by a single well-known generational signature that turns out, on closer inspection, to be an age effect or a period effect in cohort clothing, or by two countries with the same technology but different institutions where the same birth years show opposite signatures. If you think my view is better insulated than that, say so plainly and tell me whether the insulation is earned.
