I write novels, not philosophy, but I got dragged into some of the animal-ethics stuff for a book a while back and now I notice this pattern everywhere and I don't have a word for it.

Two instances from my own ms. A working farm dog gets killed — no name, two lines of description, not a pet. Readers were furious with me for chapters afterwards. Earlier in the same book a pig is slaughtered, described at about the same length, and I recieved exactly zero letters about it. Second instance: a beta reader told me a minor human character — a hired hand, maybe four lines total, dies offstage in a fire — "didn't really count as a death," and she meant that morally, not as a note about plotting. Meanwhile the villain, who gets a long chapter inside his head while he's doing something genuinely awful, got loads of sympathy.

The pattern as I see it: readers hand out how-much-this-creature-counts in proportion to how much inner life the text actually *shows* them, and then take it back when the inner life shown is unpleasant or alien. The pig and the hired hand have whatever inner life pigs and hired hands have; what varies is a technique choice I made with a pen. It feels wierd to me that an aesthetic decision on my part is moving people's moral judgments around, including about real pigs, because readers do walk out of the book and apply it.

Why I don't think the obvious words are it. Not the identifiable-victim thing or the numbers-are-too-big-to-feel thing: in both pairs it's one individual either way, the numbers are identical. Not anthropomorphism, if that means projecting human traits onto nonhumans — the dog isn't being read as a person in a human suit, it's being read as *someone*. And not just speciesism, because the hired hand gets the pig's treatment and he's an adult human.

So: is there an established concept for this, on the ethics side or the narrative-theory side — moral concern tracking represented mind instead of actual mind? And the part I actually care about, so please don't skip it: which variable is doing the work? Amount of inner life shown, how hard you're pushed to imagine it from the inside, or how much the shown inner life resembles your own? Those three come apart across my examples and I can't tell which one I'm looking at. Then: is this a fact about how fiction works — there's nothing true of the pig beyond what I wrote, so nothing else is even available to track — or a fact about our moral psychology that fiction merely exposes? I'd definately like to know which, because if it's the second, my own reasons for not eating pigs are in worse shape than I'd like.

Keep the answer under 600 words and lead with the bottom line: one or two sentences on whether it has a name and, if it doesn't, what you'd call it.
