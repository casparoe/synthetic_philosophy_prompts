So a friend of mine is going through IVF at the moment and it's got me and a few people from my very informal reading group stuck on something. The thing that struck us is how picky the whole setup is about which reasons for choosing one embryo over another count as respectable. The clinic will happily discard embryos with the wrong number of chromosomes, will test for a specific disease if both parents are carriers, and — at least where I live, with some approvals — will let you pick an embryo that's a tissue match for a sick older sibling. But the same clinic acts as if ranking embryos by anything like intelligence or height is straight-up beyond the pale, and non-medical sex selection is either illegal or something they'll quietly steer you away from. At the same time, the very same health system nags pregnant women about folate and alcohol, and basically everyone I know agrees that a woman who drinks heavily all through a pregnancy has wronged the child she ends up having. That last bit is what I can't square. If we already accept that prospective parents owe it to the child who will exist to make that child as well off as they reasonably can, then why isn't "take the embryo with the best expected life" the obvious next step? Isn't that just procreative beneficence, and isn't it weird to accept the duty in the pregnancy case and reject it in the selection case?

So here's what I actually want: what does the practice as it exists presuppose? Is it committed to something like a treatment/enhancement distinction, or to a sufficiency view where you may select to avoid a bad life but there's no reason at all to optimize above the threshold? Does it secretly lean on some claim about the non-identity problem — that we can't benefit a child by creating them, but we can harm them by creating them in a bad state? Does it presuppose that parents have a domain of reproductive discretion that simply runs out at some point, and if so, why exactly there? And I'd really like the distinction between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely fits with: could someone who accepts full-on maximizing procreative beneficence still endorse the clinic's actual rules for second-order reasons — the tech being noisy, worries about where a market in traits ends up, worries about what it says to already-existing disabled kids — or is that person stuck defending something closer to the practice I actually see?

One more thing, because we invented it halfway through the argument and none of us could work out whether it's a genuine alternative or just the same view in a costume. Call it "the floor rule": you may use any selection technology to minimize the chance that the child ends up below some decent minimum of well-being, and you're encouraged to, but you may not use it to improve a child's prospects once they're already above the floor, even when the improvement is large and basically free. Is the floor rule actually a different theory from the one the clinic is running, or is it just a description of it? Does it dodge the expressivist worry about Down-syndrome screening, or does cutting off at the floor say something just as bad? And concretely: if a clinic swapped its current implicit theory for the floor rule, or for straight procreative beneficence, what would visibly change — who gets funded, what counselors say, whether declining selection starts to look like negligence? Sorry, that's a bit of a jumble, but I'd rather have the mess than a clean answer to the wrong question.
