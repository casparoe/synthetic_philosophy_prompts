I spent forty years in zoology, most of them on learning and discrimination in fish and cephalopods, and since retiring I have been working through moral philosophy properly for the first time, largely on my own. I should say at the outset that I live too far from a university to use its library, and in any case I am not asking for a reading list: I want to understand a position well enough to argue with it myself, not to be directed to scholarship I cannot obtain.

Recently I read a well-reviewed trade book on the history of our thinking about animals. Its chapter on Kant is the most confident in the volume, and I noted the gist of its account like this:

Kant's position is brutally simple: animals are things. Only a being that can act on universal laws and hold itself to reasons is a person, and only persons are ends in themselves; no non-human animal qualifies. It follows that we have no duties to animals at all — nothing we do to an animal can wrong it, any more than breaking a spade wrongs the spade. What looks like a duty to animals is really a duty to other human beings: cruelty to animals is wrong only because it hardens us, since a person who is cruel to animals will end up being cruel to people. The wrong therefore lies entirely in the agent's character, and nothing about what the animal suffers enters into it at all. A Kantian can accordingly have no principled objection to eating animals or experimenting on them, provided the work is done without sadism. And because the whole argument rests on the factual claim that animals have no inner life, everything we now know about apes, corvids and octopuses simply refutes it.

My difficulty is that I cannot tell which parts are Kant, which are the author's compression, and which are his opponent's caricature. I am fairly sure the final move is too quick — I would not know how to design an experiment that settles whether an animal acts on universal laws, and I doubt my own results could refute an ethical argument that directly — but I am not confident about the earlier sentences, and I suspect the author has quietly run together claims that the view keeps apart. I have also come across, in the student notes from Kant's ethics lectures, a passage about a man who shoots his dog once the dog can no longer work; the book cites it as proof that Kant was indifferent to the animal's fate, and I am not sure that is what it shows.

Three distinctions I have been trying out may or may not be the right ones: first, the ground of a duty as opposed to what the duty is about — a duty could be grounded in something about the agent and still be, in content, a duty concerning an animal; second, reading the relevant rational capacity as a capacity an individual actually possesses at a time, as opposed to a mark of a kind whose mature members normally possess it; third, being a creature that can be harmed and can matter, as opposed to being a creature that can be wronged and can press a claim. Are these distinctions real ones in this neighbourhood, is the first roughly what the passage above conflates, is the second genuinely available to someone working in Kant's framework, and is the third a distinction with a difference for a view on which duties run only to persons?

Concretely, I would like a sentence-by-sentence assessment: what a specialist in Kant's ethics would accept as stated, what they would wince at as a compression or simplification, and what they would reject; whether the practical conclusion about eating and experimenting survives correction of the earlier claims; and, for each of my three distinctions, whether it is genuine, whether it is doing the work I suppose, and whether I have drawn it in the wrong place. If any of the surrounding dispute is merely verbal, I would like to be told which part and why.

One last request: if the corrected version of the view is weaker than the book's but still gives me less than I hoped for, please say so plainly. I am writing a short paper for a local philosophy group on whether the comparative evidence I spent my career gathering bears on Kantian ethics at all, and I would much rather learn now that it bears on it only indirectly than discover later that I have built an essay on a misstatement of its target.

H. R. Vandermeer, Emeritus Professor of Zoology
