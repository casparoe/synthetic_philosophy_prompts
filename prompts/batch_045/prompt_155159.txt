You are a professional philosopher who works on population ethics, infinite decision theory, and the formal theory of value, and who has also spent time teaching students with very uneven mathematical backgrounds. You are careful, explicit about the formal machinery you rely on, and willing to say when a distinction does not do the work its defenders claim.

I am building a two-page handout for a graduate seminar module on infinity in ethics. The module meets once, for sixty minutes, and the students range from people who enjoy set theory to people who took one logic course. I want the handout to be a self-sorting decision tree, in the spirit of a choose-your-own-conclusion flowchart: a reader starts from a commitment almost everyone in the room shares (roughly: some possible outcomes contain infinitely many lives, or lives of infinite duration, and this is not a reason to stop doing ethics) and walks through a series of branch questions until they arrive at a named position about how such outcomes are to be compared and chosen between.

Please design that tree, and think about the discriminating questions carefully before you commit to them. I would like you to start by sketching, in a short section I can delete later, a list of candidate branch questions; for each, say what it would separate and why it fails if it fails (too close to a label, answerable only by someone who already knows the literature, ambiguous between two different formal claims, or insensitive because every position answers it the same way). Then give me the tree you would actually print.

The tree should have six to eight branch nodes and roughly ten to fourteen leaves. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first fix the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions inside that space, then mark the cells nobody occupies and give a one-line diagnosis of each (incoherent, merely unattractive, or coherent but unexplored and worth a paper). Where two positions travel together through your whole tree and cannot be pulled apart by any of your nodes, flag that openly rather than inventing a question to separate them.

Every branch node must be phrased as a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads, and each should be keyed to a concrete case rather than to an abstract claim about infinity. I need these cases to appear:

A hotel with countably many rooms, all occupied: one world in which the first infinitely many occupants are ecstatic and the remaining infinitely many are in mild suffering, and another world containing exactly the same lives, one-for-one, but interleaved so that every ecstatic life is followed by a suffering life. Same multiset of lives, different arrangement.

A single infinite sequence of lives whose well-being levels form a series that converges, but only conditionally: the partial sums approach a finite limit in one ordering, and can be rearranged to approach any real value at all. The question at stake is whether such a world has a determinate total and whether the temporal or structural ordering of the lives enters into it.

Two worlds with the same cardinality of good lives and the same cardinality of bad lives, in which the proportion of good lives tends to a half in one world and to one in the other, so that cardinality says the worlds are the same size while the limiting relative frequency says they are not.

A world of pure bliss to which exactly one further life of mild suffering is added, and the parallel case where finitely many blissful lives are added to a world that already contains infinitely many suffering ones. These should be used to test whether finite differences still register against an infinite background.

A choice between a certainty of a small finite good and an extremely small probability of changing the welfare of infinitely many lives, where the expected-value calculation either diverges or depends on a convention for multiplying an infinitesimal by an infinite magnitude.

Please also include a branch that separates outcomes infinite in number from outcomes infinite in magnitude, that is, from finitely many lives each of which contains infinite welfare. I specifically want to know whether the tree treats those two shapes of infinity symmetrically, and where the asymmetry, if any, has to be introduced.

Among the leaves, I need at least the following positions located and named, with a one-sentence statement of each one's most awkward commitment: the view that the presence of infinite value renders ordinary comparisons undefined and leaves us practically paralysed; the view that infinite stakes always dominate finite ones, however improbable; the view that total welfare should be represented in a non-Archimedean ordered structure so that finite differences still register against an infinite background; the anti-aggregative view on which adding good lives to an already good world changes nothing; a person-affecting restriction that survives into the infinite case; a view that compares worlds by limiting relative frequency or density rather than by cardinality; a position that is sensitive to the location or ordering of lives within the structure of the world; and a view on which ethics simply has no application to infinite comparisons, so that only finite ones are evaluable. If some coherent combination of your branch answers corresponds to no position anyone has defended, keep it as a leaf labelled unnamed and say in one line what a defender would have to show.

Two further constraints. First, the handout has to be self-contained: any formal machinery you use, whether cardinality, orderings, limiting relative frequency, non-Archimedean structures, conditional convergence, or expected value with non-finite magnitudes, must be glossed in a sentence or two at the point of use, so that a student with only introductory logic can answer honestly. Second, the instrument must not depend on outside sources: no citations, no reading lists, no attributions of views to particular authors, and no reliance on details a reader or a model without web access would have to look up. Where a position is standardly given a name, give the name and also restate the position in your own words, so that the tree works for someone who has never heard the name.

Finally, add a short scoring sheet at the end, mapping each answer pattern to its leaf, plus a paragraph on what will predictably go wrong when this is used in a sixty-minute session: which node students will misread, which one they will answer strategically so as to avoid a leaf they dislike, and which part of the underlying problem the tree cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the discussion. Aim for about 1,200 words for the tree and its apparatus, before the scoring sheet.
