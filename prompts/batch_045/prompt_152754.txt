I'm in a graduate seminar on population ethics and decision-making under moral uncertainty, and I am trying to build a response paper around the notion of a *safe Pareto improvement*. As it was introduced in my seminar, the idea runs roughly like this: a policy P is a safe Pareto improvement on the status quo S just in case, for every moral view to which the agent assigns non-negligible credence and every outcome her evidence leaves open, no one is worse off in the P-outcome than she would have been in S, and someone is better off. The attraction is supposed to be that it lets a badly uncertain agent act: because the verdict is unanimous across the axiologies she is torn between and robust across the empirical possibilities she cannot rule out, she can recommend P without first settling which axiology is correct or which outcome will obtain. I have been testing the notion on the kind of case my seminar keeps returning to — a large public-health programme funded out of general taxation, most of whose benefits land two or three generations out — and the argument I ended up with seems to show that the notion cannot do that work at all. I would like to know where it breaks.

Here is the argument, which I wrote out for myself and now find suspicious because the conclusion is one I cannot accept.

(1) Take the definition above: unanimity across the agent's serious moral views, robustness across the outcomes her evidence leaves open, and a person-affecting comparison in both clauses.

(2) Any policy of the relevant kind changes who comes into existence: the children conceived under P are not the children who would have been conceived under S, and the same holds for their children.

(3) The comparison inside the definition is person-affecting: *is she worse off than she would have been* presupposes that she figures in both outcomes. So the test can only range over people whose existence and identity are invariant across the outcomes the agent's evidence leaves open.

(4) That leaves two options for policies that change who exists. Either restrict the test to those invariant people (branch A), or extend it to people who figure in only one of the outcomes by comparing their condition not with their condition in the alternative outcome but with their never having existed at all (branch B). A person-affecting comparison between someone and no one is not a comparison, so there is no third possibility.

(5) Under branch A, the costs and benefits of any existence-affecting policy fall almost entirely on contingent people, so the test has nothing to bite on. Since (2) holds of every substantial long-term policy, the class of safe Pareto improvements is empty for exactly the cases the notion was introduced to settle, and it yields no guidance at all.

(6) Under branch B, anyone whose life is on balance worth living is not made worse off by being brought into existence, so the first clause is satisfied by any policy that does not create lives not worth living; and the second clause is satisfied by the existence of any worthwhile life whatsoever. So a policy that costs no one who would otherwise have existed anything — financed out of funds that would otherwise simply have gone unused — and that brings it about that one extra child is born with a life barely worth living would count as a safe Pareto improvement. That is the repugnant conclusion in miniature, and *safe* was supposed to mark off the cases that are beyond reasonable moral dispute, so calling it safe is the opposite of what the notion was for.

(7) So the notion is either empty (branch A) or empty of interest (branch B); either way it does none of the work it was introduced to do.

I would most like to keep (2) and (3): the non-identity point and the person-affecting reading of the comparison both look to me like hard data about the case rather than stipulations I am free to drop. What I cannot tell is whether the fork in (4) is a genuine dichotomy or an artifact of how I set the definition up in (1), and I have a lingering sense that (6) is reading the *someone is better off* clause differently from the way (5) reads the *no one is worse off* clause. Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine when I wrote it, and what each available fix commits me to: what it costs to keep the person-affecting reading in (3) and abandon the requirement of unanimity across moral views; what it costs to abandon the person-affecting reading instead; and what it costs to move to an expectational criterion that compares how strongly each view favours P rather than asking whether each view calls P an improvement. I do not need citations, a bibliography or suggestions for further reading — I only want the diagnosis and the price list.
