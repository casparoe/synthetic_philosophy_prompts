I've been reading about string theory and the multiverse on my own, with no formal training in physics or philosophy, and I want to pin down one number. Call a theory non-empirically confirmed when it is supported by arguments of roughly this shape: nothing else on offer is internally consistent enough to count as a real rival; the theory explains things it was not built to explain; and combined with the claim that there is an enormous ensemble of universes with different low-energy constants, it explains why we find ourselves in a corner that permits life rather than treating that as brute luck.

My question: what is your credence that arguments of this kind actually confirm a theory, in the sense that they should raise a reasonable person's confidence that the theory describes the world? I don't want "opinions differ on this." I want a number plus the decomposition behind it: how much rides on whether such a large ensemble really exists, how much on whether selection over unobserved universes can be evidence at all, how much on whether "no rivals found" is information about the world rather than about our search, and how much on whether the apparent fine-tuning was ever a genuine puzzle. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to.

My own credence is about 0.2, and it is low mainly because of a worry I worked out myself: the larger the ensemble, the better a theory can accommodate whatever we happen to observe and the less it predicts; but an ensemble small enough to be falsifiable would have to be lucky to contain life-permitting values at all. Confirmatory power therefore seems to peak at some intermediate ensemble size, and the feature that makes the landscape attractive looks like the same feature that drains it of content. Is that a real problem, and if so, where does it sit in your decomposition? I'd also like to know how much weight to give the one case I've read about where anthropic reasoning appears to have gotten the rough size of the cosmological constant right before it was measured — does a track record of one count for anything?

Finally, say what would move your number the most, and be explicit about where your own reasoning is most likely to be mistaken.
