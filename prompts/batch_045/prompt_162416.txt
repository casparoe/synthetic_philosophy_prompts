I work on the preference-modeling stack for a large language model, and a design question that landed on my desk has turned out to be a philosophy question in an engineering costume. I'd like you to translate it, and then tell me where the translation breaks.

The setup. We collect pairwise preference labels from roughly forty annotators. At the moment we fit one reward model per annotator: a Bradley–Terry model over trajectory comparisons, each with its own learned temperature, each used to shape that annotator's slice of the policy under its own KL budget. When we need a single training signal we normalize the per-annotator scores and average them. As statistics this is unproblematic. As a way of deciding whose experience counts for how much, it seems to be doing something I can't state, and I want to be able to state it.

The question I'm told is the philosophical one: whether utility is interpersonally comparable, and how much. Whether we can only rank one person's own outcomes against each other (ordinal and non-comparable across people), or can compare gains and losses across people up to a per-person multiplicative constant (cardinal but unit-comparable only), or can also compare levels, which requires a common zero. I know the shape of the dialectic at second hand: a classic aggregation argument to the effect that if everyone's utilities are von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities and you accept a Pareto condition, social welfare must be a weighted sum; a Rawls-style reply that parties choosing behind a veil of ignorance would refuse that gamble and take maximin instead, appealing to the separateness of persons; Sen-style complaints that the apparatus has quietly helped itself to an informational assumption the data never supported; and the extended-sympathy move, where I compare by imagining myself in your position under an equiprobability assumption about who I will turn out to be.

What I want is that dialectic restated in the vocabulary I actually use, as literally as you can manage: reward models and the positive-affine freedom each one carries; per-annotator temperature and the fact that scale is separately unidentified per annotator; off-policy versus on-policy reward drift under a KL penalty; reward hacking; calibration; label noise and annotator disagreement; distribution shift between annotator pools; scalarization; and the fact that a reward model is a function of trajectories rather than of a person's life.

Then I want the residue report, in three parts. First, what translated cleanly, with the places where the mapping is exact rather than merely suggestive. Second, what you had to fudge — name the points at which you quietly imported one of my framework's assumptions and called it translation, because I would rather be told than flattered. Third, what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. My suspicion is that the separateness of persons is literally unstatable in a framework whose only objects are trajectories, parameters, and rewards, and that the interesting question is whether this is a defect of the framework or a sign that the objection was never doing real work. Please test that suspicion rather than confirming it.

One more thing, which is the actual decision in front of me and which I'd like you to treat as a probe rather than answer directly. A concrete proposal has been floated for getting us all the way to level comparability. Give every annotator a fixed anchor set of trajectories; have them rate the intensity of each preference on a bounded scale; have them also mark their own just-noticeable-difference threshold on that scale; then express every intensity as a number of that annotator's JNDs away from a shared anchor, which is supposed to make both the unit and the zero point common across people. Tell me what this procedure actually buys, in the philosophers' terms — which invariance it breaks and which it leaves intact — and what the corresponding move would look like if someone attempted it on human welfare. If the analogy turns out to be exact here, that is the single most useful thing you could tell me.

Write in prose with light structure, aim for something I can read in ten minutes, and don't hand me a bibliography or any citations: I can't look anything up, and I don't want references to chase.
