I'm giving the opening remarks at a one-day conference in memory of Hugh Sanderson, who supervised my doctorate and with whom I then argued — by letter for the first decade, then in print — for thirty-one years. Our dispute was about whether the agreement results in formal epistemology have any teeth: he held that a shared prior is a genuine coherence constraint, so that persisting disagreement between apparent peers is a symptom to be diagnosed and, where possible, repaired; I held that the common prior assumption does all the work, that it is satisfied almost nowhere outside a model, and therefore that the theorem is a consistency check on ideal agents rather than a rebuke to actual ones. Neither of us ever moved, and by the end we had each, independently, put 0.95 on our still disagreeing in ten years — which is the only number the two of us ever agreed on. He died in March. I have ten minutes and, by his own instruction, a typed note asking that no speaker at the event use the phrase "agreeing to disagree" — not out of piety but because both of us thought it names an impossibility, not an achievement.

The room is the difficulty. There will be about forty people who know the result and its extensions cold — formal epistemologists, decision theorists, a couple of economists, several of his former students — and they will hear any false step. One of them, his last doctoral student, is giving the second talk and will argue that Hugh's final papers effectively conceded our dispute to me; I think that is a misreading, I have to sit down afterwards and listen to it, and I don't want the remarks either to foreclose that reading or to pick a fight with it. The rest of the room is his second wife, his two adult daughters, his sister, and a handful of people from the village where he kept bees and ran the choir, who know nothing of any of this and who need to hear that the man they loved was loved, and taken seriously, in the place where he spent his working life. So no victory lap, no "we were each a little right," no staged resolution, and nothing that lets his family hear thirty-one years as a spat.

Here is what I want the formalism to do, and I'd like you to do it better than I can. First: agreement on one number is not agreement about the world. Two people can hold exactly the same posterior for one event while being disposed to move in opposite directions on the next piece of evidence, because what they have pooled is the number rather than the information behind it; I have started calling this concordance without consensus, and I want it to land for the people from the village as something other than a consolation prize. (For the specialists: we ended about two percentage points apart, and I want that described as what it is — the approximate versions of the result, with priors that are close rather than identical or beliefs that are nearly but not quite common knowledge, bound the gap without closing it, and two points is not zero.) Second: a shared prior today is entirely compatible with expecting to be disagreed with tomorrow, since each of us expects his own future opinion to average out to his own present one. So the honest form of words is not that we agreed to disagree but that we agreed, right now, and expected to be carried apart by whatever came next — and I want his daughters to hear that as devotion rather than as detachment. Third: in thirty-one years we never once bet each other, not even a bottle of wine, and I want the speech to make something of the fact that the offer itself would have been the evidence. And then the letters: there is a result, I believe proved for finite sets of possibilities by Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis, that agents who honestly announce their posteriors to one another, over and over, must after finitely many rounds announce the same number. We announced ours to each other for thirty-one years and never did. So one of the conditions failed — the space of possibilities was not finite, or something was lost in transmission between us, or one of us was not reporting the number he actually had — and I don't get to say which. I want the speech to say that without settling it, in a tone that is neither an accusation against a dead man nor a confession.

Please write the remarks: ten minutes, complete draft rather than an outline, in my voice, which is dry and not given to uplift. Do not invent biographical detail about Hugh that a member of his family could contradict — if the speech needs a concrete memory, leave a bracketed placeholder and say in a note what kind of memory to drop in there; bees and the village choir are the only material I can vouch for. After the text, add a short note naming the two or three sentences where the formal claim is load-bearing, what the non-specialists will hear in each, and — honestly — what will go wrong if I read this in a room where I am also grieving.
