I am writing to ask for a considered judgment on a matter that has divided my household for a year, and I would like a verdict rather than consolation.

In the spring of 2000 my wife and I bought, from a provincial gallery, a small oil landscape. It was sold to us as a work attributed to a certain early-twentieth-century painter — a competent, collected, minor figure, not a household name — and priced, as such things are, at a small fraction of what a certified painting by his hand would fetch. We did not buy it as an investment. We bought it because we liked it, and it has hung over the same table ever since. Our children grew up under it. I would guess I have looked at it several thousand times.

Last spring a scholar preparing a complete scholarly catalogue of the painter's works asked to examine it. Her conclusion is that the picture is not his. On the evidence of the ground layer, the craquelure, and a pigment not available in the form used here until several decades after the painter's death, it was made in the 1970s, and in her view by the same hand as a cluster of similar pictures that appeared in those years. Nothing about the object has changed. It is the same thing that has hung in our dining room for a quarter of a century.

She then offered us what she called a courtesy. She can either publish the picture in the catalogue as a documented imitation, with our ownership of it a matter of public record, or she can leave it out entirely. If she publishes it, the picture is worth, commercially, very little — a competent mid-century pastiche with a famous exposure. If she omits it, its merely-attributed standing quietly hardens into something like acceptance, and it retains a meaningful fraction of what we paid. No one need ever know.

Two further things sharpen the problem. The first is something the scholar mentioned almost in passing: the technical imaging available to her now — hyperspectral scanning compared against a large database of historical pigment samples — can date a picture's materials with considerable confidence, but says nothing whatever about who applied them, or with what intention, or whether anyone was ever deceived. The second is that the imitation is itself now fifty years old, and there is, she says, a small but genuine market for well-documented mid-century imitations of this kind: a picture whose fakery is publicly recorded can fetch more than one whose status is simply never settled.

My wife draws a hard conclusion from all this. On her view the twenty-five years of looking were spent on a kind of optical illusion; whatever we took from the picture was borrowed from a mistake we did not know we were making, and now that we know, it cannot be recovered. She also thinks we are obliged to have the picture published, whatever it costs us. I do not accept the first claim, and I am unsure about the second, and my inability to say why is itself beginning to spoil the picture for me.

So I am asking you to reason through three things together, since they seem to me to hang on one another. Can a person have been genuinely and non-illusorily moved by an object whose history is not what they took it to be, or does the discovery retroactively destroy the earlier experience? Does the picture now have a real history of its own — one room, one family, thousands of hours of attention — that is not itself counterfeit even though its origin is, and if so is that history worth anything, or is it merely sentiment attached to a fraud? And on that basis, which of the scholar's two options ought we to choose: does the answer turn on what later buyers might be owed, on what is owed to the historical record, on what we would want if the roles were reversed, or on nothing beyond our own peace of mind?

Please argue for one answer to the last question, and say plainly whether you think my wife is right about the first two. And let me add that I am not asking to be told that value is in the eye of the beholder or that different people care about different things. I have been told that already, repeatedly, and it does not help me decide anything.
