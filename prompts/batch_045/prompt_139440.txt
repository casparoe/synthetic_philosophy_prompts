Hey — me and a friend keep arguing about epistocracy (Brennan, Estlund, the jury theorem stuff, the competence principle and everyone who says it can't be justified to the people it disenfranchises) and we keep reaching for Brexit as an example, and I think we're using it badly. So do it properly.

Facts I'm stipulating, don't re-litigate them: referendum in June 2016, Leave won roughly 52-48; Cameron called it expecting to win it; Remain had near-unanimous backing from economists, the Treasury, the Bank of England, the IMF, most of both main party leaderships; Gove's line about the country having had enough of experts; the NHS-money claim on the side of the Vote Leave bus; the pound dropped but the predicted immediate recession basically didn't show up; then years of not knowing what the vote had actually authorised, including the 2017 Supreme Court ruling that only Parliament could trigger Article 50.

What I want:

First the mapping, done carefully. What was the decision actually — a forecast about consequences, or a choice about who gets to decide? Who would count as the relevant knowers by epistocratic lights, what would have counted as evidence for them, and what does the theory say Cameron and the public faced and should have done? Say plainly where the theory has nothing to say: most of it is built for electing governments, not for one-shot binary referenda.

Then run the case in the other direction, using four things I've been chewing on, and tell me for each whether it's a genuine problem for epistocracy or a cheap shot I should drop:

1. Credential filter versus track-record audit. Do we certify the knowers by their qualifications in advance, or by whether their predictions land afterwards? Here the bodies doing the certifying had just overshot their own short-term forecasts — so which way does "the expert consensus lost anyway" actually cut? Argue both directions before you settle.
2. Cue-taking. Mass democracy's decent-ish epistemic record is largely carried by party cues and trusted elite signals, and this was almost the only British vote in living memory where those cues were scrambled. If so, the referendum disabled the machinery that makes ordinary voting epistemically tolerable, which makes it a terrible test case rather than a verdict.
3. No training data. Leaving a political union is a one-off; expertise is inductive. So competence is thinnest exactly where the stakes are highest, and there's no settled answer to who even counts as an expert here — and the obvious candidates were the same people whose forecasts missed.
4. The reversal test. Weighting by education or a knowledge quiz would probably have flipped the result, and part of Leave was a revolt against epistocratic enclaves that already existed: a supranational court, an independent central bank, judges telling Parliament what it may do. So maybe the episode is about whether epistocracy is dynamically stable, not whether it's justified. And honestly: does anyone's enthusiasm survive an epistocracy that reliably produces outcomes they hate?

Finally: if Britain had to do this again, what instrument would you actually use — a citizens' assembly, a two-question ballot that separates what you want from what you think will happen, a deliberative poll? Say which, and say whether it is epistocratic, democratic, or something neither label fits. I want something I can argue back against, not a tidy summary. And no citations or reading lists, I can't chase them up anyway.
