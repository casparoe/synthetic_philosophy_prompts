I work as the clinical lead of a forensic assertive outreach team in a large city, and I need help turning an idea I keep half-using into something my staff can actually run. Every week we hold a short case round — about fifteen minutes per service user, eight staff in the room, most of them with no training in philosophy whatsoever — in which we decide, for each person on our caseload, which of two stances we are going to take toward them for the coming month: a moral-protest stance, in which we treat them as someone we can properly resent, reproach, forgive, or make demands of, and whose conduct we describe to them in those terms; or a management stance, in which we treat their behaviour as something to be treated, managed, compensated for, or controlled, and deliberately drop the language of blame. The decision gets written into the file in plain language, it has to be defensible to a mental health tribunal and to the person's family, and it is reviewed monthly by our consultant psychiatrist, who signs off on it. At the moment the decisions are made by feel, and they drift: staff who like a service user manage them, staff who find a service user frightening moralise at them, and we have no way of catching the drift.

What I want is a decision procedure, in the order the questions should be asked, built on the distinction that P. F. Strawson draws in his essay on freedom and resentment between the participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, together with his distinction between considerations that excuse and considerations that exempt — as I understand it, an excuse leaves the person inside the moral community but denies that this particular act calls for resentment, because he did not mean it, or could not help it, or had no acceptable alternative, whereas an exemption takes the person out of the scope of moral address altogether for as long as it lasts. I would like the procedure to preserve that second distinction, since I think it is the one we get wrong most often, and I would also like you to tell me which parts of Strawson's apparatus I should deliberately drop because they will not survive translation into a fifteen-minute conversation among staff who will never read him. Please mark explicitly where a question is answered no and the procedure simply stops, where the answer is not a fact but a value judgment that has to be handed to a person rather than settled by the test, and what should count as a sufficient answer to each question when we do not have the information we would like. One thing I have been told about Strawson is that his target was the worry that determinism might make our practices of blame incoherent, and that his answer is that the worry is idle because the practices are not answerable to that question; if that is right, I want the procedure to say so, because our staff ask exactly that question in every single case — was he really able to do otherwise — and I suspect the honest answer is that the procedure has nothing to say about it and should tell them to move on.

I would like you to apply the finished procedure to three of our actual recurring situations. The first is a man with bipolar disorder who, during a hypomanic episode, ran up serious debts and made threats to his mother; he is now well, he is ashamed, and his mother insists that we hold him to what he said rather than write it off as his illness. The second is a woman with a long-standing diagnosis of emotionally unstable personality disorder who has repeatedly broken the commitments in her care plan over several years; my team has got into the habit of managing her rather than reproaching her, and I am fairly sure that if your procedure is faithful to Strawson it will tell me that this is a withdrawal of moral address that we have no business making, and that we have been doing her an injury while congratulating ourselves on being kind — if that is what it says, please say it plainly, because I will not enjoy hearing it but I would rather hear it from the procedure than from a coroner. The third is a man who invokes his own diagnosis and asks us, in these words, to stop holding him to his promises because it was not him, it was the illness; here I want to know what the procedure does when the exemption the person claims is not one the staff can simply switch on, and where his own self-reactive attitudes, and our resentment, belong in the answer. Please also tell me, at the end, what such a procedure cannot do — which of our questions it leaves exactly as unanswered as they were before. I would be grateful if you could write your reply as continuous prose, the way you would write a careful email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
