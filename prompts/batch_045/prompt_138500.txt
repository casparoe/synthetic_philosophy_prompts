my old colleague has a podcast where he does big ideas for managers, and the other week he did an episode on the tragedy of the commons. I need to know how much of it to trust, because im on the committee of our housing co-op and we are currently having exactly this argument. His version went roughly like this:

"Hardin's argument is pretty simple. Picture a pasture open to everyone. Each herder keeps the whole benefit of adding one more cow but shares the cost of the extra grazing with everybody else, so adding cows is rational for each and fatal for all — that's the tragedy. The moral is that any resource that isn't privately owned ends up wrecked, which leaves you two options: carve it into private property, or hand it to the state to police with quotas and fines. Then Elinor Ostrom came along and showed that was too pessimistic. She spent decades collecting real cases — alpine meadows, irrigation systems in Spain and Nepal — where ordinary people ran a shared resource for centuries without destroying it, by making their own rules and keeping an eye on each other. So the real lesson is that communities can self-organise, and Hardin's model is an artificial setup where the herders aren't allowed to talk or make rules. And under the hood the tragedy of the commons is the same problem as public goods: everyone free-rides, whether it's overgrazing a field or refusing to chip in for the lighthouse."

Our actual situation, which is why I care: about 40 households share a borehole and an electric pump. Everyone pays an equal flat share of the electricity, so nobody pays per litre — your marginal litre is free. There's a meter on the pump and a shared spreadsheet logging each household's litres, updated monthly, so monitoring is basically free and everyone can see everyone. The households with big gardens use maybe three times as much; they're also the only ones who could afford their own borehole, and two have started looking into it. The aquifer has a nasty feature: if the water table drops past a certain point we'd have to replace the pump with a deeper one, and the deeper pump costs more than the whole co-op could raise, so it's a cliff rather than a slope. And separately, nobody will pay into the maintenance fund or show up to work parties.

Two attempts at rules — a watering rota, then a no-watering-between-10-and-4 rule — both quietly died, not because people didn't know who was breaking them but because nobody wanted to be the one to tell their neighbour off.

Can you go through his summary and tell me what someone who actually works on this stuff would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? And does his conclusion follow from the bits that are right? Then: does our case even sit inside the "communities self-organise" box, or are we one of the setups he waves away? Does it matter that the heaviest users are the ones with an exit? Does free-and-useless monitoring break the mechanism he's pointing at? Is the cliff the same dilemma or a different one? And is people not paying into the maintenance fund the same problem as people over-pumping, or a different animal needing a different fix? I want to believe the third-way bit because it flatters how we like to think of ourselves, so tell me if I'm being flattered. Also the lighthouse thing — I have a vague memory that's a famously bad example, but I can't remember why, or if I made that up.
