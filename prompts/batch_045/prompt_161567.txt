You are a philosopher of mind who also knows the vision and attention literature well. Before you write anything, spend some time brainstorming: try to formulate a principle, test it against every case below, notice where it strains, and only then draft your answer. I want a considered verdict, not a first pass, and I want you to be explicit about where your own recommendation forces me to change my mind.

I am trying to work out what I actually believe about cases where the timing or the content of a conscious experience looks underdetermined by what is going on in the brain, and I want to know whether my reactions add up to one principle. Here they are, given as gut verdicts:

1. Color phi: a red dot is flashed at one location, then a green dot at a nearby location about a hundred milliseconds later, and viewers report a single dot travelling between them and turning green partway along. I say: yes, that is genuinely experienced, not a guess made afterward — but no, there is no fact of the matter about when in that interval the green entered consciousness. Whether the color change was felt before the second dot was processed strikes me as an empty question.
2. Metacontrast masking: a target disk is followed so quickly by a surrounding mask that viewers deny seeing the disk, though forced-choice guessing sits slightly above chance. I say: no conscious experience of the disk at all — this is not an experience that was later erased.
3. Blindsight: a patient with damaged striate cortex discriminates stimuli in the blind field above chance when made to guess and denies seeing anything. I say: no conscious visual experience of those stimuli, though I will allow they consciously feel something like a hunch.
4. The blind spot: the receptor-free region is filled in, so the field looks unbroken. Here I go the other way from case 1 — I do not think anyone consciously sees the filled-in region, and I think there is no fact of the matter about what it looks like there.
5. A dual-response case I invented: under masking, a subject says aloud "I saw nothing" while, at the same time, a manual forced-choice response and an anticipatory eye movement both indicate the target's location. I say: there is no univocal answer to "did they see it" until you say which probing channel you mean.

Cases 1 and 4 are the pair that worries me: in both, the visual system supplies content that was never in the stimulus, and I call one conscious and the other not. What principle about the conditions under which a content becomes conscious — and about whether there is a determinate time at which it does so — captures this pattern? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should therefore give up. I do not want a survey of the Cartesian theater or the multiple drafts picture; I want to know whether my own verdicts commit me to one of them or to something else. Finally, apply your principle to two cases I have not settled: the tactile analogue of phi, in which taps delivered in sequence at the wrist and then near the elbow are felt as a series of taps hopping up the arm; and a dreamer woken after a few minutes who reports a dream that seemed to unfold over an hour. Is there a fact of the matter, in either case, about when the intermediate events were consciously experienced?
