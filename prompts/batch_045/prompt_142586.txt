Please take the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in aesthetics and the philosophy of art, and who is in the habit of reconstructing an author's argument as charitably as possible before criticizing it. Think carefully, and do the following before you write anything you intend me to read: in a few sentences of your own notes, list every distinct condition that Tolstoy, in What Is Art?, builds into his account of genuine art, and then check this case against each one separately. Then give me your answer.

Here is the question. As I understand Tolstoy's position, he replaces all the usual aesthetic apparatus with one clean, operational criterion: a work is good art to the degree that it infects its audience with feeling, the more widely and strongly the better. That reading has a consequence my reading group keeps circling back to, and I would like it settled.

The case: a lab I follow ran a public screening last year of a short film assembled end to end by a generative video system. The script, the shots, the score and the edit were all chosen by an automated search over a huge space of variants; the only human contribution was a caption file. So there is no person who had any particular feeling to convey, and no one who could be said to have been sincere or insincere in making it. Yet the response was remarkable and, by the measures the lab used, unusually consistent: viewers across very different backgrounds reported a felt closeness to strangers and a settled intention to act more kindly toward them, and the effect persisted in follow-up interviews. On my reading this is precisely the sort of feeling that the highest art, for Tolstoy, is supposed to produce, and this film produces it more dependably than almost any celebrated novel of the last century.

So: does Tolstoy's theory, taken on its own terms, have to rank this film above the plain moral fictions he admires? If it does not, is he simply smuggling in a second criterion to protect his own taste? And is the real lesson here that a purely audience-side test of art cannot answer any question about what art is for? Where, if anywhere, has my reconstruction gone wrong?

Please answer in British English, in no more than 350 words, and without any bibliography, citations, or appeals to secondary sources; I want your own analysis of the theory and the case.
