I'm writing up my position on cooperation for a reading group in philosophy of biology, and before I present it I want someone to check whether the package actually holds together. Here is where I think I stand:

(1) In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger — including a stranger I have good reason to think reasons about the situation roughly as I do, as long as our two choices are causally independent of one another — defection is the uniquely rational act. The dominance argument is decisive, and I don't think indirect or "be the sort of person who..." moves change that.

(2) In an iterated prisoner's dilemma of known, finite length, I reject the backward-induction conclusion. I'd cooperate for most of the game, and I treat the unraveling argument as telling us that something in the standard setup is wrong, not as advice about what to do.

(3) The main evolutionary explanation of cooperation among non-kin is reciprocity: direct reciprocity first, indirect reciprocity through reputation second. And I want this to be real altruism in the following sense — the cooperative act leaves the actor worse off, and the correct explanation of why it happens is not that the actor is pursuing its own long-run interest.

(4) In the indefinitely repeated game, the folk theorems mean the structure of the game by itself can't tell us which equilibrium will get played; that is an equilibrium-selection question, and the evidence that does the work there — the early computer tournaments, evolutionary simulations — is empirical rather than a matter of the solution concept.

(5) In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two agents who know they are near-copies of each other, cooperation is rational for each: if I defect, so does she, and that reasoning is sound in that case.

(6) All of this bears on why we have the moral intuitions we have — about fairness, desert, cheat detection — but on whether those intuitions are correct it has nothing to say.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me. I suspect (1) and (5) are the live pair, but I'm not confident I've located the real problem, and I don't know which commitment I'd give up. Rank the tensions by severity, say which single revision would clean up the most, and tell me if any part of the trouble is only about words. Finally, mark clearly which of your claims are settled as far as people who work on this are concerned and which are genuinely contested — I don't want to present a contested position as the consensus.
