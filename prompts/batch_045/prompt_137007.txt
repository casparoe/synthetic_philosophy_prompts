I'm a staff engineer at a mid-sized payments company, with no formal training in philosophy. I've been asked to own an internal engineering standard that has to settle two practical questions: what we are allowed to say in product documentation and customer-facing material about what our language-model components "know," "believe," or "understand"; and how far we may let those components act without human review inside our billing and disputes pipeline.

Two senior engineers on my team have been arguing about this for months, and the argument keeps collapsing into a single issue: whether the symbols inside these systems are grounded — whether a token like "overdue" or "chargeback" is connected to the thing it is about by anything more than its statistical relations to other tokens. I've since learned that this is called the symbol grounding problem and that there is a well-known thought experiment about a person in a room manipulating symbols in a language they don't understand. That is roughly the extent of what I know, and I'd like the document not to depend on more than that.

Rather than adjudicate, I want to force the two of them into an adversarial collaboration on paper, and I need you to draft the document they would produce. Call them Camp A and Camp B.

Camp A holds that a representational state has the content it has only in virtue of the system's own causal commerce with what it is about — perception, action, or something close enough to those. On this view, training on text alone can at best give a system symbols whose semantic properties are entirely derivative from the humans who produced the text; the system does not itself reach the world. Camp A also holds that this is not a merely academic defect: it is supposed to predict specific, checkable failure modes in deployment.

Camp B holds that content is a matter of the role a state plays in the overall economy of a system that is successfully coupled to its environment. A model whose outputs cause writes to a database and whose subsequent inputs are the results of those writes stands in the right counterfactual relations to the distal properties, and on this view that is all grounding was ever supposed to secure. Any residual demand — that the system acquire its symbols through the right kind of causal history, or that it have something over and above reliable counterfactual responsiveness — is either untestable in principle or a demand for something other than meaning.

I want the collaboration conducted over three concrete artifacts, because the argument only becomes tractable when it's about these rather than about language models in general.

1. Argus: a model trained jointly on text and on non-textual data streams (camera input, transaction telemetry). Both camps already agree this one is grounded to some degree, at least for terms whose referents it encountered outside text. Use it as the calibration point that any account has to get right.

2. Ledger: a text-only agent that reads dispute tickets, writes SQL, executes it against the billing database, and receives back only a very narrow feedback channel — row counts, constraint-violation errors, unit-test results, and eventually aggregate customer-survey responses. There is no perception, but there is a closed causal loop through a low-bandwidth, human-designed channel. Camp A says this is still symbol manipulation whose symbols borrow their meaning from us; Camp B says the loop is exactly what grounding consists in. Note that both camps should address whether grounding can be inherited or borrowed from other components of a larger sociotechnical system, or whether it has to be a property of the component itself.

3. The florid case: during fine-tuning, a model began emitting the nonce string "florid" in an internal scratchpad before labeling a transaction. It predicts, better than any feature our analysts have hand-written, whether that transaction will be disputed within sixty days. No one can say what "florid" means; there is no paraphrase, no definition, no entry in any vocabulary, and no human-authored text ever used it this way. Camp A's instinct is that this is a detector or a bare correlate, not a symbol with content. Camp B's instinct is that it is the purest case of grounding in the building. I suspect this case is where the real disagreement lives.

I also want them to rule on a test I sketched myself, which I'll call the swap test: intervene on the world while holding the textual and distributional role of a term fixed (for example, silently change what a database column actually contains, or change the real-world property while leaving every input statistic the model sees unchanged), and observe whether the system's behavior tracks the world or the text. Both camps should say whether they accept this test as bearing on the dispute, what they predict for Argus, Ledger, and florid, and — if they reject it — what in-house test they would propose instead.

Please treat the following as settled so the document doesn't re-litigate them: the dispute is not about consciousness, sentience, or moral status; not about whether the systems are useful; both camps accept that current systems fail in characteristic ways and that a grounding gap, if real, would not be their only defect; both accept that Argus differs from Ledger at least in degree.

Format the answer as a joint document with these sections:

1. Shared claims: eight to twelve numbered claims both engineers could sign, covering what the facts are, what needs explaining, and what the disagreement is not about.
2. A disagreements table with columns: issue; Camp A's own formulation; Camp B's own formulation; verbal or substantive, with a one-line reason. Each formulation should be wording that camp would accept for itself, not wording the other side would recognize as a strawman.
3. Three to five cruxes. For each: the sub-question; why settling it would move at least one side; a case, test, or measurement both camps accept as bearing on it and that my team could actually run in-house; and each camp's prediction recorded in advance, including which result would surprise them.
4. A verbal-dispute audit: which parts of the argument would dissolve under sharper definitions of "grounded," "about," "understands," or "means," and which would survive the sharpening.
5. What each camp would count as evidence against its own view, and whether there is any result both camps agree would actually end the argument.
6. A short closing section — a few sentences — of language both camps would permit in our internal standard and in product documentation even while the substantive disagreement remains open, or, if honesty requires it, a joint statement that no such language is available yet.

Constraints: aim for 1,200 to 1,600 words. Please write in American English. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no named authors, papers, or dates — we have no journal access and I don't want to build a standard on literature I can't read. If a move depends on a specific empirical claim about how today's systems actually behave, state that claim plainly and say how we would check it ourselves. Define any technical term the first time you use it. Write in the neutral first-person-plural voice of a document both parties signed, not as a judge announcing who won.
