Medical and public-health institutions sort the causes of disease into two kinds, and the sorting dictates the response: when a condition is called genetic, the response is individual — polygenic risk scores, screening, counseling, targeted drugs — and when it is called environmental, the response is collective — regulation, bans, litigation against whoever introduced the hazard. The same institutions will also report that a trait is, say, 80% heritable and, without apparent tension, that it has moved enormously within a few generations because of diet or schooling; they will classify a condition as genetic and then treat it by changing what the patient eats, and classify a condition as environmental while still managing it one patient at a time.

What notion of a cause does this sorting presuppose? I can see at least three candidates for what 'genetic' might be doing in it: a difference-maker among the variations actually present in a population, so that a hazard to which everyone in the population is exposed — lead in the single shared water supply — counts as causing none of the disease associated with it, because it explains none of the variance; a link in the process that actually produced this patient's condition; or a cause that is especially difficult to intervene on. Do these three genuinely come apart, and which of them does the practice strictly require, as opposed to merely sit well with? Is the practice's own pair of routines — heritable conditions routed to environmental treatment, environmentally produced conditions routed to individual risk management — coherent under the notion it seems to use? And what would institutional response look like if 'genetic' were understood in the intervention-based way instead: would anything still be handled one person at a time, and would the genetic/environmental split still earn the work it currently does?
