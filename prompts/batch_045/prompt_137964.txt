I am one of the two student representatives on my school's access fund committee, and I would like help turning a comparison we have to make roughly forty times a year into a procedure we can actually run.

The fund is about three thousand dollars a term from a small endowment, given out in amounts between fifty and four hundred dollars to students who would otherwise miss out on something at school: a trip deposit, a calculator, a music exam fee, winter boots, a bus pass. We meet for ninety minutes twice a term with the staff chair, our business manager, who signs off on everything but does not read the applications beforehand. Applicants write one short paragraph saying what they need and why it matters. We decide who gets what, write one sentence of explanation we would be willing to show the applicant, and are expected to be consistent with what previous committees did. The two of us should reach the same answer from the same file, because next year two different students will be doing this job. In practice we have about four minutes per request.

The problem is that we are comparing how much different people would benefit, and the paragraphs are written on private scales. We tried asking applicants to rate how much the money would matter to them out of ten and nearly everyone wrote nine or ten. We then tried giving everyone the same set of short written scenarios and asking them to place themselves relative to those instead, to force the scales onto a common axis; that failed differently, because applicants describe themselves relative to the scenarios in idiosyncratic ways and because once they realised the anchors mattered, they started writing themselves into them.

The other thing I have been experimenting with, and cannot make work, is a direct trade question on the shortlist. For each pair I ask: would you rather be given the other student's grant and their problem, or your own? And then a follow-up: if you were given the other student's grant and their problem, how much of the fund would we have to add on top for you to be indifferent? The numbers come back inconsistent in a way I do not know how to handle. Amara says she would need forty dollars added to Dev's package; Dev says he would need ninety added to Amara's. Both numbers cannot be honoured. With three students we get cycles: Priya would take Amara's package plus fifty, Amara would take Dev's package plus thirty, Dev would take Priya's package plus twenty, and breaking the cycle costs money we do not have.

What I want is a written procedure: the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, what to do when a question cannot be answered from the file, where the procedure stops and returns a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be run by a sixteen-year-old with no economics training, on a single paragraph of evidence, in a few minutes, and it has to be robust to the fact that applicants will learn how it works. It should be explicit about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. It should also say when partial funding is the right output and when a request is lumpy enough that part of the money is wasted.

Three distinctions I think have to survive the simplification, and I would like you to disagree if you think otherwise. First, comparing how well off two applicants are to begin with is not the same as comparing how much each would gain, and we have partial evidence for the first because the school knows who is on a fee waiver, but almost none for the second. Second, two hundred dollars is not the same thing to a student on a fee waiver as to one whose parents are just above the threshold, so using dollars as the common yardstick may already be a mistake. Third, self-reported intensity systematically favours the articulate, the ambitious, and the student whose background makes stating your deserts normal, and it penalises the student who has quietly adjusted her expectations downward or the one who is easy to please and asks for sixty dollars.

Please run the finished procedure on these, from this round's shortlist, with eleven hundred dollars left to allocate:

Amara, on a fee waiver, one hundred and twenty dollars for a winter coat and boots, written plainly, walks twenty-five minutes each way.
Dev, not on a waiver, four hundred dollars towards a nine-hundred-dollar national music exam fee, by far the most persuasive paragraph, says stopping now wastes six years of lessons.
Priya, just above the waiver threshold, sixty dollars for a bus pass, two lines, has been walking.
Sam, two hundred and fifty dollars for a gaming headset to join the school esports club, which he describes as the only place at school where he has friends.
A request submitted by a counsellor on a student's behalf, three hundred dollars, with the student's permission withheld and no details disclosed to us at all.

I expect the honest application of this will tell me to fund Dev's exam ahead of Amara's boots, and I do not think I could defend that to the school. If the procedure really does say that, I want to see exactly which step produces it and whether that step is one I am entitled to reject. I also want to know what to do with the counsellor's request, where there is no comparison to make: whether a fixed priority, an equal split, or a lottery is the defensible answer when the evidence runs out, and why.

Finally, please say plainly what the procedure cannot do, and what one line we should record per decision so that next year's representatives, who will not know any of this, can reconstruct why we chose as we did.
