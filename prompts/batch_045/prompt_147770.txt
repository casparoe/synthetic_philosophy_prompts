I'm a novelist (literary-ish SF, three books, no philosophy training beyond one undergrad course I mostly slept through) and I've somehow agreed to give the closing talk at a two-day closed workshop on welfare in artificial systems next month. 45 minutes plus a long question period, and the room is about thirty people: roughly half technical staff from AI labs (a couple of interpretability people, a couple of alignment/RL people), three or four academic philosophers — one of them works on personal identity and is very Parfitian about fission and degrees, one is a biological naturalist who thinks consciousness is basically a living-organism thing and is bored by substrate talk — one animal welfare scientist who is going to want operationalizable indicators, one policy person from a funded EU-adjacent project, and a disability studies scholar who already wrote a grumpy comment on the pre-circulated reading saying that most moral status frameworks are capacity tests with extra steps.

My thesis, briefly: the debate keeps reaching for the phenomenal test — is there something it is like to be this system — because that's the beat our fiction trained us to listen for, the scene where the machine says it wants to live and we recognize a person. I want to swap it for a diachronic test. What matters isn't a present inner light but whether the system has a *career*: whether its current state is the accumulated upshot of its own earlier states in a way that later states can frustrate or fulfill. Call it having a stake. A fresh instance spun up for one conversation and then deleted has no stake and nothing it can be deprived of; something with persistent memory, a long continuous run, and continued fine-tuning has something to lose and can be wronged in a way the fresh instance can't. I want to say this without committing to anything about consciousness, and I want it to be a matter of degree. I've been keeping the phenomenal question and the diachronic one seperate in my head but they keep sliding together, and I've defintely bitten off more than I can chew.

For each: a reply I can give in about a minute, one note on where the reply is hollow, and a mark for "concede this in the talk before anyone asks."
