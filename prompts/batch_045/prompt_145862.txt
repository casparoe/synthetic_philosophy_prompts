im writing a response paper for a history of economic thought seminar and ive backed myself into a corner, so i need somebody to build the strongest possible case for the reading i want to defend, not a balanced survey of it.

The thesis is roughly this: Hume's own account of how new money actually works hands the mercantilists a real argument, and his polemic against them survives more by luck than by right.

Stuff im taking as given, paraphrased loosely, no citations needed: for Hume money is not one of the objects of trade but the instrument that oils the wheels of exchange; in the long run the quantity of money just sets the price level, and a country that somehow accumulates more coin than its share will find its prices bid up, lose sales to its neighbours, and bleed the surplus back out again until everything is in line; and — this is the part i care about — in the interval between the new money arriving and prices fully catching up, the extra coin passes through more hands and rouses people to greater industry, which he treats as real but temporary.

Heres the distinction i want to inject, because i dont think ive seen it drawn this way. Call the temporary bit the pass-through effect: extra demand while prices lag, gone once prices adjust. Call whatever durable residue it leaves the ratchet effect: skills learned, workshops built, credit relations formed, and above all habits of industry acquired by people who previously spent half their time idle. Those persist after the coin has drained away, so the country ends up permanently more productive at the same money stock and lower prices. My claim is that Hume is definately committed to a genuine ratchet effect, and not by accident — the persistence of acquired habit is one of his central commitments about human beings generally, so he cant treat industry as something that switches off the moment the stimulus does.

So please argue the mercantilist-friendly conclusion: if each increment of new money leaves a durable increment of real capacity behind, then a policy aimed at keeping the balance of trade continuously favourable — note, at a flow, not at hoarding a stock of coin — has a real justification, and Hume's mockery of that policy requires the ratchet to collapse into pass-through, which his own psychology of habit forbids. Set it up concretely with a stylised case: a poor country with lots of half-idle labour, artisans who work six months a year because demand is thin, and a sustained inflow of specie. Track two decades, including the second round where the now-more-productive country is bleeding money back out and competing more cheaply than before.

Then the hard part. Give the two or three best Humean replies and show why they only partly work. One will be about slack: maybe the ratchet bites only while there are idle hands and runs into diminishing returns. One will be about timing: the drain may operate much faster than habit-formation, so no policy can keep the country reliably on the good foot. A third might be that the apparatus needed to force a surplus — bounties, prohibitions, the whole machinery — costs more in misallocation and lost liberty than the ratchet gains are worth, which would make Hume's real objection political rather than economic. Say whether that third one just shrinks my thesis into something weaker but still worth having. And tell me honestly where the argument is weakest. I want the argument itself, so please dont give me a reading list or point me at secondary literature.
