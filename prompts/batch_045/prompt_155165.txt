Lawyer here, been reading my way into the philosophy of punishment on my own for a while (not a philosopher, so sorry if this is half-formed). The thing I cant get a handle on is that desert seems adjustable in only one direction. Give an offender a worse childhood, more coercion in their situation, fewer real alternatives, and everyone agrees the sentence should come down. But there's no matching move up: the person who had every alternative open to them and did it anyway doesn't seem to deserve *more* than the standard sentence for that crime, just the standard sentence. So "what this crime deserves" starts to look like a ceiling that gets discounted rather than a genuine midpoint, and I can't tell whether that's a deep fact about culpability or just an artifact of us being able to see deprivation but not privilege. It gets worse when I combine it with the retributivist who also wants the state to investigate offenders properly before sentencing: better information then reliably pushes sentences down, so a retributive system that keeps getting better at finding out the truth starts drifting toward punishing almost nobody.

Does this have a name? It's somewhere near moral luck, but the luck here all runs one way, toward making the agent less culpable; and it's near the business about desert being a cap rather than a mandate, but that seems to be a claim about whether the state must punish, not about the shape of the scale underneath. I don't want a label dropped on me — I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where it stops. Same phenomenon, or a different one, when the discount comes from what the offender did after the crime rather than from their background?
