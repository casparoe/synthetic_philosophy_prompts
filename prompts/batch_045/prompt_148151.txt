I work in a bike shop, and me and four friends have a pub reading group that meets every other Tuesday. We've been stuck on the same thing for about three months now. I get why panpsychism is tempting: physics tells you what stuff *does*, not what it is like from the inside, so if the inside of an electron is a tiny bit experiential you never have to explain how experience shows up later out of something totally non-experiential. Fine. What I dont get is the step from there to me. Right now I am having one experience of a red mug, some traffic noise, and a sore shoulder, all at once, all belonging to the same someone. How do billions of tiny experiences add up to that? I've been calling it the adding-up problem in my notes; I gather other people call it the combination problem. Whatever.

I've been splitting it into three complaints, because they feel different: the who-complaint (how do many little subjects make one subject instead of just a crowd of them?), the what-complaint (where does the red-ness of my red come from, if the little experiences only have their own little qualities?), and the shape-complaint (why does the structure of the combined thing line up with the structure of the brain at all?). Maybe those arent really three separate problems — tell me if I'm cutting it wrong.

My pet idea, for what its worth, I call the committee model. Think of a company: it can sign a contract and get sued, its decisions are real, it has a bank account, and yet there is no extra person sitting in head office who *is* the company. The employees are real in one way, the company is real in a thinner way, and nobody had to merge into anybody. So maybe a "me" is what the committee of micro-experiences does, not one more member of it. The bit I can't make work: when I have a headache, whose headache is it? The committee's? And if the committee is deciding, does somebody have to *feel* the deciding — because that just sounds like one more member again.

Anyway. What I want from you is a handout for the group: a taxonomy of the ways people have answered the adding-up problem. Not a quiz — I want a tree where every branch is a plain question someone in the pub could answer without knowing any of the labels ("does the big experience exist over and above the little ones, yes or no?"), and every leaf is a position. Please build it from the questions first and then drop the positions into the space, rather than starting from a list of -isms I could look up. Use these as branch questions wherever they fit, in whatever order makes the tree cleanest: the split-brain case (sever the connection between the hemispheres — now how many subjects?), the slow replacement case (swap one neuron at a time for a chip that does the same job — does the experience fade gradually or wink out at some point?), the stadium case (fifty thousand people each holding up one coloured card — does the stadium see a picture?), and the anaesthesia case (I go under, the big experience stops, but the little stuff presumably hasn't gone anywhere).

Leaves I want on there somewhere, under their usual names if they have them: straight micro-level panpsychism where the big experience is built out of the little ones; the version where the little subjects actually fuse or get bonded into one; the version where they don't fuse but the big experience is a further brute fact that pops out; the version where the fundamental properties are only proto-experiential and combine into real experience; the cosmos-first version, where the one big subject is basic and the little guys are the problem instead; the version where the micro properties are experiential but the macro facts are grounded rather than composed; the illusionist exit; and the drop-the-subjects exit, where there are experiences but nobody having them. If a slot is genuinely empty in the literature, write "unnamed" rather than inventing a name. Eight to twelve leaves, dont go over.

Also: for each leaf, one sentence on the most awkward thing it's stuck with. Then the combinations of answers your tree allows that nobody actually holds — I want at least three, each with a word on why the seat is empty (is it incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unoccupied because nobody bothered?). Then any two leaves your tree can't actually tell apart: say so instead of pretending the questions separate them. And tell me where my committee model lands — if it collapses into a leaf you already have, say which one and what I've missed; if it really is its own leaf, give it a name and say what it still owes us an explanation of.

No author names, no papers, no further reading — I can't check any of that and I don't care who said it first. Keep the jargon in a glossary at the top; the rest should be readable out loud over a pint.

Sections in this order, please: "Plain-words glossary" (about 120 words); "The tree" (600–700 words, the main event — indentation, arrows, whatever reads best as plain text); "What each leaf is stuck with" (about 250 words); "Empty seats" (about 250 words); "Where the tree gives out" (about 180 words); "Where my committee idea lands" (about 250 words).
