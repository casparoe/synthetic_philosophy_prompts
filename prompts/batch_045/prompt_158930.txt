I run a monthly philosophy discussion group at the library — about a dozen regulars, mostly adults who never studied the subject, one retired programmer who thinks in terms of loops and stack traces, and one guy who took a logic course back in the eigthies and keeps half-remembering just enough to be dangerous. I stupidly agreed to do 90 minutes on the liar paradox next month, and what I actually want to get across is why it's a problem for any account of truth at all and not just a cute riddle about self-reference. So please don't explain the paradox to me — I've read enough to be dangerous myself — plan how I should explain it to them. What has to be on the whiteboard in what order, and why that order (what genuinely depends on what)? We don't do handouts or assigned reading, so everything has to come out of what I write on the board and examples they already accept.

The thing I keep going back and forth on is the example. I've been playing with a three-sentence version written on the board: S1 says "S2 is not true", S2 says "S3 is not true", S3 says "S1 is not true". I like it because no sentence mentions itself, so the "oh it's just self-reference, ban that and we're done" move dies immediately, and because it's completely symmetric, so nobody can point at one line and say *that's* the broken one. But I'm not sure it should carry the whole session, or whether the plain one-line "this sentence is not true" has to come first and the three-sentence cycle be the escalation. Tell me which example should carry the thing and exactly what it needs to have and to lack: no empirical content (so nobody says "it's just contingent"), no indexicals maybe, no vague words, whatever else. Also, where in the sequence do I nail down the difference between "false" and "not true"? I have a feeling that distinction is load-bearing for the whole payoff and that if it's not in place early the last twenty minutes will collapse.

Misconceptions I'm expecting, in roughly the order I think they'll arrive: "just forbid self-reference" (the cycle is meant to kill that, but is that the right moment?); "it's meaningless, so it has no truth value" — and then one of them will definately say "if it's neither true nor false then it isn't true, so it *is* true", and I don't know whether to defuse that early or save it as the finale; "just call it false and move on"; "language is vague, this is like the sorites"; the programmer will say it's an infinite loop and therefore an artifact of how the sentences are wired rather than a real problem; and the logic-course guy will produce some hierarchy-of-languages fix, at which point I want to be able to say in one sentence why that's expensive for a language like English where we happily say "everything Einstein ever said is true" without indexing anything. For each of those: where exactly in my sequence do I head it off, and what's the one line that does it? And what should I openly refuse to get into at this level and just say so out loud? Finally, give me one question to put to the room in the last five minutes that would tell me whether the idea landed rather than whether they enjoyed it — I was toying with writing up a four-sentence version of the cycle instead of three and asking which lines are true, but if that's a bad check tell me why and give me a better one.
