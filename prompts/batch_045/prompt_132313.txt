I am the acquiring editor for a monograph, *Exploitation after Automation*, by a Marxist political economist who argues from cases rather than from exegesis. Six of the seven chapters are delivered; each ends with a ruling on two questions about the worker in question: is this person exploited, and is this person alienated. Chapter seven exists only as an outline. Before I commission it I need to know whether her verdicts so far are running one rule, and what that rule would say about the case she has sketched.

Her ledger, with her reasons compressed:

1. A rideshare driver who owns her car, pays a per-trip commission, sets her own hours, and has no comparable alternative. Exploited: yes. Alienated: yes — the routing and the rating system direct her work and judge it.
2. A well-paid staff engineer with real discretion over method and design and no claim on the codebase she writes. Exploited: yes. Alienated: no — she recognizes her own judgment in what she makes.
3. A self-employed potter who owns her wheel and kiln and chooses her own designs for a competitive market. Exploited: no. Alienated: no.
4. A newsletter writer who sells her entire archive and her pen name to a platform for a lump sum at full market value; the platform then hires ghostwriters to go on publishing under her name. Exploited: no — she was paid for all of it. Alienated: yes.
5. An unpaid open-source contributor whose library is vendored into a commercial product under a permissive license she chose herself. Exploited: yes. Alienated: no.
6. A member of a worker-owned data-labelling co-op that contracts with AI labs; the buyers write the labelling protocol, the co-op keeps the surplus. Exploited: no. Alienated: yes.

Note that cases 2 and 5 are exploited but not alienated, and cases 4 and 6 are alienated but not exploited.

The outline for chapter seven: a hospital radiologist in a public system, covered by a strong union contract, paid a large premium with informed consent to have her diagnostic reasoning recorded — think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking, final reads — and distilled into a model. The model is owned by a consortium in which her union holds a minority stake and receives a share of licensing fees. Deployment begins as a second reader and extends to clinics with no radiologist on site; within three years the number of consultant posts falls and the fee per read falls, though licensing revenue offsets part of the loss. She remains the best-paid member of her department and says she would take the same deal again.

I would like: her likely verdicts on each of the two questions, with the reason; the smallest set of rules each of which fits all six delivered cases, stated precisely enough that their predictions about chapter seven differ — I suspect one is a surplus-labour rule and another a property-relations rule of the withdrawal-test kind, and I cannot rule out a third on which the union's minority stake makes the workers collectively self-exploiting; which of those rules she is actually running, and why; the minimal variant of the radiologist case, or a further case, that would separate the surviving rules; and whether her usage really treats exploitation and alienation as independent, or whether one of the rules you extract collapses the second into the first and so forces a reinterpretation of one of her six verdicts. If you think the ledger is already inconsistent, say which verdict she would have to give up.
