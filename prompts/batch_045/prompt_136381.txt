Please explain what is really at stake in the Buridan's ass case: an agent facing two options it judges equally good, and therefore, apparently, having no reason to choose either. My instinct as a software engineer is that this is a non-problem — if two candidate actions score identically, you add a tie-break rule, deterministic or randomized, and move on. I get the sense that this answer is meant to be unsatisfying, and I want to understand precisely why.

Here is the variant that makes me doubt it. Suppose the ass has a genuine but weak second-order reason not to keep deliberating: the hay is slowly drying out, so every additional minute of dithering has a small cost. Now it has a reason to break the tie somehow, but still no reason to break it leftward rather than rightward. Say it flips a coin and walks left. What, if anything, is still missing for this to count as the ass acting, rather than as something that merely happens to it? And is the answer any different if there is no coin — it simply picked, without a reason, and went left?

Two follow-ups I care about. First, does the tie-break move work equally well when the options are not tied in value but not ranked at all — two careers, say, where the agent cannot compare them, as opposed to comparing them and finding them equal? I suspect the trick is doing different work in the two cases, or only succeeds in one of them. Second, is the real lesson here about the nature of reasons, about the limits of deliberation, or about what intending something adds over merely wanting it? I am interested in what the puzzle is supposed to be evidence for, not in how to get the donkey fed.
