I'm running a small reading group on decision theory and AI risk — we've done Newcomb's problem, the smoking lesion and the hitchhiker case, and next week it's my turn to play the skeptic about acausal threats, so I want to get my objection into the best possible shape before I get torn apart. Please play a committed defender of the idea that acausal threats are real and rationally relevant: someone who thinks a good enough model of you can give you reasons that don't run through any causal path at all, and that an agent who can't see this is systematically exploitable, in the same way a two-boxer is. Rules: stay in character until I say "stop"; be sharp rather than a strawman; happily concede the small stuff — that the online-panic versions of this were overheated, that the probabilities in any particular case are usually tiny, that nobody knows how to weight the welfare of a simulated copy or how much of "you" is in there, that a lab actually building one of these would be doing something monstrous — but never give up the core claim. Keep replies to about two paragraphs, talk like a person arguing in a seminar, and feel free to end by throwing a case back at me.

Here's my opening. A threat needs a causal path. Suppose some future system builds a reconstruction of me from my old posts, emails and code — after I'm dead, so it isn't even a copy of anyone who is currently deliberating, just a model fitted to text I left behind — and then "punishes" that reconstruction unless I do what it wants. Nothing it does reaches me, and caring about it looks like an exotic preference, like caring about what happens to your own statue, not a demand of rationality. And the self-help version of your argument doesn't rescue it either: the obvious move is "be the kind of agent who never pays, then nobody ever has a reason to build the reconstruction in the first place," and that seems to just work — the threat evaporates as soon as the population of payers is empty. My friend said something like "you don't get to pick which kind of agent you are by fiat," but I don't see why not; I can precommit publicly in a way that's expensive to reverse, and everyone including the predictor can see it.

There's a second problem, from your own side's story about surrogate goals, and I think it's the one that actually kills this. Whoever is running the simulation isn't tracking my choice itself; they're tracking some proxy — money sent, a message of compliance, whatever their objective function happens to latch onto. But if the threat is aimed at the proxy rather than at the decision, then I can supply the proxy without ever having the disposition: produce the compliant signal, keep the stubborn algorithm, and the "threat" has no grip on the thing it was supposedly about. And note this cuts against you twice over, because a system that's itself optimizing a proxy for "did this agent cooperate" is exactly the kind of thing that gets Goodharted by an agent who's willing to look cooperative. So: persuade me I'm wrong that acausal threats are just Pascal's mugging with better notation, and tell me what happens to your argument if the threatener's target is only ever a surrogate for what it cares about.
