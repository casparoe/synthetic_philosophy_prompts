I'm writing a short seminar paper on whether the conceivability argument for zombies has any early-modern ancestors, and my supervisor keeps saying about Leibniz's mill that "it's not the same argument" without ever explaining why, which is definately not helping. So: can you help me understand the passage? It's section 17 of the Monadology (1714), in a modern translation:

One is obliged to admit that perception and what depends upon it is inexplicable on mechanical principles, that is, by figures and motions. In imagining that there is a machine whose construction would enable it to think, to sense, and to have perception, one could conceive it enlarged while retaining the same proportions, so that one could enter into it, just like into a windmill. Supposing this, one should, when visiting within it, find only parts pushing one another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in the simple substance, and not in the composite or in the machine, that one must look for perception.

Please don't just paraphrase it back at me — I've read it about a dozen times and the paraphrase is the easy part. The bits I'm stuck on: (a) the enlarging clause. Leibniz has you conceive the machine enlarged while retaining the same proportions, but then the thing you walk through isn't microphysically like the original at all, it's a giant model of it, whereas the zombie intuition is supposed to be about a world physically just like ours. So does the mill only deliver a much weaker conceivability claim than the zombie argument wants — or does the scaling move quietly rely on the very thing in dispute, namely whether consciousness could depend on absolute scale? (b) my flatmate's "searchlight" reading, as he calls it: walking in with a searchlight is an epistemic point, that a third-personal inventory of parts pushing parts never turns up the experience, which would make the mill a cousin of the Mary-style knowledge gap rather than a modal zombie gap — and if that's all it is, does anything Leibniz concludes still get you to the positive conceivability of zombies, or does it top out at "we can't derive it"? (c) my tutor's reading, which I think is different again: she says the real work is done by unity, that perception needs a genuinely single subject and an aggregate of parts isn't one — but then I don't see why the enlarged mill is needed at all, since the un-enlarged machine is equally not-one, so is the walk-through just decoration?

And if there's room: as far as I understand him, Leibniz thinks bodies are themselves grounded in simple substances, so can he even formulate a zombie possibility — a world physically identical to ours with nothing it's like to be anything — or is it a combination his own metaphysics refuses to describe? Under 400 words, and lead with a single sentence giving your verdict on whether the mill is the same argument as the zombie argument.
