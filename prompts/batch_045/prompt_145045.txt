Write a parody advice column, headlined Ask Dr. Ordinal, of the kind that runs in the Saturday magazine of a serious national newspaper. Dr. Ordinal is the Director of the Bureau of Revealed Welfare, a government agency charged with certifying whether citizens have been made better or worse off. The Bureau operates under a statute that you should invent and name, setting out two or three of its clauses in the standing header; its guiding principle is that a person's good is nothing over and above what their choices reveal, so that questions about what people say they want, about how their wants were formed, and about how much better off one person is than another are all ruled out of order.

The column should consist of a standing header of no more than forty words followed by three letters and three replies. The letter-writers should be: a hospital nurse who accepted a thirty per cent pay cut rather than lose her job, and who wants to know whether the Bureau will record her as worse off; a mother whose teenage son, since his school closed, spends every day alone in his room and insists he is content, and who wants to know whether the Bureau can call that a harm; and a couple whose house burned down, who ran out barefoot into the street in February, and who have been denied a compensation payment on the ground that they chose it.

The comedy has to come from getting the theory right rather than from inventing a strawman, so that someone who has actually worked on the meaning of utility recognises the joke as fair. In particular, Dr. Ordinal should: treat a choice made inside a constrained feasible set as a full-blooded preference, so that the nurse has revealed a preference for her new bundle over her old one, and the fact that the old bundle was not on offer is not evidence against this; decline to treat the son's reported contentment, or his mother's worry, as data, on the grounds that utility is only a numerical representation of choice behaviour and is identified only up to any increasing transformation, and therefore is not a quantity of happiness that could be measured, summed, or compared between persons; invoke a de gustibus-style clause to refuse any inquiry into where a preference came from; and, when a letter's data will not cohere, restore coherence not by admitting error but by redescribing what the choice set was, or by declaring that tastes changed between one Tuesday and the next. Use at least four of the following terms, correctly: budget set, weak axiom of revealed preference, generalized axiom of revealed preference, revealed preference relation, rationalizable, ordinal utility, increasing transformation, indirect utility.

The final reply should push the statute's logic somewhere the reader recoils: a modest proposal in miniature, in which the Bureau certifies as a welfare gain something that plainly is not one. Dr. Ordinal must stay deadpan and internally consistent to the end: no winking, no ironic aside, no smuggling in of coercion, distress, or interpersonal comparison under another name. Name no real economists, quote no real texts, and let the jargon do real work rather than substituting for an argument. No more than 600 words in total, counting the header.
