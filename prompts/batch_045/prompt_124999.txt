hey — I'm the night charge nurse on a locked memory-care floor, 28 residents, mostly mid-to-late stage Alzheimer's with a couple of vascular dementia, and my aides make maybe forty of these calls a shift. Our written policy is one line, something like "use therapeutic truth-telling and avoid causing distress," which everyone reads differently, so I want to replace it with an actual procedure.

The reason I'm asking you and not a textbook: someone told me Augustine held that every deliberate lie is a sin, including lies that would save a life, but that you're allowed to simply *not say* things — concealing the truth isn't lying — and that what makes an utterance a lie is the will to deceive rather than the falsity of the sentence. That sounds weirdly workable at 3am, and I'd rather build the card around it than around "do the kindest thing," which is what got us into this mess.

So: a decision procedure that fits on one side of a laminated index card. Max four or five steps, runnable in about fifteen seconds, by an aide who's been on the job three weeks and is being pulled down a hallway by someone who thinks she's late for work. For each step: the question asked, what counts as a good enough answer, where the card stops with a hard NO, and where it hands the call up to me or to the family because it's a values question and not a fact question.

Four situations I dont know where to put in the tree, and I want them placed, not just discussed:

- borrowed voice: the daughter texts "tell mom I'll be there Sunday," she isn't coming, and the aide says "your daughter says she'll be here Sunday." Nothing false is being said about what the daughter said, but the point of saying it is that mom believes she's coming.

- standing fictions: we have a painted bus stop by the exit door and two therapy dolls in the day room. Nobody asserts anything; the room is just arranged to produce a belief. Is that even the card's business?

- drift: a resident asks something perfectly coherent inside 1961 — "has my husband finished at the yard?" — where any true answer is either noise to her or a fresh bereavement. Can the procedure allow answering a different question that happens to be true?

- ninety-second uptake: if she won't remember this exchange in two minutes and may not be tracking the sentence at all, does the intent-to-deceive test get off the ground? I don't want a rule that quietly amounts to "it's fine if they won't remember."

Also give me three memorizable sentence-frames that pass the procedure for "where is X / when is X coming," since that's most of the volume.

Then run the finished card on three cases: (1) Mrs. K, 3am, wants to know when her mother is picking her up; her mother died in 2001. (2) Mr. R, about to get a catheter change he's had before and hated, asks "is this going to hurt?" — it will. (3) Mrs. O's son died in a crash last month; she forgets within ten minutes and grieves it as new each time; the family has asked us to tell her he's fine.

One more thing: I gather Augustine also grades lies, worst to nearly-excusable, instead of just banning them in one lump. Tell me whether that grading belongs on the card at all, or whether it's only good for how we write up an incident afterward — and if it's the latter, what we'd actually use it for.

Finally, be straight with me about what the procedure can't do: which of these it will spit out an answer on that I'll then have to overrule, and which calls genuinely need a person's signature.
