I am a novelist, and I need a short story in which a philosophical puzzle is load-bearing rather than decorative.

Here is the setup I have in mind. A restorer of early electrical apparatus builds a replica lamp with a thick carbon filament: it does not snap between lit and dark, but warms and cools along a time constant of a couple of seconds, so that its brightness at any instant is the accumulated residue of however long current has lately been flowing through it. A mechanism toggles its switch at one minute, then at half a minute, then at a quarter, and so on, all of it finished by the end of the second minute. Suppose that at two minutes the filament settles into a steady partial glow: a perfectly definite physical state, fixed by the switching schedule, and computable in advance by anyone who knows the time constant.

My worry is that this has quietly changed the subject. In the usual version of this puzzle the lamp is ideal, and the familiar moral is that the description of the task simply fails to determine what state the lamp is in at the limit, while another reading takes the lesson to be that such a task cannot be completed at all. A lamp with thermal inertia seems to supply the determinate answer the ideal case lacks, but I cannot tell whether that answers the original question or merely substitutes a better-behaved object and answers a question about it instead.

What I want from you: a story of eight hundred to twelve hundred words built on this setup, with a protagonist who has something at stake in which of those readings is right, a two-minute wait made physically felt as the intervals collapse, and an ending that lets the answer show itself without any character explaining it.

Then, in a short paragraph after the story, tell me where I have gone wrong about the philosophy. Does making the lamp physically realistic dissolve the puzzle, evade it, or bring out a feature of the ideal case that my version loses? Say plainly which, and say what the ending of the story ought to imply about it.
