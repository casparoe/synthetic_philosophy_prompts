I'm in a graduate reading group on the philosophy of psychiatry, and last week's session was on Szasz's *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961). I came away thinking that whatever else one says about him, the core of his position has simply been refuted by the last sixty years of neuroscience, and I'd like to know whether that's right before I defend it again. My reconstruction is this: Szasz's case rests on a lesion criterion — a genuine disease has to be anchored in a demonstrable bodily abnormality, and since none had been found for the conditions psychiatrists treat, psychiatric diagnoses could not be diseases but must be something else, metaphors or moral condemnations wearing medical vocabulary. But read that way, it looks like an ordinary empirical bet about what future instruments would find, and I take it that he lost the bet: we now have replicated neuroimaging, neurochemical, and genetic findings for schizophrenia, depression, and PTSD — group differences in brain structure and function, dopaminergic and serotonergic findings, polygenic risk scores, the works. The antecedent of his argument has been falsified; the lesions were there, we just needed better tools. Early in the text he writes: 'Mental illness, of course, is not literally a "thing" — or physical object — and hence it can "exist" only in the same sort of way in which other theoretical concepts exist.' I read that as a placeholder for exactly this kind of future discovery. And it seems to me the consequences follow: if these really are brain diseases, then the rest of his polemic against civil commitment and the insanity defense loses its footing, because treating people under mental health law is then just medicine with a paperwork delay rather than state coercion disguised as therapy.

A friend in the group says I have the structure of the argument backwards, for two reasons, and I can't tell whether they are evasions or real points. First, she invokes what she calls the conversion clause: Szasz never denied that some conditions currently called mental illnesses would turn out to be bodily diseases, and the standard historical illustration is the dementias of tertiary syphilis, which were counted among the mental illnesses until the spirochete was identified and were then reclassified as a brain disease. On her reading, a successful lesion-finding would not refute Szasz but vindicate him, by confirming that the condition was never a *mental* illness at all — the diagnosis would migrate out of psychiatry, not be validated within it, which means his thesis cannot even in principle be falsified by the evidence I'm appealing to. Second, she says I am confusing "myth" with "nothing at all": Szasz granted that people suffer and that their suffering is real, and described their difficulties as problems in living; his claim was about what kind of thing a diagnosis is and about what psychiatric classifications *do* in the hands of the state, not about whether brains are involved. To me that sounds like a lawyerly retreat — if you say there is no such thing as mental illness and then the lesions show up, the honest thing is to concede you were wrong — but I'll grant I may be missing something about how a conceptual claim differs from an empirical one.

So I tried to construct a case that would force the issue, and we disagreed about it too. Suppose we had a perfectly reliable biomarker — call it a neurosignature — present in everyone who meets diagnostic criteria for a given condition and absent in everyone else, with no false positives and no false negatives, so that it is as diagnostically clean as a blood test for syphilis. But stipulate two things: nobody has any account of what it is a dysfunction *of*, in the sense that we have no story about the species-typical or design function of the system in question, so we can only say "it differs," not "it fails"; and the people who have it show no impairment of reasoning, agency, or capacity to consent — they simply act in ways that impose costs on others, which is why they came to clinical attention. I claim that in that world Szasz has to concede: a real, reliable, in-the-head, bodily difference that tracks the diagnosis exactly. My friend says the neurosignature would be a fingerprint rather than a disease, and that this shows the bet I attributed to Szasz was never his bet. Could you say where I'm going wrong? Is my "he made an empirical prediction and lost" reconstruction defensible as an interpretation of him at all? What would have to be true for his thesis to be genuinely refuted rather than quietly reclassified around — and if the answer is that nothing empirical could refute it, does that tell against the thesis, or only against the standard I'm judging it by? I'd also like to know whether my inference from "these are brain diseases" to "his argument against commitment collapses" is even valid, since she says that step is a non sequitur and I don't see why.
