Our decision-theory reading group — six of us, meeting on Sunday afternoons in the living room of a group house in Berkeley, working slowly through a battered used copy of Metamagical Themas that someone found at a library sale — has assigned each member a one-page "position dump" to circulate before we present, and mine keeps coming back from the others with marginal notes I can't answer. Two of them in particular get under my skin: one says superrationality is just evidential decision theory with an unjustified correlation parameter smuggled in, the other says it is functional decision theory before it had a formalism. I think both of those are partly unfair, but I can't say exactly why, and I suspect the reason I can't is that my own package doesn't hang together. So here it is, as flatly as I can state it: (a) in a one-shot monetary prisoner's dilemma against an exact computational copy of myself — same code, same inputs, running on another server, no causal contact — I cooperate; (b) I also cooperate against a stranger who is merely a careful, unhurried reasoner and not a copy of me at all, because I take the point of superrationality to be that correct reasoning on a symmetric problem has a single answer, not that my neurons have to be type-identical to yours; (c) elsewhere I am a causalist — I think evidential decision theory gives the wrong verdict in lesion-style cases where the correlation is perfectly real but not causal, and I want a theory that tracks causal structure; (d) I read Hofstadter's gloss about there really being only one decision being made as a metaphor, and I do not think my pressing a button causes, or logically fixes, the output of a separate agent; (e) I accept the Platonia verdict, send the telegram with probability one twentieth, and I am comfortable there precisely because the superrational answer and plain self-interested best-response coincide (maximizing p times (1−p) to the nineteenth gives p = 1/20) — my discomfort only begins where the two come apart; (f) for a degraded copy, one that matches my action with probability r and otherwise flips a fair coin, I cooperate only above a threshold — the last time I did the arithmetic with Hofstadter's dollar payoffs I got something a bit over 0.2, so I cooperate against a copy that tracks me a third of the time and defect against one that tracks me a tenth of the time; (g) I abstain in national elections and do not think superrationality obliges me to vote, even though I cooperate in a million-player one-shot dilemma where my causal footprint is equally negligible; (h) if, after the fact, the experimenter credibly tells me my copy has already defected, I defect too, and take the dollar.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, ordered by severity rather than flagged uniformly; for each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me downstream. Some of these I expect to be fine — my guess is that (h) only looks like a betrayal of the correlation idea and dissolves once you notice the information set has changed, and that (g) is defensible because the electorate is not a set of agents facing my problem symmetrically — but I'd rather you tell me if that's wishful, and I'd rather you tell me if a pair I haven't noticed is the load-bearing problem. In particular I want to know whether (b) can survive (c) and (d) at all, whether the graded threshold in (f) is compatible with the categorical "one right answer" reasoning in (b) or whether it quietly turns me into the evidentialist my friend accuses me of being, and whether (d)'s demotion of the single-decision idea pulls the rug out from (e) and (b) while leaving (a) standing. Finally: give me the single revision that would clean up the most, name the one commitment you'd call a freebie I could drop at no philosophical cost, and tell me which item I am most likely to be simply wrong about.
