I've been sitting in on a decision-theory reading group — mostly philosophy graduate students plus two computer scientists — and last week two of the regulars, Marek and Dana, got stuck on a variant of the counterfactual-mugging setup and asked the room to referee. Nobody would, and I have been turning it over since. The case: Omega flips a fair coin in private. If it lands heads, Omega comes to you, asks for $100, and that is the end of the matter. If it lands tails, Omega never approaches you; instead it puts the very same question to a deliberately lossy model of you, and the real you receives $1,000 just in case the model says yes. Lossy means: with probability 0.6 the model returns whatever decision you actually arrive at, and with probability 0.4 it answers by an independent fair coin toss. You know all of this, and you are now being asked — so you know the coin came up heads. Since the tails-branch money is already settled either way, both causal and evidential decision theory tell you to keep your $100; the case is built so that the only thing that could motivate paying is scoring your whole policy, the mapping from being-asked to answering, from behind the coin rather than from where you now stand.

Marek said that even with the noise an updateless agent pays, and pays comfortably: switching from the policy of refusing to the policy of paying moves the model's probability of saying yes from 0.2 to 0.8, which is worth 0.5 × 0.6 × $1,000 = $300 in the branch where the money is, against the $100 you hand over only in the heads branch — $50 in prior expectation — so paying beats refusing by $250, and the recommendation survives all the way down to cases where the model reproduces your decision only about ten percent of the time, which shows the objection that you cannot really influence a noisy model does not bite. Dana answered that this threshold is the problem rather than the solution: just above it, the theory has you spend $50 in expectation to gain a few dollars through a channel that is nearly all noise, and just below it, it refuses, so the verdict rides on a quantity — how much your decision moves a computation that is not you — that is not a frequency you could ever sample, and note too, she said, that the stipulation that the model reproduces your decision with probability 0.6 is already a counterfactual claim about a different computation, so the case is not really confronting the theory with mere statistical correlation; it presupposes the very notion of influence across computations that the theory needs. Marek replied that the same is true of causation and it does not bankrupt causal decision theory: the agent is not estimating a correlation but scoring policies against a prior over ways the world could be, 0.6 is a structural feature of the setup, and a threshold in it is just expected utility being continuous in the payoffs; and the complaint that the margin comes from the coin halving your cost is not an objection, because from behind the coin a cost you incur in one branch really does weigh half.

Dana then said: hold every probability fixed and change only the structure. Suppose the model is not computing your decision at all. It reads some upstream fact about you — a disposition you can introspect — correctly with probability 0.6, guessing by coin toss the rest of the time, and it says yes iff it reads you as the paying type; you pay iff you are that type. Then the probability that the model says yes given that you pay is still 0.8 and given that you refuse still 0.2, and the joint distribution over the two answers is numerically what it was before; but now nothing you decide moves anything, because your type screens the model's output off from your choice, and scoring policies from behind the coin returns don't pay. So the theory's verdict flips with no change in any probability, which shows it is leaning on a partly causal or logical notion of what one's policy moves, and it has no way to say which structure it is in or what to do when it is unsure. Marek said the verdict should flip, that the two setups are different worlds and a theory that answered them alike would be blind to exactly the difference that matters, that the notion in play is just which computations in the world implement your policy, and that all she had shown is that the theory is not a function of the joint distribution over decisions alone — which was never on offer. Dana closed by saying that updatelessness is then doing less work than advertised: in the perfect-simulation version you can tell a supporting story, that you would have wanted to bind yourself before the coin and the theory gets you the benefit of that without a device, but at 0.6 that story covers only part of the recommendation and in the read-your-type version none of it, and what is left is the bare claim that your policy carries some fraction of the decision-weight in another computation — a claim doing all the work with nothing behind it. So, she said, the theory either pays on the strength of near-zero correlation, or needs a measure over partial similarity that it does not have, or is quietly doing expected-utility bookkeeping over branches it was supposed to have no special view about.

I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of this, and I would rather have a careful verdict than a winner. Could you go through the exchange move by move — checking Marek's arithmetic as part of it — and tell me who is right about which specific claim, where the disagreement actually turns, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
