Next thursday I'm presenting chapter 3 of my thesis at a two-day workshop our department is co-running with the palliative care research group at the med school, and I need Q&A prep more than I need feedback on the slides at this point.

The room will be roughly: two analytic personal-identity people from my side (one is a Parfit-style reductionist who thinks survival is already a matter of degree and that identity is the wrong thing to care about; the other is an animalist who thinks we're human organisms full stop and that most of this area is poetry with subscripts), three or four grief researchers/thanatologists who work on continuing bonds and meaning-making and who talk in measures and scales rather than possible worlds, and one cognitive neuroscientist on my committee who's sympathetic but will want to know what any of this would look like implemented. I get 40 minutes and then about 15 of questions.

The chapter: I start from the Hofstadter picture (I Am a Strange Loop, 2007) that a self is a self-referential pattern and that patterns can be partially instantiated, so that after someone dies there can be partial, reduced versions of them still running in several other brains at once — and I want to say that's literal, not a consoling metaphor. My additions:

(a) survival-in-another is scalar along three axes: structural fidelity (how close the version running in the host is to the original's loop), causal provenance (whether it got there by the right kind of route — shared life, letters, voicemail — versus reconstruction from an archive by someone who never met her), and dynamic potency (whether the version actually drives what the host does or just sits there inert).

(b) the degrees don't add. If roughly 0.4 of Anne is in her daughter and 0.35 in her old graduate student, Anne doesn't have 0.75 survival; I argue for something closer to the max, because summing would imply that smearing yourself thinly across ten people beats being densely present in one.

(c) except there's an integration bonus: if the daughter and the student later co-write Anne's biography and end up sharing one much richer model of her than either had alone, I want the degree to go up, because now there's a single structure richer than either. I know that sits badly with (b) and I haven't fully fixed it.

(d) fidelity can come apart from contact. My headline case is the biographer who never met Anne, works only from letters and drafts, and ends up with a higher-fidelity version of Anne than Anne's own son carries. I claim she has more of Anne on the fidelity axis while the son wins provenance and potency, and I genuinely don't know how to weigh the axes against each other. I also have a disowned-heir case (the best version survives in someone Anne despised; I say that doesn't lower the degree) and no answer at all to what happens when a host dies.

Give me the prep sheet. Eight-ish objections, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each tagged with who in that room raises it and phrased the way they would actually phrase it — I need the animalist's version and the thanatologist's version of what is arguably the same worry, in their own vocabularies. For each: a reply I can give in about ninety seconds, and where that reply is soft. Then: what I should concede in the first two minutes so the room doesn't spend the whole Q&A on it, what I should hold even if it costs me, which of my own weak points I should walk straight up to rather than hope they miss (I suspect it's the integration bonus vs. non-additivity thing), and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
