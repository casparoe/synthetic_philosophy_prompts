Suppose we were able to present David Hume, in his own lifetime, with the following package of ideas, translated into terms he would accept, and ask for his reaction to them as a whole.

First, the no-free-lunch theorems. Take every possible problem a learner could face — every possible way the observed data could be continued — and give them all equal weight. Averaged over that uniform spread, every learning method does exactly as well as every other: the method that extrapolates cautiously, the method that guesses at random, and the method that does the precise opposite of whatever the evidence suggests all score identically. No method is better than another unless the problems it faces are drawn from a distribution that is not uniform.

Second, the escape route usually taken from this result. The problems we actually meet are said to be very far from a uniform sample: they are highly structured and compressible, and a learner that prefers simpler hypotheses will beat its rivals on them. There is even a universal form of this preference — a single prior that weights hypotheses by their simplicity — about which it is claimed that, provided the world is produced by some computable process, it closes in on the truth in the limit no matter where it starts.

Third, a fact offered in support of that escape: the claim is empirically checkable, and it checks out. Simple equations fitted to yesterday's observations keep predicting tomorrow's; the same small stock of mathematical forms recurs in phenomena with no visible connection to each other. Call this the observed compressibility of the world.

Please tell me what Hume would say about all this, and structure the answer as follows.

1. Open with one paragraph written as Hume himself might have written it, arguing in his own vocabulary — relations of ideas, matters of fact, custom or habit, the supposition that the course of nature may change — without translating into modern terminology in that paragraph.

2. Then, in your own terms: would he treat the no-free-lunch theorems as the rigorous vindication of his own negative claim, or would his fork imply that a demonstrative result about the relations among ideas can neither supply nor remove any reason to expect the unobserved to resemble the observed? Does his answer differ depending on whether the theorems are offered as a proof that induction fails or as a proof that it needs an extra premise?

3. Where would he file the preference for simplicity: as an instance of custom, as a conceptual or demonstrative truth, or as something for which his fork has no shelf? Would the observed compressibility of the world move him, or is the observation that simple models have worked so far simply one more induction, so that appealing to it concedes the point?

4. A counterfactual: had the theorems come out the other way — one method provably dominating all others under the uniform average — would that have given him the demonstrative argument he denies is possible? Answer yes or no before explaining.

5. Finally, mark your own extrapolation: which parts of this reply follow from what Hume actually argues, and which are you inventing because the case never arose? Say whether, on your reading, he emerges more skeptical about induction than the contemporary discussion assumes, or less.
