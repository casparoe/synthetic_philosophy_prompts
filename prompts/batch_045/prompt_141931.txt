My colleague Priya does "decision design" for our trust — she's the person who gets asked whether some panel should decide by majority vote, by a weighted vote, by deferring to one named expert, or by something else entirely — and I've got to write the briefing paper for a case she's ruling on next week, so I'm trying to work out in advance what she'll say.

Her verdicts so far (I've sat in on most of them):

- Twelve strangers on a binary question, no deliberation allowed, each a bit better than chance: approved, plain majority, no conditions.
- Three radiologists reading the same scan who confer before voting: rejected. "Three votes, one brain," she said — either two genuinely independent reads or just defer to whoever has the best recent audit score.
- A national referendum, tens of millions of voters, a technically fiddly yes/no question, average competence round about 51%, everyone getting the same news: rejected, "a coin flip with better branding." She wouldn't say whether the problem was the 51% or the shared news.
- A nine-person investment committee, tracked accuracies spread from 0.55 to 0.88, all working off the same model output: approved, but only with weights proportional to the log-odds of each member's tracked accuracy. She explicitly rejected both one-person-one-vote and "just ask the best one".
- A screening panel where missing a case is catastrophic and a false alarm is cheap: rejected simple majority, wanted a quota of two out of seven.
- A committee that voted alphabetically with each vote shown as it was cast: rejected, wanted simultaneous secret ballots.
- A team who wanted to majority-vote twenty sampled outputs from the same language model: rejected, same one-brain objection.

The new one: our cancer network wants its multidisciplinary team to decide whether to operate on borderline patients by simple majority. Seven clinicians, each with two years of audited individual accuracy: 0.71, 0.70, 0.69, 0.66, 0.64, 0.61, 0.58. They all see the same commercial risk score before they vote, then confer for about 25 minutes, then vote. In the audit their errors correlate at roughly 0.3. The risk model alone scores 0.83 on the vendor's validation data, but it was trained on other hospitals and has never been scored against outcomes in our population. Failing to operate on someone who needed it is much worse than operating on someone who didn't, though both are bad.

So: does she approve it as it stands, approve it with conditions, or kill it — and if it's conditions, which ones (drop to deference to the surgeon, who is the 0.71 and also the one holding the knife; log-odds weights; count the model as an eighth voter; withhold the score until after a private vote; shift the quota)? What's the rule she's actually running? I read the record as "count distinct signals, not heads"; my mate reads it as "weight by log-odds and collapse onto the best expert when the spread is wide"; I suspect there's a third reading we're both missing. Which of those diverge on this case, and where exactly? And what extra hypothetical, or what single extra number about this panel, would actually tell the readings apart — I can probably get her to rule on one more case if I pick the right one, but I don't want to waste the ask.
