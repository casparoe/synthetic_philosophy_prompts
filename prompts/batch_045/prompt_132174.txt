I'm a novelist at work on a book narrated by a character who denies her own existence, and in the margins of the manuscript I've written down, as plainly as I can, what I take myself to believe about emptiness. Before I let the narrator argue any of it, I'd like an audit of whether the set is jointly tenable:

(1) To be empty is nothing more than to be dependently originated. Emptiness is not a property that things possess, and it is not a hidden reality standing behind or beneath them.

(2) Emptiness is itself empty. There is no absolute, ground, or final "suchness" that the word names, and any reading that turns it into a metaphysical posit has gone wrong.

(3) All four alternatives of the tetralemma are to be rejected, the rejection is non-implicative rather than a fifth position, and the Mādhyamika properly argues by drawing out the consequences of an opponent's commitments instead of asserting a thesis of her own.

(4) Still, those arguments are sound, they give me genuine reasons to revise what I believe, and "things are empty" is something I am prepared to assert in my own voice — and to call true.

(5) A thing's being empty nevertheless constrains what can be truly said about it: some readings of a novel are simply wrong, and there is a fact of the matter about what my character would do in a scene I never wrote.

(6) Persons are empty as well, so my authorship is a conventional designation rather than a power of bringing something into being from nothing — and yet the book's source is me and not the network of causes that produced it, and I could have written it otherwise in the sense that would make blame or praise for it appropriate.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? For each real conflict, say what would have to be given up and what that would cost me, and rank the conflicts by severity rather than flagging them all as equally bad. I don't know where the trouble is, so please discriminate rather than diagnose everything at once.
