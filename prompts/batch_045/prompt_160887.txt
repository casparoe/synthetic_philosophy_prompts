I am delivering the eulogy this Saturday at a memorial service for Dr. Miriam Ashdown, who taught Buddhist philosophy at a small liberal arts college for thirty-eight years and who had, by every account, become a Madhyamaka thinker all the way down into her habits of speech. The service is in a rented Unitarian hall. Perhaps a hundred and twenty people will be there: half from the college (philosophy and religion faculty, a score of her former students, and a Tibetan scholar who spent two decades disagreeing with her in print and came anyway); her sister Padma, a practicing Theravādin who will perform a dedication of merit immediately after I sit down; her son Daniel, an engineer and a cheerful atheist who will flinch at anything that sounds like incense; and a scattering of neighbours who knew her only as the woman who ran the community garden and never once lost an argument with the zoning board.

Miriam left a note. It says: don't tell them where I am. Tell them what I was doing.

Three things make that harder than it sounds, and I need the speech to respect all three.

First, the four corners. She thought the fourfold question about the fate of the liberated after death — does she exist, does she not, both, neither — was not a puzzle to be solved but a question whose asking was the problem, and that the refusal to answer it was the most practical gesture in the canon. So the eulogy cannot settle her anywhere: not in an afterlife, not in a clean annihilation, not in some both-and, not in a mystified neither.

Second, what she called the velvet fifth corner: the move of treating emptiness itself as a warm, ineffable something — "she is gone and not gone, she is beyond what we can say" — which is exactly the consoling thing a funeral wants. She regarded this as the one error the fourfold negation cannot cure, because it hides inside the cure. It has to be refused without the refusal sounding like coldness.

Third, she held that a Madhyamaka argument has the shape of a repayment rather than a deposit: it hands an opponent back the consequences of what he already claims and leaves nothing of its own on the table. Handing her a view about death at her own memorial would misrepresent how she thought.

What she did permit, and what I think has to carry the whole speech, is the ordinary: emptiness, for her, was precisely what lets conventional things work, so "Miriam is dead," "she would have hated this hymn," "her students came" are entirely in order and need no hedging. The discipline governs metaphysical placement, not grief.

Please write the eulogy: roughly seven minutes read aloud, in plain English, with no more than two Sanskrit or Pali terms and each one glossed in a clause. It must comfort people who are not philosophers, leave room for Padma's dedication of merit without either underwriting or undercutting it, give Daniel something true that he won't hear as woo, and contain at least one moment that only the philosophers will recognise while everyone else hears a plain human remark. Then, separately, add a short note for me alone — not for the room — marking the two or three places where the speech is doing something the four-corners discipline requires, and what a careless eulogy would have said at each of those points instead.
