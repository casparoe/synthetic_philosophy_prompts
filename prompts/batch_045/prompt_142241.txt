For two years the joint seminar I convene between our philosophy department and the psychiatry faculty has failed to move past a single question: whether mental illness is real in the way physical illness is real, or whether, as Szasz argued in *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961), it is a metaphor that medicine has borrowed in order to do moral and legal work. The stalemate is not for lack of agreement about the phenomena. Everyone grants that people suffer terribly and are sometimes incapacitated by the conditions in question; that many of them are helped by drugs, by talk, and by being removed from intolerable situations; that the official categories are syndromic, heterogeneous, and renegotiated every decade or so for reasons that are partly social; and that a great many people are treated against their will. Nobody in the room denies any of this, and yet the two camps have not shifted by a millimetre.

The Szaszian camp says three things. First, that a disease requires a demonstrable lesion or a physiological dysfunction, and that no such thing has been found for the conditions psychiatry treats. Second, that what remains is conflict, deviance, and suffering — problems in living — and that calling these illnesses is a category mistake rather than a discovery. Third, and in their view decisively, that the illness label is not descriptive but operative: it transfers a person from the jurisdiction of morals and law into the jurisdiction of medicine, and so licenses coercion while excusing responsibility. Their favourite piece of evidence is a historical pattern: whenever a syndrome that psychiatrists treated acquires a demonstrable somatic pathology — the old general paresis of the insane being the canonical case — it is quietly reclassified as a neurological disease and leaves psychiatry. The medical realists reply that the same pattern shows only that psychiatry is a young science that keeps succeeding; they add that many respectable medical categories are syndromic and value-laden, that responsiveness to intervention is itself evidence of mechanism, and that in any case the coercive conclusion is a non sequitur, since the reality and treatability of suffering do not depend on how we settle the concept. A third group occupies the middle: disease is irreducibly value-laden, they say, so the interesting question is whether the dysfunction component can be cashed out for psychiatric kinds, and the realists and the Szaszians are both wrong in the same way.

I have a hypothesis, which I offer only as one. The word illness strikes me as doing double duty — as a descriptive term and as an authorization-conferring one — so that part of the fight is a merely verbal dispute over which kinds get to bear a contested honorific, and part of it is a normative dispute about coercion and paternalism that no amount of taxonomy will touch. But if that were the whole story, the dispute ought to dissolve once the distinctions are drawn, and my seminar's experience is that they don't: when I separate the descriptive from the authorization-conferring question, both sides feel cheated rather than enlightened. That makes me suspect a residual empirical disagreement — a bet about whether psychiatric kinds are on their way to becoming natural kinds, and about whether they can be, given that they are unstable under description in a way that infectious diseases are not: people come to experience, report, and even inhabit their distress in the vocabulary the diagnostic manual supplies, so the classification partly constitutes what it classifies.

To force the issue I proposed to the seminar what I called the two-futures test. Imagine a future in which psychiatry acquires a taxonomy that cuts nature at the joints — one causal mechanism per category, a common course, a specific intervention that reliably normalizes the mechanism. Now imagine a second future in which no such taxonomy ever arrives: the categories stay syndromic and perpetually revised, and the interventions work about as well as they do now. My claim was that if Szasz's thesis is a substantive empirical thesis, the first future refutes it and the second vindicates it, whereas if it is really a thesis about concepts or about coercion, neither future settles anything. Half the seminar said the test is question-begging, because a psychiatric category with a single demonstrable mechanism would simply have become a case of medicine, which is precisely what Szasz denied was possible. The other half said the test is irrelevant, because his objection was to the compulsory use of the illness frame on unwilling people, and that objection survives intact in either future.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a verbal dispute with a political payload, two different questions under one name, a genuine empirical bet about the trajectory of taxonomy, or a bedrock normative disagreement about the legitimacy of coercion that argument cannot reach? Are the camps disagreeing about facts, about what counts as evidence for a disease claim, or about which cases are even in the data set? Tell me what each side is actually committed to, and tell me whether my two-futures test isolates anything real or merely restates the disagreement in the vocabulary of possible worlds — and if it fails, what a better one would look like. Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about what a disease concept has to do, a finding about the stability of psychiatric kinds, an institutional change that detaches treatment from coercion, or a change of question? And if the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, I would rather hear that, with the reason, than a tidy verdict that the whole thing was verbal all along.
