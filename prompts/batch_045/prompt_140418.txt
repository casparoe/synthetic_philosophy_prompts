You are a philosopher of language with research expertise on vagueness, gradability, and semantic indeterminacy. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming a long candidate list of demands on a theory of vagueness, then winnow it: I want to see what you considered and discarded, not only the final specification.

Because adequacy is relative to a purpose, here is mine. I work on regulatory drafting: rules get written in ordinary vague language — significant delay, excessive concentration — and then have to be applied to real cases by inspectors who cannot appeal to a further rule. I want a theory of vagueness good enough to tell me what is going on when such a term is applied to a case that is neither clear nor clearly unclear, and, if a theory can, what the inspector ought to do there.

So: write me the specification. What must a theory of vagueness deliver to count as adequate, with the case or consideration behind each entry? Cover these, and add to or subtract from the list as you judge right:

- The sorites. The step premises look true, the reasoning looks valid, the conclusion is unacceptable: the theory must say where the failure is, and also why the argument is compelling rather than merely tempting.
- Borderline cases with no sorites to run. Predicates like is a game or is a work of art have no ordering of cases on which to build the argument, so a theory engineered only to block the sorites may have nothing to say about most of the phenomenon.
- Vagueness versus its neighbours: generality, ambiguity, indexical or comparison-class context-sensitivity, underspecification, and plain ignorance of a fact. Give a test that separates them, and say which of these a theory is permitted to lump together.
- The apparent unknowability of the boundary even for a speaker who fully understands the word, and the fact that the borderline region itself seems to lack sharp edges.
- Faultless disagreement: two competent speakers can seem to say conflicting things with neither at fault. Is vindicating that a requirement, or a preference of one camp?
- Vagueness that is inherited from an inexact measurable quantity, as against vagueness that no measurement could settle even in principle. Must one theory cover both, and if you say yes, what does it cost?
- Voluntary sharpening: speakers can stipulate a cut-off mid-exchange and then reason as though the indeterminacy had never been present.

For each requirement give the motivating case and mark it non-negotiable or negotiable. Then flag any subset of your requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, saying which one you would give up and what that concession buys; and say which single requirement excludes the most candidate theories. Close with one sentence each on how the ignorance view, supervaluationism, and degree-theoretic or contextualist accounts stand on the non-negotiables.
