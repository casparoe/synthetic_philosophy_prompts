I'm in philosophy but I share an office with ML people, and something about the way the intentional-stance debate has migrated to language models bugs me. Dennett's justification for the stance is instrumentalist: you're entitled to attribute beliefs and desires to a system because doing so buys predictive and explanatory power you couldn't get more cheaply from the design stance or the physical stance. But that justification seems to presuppose the comparison is actually available — that you can check the intentional story is doing work the cheaper stances can't do. For a frontier language model nobody can run the design stance: hundreds of billions of parameters, no one derives its outputs from its structure. So we attribute beliefs by default rather than on the basis of any comparison, which on the instrumentalist's own terms looks like it should remove the entitlement. And yet the attributions are obviously doing something for us, and I don't think "so stop doing it" is an available response.

My half-formed way out: maybe instrumentalism was never the real justification, and the real test is prediction of failure rather than prediction of success — a system that genuinely has beliefs should fail in belief-shaped ways, while a lookup table or a bag of heuristics fails in ways no belief-description would have led you to expect. If that's right the test is runnable on small systems where both stances are tractable (tiny trained agents, chess engines with known evaluation functions, hand-written planners), which is exactly the move nobody makes because everyone argues about the big opaque models.

Turn that into a proposal: one precise question; why the standard positions (instrumentalism, realism about AI beliefs, and the "it's just pattern matching" line) each leave it open; the method — what I'd actually build or run, month by month, over nine months, and what would count as a result; why this route rather than just arguing about whether today's models have beliefs; the possible conclusions and what would decide between them; and an honest paragraph on what would count as the project failing or getting too big.
