I run a little film club in my apartment — nothing fancy, we just order too much takeout and argue — and next week we’re doing Kurosawa’s Rashomon (the 1950 one with the bandit, the samurai’s wife, the dead husband talking through a medium, and the woodcutter at the gate). I dont have any academic background in philosophy or film; I got into this from late-night internet rabbit holes and argueing with a friend, so forgive me if I mangle the terms or spell the Sanskrit stuff wrong. I watched it again last night and it messed with my head a lot, but in a fun way.

I recently came across this Jain idea — anekantavada, or something like that — that reality is many-sided and no single statement can capture it. The version in my head (which might be a pop version, or partly the blind men and elephant story) is that every perspective is only partially true, you’re supposed to qualify claims instead of saying flatly that a thing is or isn’t, and there’s even some old sevenfold logic where, depending on the standpoint, you can say a thing is, is not, both, neither, or maybe indescribable. Some people online use it to mean all opinions are equally true, but I got the sense that’s actually a misunderstanding, and that the Jains were not saying anything goes — they thought reality really is complex and that absolutist one-sided claims are the problem, not that falsehoods become true. I think there are fancier names for parts of it, maybe syadvada and nayavada or a sevenfold predication thing, but I can’t keep them straight. I’d love it if you could correct me where I’m wrong, but please don’t just dump a textbook definition; I want to use the real doctrine, not the relativistic meme version. I don’t need citations or a bibliography, I can look that stuff up later.

What I want is a reading of Rashomon through this lens. The movie is famous for four contradictory accounts of the same death: the bandit Tajōmaru says he won a duel and killed the samurai (or at least fought bravely); the wife says she killed her husband after he looked at her with contempt and then she couldn’t go through with suicide; the dead samurai, speaking through a medium, says he nobly killed himself after his wife was shamed and the bandit rejected her; and then the woodcutter at the gate says he actually saw it and that all three were lies — the men fought like cowards and the wife manipulated them — although the commoner catches him in his own selfish omission about the dagger. Does the film dramatize anekantavada, or does it only look like it does? Is each testimony true from a standpoint in the Jain sense, or are they mostly self-serving lies that the doctrine would actually diagnose as exactly the one-sided egoism it warns against? Where does the famous ending fit — the abandoned baby, the commoner stealing the blanket, the woodcutter deciding to take the baby home anyway, and the priest saying his faith in people is restored? That ending always felt like more than just nobody knows the truth; the woodcutter is a liar and probably a thief, but he’s also capable of kindness, and the priest chooses to trust him. Is that moment a better example of many-sided truth than the courtroom testimonies are, because it shows a person as having many aspects rather than reducing them to a single story? Or is the film ultimately more pessimistic, closer to everyone lies and truth is unknowable, which I suspect a Jain philosopher would say misses the point? I want a reading that’s fair to both the film and the philosophy: don’t flatten Rashomon into a mere illustration of a slogan, and don’t stretch anekantavada until it means something it doesn’t. Point me to specific scenes or choices the group should look at, say plainly where the analogy breaks down, and end with whatever you think the film adds to the philosophical debate that straight argument wouldn’t — something about why it’s hard to live as if truth were many-sided, maybe, or why compassion and action matter when certainty isn’t available. We’ll have about two hours to talk, so a focused reading I can read aloud (or hand out) would be perfect.
