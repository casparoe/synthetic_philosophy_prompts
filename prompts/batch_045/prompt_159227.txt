I am in my fourth year of a philosophy PhD, writing on consciousness, and I am embedded in a vision and attention lab whose PI — call her M. — has spent two years of Friday reading groups building up a track record of verdicts on individual cases, and I have gradually realised that I cannot tell what rule she is running. As far as I can reconstruct them, her verdicts are these: in the partial-report and iconic-memory case she says there is phenomenal experience of more than the subject can access, something it is like for the whole array briefly with access to only a few items, and she calls dreams the cleanest case of phenomenal consciousness without access; in a classic blindsight patient with a striate lesion she says no, the residual discrimination is entirely unconscious; likewise no for the suppressed stimulus in binocular rivalry; in inattentional blindness and the attentional blink she says no and insists this is not a memory failure but the absence of anything it was like; in deep sedation and absence seizures, no; and for current language models, no, in a way she does not even find interesting. Two things complicate the picture. She has twice refused to rule at all, on hypnotic blindness and on the minimally conscious patient who obeys commands in the scanner without ever moving, saying in both cases that the evidence does not touch what she cares about. And she has said two things in seminar that pull in opposite directions: that phenomenal consciousness is what distinguishes a system that merely responds from one for whom its responses are of something, and that first-person report plus similarity to us is the only evidence she will ever have. So I can see at least three candidate rules that fit her track record about equally well — a local-recurrence rule on which phenomenal consciousness is recurrent processing in sensory cortex with access a separate broadcast-dependent business; a global-availability rule on which the question is whether the content is available to a capacity-limited workspace, so that the partial-report case counts because any item is available on cue and blindsight does not because the information is encapsulated; and a report-first epistemic rule on which her verdicts are judgments about what may be attributed rather than about what is there — and I also cannot decide whether her two refusals are themselves evidence for one of these over the others, which is part of what I want your read on.

The two new cases I want to raise at Friday's meeting are these. The first is a collaborator's patient: bilateral damage to the frontoparietal ignition network with occipitotemporal visual cortex intact, normal no-report indices in the form of orientation-specific adaptation aftereffects and pupil dilation locked to stimulus onset, no late ignition in the EEG, explicit denial of seeing anything when asked, and — the detail I find hardest to place — after extensive training he can be brought to make a differential button press to the stimulus while still denying that he sees it, although the information never shows up in any other task he is given. The second is an artificial system built with an explicit global workspace: specialist modules, a capacity-limited bottleneck, broadcast to all of them, a self-monitoring module that reports its states in language, and the wrinkle that ablating the report module leaves task performance unchanged, so the broadcast does no causal work in the system's behaviour. What would M. say about each, and which rule is she actually running? I care much less about the verdicts themselves than about the reconstruction: state the candidate rules precisely enough that their predictions genuinely diverge, say what each predicts about my two cases, give me a best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and — most importantly — describe one further case, ideally something a lab could actually run in the next few years, whose result would tell the surviving rules apart. Please argue from the structure of the dialectic and the track record as I have given it rather than from the literature: no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and no appeal to what some particular paper found.
