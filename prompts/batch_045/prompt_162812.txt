I sit on the program committee of a small foundation that is about to commit roughly three years of funding and about eight researchers to a single question: how much evidential weight the most-cited historical collapses — the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, Rapa Nui — should carry in any assessment of how fragile a globally connected, industrial, literate civilization is. At the end of the three years the committee must choose between treating those cases as genuine evidence that our own system is fragile and therefore worth spending on institutional and infrastructural resilience, and treating the comparison as a category error: rhetorically vivid, evidentially inert, and a bad reason to move money. Assume a team that can include historically and archaeologically literate researchers, a quantitative social scientist, a philosopher of science, and an economist, but nobody who can generate new primary data — no new excavation, no new proxy records.

I do not want a survey of the field or a list of things to read. I want a research agenda, with the ordering defended. Some candidate questions I have been circling:

- what I have been calling the archive asymmetry: the cases we can see are selected for having destroyed the classes and institutions that produced legible records, while systems that lost complexity without losing their archives, and systems that faltered and recovered within a generation, are nearly invisible. How much does that selection effect distort what we think collapse is?
- the unit question: whether the thing that collapses is a polity, an economy, an information system, or a population, and whether the same event can be a collapse at the scale of palaces and a mild reallocation at the scale of households. Nothing can be counted as a case until this is settled.
- the gap between complexity loss and welfare loss. My own speculative version is the local-optimum hypothesis: post-collapse societies may sit closer to a local optimum of welfare per unit of energy captured, so that falling complexity is a poor proxy for falling welfare, which would matter enormously for how bad collapse is in expectation.
- whether it matters what drove a collapse — exogenous climate shock, diminishing returns to complexity, loss of a critical input, institutional brittleness — and whether any of those driver classes plausibly transfers to us.
- the near-miss problem: we observe failures far more readily than recoveries, so the relevant base rates may be structurally unknowable, which might mean prioritizing questions whose answers change action regardless of the base rate.
- the second-order question of whether collapse framing itself has political uptake that matters: fatalism, defeatism, or the legitimation of emergency powers.

Rank these, plus anything better that I have not listed, for a three-year, eight-person program. I want the criteria made explicit and the trade-offs among them argued: how much an answer would actually move the committee's decision, tractability given the no-new-data constraint, how upstream each question is of the others, and neglectedness. Say which two or three questions you would put in year one, which of my candidates are ill-posed or rabbit holes, and what specific finding over the next three years would move a question up or down your list.
