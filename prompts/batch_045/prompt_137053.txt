I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of science seminar and we've just finished the unit on the pessimistic meta-induction. Before I start the essay, I want to know whether there is one principle behind the verdicts I keep giving about which past theoretical posits were real. My verdicts:

1. Fresnel's luminiferous ether — not real, even though the ether was load-bearing in derivations of novel optical predictions; what survived is the dragging coefficient, which drops out of the relativistic velocity-addition formula.
2. Caloric — not real, but Carnot's theorem was derived inside the caloric theory and survived it, so the theory got something right that was not the fluid.
3. Phlogiston — not real, and here I don't even want to say the structure survived; what survived is an inverted story about oxidation.
4. Dalton's atoms — real. They were load-bearing for novel predictions about combining weights in just the way the ether was for optics.
5. Bohr's stationary electron orbits — not real, though they were essential to deriving the spectral series.
6. The cosmological constant — real, or as real as anything in current cosmology, even though it was introduced for a non-predictive reason (a static universe), was then set aside as a mistake, and did no novel predictive work for decades.
7. Hypothetical, from our problem set: in 2200 the dark-matter hypothesis is abandoned; a modified-gravity successor reproduces every numerical success the dark-matter model had — rotation curves, lensing, the acoustic-peak ratios — while positing no new particles. My verdict: dark matter does not exist and the model was not approximately true, though the "extra attraction at large scales" structure survived. Variant: the successor keeps the particles and adds the modification. Then I want to say dark matter does exist.

Cases 1 and 4 make me doubt that load-bearing predictive success is sufficient; cases 5 and 6 make me doubt that it is necessary. My own attempt at a principle: commit to a posit only if (a) it does genuine work in deriving predictions that were novel and successful when made, rather than accommodated afterwards by tuning, and (b) the posit, or the structure it encodes, is preserved by a successor theory under a correspondence that does more than reproduce the numbers — the successor must explain why the old posit's proxy worked.

I can already see a worry with clause (b): it appears to make whether a posit was real depend on what happens later, and I can't tell whether case 1 passes or fails it. Please do three things. First, test my principle against all seven cases and say where it delivers a verdict I don't want. Second, repair it, or replace it with the best alternative you can state precisely, telling me which clause does which work, which case motivates each clause, and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Third, apply the result to two cases I have no settled verdict on: the quantum state in present-day non-relativistic quantum mechanics, and the extra dimensions of a presently unconfirmed theory that has no successor yet — and tell me whether the verdict changes if that theory is later superseded rather than confirmed.

Please end your reply with a single line of the form \answer{LABEL — the principle in one sentence, under forty words}.
