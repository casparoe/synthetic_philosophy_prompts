Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that dramatizes one particular answer to the question of whether generational cohorts are real kinds — whether Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z name something in the world, or whether they are labels we impose on a continuum of birth dates and then mistake for discoveries.

I do not want a satire of generational stereotypes, and I do not want an allegory in which two characters called Realist and Nominalist argue in a café. I want the fiction to do work that a straight argumentative treatment cannot: to make a reader feel the difference between a boundary that is drawn and a boundary that is found.

Some constraints on the premise:

- Set the story in a world where cohort membership has become administratively real. Some institution — a ministry, an insurance pool, a school system, a reparations commission, something of your invention — assigns every person a cohort, and the assignment has consequences. Make the consequence one where the cohort is genuinely load-bearing, so the reader can see the difference between the cohort tracking something and the cohort bringing something about.
- Build the plot around a boundary rather than a centre. The protagonist should have a contested claim to a cohort: born in a year the institution later re-cut, born across a border that cuts differently, born into a family that moved between two systems that cut differently, or sorted twice under two different rules. Give that boundary a name and a history inside the story — the year that got moved, the rule that got amended — so that its arbitrariness is concrete rather than gestured at.
- Give the story one in-fiction observation that would count as evidence for a real discontinuity: for instance, the institution's own records show a sharp kink in some outcome at a birth year where nobody ever drew a line, or the kink stays put when administrators move the official boundary. Let that piece of evidence be genuinely ambiguous — consistent with a real cohort, and consistent with the institution having simply caused a discontinuity at the old boundary.
- Include one character who defends, seriously and without being a fool, the claim that cohorts are real. Their defense should not rest on shared personality traits. It should rest on something extrinsic and relational: that a cohort is constituted by its members' shared position in a causal-historical process — a common exposure to an event, a technology, or an institutional arrangement that arrived when they were at a particular point in the life course. Then let the story test whether that kind of definition can carry the weight the institution places on it.
- Do not resolve the ontological question in the closing lines. End with a decision, a loss, or a piece of administrative business that leaves the reader holding the question.

Close with a short afterword, 150 to 250 words, that is part of the deliverable and not a disclaimer:

- State, with a number, how confident you are that the story's central device actually bears on the philosophical question rather than merely illustrating a conclusion you had already reached, and say what that device is meant to show.
- Identify the single liberty the story takes — with the cohorts, with the invented institution, or with the position it dramatizes — that you are least confident is legitimate, and say why it might mislead.
- Flag any place where the story leans on a contested empirical claim, for example about whether shared position in a birth-year cohort really produces the psychological or behavioural differences popularly attributed to it.

Do not append a bibliography or suggest further reading; the story and the afterword should stand on their own.
