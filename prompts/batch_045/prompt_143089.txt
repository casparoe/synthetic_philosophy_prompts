Here's the half-formed idea. I was at a small workshop last spring where someone from an assembly-organising outfit said, more or less in passing, that most of their recruitment budget goes on the mail-out, and that the list they mail to is basically whoever their contractor has addresses for. That's stuck with me ever since. The standard pitch for sortition is that random selection is impartial in a way voting isn't: nobody picks the winners, so no faction can rig the composition of the chamber, and you get descriptive representativeness for free. But a lottery has to draw from a sampling frame — an actual list of people — and building that list is a political act that randomness then launders. If the frame is the electoral roll, or a commercial address database, or a residency register, then non-citizen residents, prisoners, people without fixed addresses, long-term emigrants, and everyone under eighteen are simply not in the population being sampled from, and there is no stage of the procedure at which that shows up as a decision anyone made. I've been calling this the frame problem for sortition, and my suspicion — the thing I want the proposal to test — is that it's *worse* for sortition than it is for elections rather than better: with voting, the franchise is at least an openly contested political question that can itself be voted on, whereas the sampling frame arrives as a technical input, and the epistemic and legitimacy arguments for sortition both seem to quietly help themselves to a boundary decision that the theory has no resources to make.
