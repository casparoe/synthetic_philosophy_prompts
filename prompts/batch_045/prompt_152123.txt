I am a novelist applying for a nine-month residential fellowship at an institute for advanced study. The theme for the year is comparative cultures of memory, and the four cases the theme fixes are Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's contested memory of the Pacific War, Turkey's relation to the destruction of the Ottoman Armenians, and the memorial culture of the American South. Applicants may come from any field and the committee reads for the quality of the question rather than for disciplinary credentials. I need a proposal of roughly two pages that reads as a philosopher would write it, while remaining honest that the person who would carry it out is a novelist whose real deliverable is a book.

My half-formed idea, which I would like sharpened rather than replaced: acknowledgement of a historical wrong seems to require a subject who can speak in the first-person plural of the party that did it, and to an addressee entitled to hear it. The four cases differ precisely in whether that subject and that addressee are available: in one the subject is official and self-accusing; in another it is disputed, so that the same sentence is either confession or slander depending on who is taken to be speaking; in a third the state withholds the first-person plural altogether while families and diasporas keep it alive without it; in the fourth the we is subnational, racialized, and bound to a defeat, and the addressee is largely absent from the polity that remembers. A novel, unlike a monument, a verdict, or a curriculum, can stage that first-person plural without anyone actually asserting anything, and readers routinely take the staging for the act — they feel that something has been acknowledged when nothing has been said by anyone in particular. I want to call this the borrowed first-person plural, and I suspect it is both the distinctive power and the distinctive moral danger of fiction inside a memory culture.

What I need in the proposal:

A precise statement of the question. I would like it put so that it has a determinate answer and so that no is a possible answer: something about whether a fictional work can perform acknowledgement or only represent it, whether the difference lies in the work or in the uptake of the memory culture around it, and whether a work that represents acknowledgement can thereby help constitute the collective subject whose absence blocks real acknowledgement in the first place.

Why the available positions leave the question open. The proposal should locate the question between the literatures it sits between — work on collective responsibility and on speech acts, and the descriptive study of memory cultures — and say what each of them assumes that makes my case untheorized: roughly, that the subject of acknowledgement is already given, that acknowledgement is individual and second-personal, or that what deserves study is what a society in fact commemorates. I will add the references myself, so please argue against positions and argument-types and do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a literature review.

Why this approach rather than the obvious ones. Say why the four cases in comparative parallel, read for a single conceptual variable, do more work here than a deep single-case study would; why this is not a work of comparative history and what it would lose if it tried to be one; and why the novelist's method — drafting scenes as a way of testing whether a collective speaker can be forged on the page — is a method and not a hobby.

A month-by-month plan for nine months that is realistic for someone writing fiction during part of each day, including what is deliberately not being attempted: no archival research, no interview or survey work, no adjudication of contested historical claims, and an explicit statement of what gets cut if the project runs large.

The possible conclusions, stated as three or four genuine alternatives, the considerations that would decide between them, and what would count as the project's failing — for instance if the four cases turn out to differ along one already-familiar axis and everything else is decoration, if fiction turns out to be merely one instance of narrative in general with nothing specific at stake, or if the question dissolves into an empirical one about what people happen to believe.

Finally, what the institute would receive, what I would carry into the third section of my novel, and why a committee should fund a novelist to do this rather than a philosopher: deliverables for the fellowship, for the book, and, if there is one worth stating, for people who actually commission monuments, curricula, and commemorations.
