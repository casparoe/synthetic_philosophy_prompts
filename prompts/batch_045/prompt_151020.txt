Medicine files people into disease categories and then does a pile of very consequential things on the back of that filing: insurance pays or doesn't, you get into a trial or you don't, a guideline fires, you get a prognosis, sometimes you get cut open. But the categories themselves are all over the place. Some name a mechanism. Some are just a cluster of complaints with no known mechanism at all. Some are literally a number above a cutoff that a committee picked and has since redrawn. The same label routinely covers people whose insides are doing quite different things. And categories get split or junked once a mechanism turns up, which sort of suggests the old label was never the real thing — except everyone acted on it for decades and it worked fine for plenty of purposes. Also (my understanding, tell me if I've got it wrong) some cancer drugs now get licensed for any tumour carrying a particular genetic marker, whatever organ it started in, so there the pattern is the disease and the organ-based category is the incidental bit.

I've been poking at the physical/design/intentional stance stuff and at the idea that a pattern counts as real if it buys you compression or prediction better than chance, and medicine feels like it runs on something in that neighbourhood but not exactly it. What actually seems to license a category in practice is that it tells you what to do, not that it compresses a data set.

So: what view of patterns — of what makes a grouping real rather than merely convenient — does this whole practice presuppose? And here's a test case: say someone defines a category purely out of a polygenic score plus a wearable time series, no mechanism whatsoever, covering maybe half a percent of people, and everyone in it responds to the same drug. Most action-guiding thing going, but it barely compresses anything. Does the practice's own implicit theory force us to call that a real disease, or does it quietly reach for mechanism talk when it wants prestige and drop it when it's inconvenient?

Then the stance mixing: clinicians say the kidney is filtering and the body is trying to hold onto salt, in the same breath as the lab values. Does the practice need those to be true, or just handy — and is there a difference? And what concretely breaks in a hospital or a trial if we decided only the physical-stance descriptions were real? I don't think the answer is "nothing, it was all shorthand," but I can't see what gives.

Give me your actual answer in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the question back at me, and don't pad it with citations or suggestions about what I should go read.
