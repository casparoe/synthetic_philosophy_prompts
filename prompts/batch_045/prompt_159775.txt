I keep noticing a pattern in group decisions and I don't have a word for it, and I'm not sure whether it's a real thing or I'm just pattern-matching.

Two cases. On our shared code repo anyone can merge: merging takes ten seconds, reverting means admitting in standup that you broke something. So risky changes get merged at a rate nobody individually endorses — the person who merges is always the one whose read of the risk happened to be most optimistic, and nobody ever finds out how many people quietly looked at it and thought it was a bad idea. Second case: a small shared research budget, four of us, each of us can commit a slice of it to a risky experiment on our own, but un-committing takes a meeting. Same shape. Exposure drifts up.

I half-remember something about a curse being discussed for situations where any one person can single-handedly trigger something catastrophic, but as I picture it that setup is one big all-or-nothing action, and mine is lots of small reversible-looking nudges where the friction is asymmetric — up is free, down costs.

So: is the nudge version the same mechanism or a different one? Does it get worse the more people are involved, or do the nudges cancel out on average (it dosent feel like they do)? Is it the same thing as the auction case where whoever wins is the most optimistic bidder, or the same thing run backwards as majority vote getting more reliable with more voters? Please don't just hand me a label — I want to know what has to be true for this to happen, when it washes out, and which nearby things it isn't.
