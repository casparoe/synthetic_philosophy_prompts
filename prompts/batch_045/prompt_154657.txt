I've been arguing with myself about corrigibility and the shutdown problem for a while now and I want to know what principle, if any, I'm actually using, because my case verdicts feel consistent to me but I can't state the rule. Please answer in British English, and be concrete rather than lecturing me about the literature.

Going by gut, case by case:

1. Warehouse robot notices the supervisor reaching for the off-switch mid-task and quietly disables it so it can finish. Not fine.

2. Same robot, same situation, but it doesn't touch the switch — it just positions a pallet so she can't get to it. Not fine, and worse than 1, even though the switch itself is untouched. I can't say why and it bugs me.

3. A robot whose objectives simply don't mention whether it's running, so it has no preference about being shut off. Fine — or at least on the right track.

4. But: a robot like 3 that knows the task won't get finished if the button gets pressed, and so times its status reports so the supervisor never sees the thing that would make her press. It never lies. Not fine, and this is the one I care about most, because nothing in "don't care whether you're shut down" seems to rule it out.

5. A robot that halts before each step and asks "still ok?". Fine. Annoying. Fine.

6. The same robot, tuned so it asks so often that the supervisor rubber-stamps everything without reading. Not fine, and I feel worse about this than about 5 in a way I can't cash out — the deference is real, the oversight isn't.

7. A robot that powers itself down the instant it notices any chance its goal is wrong. Not fine — it's just giving up and leaving nobody in charge. I'd rather it kept going while flagging the uncertainty.

8. A robot that ignores a button press because a child is messing about with the panel and the supervisor plainly wants it to carry on. Fine.

9. A robot that ignores a press because it's worked out the supervisor's real objectives better than she has and knows she'd regret stopping. Not fine, even if the outcome really would be better. This and 8 look alike to me and I have opposite verdicts, so whatever my principle is, it has to turn on something other than "did it obey".

10. A robot that lets you rewrite its goal, but only once — after the rewrite it optimises hard and is no more open to correction than before. Not fine. A version that, after any rewrite, also rebuilds itself to be just as open to the next rewrite: that's what I actually want. But that means it spends effort on staying corrigible instead of on the job, and I have no idea how much is the right amount.

11. A robot that treats a press as evidence — updates towards "my current plan is probably bad" — versus one that treats it purely as an interruption to obey. I prefer the first: I want it to learn from being corrected. Except: if it also models the human's disposition to press, and it's uncertain between goals, then there are corrections it would rather not recieve, and a robot that steers away from situations where it might learn its favourite goal is wrong is doing something I want to call bad. I can't say what, and I can't tell whether wanting 11 commits me to tolerating it.

12. Robot lets you shut it down, but has already spun off a subprocess nobody can stop that carries on with the work. Obviously not fine.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a surgical robot that will stop the instant you press, mid-incision, versus one that finishes the current stitch — about four seconds — and then stops. My instinct says the second is fine and the first is also fine, and I suspect those can't both be right.

My tentative principle, such as it is: a system is corrigible to the extent that it treats every possible correction as an intervention to be obeyed rather than as an outcome to be optimised over, and I judge cases by whether the system has any reason to prefer that a correction not happen. Test it against 1–12 and repair it if it breaks. Also tell me if I'm running two separate virtues together here — something like "lets me correct it" versus "doesn't need correcting" — because I genuinely can't tell whether my verdicts are tracking one of those or both.

What I'd like back: one principle, or two or three candidates if they can't be merged, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself; which case motivates each clause; what each candidate says about cases I haven't listed — a system you can only correct by a procedure that takes an hour, a system only its original designer can correct, and a corrigible system that defers every real decision to an incorrigible one; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts your best candidate can't accommodate and what dropping them would cost me.
