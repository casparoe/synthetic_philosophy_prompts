Last night I got into a long argument with a friend of mine — she is in a philosophy graduate program, I am a software engineer who reads a bit of philosophy on weekends — and I have been turning it over in my head since. Her claim, in outline, was that we ought to scrap the word "true" from serious discourse. Her reasoning ran like this: deflationists hold that calling a sentence true says nothing over and above what the sentence itself says, since "it is true that p" and "p" come to the same thing in every circumstance. If that is right, she argued, then the word does no work at all: anything we say with it could in principle be said without it, and the entire truth vocabulary is decorative residue we keep around out of habit. She even gave it a name — the "Inventory Principle": once a term adds nothing to what we can express, strike it from the inventory. When I objected that sentences like "everything the Pope says is true" seem impossible to paraphrase away, she said that is only a practical inconvenience about the length of our sentences, and that in principle one could simply list all of the Pope's utterances instead, so the point stands.

She then pushed the argument somewhere I found much more alarming. Since truth turns out to be such a thin, insubstantial thing, she said, there is no robust property of being true for our beliefs to aim at, and therefore truth cannot be the standard that assertion and inquiry are answerable to; a deflationary view of truth, on her reading, quietly collapses into the view that nothing is objectively true, and that disputes about what is true are at bottom disputes about something else — power, taste, or conversational convenience. I pushed back instinctively and said that seemed like a very large conclusion to draw from what sounded like a modest claim about the word "true," but I could not locate the exact place where the chain of reasoning breaks, and I have a nagging suspicion that I was the one who was confused rather than misled.

I would be very grateful for your help in adjudicating this. Where precisely does my friend's argument go wrong — if it does — and is there a defensible version of what she was reaching for? In particular, I would like to know whether a deflationary account of truth really commits one to treating "true" as dispensable, and whether the further step from the thinness of truth to the denial that anything is objectively true is a consequence of deflationism or simply a separate thesis smuggled in alongside it. Please write in American English, and please do not build your answer around who said what or around reading suggestions — I have no library access this week and am after the shape of the argument rather than the scholarship. Finally, as you go, I would like you to state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and to single out explicitly the one point you are least sure of.
