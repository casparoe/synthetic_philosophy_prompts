Working title: The Wrong Paradox

SETUP (will be short in the real thing): The classic puzzle runs that we get genuinely afraid of the monster and genuinely grieved by Anna Karenina, but we don't believe any of it exists, and fear and grief supposedly require belief — so either our emotions are irrational or the belief requirement is wrong. The usual replies: we don't really feel it, we make-believe feeling it (or imagine ourselves feeling it); or we run our ordinary emotional machinery offline, on an input flagged as not-real.

SECTION 1 — FLIP IT. The standard framing assumes the mystery is that fiction moves us *despite* our not believing. But the datum that should actually bother us is the reverse one: real suffering, presented as fact, at scale and at distance, reliably *under*-moves us, while acknowledged fiction at close narrative range over-moves us. If belief were the active ingredient the ordering would be the other way round. So belief isn't the variable doing the work. What seems to matter is format: one identifiable person, a legible causal chain, sensory detail, a beginning and middle and end, and the sense that my attending is the right response. Call it narrative uptake — our affective system is a detector for stories, not for existence. The test I want to run: hold the content fixed, vary only the packaging. Same child, same danger, one version as a case file with statistics and a redacted photo, one as 800 words of close third person. My prediction is that people report more feeling for the fiction, and that this survives their knowing perfectly well which is which. Knowing it is invented is not the silencing factor; being a number is.

SECTION 2 — THE SPLIT, AND WHY I THINK IT TROUBLES BOTH USUAL REPLIES. Fiction-directed responses aren't one thing and they don't behave alike under knowledge. Grief at a death survives re-reading indefinitely, survives knowing the ending in advance, survives knowing it's invented. Suspense — will she get out in time — looks like it needs real uncertainty or something very close to it; spoilers do damage it, and a second viewing of a thriller is a thinner experience than the first, though not an empty one. Horror mostly survives; jump scares don't. So a make-believe account owes us an explanation of why quasi-fear is robust to knowing the ending while quasi-suspense is fragile. Saying that I imagine being uncertain seems to get the phenomenology wrong — I don't seem to be imagining that I don't know what happens. My guess: suspense tracks whether there's a live question in whatever system is generating the outcome, so it's sensitive to my epistemic state in a way grief isn't; grief just needs the event represented once, and then tracks the object.

And here's the case I haven't been able to find anyone using — call it backdated demotion. You read a true-story piece about a child in a war zone and you're wrecked by it. Months later it emerges that a field officer fabricated the whole thing. (Outright fabrication happens more than people think, and light fictionalising — composite characters, changed names, tidied timelines — is completely routine where I work.) Question: does the discovery reveal your past grief to have been irrational? Everyone I've tried this on says no, they don't feel obliged to take the feeling back; they feel angry at the fabricator, and sad about nothing in particular. The emotion doesn't get cancelled retroactively. But if the puzzle is really about belief, this is the cleanest possible test and it comes out backwards — the belief was false the entire time, the response persists anyway, and nobody thinks the response was a mistake. Meanwhile if you find out that something real you were frightened of never happened, the fear does collapse. So it isn't that belief is irrelevant to emotion in general. It's that in the narrative case something else is carrying the response.

SECTION 3 — THE BIT THAT'S ACTUALLY ABOUT MY JOB. If narrative format is the trigger, then aid communications is stuck in a bind it doesn't like admitting: to move anyone we have to supply the format, which means an individual, a name, a face, an arc, a turning point. The actual situation doesn't arrive in that shape, so we compress, we composite, we change names, we shoot on the day the light is good. Not lying exactly, but format-engineering. And the cost is that the audience's moral alarm gets trained on the single vivid case and stays quiet about the statistical mass, which is the thing we actually need money for. I don't have an ending for this section yet. I keep going back and forth between calling it a tragic trade-off we should own out loud, and calling it corrupting and saying we should stop.
