I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I'm working on a piece about whether anything inside a virtual world can really be owned, or really be damaged, so I sat through a conference panel on it and then ended up at dinner with two of the panelists, and they got into an argument that I haven't been able to settle in my own head since.

The case they were fighting over: a VR world called Kestrel, mostly a building sandbox, running about six years. Two facts about how it's set up did most of the work. First, its physics: nothing a player builds can be deleted directly by anybody, including the person who built it — the only way a structure ever goes away is "unmaking," a forty-hour irreversible process that any player can start on any structure and that only whoever started it can call off. Second, its paperwork: the terms of service say players own nothing and the devs may alter anything at will, but in six years the devs have never once intervened in a dispute between builders, and the players have written themselves a charter, with rules about where you may build and a voting procedure for contested builds. In the actual case, a builder called Marta spent something like seven hundred hours over two years on a huge interior, the Chapterhouse, that a lot of people used as a meeting place; another player, Devi, started unmaking it and finished. Devi's story is that the build sat on a transit corridor the charter reserves as commons, that people had objected for a year, and that this was enforcement of a community rule. Marta's story is that no vote was ever held, the charter's process was skipped, and unmaking is irreversible and wildly out of scale with whatever the complaint actually was. The argument at dinner, as close as I can reconstruct it from my notes — Nadia, a level designer who's friends with Marta, and Bo, who writes about property and platform governance:

1. Nadia: It was hers in the only sense that matters here. Persistence is a law of that world, not a policy; nobody could remove it except by a deliberate, costly, hours-long act; the community has treated builders' works as theirs for six years; Marta put two years into it and other people built their social life around it. "It's just bits on a server" is the same move as saying a bank balance is just bits.

2. Bo: The bank comparison is doing all the work and it doesn't hold. A balance is what it is because of a ledger no single party may rewrite at will; here there is a party who may rewrite everything, the devs, and they said so in writing. Ownership is a claim you can enforce against someone. Marta had no claim against Devi — at most a contractual expectation against the devs, and the contract says the opposite. So what happened decomposes into exactly two things: real distress, which is a harm to a person and is genuinely a harm, and a breach of the community's own rules. Reaching for words like theft imports a concept with nothing behind it here.

3. Nadia: Your test would also dissolve property in places with no police. Customary systems, a squatter's shack in a settlement with no state enforcement — people can have property practices without a sovereign standing behind them. What makes something mine is that others treat it as mine and that taking it costs something. And you can't cash it out as distress either: if Marta had never logged in again and never found out, and the rest of us had just watched the Chapterhouse go, something bad would still have happened — to the world, not merely to a mind.

4. Bo: I'll grant customary property, but customary systems come with a second feature yours didn't use: a recognized procedure and somebody authorized to enforce it. Your charter had both and Devi skipped them. So the sharp wrong here isn't destruction of property, it's punishment without process — and that's a wrong whether the thing destroyed is a cathedral or a shed. On your last point I'll meet you halfway: a world can get worse, and that's a real cultural loss. But "the world got worse" isn't the same shape as "a right was violated," and I think collapsing the two is why everybody reaches for the inflated words.

5. Nadia: I can take the process framing and still think it doesn't exhaust what happened. Look at the menu of sanctions the world actually offers: no fine, no partial demolition, no lockout, no exile — the only lever anyone has is total annihilation of something that took two years. A system whose only available punishment is disproportionate has, in practice, no property rule at all, whatever its charter says. And on the devs: if their power to erase means nobody owns anything, it also means the devs are the only owners and the rest of us are tenants at will, and the fact that our tenancy comes with zero protections is a criticism of the arrangement, not a proof that nothing was lost.

6. Bo: Grant the tenancy — but an owner who tolerates a use isn't wronged by it, and Devi's unmaking was licensed by six years of dev inaction, so there's no trespass there either.

7. Nadia: Then take the case everyone agrees about. A stranger's avatar pressing against yours in VR: nobody sensible says "it's only pixels, no touching occurred." The reason it's a real violation is that the boundary of the person now includes the avatar. If the boundary can extend to a virtual body, I don't see why it can't extend to a virtual place people actually live in.

8. Bo: The avatar case works through the body schema — the thing violated is still the person. A building isn't part of anybody's body schema, so your analogy smuggles in the very thing at issue. Your real argument is the one from social practice back at move three, and it's stronger than the analogy you just dressed it in.

At that point Nadia said something like "fine, that's a distinction I hadn't made" and they let it drop, which is definately not good enough for the piece I have to file. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move — rate each of the eight, say who's right about what, where somebody conceded too much or won a point they didn't press — and then tell me what both of them are missing? Is there a distinction neither reached for: loss versus wrong, ownership versus something like standing, the interests of the couple hundred people who used the place and had no say? I'd like the verdict on the main question too, not just the refereeing. And since I might quote you: end by saying what would change your mind — the one argument or consideration that would flip you on whether Marta was wronged and by whom — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, because I don't want to print anything more confident than it deserves.
