I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar on early modern political thought, and next week's session is on the material in Pascal's Pensées about justice and force: the complaint that what one latitude punishes as theft or incest another permits or even requires, the observation that people obey laws not because they are just but because they are laws and are believed to be just, the claim that legal authority rests on opinion and so has to be propped up with robes, buildings and ceremony, the warning that tracing any established arrangement back to its origin exposes it and thereby ruins it, and the sour conclusion that because justice is endlessly disputable while force is immediately recognisable, we ended up making what was strong just rather than what was just strong. Every year the discussion ends in the same three-way stalemate: some students read him as a cynical realist for whom justice is only the decoration power puts on itself, some as a natural-law thinker who holds that justice is real but that our knowledge of it has been wrecked by self-love, and some as a conservative about custom for whom stability rather than correctness is what does the work. I don't want the handout to settle that; I want students to be able to locate themselves in it.

So what I need is a decision tree — six or seven numbered branch nodes, eight to ten leaves — that starts from a claim nobody in the room will dispute, something like "that a rule is coercively enforced and generally obeyed does not by itself make it just," and ends in a named position on what force contributes to, or does to, justice. Every node has to be a concrete case or a claim a student can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and no node may simply ask whether they like some label. Please let these cases carry the weight of the tree if you can: (i) a boundary case, where moving a few degrees of latitude changes whether a given transfer of goods counts as theft or as a lawful inheritance, and the question is whether anything you owe changes at the border; (ii) a settled-usurpation case, where a regime whose original title was forged two centuries ago is now unquestioned and someone uncovers the forgery — does the discovery change what anyone should do, does it change what anyone should say in public, and does the length of undisturbed possession do any normative work or only predictive work; (iii) a design case, where you are advising a new constitutional court on how to present itself, knowing that compliance depends on subjects taking the law to be just and would erode if they saw how arbitrary its foundations are — should the proceedings be robed and archaic or plain and transparent, and does the causal dependence of obedience on opinion generate any reason at all to manage appearances; and (iv) an insider case, about the official who has seen through all of this and has to decide whether she owes the law only outward compliance or also some inward acknowledgement, so that the tree separates a claim on conduct from a claim on conscience. Give each leaf its standard name where the position has one, describe it in a sentence where it doesn't, and add one line on the most awkward thing its holder is committed to.

Two further things would make it genuinely useful. First, build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: note which combinations of answers the nodes permit that nobody seems to occupy, and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely untried. Second, flag any leaves your tree cannot actually separate — I suspect the three readings of Pascal I described may collapse into one another at some node, and if they do I would rather the handout say so than pretend otherwise. Please don't give me a bibliography or point me to commentaries; if you aren't sure whether a position is really defended under the name you've attached to it, describe the position and mark the attribution as one you can't vouch for. Finally, say how confident you are in the tree overall, and single out the one node or leaf you're least sure about — the place where you think the classification most likely distorts someone's view.
