I'm writing the term essay for my seminar on ethics and collective action, and before I commit to a position I want to fix the standard I'll be assessing positions by. The topic is moral trade: roughly, the idea that parties who disagree about what it would be best to do can nevertheless both come out ahead by each doing something the other values more highly than they do themselves, without either side having to endorse the other's ranking or convert their values into a shared unit. What I want from you is a specification, not a survey: what would a theory of moral trade have to deliver to be good enough to act on? Fix the purpose as a decision guide for a group — say a campaigning society or a small grantmaking body — whose own members partly disagree about what is worth doing, deciding whether to accept a proposed swap of actions or resources with another group.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. The cases I most want covered: a counterparty whose aims we take to be not merely worthless but positively harmful, so that what we would be handing over is something we think makes the world worse; a counterparty that cannot reciprocate or hold us to the deal, such as future people, non-human animals, or a system that has not been built yet; a trade that would have to be kept confidential from our own members, who would reject it, even though it is meant to advance their aims; a trade proposed on the strength of a moral view we hold with only moderate credence, where our second-favourite view would rank it quite differently; and a trade in which we must perform first, with no way to enforce the other side's part, and where their gains would also leave them a stronger bargainer against us later. I also want you to name the distinctions a theory must not collapse, and to say which of the things people demand here are genuine desiderata and which are just the preferences of one camp — I suspect the ban on ever acting against our own best moral judgement belongs in the second category, but argue it either way.

One worry of my own to resolve: if the two sides share all their non-moral beliefs, I cannot see where the gains come from, since each of us seems to be doing something we regard as at best worthless. Tell me whether that is a constraint the theory must answer or a confusion on my part.

Then flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part of the answer I care most about — and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. If any approach survives the list, score very briefly the main families of approach (gains measured in each party's own terms; a common currency of expected choice-worthiness under moral uncertainty; a compromise or portfolio view); if none survives, say so plainly. No bibliography or reading list — I have the seminar material.

Please structure the response as follows: "What the theory is for", stating the purpose you are assessing against (about 60 words); "Requirements", each with its motivating case (about 350); "Genuine desiderata versus camp prejudices" (about 100); "Conflicts", on any jointly unsatisfiable subset (about 150); "The requirement that does the most work" (about 100); "Scorecard" (about 120); and "What would change my mind", naming the requirement you would drop first, the argument or case that would make you reorder the list, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail (about 120).
