Two months ago a multi-site replication of a result my lab published returned an effect estimate roughly a quarter the size of the original, with an interval that contained zero and also most of the original estimate. I have since been asked to sit on my faculty's research-integrity committee, which is drafting a policy on preregistration and on what should happen when a colleague's published finding fails to replicate. Before I can help draft anything I need to know where I myself stand, and the available labels seem to me to hide more than they reveal: people who agree that something went wrong in a case like mine disagree entirely about what it was.

So I do not want a survey of the debate. I want a classification instrument: a decision tree that takes a working scientist from an initial commitment about what went wrong in a case of this shape to a named position about what should be done about it.

Please build it as follows.

First, construct the logical space, then populate it. Set out the four to six questions that actually do the separating work, say what combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the existing positions in that space. Do not start from a list of names.

Every branch node must be answerable by someone who has never heard any of the labels. Phrase each as a claim about a concrete case, preferably one with numbers in it, never as a question like "do you think priors matter?". At least two of the nodes should be short vignettes rather than statements of principle; you may adapt my case above as one of them.

Include the following separations among your nodes, positioning them where you think they belong:

(a) what a failed replication is evidence about: the world, the original procedure, or the literature the original was drawn from;
(b) whether the target of inference in fields like mine is a stable population-average effect, or a mechanism that would be present in some settings and absent in others;
(c) who should get to fix the design and the expected effect size in a replication: the original authors, or the replicators;
(d) whether the unit of scientific appraisal should be the single paper, the cluster of claims produced by one lab, or an entire field;
(e) and an axis I have not seen discussed and want included because I suspect it separates people who otherwise agree: whether a field's reliability should be checked by sampling from its published claims at random, in something like the financial-auditor's sense (draw a batch of claims by lot, re-run them, and care about the error rate over the batch rather than about any single result), or by targeted adversarial replication of the most famous and most contested findings.

Produce no fewer than ten and no more than fourteen leaves. Each should carry a name, or be marked "unnamed" if nobody occupies it, and one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is committed to. Among them I want at least: keep long-run error rates and fix the conduct of research rather than the arithmetic; abandon testing in favour of estimation and intervals; replace p-values with Bayes factors computed from personal priors; use default or reference priors so the prior is not a free parameter; judge evidence by likelihood ratios alone; leave the statistics alone and fix the institutions instead; the real problem is weak theory rather than weak method; and one leaf for the person who thinks the amount of irreproducibility we observe is roughly what our methods predict and is therefore not a crisis at all.

Then do two further things. Identify at least three combinations of answers your questions allow that nobody appears to occupy, and say for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has got round to it. And flag at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot separate, with the further question that would separate them.

Refer to positions by the commitments that define them; if a position has a conventional label, you may add it in parentheses after the commitment is stated, but the label must never be doing the work. Give no citations, references, footnotes, or suggested readings: several of us will read this on paper and none of us will look anything up.

Finally, trace one route through the tree for me and tell me which leaf I land on, given that I would answer that the failed replication is mostly evidence about the procedure, that the target in my field is a mechanism rather than a population average, and that reliability should be audited by sampling. If that combination lands on a leaf nobody occupies, say so plainly rather than quietly moving me to a neighbouring one.

Format the tree as an indented list, not as a drawn figure, and keep the whole answer, tree and notes together, under 1,000 words.
