I'm an analyst on a growth team at a mid-size app company — I run maybe 3-5 A/B tests a month and I write the one-slide readout that decides whether we ship. Every couple of months somebody turns up with a Simpson's-paradox-in-the-wild: variant B wins on the topline conversion number but loses in every segment we slice by, or the reverse. I've read all the usual rationalist-blog posts on this and I know the kidney-stone / Berkeley admissions warhorses, so skip the 101 — but also, nobody here has time for "draw the DAG" as an actual practice, and my teammates know SQL and at most one regression.

Can you write me a decision procedure I can run in ~20 minutes when this happens? The questions in order; what counts as a good enough answer to each; the point where the procedure stops and says "this isn't a paradox, its a measurement or data-integrity problem"; and the point where it hands off to me or the PM because the remaining question is about what we're going to do, not about the numbers.

Things it has to handle: (1) slicing on something that happens after assignment (e.g. "users who finished onboarding") versus something fixed at assignment (device, country, week); (2) our traffic mix drifts mid-test, so segment sizes aren't stable; (3) we mostly report odds ratios out of a logistic regression, and I've heard somthing about non-collapsibility — that the adjusted odds ratio moves away from 1 even with clean randomization and zero confounding. If that's right, tell me how the procedure distinguishes that from a genuine sign reversal; if I've got it wrong, correct me; (4) choosing which population to standardize to when we're only going to ship to one segment next quarter.

Run it on three cases: (a) B wins overall, loses on both iOS and Android, split and mix look fine; (b) B wins overall, loses among users who completed onboarding and among users who didn't; (c) B wins overall, loses in every country, and we can only launch in one country. At least one of those should end with an answer I don't want to hear. And be explicit about what the procedure can't settle — I'd rather say "this needs someone who actually does causal inference" than ship on a number I can't defend in the meeting.
