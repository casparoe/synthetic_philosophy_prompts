I'm writing a feature about how institutions handle uncertainty when the numbers refuse to sit still, and there's one practice whose logic I can't reconstruct. Over the last decade or so, reform-minded corners of the social and biomedical sciences have reacted to the discovery that reasonable analytic choices — which controls to include, which cases to drop, how to measure the outcome — can move a result by a great deal. The reaction has been to run the same analysis many ways (handing one dataset to many independent teams, or having a single team report every defensible combination of choices as a specification curve), to publish the whole spread rather than a preferred estimate, and to treat a claim as established only if it holds across the entire spread rather than being pooled into one best guess. A large philanthropic funder I'm profiling has pushed this into policy: it will fund an intervention only if the estimated effect is positive under every specification its analysts rated as defensible, and when nothing clears that bar it funds whichever option has the best worst case. Yet the same funder's cost-benefit spreadsheets plug in a single point estimate and a single discount rate and return one optimal recommendation, and it pays for replication studies on the stated rationale that more evidence shrinks uncertainty.

My tentative reconstruction is that the spread-publishing and unanimity rules presuppose that a body of evidence determines not one probability but a set of them, that the spread is not sampling noise which averaging would cancel out, and that we are not entitled to probabilities over the specifications themselves — since if we had them, ordinary model averaging would hand back a single number and the whole apparatus would be pointless. On that reading the practice quietly commits its adopters to something like imprecise credences plus a particular rule for deciding under them, and it rules out both the view that evidence pins down a unique rational degree of confidence and the view that the spread merely reflects our ignorance about which analysis is correct and should be averaged over with a second-order probability.

Two things complicate this for me. First, the funder builds its list of specifications by inviting rival teams to propose analyses, so the bounds of the set depend on who turned up and what occurred to them: does the practice have to treat the set as a fact about the evidence, or can it admit that the width is partly an artifact of who was in the room? Second, a source tells me that when your probabilities form a set and you update on merely partial or coarse evidence, the interval for a hypothesis can come out strictly wider than it was before — that you can learn something and end up less precise. If that is right, the replication plank of the same policy seems to presuppose a narrowing that the reporting plank denies. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this package: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, say which rival views of uncertainty it rules out and which would license it anyway, tell me whether the point-estimate cost-benefit step and the set-valued evidence step can be made consistent or whether one of them has to go, and sketch what the funder would have to change if it took imprecision seriously all the way down. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
