Her core claim as I understand it: what makes a promise bind isn't convention, social utility, or the expectations and reliance it generates, but the exercise of a primitive normative power — by saying *I promise* to you in the right circumstances I make it the case that I owe you the performance, and in doing so hand you a limited authority over that bit of my conduct, which is why breaking it wrongs *you* in particular rather than just being a failure to bring about a good state of affairs. The part I find both genuinely original and shaky is what she calls the pre-conventional argument: she thinks the Humean conventionalist line and the practice-conception people get the order of explanation backwards, because (she claims — and I am not asking you to go dig up the empirical literature, just take her claims as given) very young children already treat giving one's word as specially binding in novel, convention-free settings, and broadly similar patterns show up across cultures; on her telling the convention is scaffolding bolted onto an antecedent social-cognitive capacity, not the source of the normativity.
