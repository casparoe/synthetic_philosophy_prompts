You are a professional philosopher who specializes in decision theory and in the epistemology of very-low-probability, very-high-stakes reasoning. Please work slowly, and begin by restating each of the commitments below in the strongest form you can give it — I would rather be audited in the strongest version of my view than in the loose version I wrote down. I work on long-termist priority research, and I have been asked to write down my position on small probabilities of enormous payoffs for a group of people who will try to poke holes in it. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Value is unbounded and additive across lives: for lives of a given quality, N such lives are N times as good, and there is no finite cap on how good an outcome can be. I reject bounded utility functions because they make the difference between saving a million lives and saving a trillion lives nearly negligible, and because I can see no principled place to put the cap.

(2) I justify my own career by expected-value reasoning about existential risk: a fraction-of-a-percent chance of civilizational collapse is worth a career because the stakes include the very large number of lives that would otherwise exist.

(3) I think the mugger should be refused — and I also think this in the "pure threat, fully private" version: the mugger says he has already created 10^100 people and will torture them unless I hand over five dollars, and I stipulate that no one will ever observe my payment, the encounter will not repeat, and there are no reputational or incentive consequences either way. I still refuse.

(4) I reject both of the standard exits from this problem: I will not bound the utility function, and I will not impose a probability floor below which hypotheses are treated as zero or say that hypotheses I have not conceived of get zero credence. Both strike me as ad hoc repairs made to fit one intuition.

(5) My positive account is that the difference is epistemic rather than probabilistic: the mugger's payoff figure was adversarially selected as a function of my dispositions, and the hypothesis has no independently checkable causal story; existential-risk work has models, reference classes and observable proxies. I think I can refuse at (3) while keeping (2) because of this contrast.

(6) When the expected-value sum over hypotheses fails to converge — when a claimant can always name a bigger number — I fall back on scoring actions by their worst case across the set of priors I have not ruled out, rather than by a single-prior average.

(7) I hold the same refusal in the "many copies" variant, where the claimant says he has run 10^100 indistinguishable simulations of this choice and will reward or punish all copies in line with how the copies decide, so that I cannot appeal to my own lack of pivotality.

(8) Finally: if a claimant had an independently verified track record and a demonstrated mechanism that scales, I would pay at a large enough number, and I do not think that makes me a fanatic.
