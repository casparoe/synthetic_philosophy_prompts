I attend a reading group on Buddhist philosophy where one member meets nearly every puzzle with the sentence "there is no self," offered as a way of closing discussion rather than opening it. Last week the puzzle was whether an advance directive written decades earlier should override the present-day refusal of a patient with advanced dementia. His move was that if there is no self, there is no single persisting person for the directive to speak for and nothing for "what she really would have wanted" to be true of, so the question does not arise.

Please give me a disambiguation rather than a verdict: the distinct precise claims that "there is no self" could be making here, laid out as a menu. Separate the readings by the work they do, not by their labels — among them, at least: the denial of a permanent, independent soul; the reductionist claim that a person is nothing over and above the aggregates and their causal and psychological continuities; the claim that identity is not what matters in survival; the claim that the person is a mere convention or useful fiction; the claim that experience has no subject or owner; the metasemantic claim that "I" does not refer to an object but functions like "here"; and the therapeutic or prescriptive reading on which the sentence tells you to stop identifying rather than describes anything. For each, state the claim in one sentence, say whether it is close to trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or incoherent, and say whether and how it would actually dissolve the advance-directive question, including what else it would take down with it — ordinary prudential concern, the force of promises, resentment, or the tradition's own reliance on intention.

Two complications I want handled rather than smoothed over. First: if the "mere convention" reading is combined with the two-truths move that conventional truth is still truth, does that restore everything the dissolution needed, or is there a coherent reading on which it can be conventionally true that she wrote the directive while nothing now gives that directive authority over her? Second: is any reading that makes the dissolution work neutral with respect to karmic responsibility, or does dissolving this case cost the tradition its account of why intentions matter?

Indicate how confident you are in each classification, and single out the step in your own reasoning you are least sure of.
