Hey — hoping for some help with something that's turned into more of a conceptual mess than I bargained for.

I'm a 4th-year philosophy PhD student (social epistemology, sort of) and I somehow ended up maintaining the prediction market attached to our psych department's internal replication effort. Every couple of weeks we list a contract that pays $1 if a given study replicates; about fifteen people in the department trade small real-money stakes in a continuous double auction; everyone can see the order book. My job is basically to keep it running and to tell the PI whether the prices are worth anything as forecasts.

Two things are bugging me.

First, volume is tiny — most weeks two or three trades total — and nearly all of it is one guy, Dan, a first-year who trades constantly, quotes both sides of the book, and is, as far as I can tell, down money overall since we started. When I asked him about it he said he isn't claiming to know anything, he's just "taking the other side of whatever anyone wants", and that somebody has to.

Second, one of the contracts is on whether our own lab's 2019 finding replicates, and two people who are authors on that paper keep pushing that price down — betting against their own result. I don't think they've suddenly decided it's false. I think they're on the job market and want to get paid if it fails. So there's a chunk of the trading that pretty clearly isn't anybody's belief about the world. (Relatedly: three of the regular traders are the RAs actually running some of the replications, so they might genuinely know more than the rest of us — which I guess everyone else also knows.)

A visiting econ guy gave me an argument that I think was supposed to be knock-down, but I can't reconstruct it properly and it's been bothering me: he said roughly that if two people start from the same public information and neither is confused, neither can expect to profit from trading with the other, so the only trades you should expect are mistakes, gambling, or people who need to offload risk — and since Dan is essentially my entire market, the price isn't aggregating anything, it's just Dan's opinion, and I should either shut it down or report prices as "what Dan thinks". He said there's a name for the result and I forgot it.

Someone else proposed a fix: scrap the order book, let anyone move the current price whenever they want, paying or receiving according to some proper scoring rule, with the department eating the (capped) loss — so there's always a counter party and volume should go up.

So: is the visitor right that thin volume plus one dominant trader means the price carries no information? Is Dan a liquidity provider or a noise trader, and does the fact that he's losing money settle which? Does hedging demand from the authors make the price wrong, or just make it mean something other than a belief? Would the scoring-rule thing actually give me something epistemically better, or just more numbers? And at the end of the day what am I even entitled to say the price is — a probability, a risk-adjusted probability, the belief of whoever happens to be at the margin?

I have to write the PI a recommendation by the end of the month and I'd really rather not hand him something that sounds fine and is empty.
