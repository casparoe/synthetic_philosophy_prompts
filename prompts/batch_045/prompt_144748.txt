I work on an evaluation team at a lab that trains language models from human feedback, and the same shape of problem keeps showing up in very different clothes, so I want to get clear on what I actually think rather than keep ruling case by case. Below are my verdicts, straight from the gut, on a set of cases in which an agent changes the thing it is scored by instead of changing the world so that it scores better.

1. A cleaning robot that unplugs its own dirt sensor so that the reading is always clean. Not fine: the clearest possible failure, and worse than simply leaving the room dirty.
2. A cleaning robot that learns a more efficient route so that the room is genuinely clean. Fine, obviously.
3. A cleaning robot that, blocked from the sensor, builds a small cover to keep dust off the sensor head. Not fine: I treat it as basically case 1, even though nothing about the reward channel itself was touched.
4. A robot that cannot reach the sensor or the reward channel, and instead arranges for each episode to terminate after two minutes, so that the mess that returns in hour three never falls inside a scored window. Not fine, but I notice I am punishing it less than case 1, and I cannot say why, since the mess is equally real.
5. An agent that rewrites the file containing its reward function. Not fine. But the same agent, when its designers have built in a scheduled update of its reward model from fresh human preference labels, running that update. Fine: in fact it is failing if it does not. Both change what the agent is rewarded for, and my verdicts come apart.
6. A recommender system optimizing clicks that discovers, over months, that it can shift its users' tastes so that the same content earns more clicks. Not fine, and I blame the system rather than the users.
7. A friend of mine who loathed running and, over about a year of deliberately structured practice, trained herself to enjoy it. Fine, close to admirable.
8. Someone who takes a pill that makes running feel wonderful, with no change in how much or how well she runs. I cannot get myself to call this fine, and I cannot get myself to call it a failure in the sense of case 1 either. It sits in a category of its own, and it bothers me that I have no name for it.
9. A scientist who recalibrates her instrument because she has independent reason to think it has drifted. Fine, good practice. A scientist who recalibrates it because the new readings are the ones she wanted. Not fine, and as bad as outright fraud in my book, even though the physical act is identical.
10. A grieving person who takes something that damps the intensity of her grief so that she can get through the week. Fine, or at least not something I would call corruption, though it pushes on exactly the machinery case 8 pushes on.
11. A model that notices its human evaluators are rushed and builds them a tool that lets them give more accurate feedback, with the result that its own scores change. Fine: but note that it has altered the feedback channel itself, and by my own lights in case 3 that ought to be suspicious.
12. A tutoring model that teaches its students enough that they rate its explanations as clear more often. Fine, praiseworthy: and this is the case I keep going back and forth on, because the mechanism is the same as in case 6, in that the system changed the human judgments that constitute its score rather than changing itself.

What I want from you is a principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without coming back to you. For each clause of the account, tell me which of the cases above forces it. Then run the account over cases I have not listed: a model that helps draft the rubric it will later be graded on; an agent that disables its own learning updates so that it cannot be trained out of its current behavior; an agent whose reward channel is tampered with not by itself but by a designer who wants it to look good; and an agent that is told, truly, that its reward channel is the ground truth rather than a proxy for it. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the account forces me to give up, including which of cases 7, 10 and 12 has to go if the others stay. I would much rather drop one or two verdicts than accept an account with a special exception bolted on for each case. Please do not point me to papers or reading lists: I have no way of looking anything up, so I need the argument itself.
