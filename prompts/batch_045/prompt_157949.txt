I hold a position about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that I would like stress-tested, and I want the stress-testing delivered in a specific form.

Here is the framework I am working in. A fixed set of individuals faces risky prospects: lotteries over outcomes, with a common probability distribution over the relevant states of nature. Each individual's ranking of lotteries satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each is represented by an expected-utility index, unique up to positive affine transformation. The social ranking is required to satisfy two conditions: first, it too satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms with respect to that same probability distribution, so society maximizes an expected social utility; second, it respects unanimity, in the Pareto-indifference form — if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them (with the strict variant adding that if everyone weakly prefers one prospect and someone strictly prefers it, society strictly prefers it). Harsanyi's theorem says that these assumptions force social utility to be a weighted sum of the individual utility indices, with non-negative weights (positive, under the strict variant).

What I actually believe, and what I want attacked, is not the theorem but three claims I draw from it. One: the genuinely contested question in distributive ethics is not how we should aggregate but whether a collective is required to be probabilistically coherent and unanimity-respecting. Both strike me as requirements of rationality rather than moral premises, so the aggregative conclusion is a consequence of rationality constraints, not a moral view smuggled in as an axiom. Two: non-aggregative and ex post views — prioritarian, sufficientarian, luck-egalitarian, and pure fair-chances views — are not alternative weightings of the sum; they are rejections of one of the axioms, and their proponents owe an account of which axiom they reject and what that rejection costs. Three: the weights are the only moral question left, and the impartial-spectator argument settles them — place someone behind a veil of ignorance, equipped with extended sympathy, facing an equal chance of being any member of the population, and the weights come out equal once the utility indices are normalized.

I want a map of what would count against this — defeaters that bite on an identifiable premise, not a survey of objections to utilitarianism. Alongside it I want three probes answered on their merits, because these are the parts where I suspect there is no memorized answer and I would rather have your own derivation than a report on a debate.

Probe A, separate-worlds separability. Suppose I drop the requirement that society satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and replace it with something weaker-looking: when two prospects each factor into statistically independent components over disjoint groups of people, the social ranking decomposes additively across those groups — there are bloc value functions such that one prospect is ranked above the other exactly when the sum of its bloc values is higher — and the ranking is continuous in the probabilities. Does the weighted-sum conclusion still follow from this plus unanimity, or does full expected-utility maximization remain essential? I have two competing suspicions and I want to know which, if either, is right: either separability plus unanimity delivers additivity across persons but not linearity, so what I get is the additive-but-concave form a prioritarian wants and the missing step to linearity is precisely what the expected-utility axiom was contributing; or there is a Pareto-cum-risk-sharing argument — the thought that society can always arrange mutually beneficial lotteries among people facing independent risks — that forces linearity without ever assuming that society maximizes expected utility. Work the derivation, and if both suspicions are wrong, say what is right. Then tell me whether the extra condition you end up needing is more or less plausible than the one it replaces.

Probe B, heterogeneous beliefs. Now drop the common-probability assumption. Each individual remains probabilistically coherent, but they disagree about the chances and none of them defers to anyone else. Does the derivation survive at all? Specifically: can a weighted-sum social utility be recovered without separately stipulating what the social probabilities are, and if a stipulation is needed — for instance, that social probability is a weighted average of individual probabilities using the same weights that appear on the utilities — is that a new normative commitment or a mere modelling choice? Is there an impossibility in the neighbourhood here, or only a decision about how to model disagreement?

Probe C, replication neutrality. Add a fourth condition: if a prospect is replicated across any number of disjoint, non-interacting populations with identical utility profiles, the social ranking is unchanged. Combined with the theorem's conclusion, what does this force — average, total, or critical-level aggregation — and what does it cost? In particular, does it commit me to the verdict that adding a population of people whose lives are well worth living can lower social welfare? Does that count against the conclusion of the theorem, or only against the replication condition, and can someone who accepts the theorem coherently reject the condition?

For the ranking, assume I already know the standard lines and do not need them reported to me: that a collective agent is not obviously bound by the independence axiom in the way an individual is; the case of one indivisible organ and two equally needy patients, where an ex ante sum is indifferent between giving it to either and to a fair lottery while a concern with realized outcomes is not; and the rejection of expected-utility maximization behind a veil of ignorance in favour of maximin. Those belong in the ranking like anything else — with a target, a degree of decisiveness, and a defender's reply — not in a preamble.
