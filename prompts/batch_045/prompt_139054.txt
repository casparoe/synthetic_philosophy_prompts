I'm giving a 30-minute internal talk at an ML lab next week, followed by 20 minutes of Q&A, and I want to walk into the questions prepared rather than hopeful. The room will be about ten people: six or seven research engineers (mostly RL, a couple from the pretraining side, one who does inference infrastructure), the lab's safety lead who thinks in threat models and eval suites, and one visiting decision theorist who will immediately start running expected-utility arguments. Roughly a third of them think the whole shutdown problem is speculative and want to know what the proposal costs in training compute and in benchmark performance. Nobody there is a philosopher; nobody will be moved by claims about the nature of agency.

My thesis: corrigibility is not a property of a utility function but of a training signal, and we should aim to make the shutdown event decision-theoretically *inert* rather than to make the agent *indifferent* to it. The standard move in this literature is the off-switch setup, where the agent is uncertain about the objective and therefore content to let the operator press the button and find out; I'll argue that this gets the wrong target, for two reasons. First, indifference is empty: an agent that doesn't care whether it is shut down also doesn't care whether you are shut down, or whether the button gets pressed by someone who shouldn't be pressing it. Second, it isn't self-preserving — an agent that expects to be modified later, or that can modify itself, has no reason to keep the indifference, and every reason to remove it.

My positive proposal, which the questions will target: train with an exogenous interruption hazard and *shadow rollouts*. A fraction of episodes is terminated early by a timer whose distribution is fixed in advance and does not depend on anything the agent does. Those episodes are still scored, using an off-policy estimate of the return the pre-interruption policy would have obtained had it been allowed to continue. So (i) there is no gradient toward avoiding or seeking the button, because the interrupt is independent of behaviour, and (ii) — the load-bearing claim — because the hazard is exogenous and known, the event "I was shut down" carries no information about the world and none about whether my plan was correct. What we get is not an agent that values shutdown equally, but an agent whose policy is approximately invariant under learning that a correction is coming. I'm calling this shutdown-invariance, and I'll claim it's the fixed point we actually wanted.

I know there is a cost I should concede up front: the scheme spends episodes on interruptions, and the shadow-rollout estimator is itself a learned model that the agent could in principle influence.
