I sit on the editorial board of a magazine that commissions popular history, and for as long as I can remember the same argument replays itself whenever we run a piece on Troy, Arthur or the Exodus. One side makes invention the default: without attestation from a source composed within a generation or two of the events — ideally the administrative records of a literate neighbour — a tradition is to be treated as later storytelling until something external anchors it. The other side makes memory the default: traditions do not arise at random, nobody invents a siege at a particular named site or a people's escape from a particular powerful neighbour out of nothing, and the silence of archives that were never looking for our story proves very little, so the burden sits with whoever claims pure fabrication. Both sides command the same small stock of facts — destruction levels at a site in roughly the right place, the several centuries separating the earliest surviving texts that name the British war-leader from the battles they describe, the absent mention in an unusually well-documented neighbouring bureaucracy — and neither has moved in years.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about evidence at all but several disputes run under one heading: a disagreement about default priors and where the burden of proof belongs, which may really be a question about what a historian's errors cost rather than about the world; an unresolved question about the identity conditions of a story — how much a tradition may change in transmission and still be the same story about the same events, which is what a historical core would have to consist in; and a hidden empirical question about how orally transmitted material actually degrades, on which we have only modern analogies and no measurements from these cases. Is that roughly right, or is something else doing the work — an asymmetry between the professional penalties for a false positive and a false negative, or two different questions (whether the event occurred, and whether the tradition preserves a memory of something) that would come apart differently in each of the three cases?

Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction we are failing to make, a decomposition of the question into parts that can be answered separately, a formal point about priors and evidence, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Say too where your diagnosis is most likely to fail, and what would lead you to abandon it. Please answer in American English.
