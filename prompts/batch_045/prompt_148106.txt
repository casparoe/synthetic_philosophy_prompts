I'm writing a response paper for a seminar on the epistemology of testimony, and I want to spend it on moral testimony rather than on the more familiar puzzles about perception or memory. As I've been reconstructing the debate, one side holds that there is something distinctively defective about forming a moral belief on another person's say-so — a defect that is supposed to survive even when the advisor is sincere, well-informed, more perceptive than you, and the belief you end up with is true — while the other side holds that moral testimony is just testimony, so that any worries we have are the ordinary worries about reliability, bias and vetting that apply to any source. I don't need to be walked through that standoff again; I want the essay to make progress on a distinction that seems to me to be doing hidden work inside it. When the first side complains about the kind of basis you end up with, it might mean either that you lack the grasp of the reasons that moral belief is supposed to involve, so that your state is not the state it purports to be, or that even a true belief acquired this way bypasses something you owe — thinking for yourself, owning your judgment, being able to act for the reasons there actually are. These are different complaints with different costs, and I suspect they come apart in the cases below.

The cases I keep circling are these. First: an advisor you respect tells you that firing a long-serving contractor was the wrong thing to do, but you know she weights loyalty and partiality considerably more heavily than you do, and you suspect her verdict depends on that weighting. Is deference here worse than deference to someone whose values match yours, or better, because she sees a consideration you underweight? Compare the case where the advisor's advantage is not different values but different experience — someone who shares your principles but has lived through the kind of situation you are judging, and who tells you that a remark was degrading. Second: a committee of five, none of whom is more morally perceptive than you are, issues a verdict; the members voted for partly disjoint and partly conflicting reasons, no single member's reasoning is one you could reconstruct, and yet the group's verdict is plausibly better than anything you would reach alone. Is deferring to that worse than deferring to an individual, and if so, what exactly is missing? Third: the vetting problem — picking a reliable moral advisor seems to require the very moral competence you are supposedly unable to exercise, which threatens to make wholesale deference either circular or arbitrary. And a fourth question that ties them together: if you later go through the reasoning yourself and endorse the verdict you originally took on trust, does the ratification cure the defect, or is the defect in how you first acquired it?

What I want is an essay that settles which of the two readings of the complaint is the more defensible one, and then runs that reading through the cases rather than just asserting it: verdicts from value-divergent advisors, verdicts from experience-advantaged advisors, verdicts from groups whose reasons are disaggregated, and the status of after-the-fact ratification. Give the strongest version of the reading you reject — I'd rather see a real argument against a steelman than an easy win — but commit to a conclusion about where moral deference is and isn't acceptable, and about whether the phenomenon of institutional or aggregated moral deference is a counterexample to the whole pessimist framing or just a hard case for it. Argue from the considerations themselves; I don't need references or a bibliography, and I'd rather have close attention to the four cases than a survey of positions. Around 1,000 to 1,300 words.
