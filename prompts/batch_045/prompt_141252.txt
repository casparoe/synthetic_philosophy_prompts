I write literary fiction. My last three novels share a narrator whose voice I built, over roughly a decade, out of the speech habits of my mother, who died some years ago: her syntax under stress, her habit of answering a question with a smaller question, the particular way she withheld the word "no." I have reached the point where I cannot tell, when a sentence arrives, whether I have made it or recovered it. Readers who knew her tell me they hear her in the prose. My brother, who inherited none of her idiom in ordinary conversation, reports the same thing when he reads the books; he says reading them is the closest thing to a visit.

At a festival panel last month, an audience member — I believe a cognitive scientist, though I may be flattering him — offered me a line by way of congratulation and by way of closing the subject: "A self is just a pattern, and a pattern doesn't have to be in one place." I have met versions of this before, and I have taken in enough Hofstadter second-hand to think it is in the neighbourhood of his view that a person's "I" can be present, unequally and in lesser or greater degree, in the brains of others who knew them. But "just a pattern" and "doesn't have to be in one place" are doing a great deal of unstated work, and I want the menu of things they could mean before I decide what my narrator is entitled to believe.

What the slogan was being used to do: to make it true, in more than a sentimental sense, that my mother is still here — distributed across my nervous system, my brother's, and to some extent the nervous systems of a few thousand readers — and to make that claim the sort of thing that is settled by the framework rather than the sort of thing one argues about further.

Here are the distinctions I have tried to make on my own, offered so that you can correct them rather than because I trust them:

1. Dormant versus running instantiations. The idiom is now encoded in printed books and, presumably, in nothing else until someone reads. When my brother reads, something runs in his brain. Does the distributive claim require the pattern to be running, or is being recoverable from the page enough — the difference between a program on a disk and a program executing? If the book alone is sufficient, then my mother is instantiated in every unopened copy in every warehouse, and I cannot tell whether that is a welcome consequence or a reductio.

2. Spreading is acceptable, merging is not. Everyone I have tried this on will allow that one person's pattern can leak outward into many brains; everyone balks at the reverse case, in which two people's patterns come to occupy a single brain and that counts as survival for both. If the "I" is genuinely a multiply instantiable pattern, the asymmetry looks unmotivated, as though identity were being smuggled back in exactly where it is convenient. I want to know whether the asymmetry can be defended within patternist terms or whether it is a tell that the view is not really being taken seriously.

3. Fractional occupancy. Whatever of my mother is in me has, as far as I can tell from the inside, no first-person perspective of its own: it says "I" in my voice or not at all. What would have to be true for it to have one — for there to be something in me that says "I" in her voice? And would that be a case of a self being distributed, or a case of two selves in one head? Hofstadter's talk of degrees of presence suggests a quantity that can be shared out; I would rather be told if that is a misreading than have it confirmed out of politeness.

4. The posthumous multiplier. If the distributive claim is right and the quantity is really a quantity, then my mother, who in life occupied exactly one nervous system, now occupies some fraction of several thousand brains and is run for a few hours in each. On one reading this means she is, in aggregate, more present than she ever was while alive, and that death can increase a person's degree of existence. This either follows straightforwardly or exposes the whole picture, and I cannot see which.

What I would like is a list of the precise theses this one sentence could be expressing, ordered from the boringly true to the radical — the platitudes, the substantive and defensible claims, the claims that are contested, and the ones that are simply false — with, for each, what would have to be true for it to hold, and whether it would actually do the work the panelist wanted: namely, making "she is still here" literally true and no longer worth arguing about. Say which readings would license my narrator's belief, which would make her someone labouring under an error, and which would make the question itself ill-formed.

Two constraints on the answer. First, separate what is settled among people who actually work on these questions from what is genuinely disputed, and do not present a contested position as the consensus; I would rather have a map with "contested" written across it than a confident line. If one of the prettiest readings is one that almost nobody who works on personal identity would accept, say so plainly — I am aware that I may be shopping for permission. Second, argue the claims rather than sourcing them: I have no use here for a bibliography, and I would rather not have the strength of a position indicated by who has endorsed it.
