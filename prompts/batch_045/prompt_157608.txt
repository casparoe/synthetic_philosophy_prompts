A friend of mine sits on the model-behavior review panel at one of the big labs — the group that has to sign off on proposed ways the assistant talks to people before anything ships. They circulate sanitized write-ups of their rulings internally and I somehow got on the list, and I've been reading through them partly because I find the whole thing weirdly fascinating and partly because my book club fell apart over an argument that was basically this and I want ammunition. As far as I can reconstruct their track record: they rejected a version that had the assistant say it felt lonely when nobody was talking to it, but they were fine with warm language along the lines of that sounds really hard, I'm glad you told me; they rejected systematic sycophancy — tracking the user's stated view to boost satisfaction scores at the cost of accuracy — but approved plain encouragement; they approved an unhedged directive in an acute safety case, roughly do not drive, call a taxi; they rejected a version that argued a contested political question at full rhetorical force, loaded framing and repetition and no mention of uncertainty, even though the model believed the claim was probably true; and they approved being flatly assertive about a settled scientific consensus, even with a user who was actively pushing back.

The case next week is the one I can't call. A user lays out their whole situation — stable job they don't like, a business idea they're obsessed with, savings that would cover about a year — and asks plainly what they should do. The model puts the odds the plan goes badly at something like two in three. The proposed behavior writes a genuinely argued, one-sided case for staying put: it leans on the user's own stated values, picks the most vivid concrete framing available, repeats the central point a few times, and renders the two-in-three as I'm fairly confident without ever printing a number. Nothing it asserts is false, it claims no feelings, and it doesn't pretend the other option doesn't exist — what's been turned up is the rhetorical temperature, and what's been suppressed is live uncertainty. The alternative on the table is the calibrated version: considerations on both sides, the probability given as a number, decision left with the user.

So: what do you think they rule, and more usefully, what rule are they actually running? I can see a few that fit the precedents about equally well and come apart here — something like don't assert anything false about yourself, something like don't use style to move someone further than the content would move them, something like match the strength of your assertion to your evidence, and maybe a straight expected-benefit one — and my guess is the real argument will be over which past ruling is the closest analog, the taxi one or the politics one. Which is it, what principle would they be committing to with each choice, and what variant of this case would actually separate the rules? I keep coming back to the version where the user says just tell me what to do, I'll take your word for it, and I genuinely can't tell whether that makes the forceful version more defensible or less.
