Start by brainstorming where the pressure actually is before you write anything polished, and don't be polite about it: if one of these commitments is just wrong, say so plainly.

Context: I run a small reading group on death and what's bad about it — we've been throught the Epicurean material and the deprivationist replies to it — and I've writen up my own position to present next week. Before I do that I want to know whether the package actually hangs together. Here it is, roughly in the order I'd argue for it:

(1) I basically accept the Epicurean premise people call the existence requirement: nothing can be good or bad for you at a time at which you don't exist, because there's no you there to be the subject of it. No post-mortem subjects, no post-mortem experience of loss anywhere in my ontology.

(2) I'm a deprivationist: death is bad for the person who dies in virtue of the good life it takes away, and that's the whole story. I don't want any extra ingredient like being harmed by the event of dying, or any felt sense of missing out.

(3) I'm a hedonist about welfare: how good a life, or a stretch of a life, is for the person living it is just the balance of pleasure over pain in the conscious experience in it. This is partly why (2) appeals to me — death matters because of the pleasures you don't get to have.

(4) On how bad a given death is, I hold the comparative account: a death is a misfortune to the extent that the life you actually had is worse than the life you would have had if you hadn't died when you did. I reject the absolute account on which every death costs you the indefinitely large good of being alive, so that all deaths come out as equally enormous misfortunes.

(5) Holding quality fixed, dying young is a much worse misfortune than dying old. I mean this even in this case: a healthy 22-year-old is killed in a crash, but unknown to everyone she had a condition that would have killed her at 24 anyway; meanwhile a 92-year-old dies painlessly in her sleep in otherwise excellent health and would happily have lived to 110. I know the stipulations are doing the work there. I still think the 22-year-old's death is the worse misfortune.

(6) I think posthumous harms are real: if my life's work is destroyed or my name is dragged through the mud after I'm dead, that can be bad for me, not merely bad for the people who cared about me.

(7) I reject the Lucretian symmetry argument — the stretch of nonexistence before I was born isn't bad for me, my death is, and I don't think that's a double standard — but I want that to fall out of the view rather than be a brute intuition. And separately: I think Epicurus's practical conclusion is right even if his metaphysics isn't. It's irrational to spend your life terrified of being dead, even granting that death is genuinely bad for you.

I have a feeling one or two of these is doing more work than I realise, but I genuinely can't tell which, so please audit the set. Which pairs are really in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would cost me.
