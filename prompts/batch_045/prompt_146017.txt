I have recently retired from a career in experimental physics and have begun reading in philosophy, where I have come upon the two-envelope paradox. My own view is that it is a problem in elementary probability rather than a problem in philosophy. The two envelopes are exchangeable: whatever procedure filled them, the ordered pair consisting of the envelope in my hand and the other one is distributed exactly as the pair in the reverse order. It follows that the expected contents of the two are equal, that the expected gain from switching is zero, and that the notorious argument for a gain of 5/4 must be sliding between two different referents for a single symbol. On this diagnosis the prior distribution over the amounts does no work at all, and the resolutions one encounters which turn on adopting a proper prior, or on whether the mean of the smaller amount is finite, are addressing a pseudo-problem. A former colleague with whom I have been corresponding denies this. He asks me to suppose that the smaller amount is 2^n with probability proportional to n·2^(-n), for n = 1, 2, 3, and so on, and claims that then, whatever value I find when I open my envelope, the expected gain from switching is positive — for an observed 2^n he puts it at 2^n/(3n−2). I reply that this case is degenerate: the mean of the smaller amount diverges, so the expected gain is not a well-defined quantity, and an undefined quantity cannot favour switching. Who is right about what here? And more generally, is exchangeability really the whole story, or does some genuine difficulty survive it?
