After four decades as a field entomologist I have, in retirement, begun reading philosophy properly for the first time, and I now sit as the only natural scientist on a five-person advisory panel that allocates a modest budget — part research, part intervention — for a conservation funder. Our remit requires us to keep a standing list of taxa that are "in scope": either because they are candidates for welfare-relevant consideration in their own right, or because they are candidates for research funding only. Over three years we have issued the following rulings, and I have come to think that our record is thinner than our confidence.

We placed cephalopods and then decapod crustaceans in full scope, the minute citing identified nociceptors, modulation of response by analgesics, and flexible motivational trade-offs. We placed honey bees in full scope over one dissent, notwithstanding that no nociceptor has been identified in them, on the strength of associative learning, trade-off behaviour, and a reported judgment-bias effect. We declined to treat an ant colony as a single subject: our unit, the minute says, is the individual organism. We excluded soil nematodes altogether, at abundances the briefing note puts on the order of ten to the twentieth, with the minute recording that "abundance is not evidence." We placed wild adult mosquitoes — vast numbers, no established behavioural marker within the group, and no tractable intervention — in the research-only category, and the minute gives both grounds without ranking them. We placed individual ant workers in full scope, at abundances on the order of ten to the sixteenth, with learning and trade-off data but no nociceptor account; the chair's note records that two members were moved chiefly by the per-capita evidence and one chiefly by the numbers. And we placed a rare cave isopod, perhaps ten thousand individuals in total and the full marker set, in full scope at the lowest priority.

The proposal now before us concerns a globally distributed group of soil Collembola whose standing population the briefing note puts on the order of ten to the fourteenth and possibly higher. Within the group itself nothing has been established: no nociceptors, no learning work, no judgment-bias work. But a confamilial species, from which the group diverged roughly a hundred million years ago, displays the full marker set, and the relevant neuropils are homologous by the usual criteria. No intervention at the level of an individual animal is conceivable. There is, however, a cheap change in tillage and harvest practice across roughly ten million hectares which, on our own hydrology note, would reduce by close to an order of magnitude the frequency with which these animals are caught in desiccation events at harvest — a change that is a welfare gain only if the states in question are valenced, and a modest net cost if they are not. Our elicitation, such as it was, put the median credence that the group has valenced states near one tenth, with a spread from perhaps one in a hundred to perhaps two in five.

I would be grateful for your help in reasoning this through rather than for a verdict on the substance.

Sections, in order, with the lengths I have in mind:

**Rules consistent with the record** — about 300 words. Set out the distinct rules that generate all eight of our rulings: something like straight expected-multiplication of numbers against credence; an admissibility threshold requiring a marker in the taxon or a close homologue; a tractability or leverage filter; and any others you think fit us equally well.

**What each rule implies here** — about 250 words. A small table is welcome: rule, verdict on the Collembola proposal, and the single consideration doing the work.

**Recommended decision** — about 250 words. Which ruling best coheres with what we have already done, with your reasons for preferring it over the alternatives.

**Closest precedent, and how far it carries** — about 150 words. I take the mosquito ruling to be the obvious candidate, since it pairs the same two grounds we now face separately; tell me whether that is right and what the disanalogy is.

**What we would be committing to either way** — about 200 words. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, state plainly the principle we would be adopting with each of the live options, including the principle we would be adopting by choosing research-only as a compromise.

**The case that would settle it** — about 150 words. One further case, described concretely enough to be recognisable, whose ruling would best discriminate between the surviving rules.

**On the arithmetic of very large numbers and shared credences** — about 200 words. I keep catching myself multiplying as though the credence were a frequency: ten to the fourteenth times one tenth, giving animals that matter in the trillions. Colleagues tell me the credence is a single shared one, so the product is the expectation of one bet rather than an enumeration, and that no law of large numbers does any work for us here. I would like to know how much of my instinct survives that correction, and what we should actually be computing when we compare a very abundant, poorly evidenced taxon against a scarce, well-evidenced one.
