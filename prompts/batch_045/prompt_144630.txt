hey — I run a metaphysics reading group and I've got one endurantist and one perdurantist who have now had basically the same argument six times, and I want to force an adversarial collaboration on them instead of letting it happen a seventh time. So: draft the joint statement both of them could sign.

Stipulate these up front so you dont waste words on them: both are eternalists about time, both accept that ordinary change is real, both grant some reading on which "the candle is straight" is true at one time and "the candle is bent" true at another without contradiction, and both accept that there are properties — moving, accelerating, surviving an operation — that look like they're had only over intervals rather than at instants. Neither side gets to treat special relativity as a knock-down, and neither gets to appeal to what ordinary folk say about "persists."

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section, and I mean substantive ones, not "change happens." Things like: what exactly the classic argument from temporary intrinsics does and doesn't establish; whether "wholly present at a time" can be analysed without circling back to the very notion in dispute, or has to be taken as primitive; whether the endurantist is stuck with primitive time-indexed properties and whether that's actually a cost; whether either view has an easier time with coinciding objects.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the point at issue; the endurantist's own wording, phrased so my endurantist would sign it; the perdurantist's own wording likewise; and your verdict on whether that row is a genuine disagreement or only a verbal one.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. These I want covered, plus whatever you think I'm missing:

- The Flicker: a thing that exists, goes out of existence, and then comes back later — intermittent existence. What does each say, and does either handle it more gracefully?
- The Heraclitean Rock: stipulate an object about which no de re modal claims are true at all — nothing is true about what could have happened to it, only about what does. Is there then any difference left between "one thing wholly present at each moment, with time-indexed properties" and "a sequence of instantaneous stages hooked up by temporal counterparts"? If the modal differences were doing all the work in this debate, I want that said out loud.
- Interval-only properties: can either side give an account of being in motion that doesn't quietly borrow the other side's machinery?
- The translation test: suppose someone produces a scheme for translating between the two formalisms that preserves everything except that one side has a primitive "is wholly located at" and the other a primitive "is an instantaneous temporal part of", and there's no neutral characterization of "wholly" or "temporal part" that both accept. Is the dispute then merely notational, or is there a residue? Say what moves are left to each side.
- Suppose (I'm stipulating this, don't try to look it up) that a decent cross-cultural developmental study found that young children track objects by spatiotemporal continuity and never by anything stage-like, and that adults' verdicts on a fission case split by culture. Would either of my two count that as evidence about which theory is true? I want both to commit, in advance, to what they predict such a study would find.

4. A short closing section: which parts of this fight were, on your view, always only about words; which are substantive; and if some part is substantive but neither side has any argument left that the other doesn't already know, just say that plainly.

Skip citations and reading lists, I've read the material — I want the dialectic, not the bibliography. And at the end tell me how confident you are in the document overall, and single out the one claim in it you're least sure about.
