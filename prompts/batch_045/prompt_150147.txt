I work as a software engineer on a biodiversity data platform. We ingest observation records, museum specimens and genome sequences from a few hundred partner institutions and expose them through one API, which means my team owns the schema: the fields for taxon, for range, for conservation status, for captive-bred versus wild, for hybrid status, and for the native/invasive flag. Every time we touch that schema I end up in an argument I don't have the vocabulary for, so I've been reading a little philosophy of science on the side, and I keep coming across the question of whether the categories the sciences use correspond to real joints in nature or are boundaries we draw for our own purposes. I'd like to know what the practice I'm encoding actually commits me to. Three things in it strike me as pulling in different directions. We spend enormous sums eradicating organisms that are judged to be in the wrong place — cane toads, rats on islands, lionfish in the Atlantic — with no concern at all for the individuals being killed, and the wrongness is entirely about where they are and how they got there; yet when a population is judged doomed by climate change, the standard proposal is to move it somewhere it has never occurred, deliberately, at public expense, with the same "humans transported this organism" fact now counting as therapy rather than pollution. A named subpopulation can be listed and protected, while the offspring of that same population and a common, unlisted relative are treated as contamination: protected before the cross, unprotected or actively removed after it. And the schema has no field at all for an animal whose genome has been edited to resemble an extinct form, because nothing in our ontology says whether such a thing is a member of a kind, a pretender, or a new one. To get unstuck we drafted an internal proposal: replace the categorical native/hybrid/pure flags with a continuous "lineage fidelity" number, the share of an individual's genome traceable by descent to a historical reference population, and make funding and legal protection a threshold on that number. I like its computational cleanliness, but it seems to sneak in a strong claim: that what matters sits in the genome, and that a genetically "pure" animal with no role left to play is worth more than a hybrid doing exactly the ecological work everyone says they care about.

So my question is what the existing practice — the listing, the killing, the protection, the transport, the purity rules — presupposes about kinds. Does it only make sense if species and subpopulations are natural kinds, real divisions out there that our labels track, the way elements seem to be? Or can it be reconstructed on a view where a species is a historical lineage, or a cluster of properties held together by some mechanism, or a lineage-plus-ecological-role, or even a convention that a community adopts for its purposes? I want the implicit theory stated explicitly, together with an honest separation of what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, and a map of the places where it is underdetermined and several rival theories would license exactly the same behavior. In particular: is there a single coherent notion of "natural" or "belongs here" under which the eradication of invasives and assisted migration are consistent, or is the practice just inconsistent? Does protecting the lineage while killing the hybrid and the invasive animal commit anyone to the claim that kinds, rather than the individuals composing them, are what have value — and if so, is that commitment avoidable? And concretely: if we abandoned the idea that these categories find real joints and treated every taxon in our database as a human-drawn boundary, what would have to change in conservation practice, and what would turn out to be only paperwork? I'd rather have the argument than a reading list.
