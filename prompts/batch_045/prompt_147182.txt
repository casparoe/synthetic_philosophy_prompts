Here is where I am. I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, writing on the normativity of logic, and I have become convinced of something I will call basic-inference skepticism. Lewis Carroll's 1895 Mind dialogue is usually read as a small puzzle about how to formalize inference. I read it as a genuine limitative result about justification: no argument can provide a non-circular, non-regressive reason to conform to a basic rule of inference such as modus ponens. The reason is structural rather than skeptical in the usual sense. Any justification would itself have to be deployed, and deploying it requires making a transition; so whoever has not yet made the transition can accept every premise offered — including any premise that states the rule, or states that the rule is truth-preserving, or states that accepting the premises commits one to the conclusion — and still withhold the conclusion. The demand re-arises at every level, and nothing in the series of added premises ever forces the move. I also think the standard escapes fail, and I want your verdict on my reasons for thinking so: (i) the rule-versus-premise reply, on which following a rule is an ability or disposition rather than assent to a further proposition, seems to me to relabel the phenomenon rather than answer the Tortoise, since he can coherently be described as lacking the disposition; (ii) the meaning-constitutive reply, on which the meanings of the logical constants settle which transitions are correct, seems to me to yield correctness without any purchase on the question of what reason the Tortoise has to care; (iii) the broadly pragmatist or vindicatory reply, on which deduction is warranted because conforming to it is reliably truth-preserving or because no alternative practice is available, strikes me as inheriting precisely the structure of the standard retreat about induction, and I am unsure whether that analogy is a defence of my position or a confession about its dialectical status.

5. Then the item I actually lose sleep over, which you should treat as a crux rather than as one more item on a list. Suppose we stop modeling acceptance as an all-or-nothing attitude and model it as credence. The demand that modus ponens makes then looks less like an additional premise than like a synchronic constraint on a credence function — for a material conditional, roughly, that credence in the consequent be at least the sum of one's credence in the antecedent and one's credence in the conditional, minus one. Constraints of that kind have fixed points in a way that added premises do not: a credal state can satisfy the constraint without containing any further proposition in its scope, and any further proposition one adds is already governed by it. So the regress may be an artifact of representing the Tortoise as assenting to propositions and demanding more of them, and it may dissolve when the attitude is graded and the requirement is global. My own reply is that a constraint is still a norm, and a sufficiently stubborn Tortoise can ask why he should bring his credences into line with it, whereupon the regress reappears one level up.
