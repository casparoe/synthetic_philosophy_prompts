I've just joined my department's comprehensive-exam committee for the first time — I sat the same exam myself two years ago, and I am now on the other side of the table, holding the epistemology question bank I inherited from a colleague who retired. Almost every closure item in that folder can be passed with the same three paragraphs: recite Nozick's two tracking conditions, run the familiar zoo case about the animal in the pen that might be a cleverly disguised mule, observe that tracking therefore fails closure under known entailment, and announce either that this is a fatal cost or that it is precisely the feature that disarms the Cartesian sceptic. I want to replace that with one self-contained, closed-book, ninety-minute question that a candidate who has only memorised that routine cannot get past a B on. It should not depend on having read any particular paper — no citations, no reading list, nothing that turns on who said what first.

The fragment I keep circling is one I have started calling route-relativity, and I would like the question to force candidates to work on it rather than to report it. Setup: the subject knows, by looking, that the animal in the pen is a zebra, and competently deduces that it is not a disguised mule. Taken one way the deduced belief does not track, because in the nearest worlds where it is false the subject still believes it; but suppose the subject instead deduces the very same conclusion from the same perceptual belief together with an independent auxiliary premise — say that the zoo's official inventory lists no mules on the premises, or that last month's DNA log for that enclosure came back clean — where the auxiliary premise is itself false in exactly the disguised-mule worlds and the subject's hold on it is safe rather than merely sensitive. If that is right, then whether the subject knows the conclusion depends on which route was taken to it, even though in both cases every premise is known and the entailment is the same. I think the interesting part is what follows: whether this is a genuine discovery about knowledge or an artefact of two features of Nozick's machinery — the relativisation of tracking to a method, and the fact that sensitivity constrains only the nearest worlds in which its own target is false, so that it says nothing about somewhat more remote false-worlds such as the disguised-mule ones — and, decisively, what happens when the tracking theorist tries to patch it. My suspicion is that any patch either collapses into a restatement of the theory's own verdicts, so that no closure principle has really been offered at all, or strengthens the condition enough to restore so much closure that the anti-sceptical payoff goes with it: you get to keep closure only by losing the claim that someone can know they have hands while failing to know they are not envatted.

What I need from you is the finished item. Give me the exam question itself, phrased so a candidate can answer it in ninety minutes without any secondary reading, and phrased so that it requires constructing and comparing cases rather than summarising a debate. Then give me a model answer of roughly six hundred words. Then give me a marking scheme out of twenty built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. Finally, state explicitly which moves separate an A answer from a B answer: I want the borderline to fall somewhere other than on how fluently the candidate can name the standard examples.
