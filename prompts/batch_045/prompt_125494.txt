I am writing the final essay for a third-year seminar on self-knowledge and the first person, and I want to write it on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day read through Moore's paradox. I would be grateful for a substantial reading that I can argue with rather than a summary: somewhere in the region of 1,200 to 1,500 words, at the level of a strong seminar paper rather than an introduction.

The attraction of the pairing, as I see it, is this. Stevens narrates in the first person, in a continuous present of reminiscence, and he repeatedly puts forward claims about his own past and his own allegiances in forms that he then hedges, qualifies, or quietly withdraws. But the memoir is retrospective, and the standard diagnosis is that the past-tense counterparts of the omissive and commissive sentences are not absurd at all, and that neither are the second- and third-person counterparts. So my central question is where, if anywhere, the genuinely Moorean phenomenon survives in this book, and what its surviving there tells us about what the paradox is a paradox of.

In particular I would like you to do the following.

First, say whether the strain in Stevens's narration is best located at the level of the sentence, the speech act, or the standpoint of self-ascription, and connect that to the two standard families of explanation: the assertion-theoretic one, on which assertion expresses belief or is governed by a norm of belief, knowledge, or reasonable belief; and the doxastic or transparency one, on which I settle whether I believe that p by considering whether p. A related wrinkle I would like addressed is that Stevens's narration is dense with first-personal knowledge claims of the form he knows that Lord Darlington was an honourable man, and if knowledge entails belief then such a claim already commits him to the very attitude he elsewhere disavows. Does that make his narration stranger than the ordinary Moorean sentence, or does it suggest that the paradox is really about assertion and not about the attitude at all?

Second, read two or three scenes closely and say in each case whether we are hearing a Moorean avowal, an ordinary report of a former belief, a case of self-deception, or something else again. I have in mind the episode of the two housemaids dismissed on Lord Darlington's instruction for being Jewish, which Stevens reports together with Miss Kenton's protest and his own compliance and then files as insignificant; the visits Miss Kenton pays to his pantry with flowers and the professional explanation he offers for his own reading matter; and the conversation on the seafront bench, where he concedes that Lord Darlington at least made his own mistakes and finds that he cannot claim even that for himself.

Third, assess a diagnostic I have been toying with. Suppose we run the Moorean test on the neighbouring attitude of acceptance rather than belief, in the technical sense of a voluntary, action-guiding policy rather than a truth-aiming state. If Stevens's posture towards the proposition that he served a great gentleman is acceptance rather than belief, then the analogue of the Moorean sentence should lose its absurdity, which would explain why his narration reads as strained rather than mad. Is the contrast between the two attitudes sharp enough to bear that weight, and does the novel adjudicate between them or merely illustrate both?

Fourth, tell me where the reading fails. If self-deception just is a matter of saying what one disbelieves, the Moorean apparatus may be redundant here; and if, as some hold, there is nothing absurd about believing the content of a Moorean sentence and the problem belongs to assertion alone, then a novel that gives us access to what a character thinks may be unable to instantiate the paradox at all, whatever its narrative technique.

Finally, say what you think the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments do not, including anything that follows from the reader's own position, since we occupy the peculiar standpoint of hearing his avowals in the first person while attributing belief to him in the third person.

I have the novel to hand, so I need no plot summary and no citations; please name and locate scenes rather than quoting them, and say plainly where you are going beyond what the text supports.
