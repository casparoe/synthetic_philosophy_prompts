I'm giving a talk at an AI lab next month to the interpretability team — mostly folks who spend all day on features, circuits and activation patching (swap activations between runs, see what changes) — plus a philosopher of mind who got dragged along and is a hardliner about the causal exclusion argument, and the team lead, who is a no-nonsense empiricist. Thesis: the exclusion argument doesn't go quiet when you swap the person for a language model, it becomes decidable. We can in principle write down the complete micro-causal story of any output — every weight, every activation — so if the argument is sound, then saying the model took the longer route because it judged the bridge wouldn't hold is telling a causally idle story about a redundant epiphenomenon. But when you steer or patch a mid-layer feature along a direction that reads like a propositional attitude, behaviour moves the way the attitude story predicts. I want to argue this isn't the overdetermination horn: the high-level variable isn't a second competing event, it's the same causal structure described at a grain where the counterfactuals are stable, so "the micro-level is causally sufficient" turns out to be a claim about grain, not about exclusion.
