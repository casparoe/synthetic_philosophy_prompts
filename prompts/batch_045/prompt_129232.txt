I work in mechanistic interpretability. My working vocabulary is the standard machine-learning one: parameters, activations, latents, features and dictionaries, probes, causal abstraction and interchange interventions, training and held-out distributions, out-of-distribution generalization, underspecification and non-identifiability, objective functions, ground-truth labels, world models, compression. I read and write papers that use these terms comfortably, and I have no formal training in philosophy.

A colleague told me that a problem I keep running into was already stated, twice, by Paul Benacerraf: once in a 1965 paper arguing that the natural numbers cannot be identified with any particular sets, and once in a 1973 paper arguing that we cannot simultaneously have a semantics for mathematical language of the ordinary referential kind and an epistemology of the causal kind that our accounts of knowledge elsewhere seem to require. Here is how I have reconstructed the two arguments, and I expect I am garbling them.

The first: there are many adequate ways of encoding the natural numbers as pure sets, the two textbook ones being the Zermelo and the von Neumann encodings. Both make every ordinary arithmetical statement come out true, but they disagree on further questions that arithmetic itself does not settle — on one encoding the set playing the role of 1 is a member of the set playing the role of 3, and on the other it is not. Because the encodings are indistinguishable by every standard internal to arithmetic, there is held to be no fact of the matter about which one the numbers really are, and the moral drawn is that numbers are not objects with intrinsic non-arithmetical properties at all but rather positions in a structure. The second: we want a theory of mathematical language on which its sentences have truth conditions fixed by reference to objects, in the same style as our best semantics for ordinary language, and we also want a theory of mathematical knowledge on which our beliefs are reliably connected to what they are about, since elsewhere we explain knowledge by way of some causal relation between knower and known. Abstract objects are causally inert, so the two demands cannot be met together; and if we repair the epistemology by tying truth conditions to proof, it stops being clear why those conditions should count as conditions of truth rather than of assertability.

The case from my own work that made my colleague think of this is the following. We trained two models, call them A and B, from different random seeds on the same corpus of formal proofs and informal mathematical prose, and both reach superhuman accuracy on a held-out theorem-proving benchmark with statistically indistinguishable error profiles. Our interpretability tools recover an invertible map from each model's internal encoding of numerals onto sets, and A's encoding satisfies the von Neumann recursion while B's satisfies the Zermelo recursion; both descriptions predict held-out behaviour equally well, and both generalize comparably. We then built a third system, C, which matches A and B on the benchmark but whose internals we can only describe as a compressed memo table plus pattern matching — no structure isomorphic to anything we recognize. A fourth system, D, was trained with an auxiliary objective rewarding representations isomorphic to the von Neumann encoding and does generalize better than A, B, and C on a harder distribution. My group argues about whether there is a fact of the matter about what A and B represent, whether C's success counts as the same achievement at all, and whether D's better generalization is evidence that its encoding is the right one, or only that it is the more useful one.

What I want is a systematic translation, not an explanation pitched at a beginner and not an analogy offered as an argument.

First, restate both of Benacerraf's arguments in my vocabulary, as precisely and as unflatteringly as you can: what in my vocabulary plays the role of reference, of truth, of causal contact, of reliability, of understanding. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call them translation; where you have to pick a term that already begs a philosophical question, say so.

Second, restage my A/B/C/D case as an instance of the identification argument, and tell me whether the instance is genuine or whether I am equivocating between my notion of two descriptions fitting the data equally well and the philosopher's notion of two reductions being equally adequate. Say explicitly what changes if I add a fifth system: a hand-written proof checker, never trained, whose correctness is guaranteed by construction and whose semantics were put there by its designers.

Third, the residue report. Which claims in the philosophical arguments have no counterpart in the vocabulary I actually use, and for each, is that because my vocabulary is missing a distinction it ought to have, or because the philosophical claim was less determinate than it looked? I am especially interested in two suspicions of my own, which I want you to rule on rather than restate: that my field's notion of non-identifiability is structuralism adopted as methodology without argument, and that benchmark accuracy is a notion of correctness that neither horn of the dilemma permits, since it is neither reference nor causal contact but disposition to agree with a verification procedure.

Take a definite position on each of these. Where you go beyond what the two 1965 and 1973 papers actually establish, mark that; where a philosopher of mathematics would dispute what you have just said, say what the dispute turns on. I am not asking for references or further reading — I can't follow them up and I want the argument, not the literature. What I do not want is a survey of the options with the verdict left to me; commit, and give me your reasons.
