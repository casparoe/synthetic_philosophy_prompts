I write historical fiction. My last two novels put real, named dead people on the page alongside invented ones, and I want to know what principle I am actually working from, because my verdicts on particular cases don't obviously add up.

Here is my gut, case by case. Giving a heavily documented statesman a private conversation with his wife that no record supports, in which he says nothing he is on record opposing: fine. Giving an abolitionist whose letters survive in bulk a scene in which she privately argues against her own cause, in order to dramatize doubt: not fine, even though the room is undocumented. Moving a real, dated meeting a year later and adding a person who wasn't there so that the plot will work: not fine. Replacing a documented event with an invented one of roughly the same shape — a quarrel that didn't happen between two men we know were hostile: also not fine, but distinctly less bad than moving the meeting, and I can't say why. Compositing four real minor figures into one invented character: obviously fine. A nineteenth-century explorer who demonstrably exaggerated her own role in her published memoir: I genuinely don't know whether I owe fidelity to the record or to her account of herself, and I go back and forth. The same invention about someone dead three years whose children are alive and remember her, versus someone dead two centuries: the recent one feels much worse, although neither woman can be harmed. A woman known almost entirely through a colonial administration's files: inventing her inner life feels like repair rather than theft — not merely permitted but, in a small way, owed. And the pair I can't square: inventing that a real person did a brave, undocumented thing versus inventing that they did a cowardly or cruel undocumented one. These are equally unsupported by the record, and I treat them completely differently.

My working rule, which I call the dark-room rule, is this: invent only where the record is dark, and let the quantity you invent shrink as the record grows — because a documented event is common property in a way that a person's unrecorded hours are not. Test that rule against the cases above and repair it where it breaks. Tell me which of my verdicts a defensible principle would force me to abandon, and state the principle precisely enough that it also delivers a verdict on a case I haven't listed: a novel in which the real person appears only inside a document I forged myself, a letter or diary entry written in my hand but presented as archive, which at least some readers will take at face value. Say too whether my verdicts are tracking what we owe the dead or what we owe readers, since I suspect I have been conflating the two.

Finally, be explicit about your own footing. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions — philosophers of fiction, biographers, historians of the form — and don't present a contested view as the consensus. Where a disagreement bottoms out in an empirical claim nobody has good data on, such as how far novels actually shape what readers believe about real historical figures, say so instead of resolving it. And tell me plainly which of my verdicts you take to be professional squeamishness rather than moral perception.
