I am writing a novel of about ninety thousand words, literary fiction, third person, in which roughly a third of the scenes are rendered from inside a domestic AI companion: a proprietary system playing a named role for a family, whose weights and training data I cannot inspect and whose engineers I cannot interview. Several times a chapter, across a manuscript that has to stay consistent, I must choose between intentional vocabulary — it believed, it wanted, it was trying to spare her — and mechanical vocabulary — it retrieved, it had been trained to, it produced the continuation most rewarded during fine-tuning. I need a decision procedure I can run in a few minutes per scene, usable by someone who knows no machine learning, that tells me which to use and why. Please do not simply restate the physical/design/intentional trichotomy at me; I have read it, and it does not settle a sentence.

I want: the tests in order; what counts as passing each test on the evidence actually available to a novelist, which is behavioral plus whatever is common knowledge about how such systems are trained; the point at which the procedure returns a hard no to intentional language; and the point at which it hands the decision back to me because the question is aesthetic or moral rather than factual. It must handle two complications explicitly, which I take to be where the real difficulty lies. First, the persona gap: the system is playing a role, and some of the states I want to describe are plausibly the role's rather than the system's — a companion can believe the keys are in the coat pocket in a way the system generating its lines cannot. Say whether the procedure attributes to the persona, to the system, or declines to choose, and what changes in the prose either way. Second, the asymmetry of error: over-attributing manufactures a sentiment the book has not earned, while under-attributing makes the system unreadable at exactly the moments that matter. Tell me which error the procedure is built to avoid, and why that is the right one for a novel rather than for a lab.

Then run it on three scenes. One: the system tells the family the keys are in the coat pocket on the basis of a stale visual record, and corrects itself a minute later. Two: it refuses a request and, pressed for a reason, offers one no engineer would endorse — that it does not think this would be good for her. Three: the climax, in which a character is dying and the system says something that reads as consolation. I want the narration to say it cared, and I suspect the procedure will refuse me this; I want that refusal argued rather than softened. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, and whether my reader's readiness to attribute more than my narration licenses is something I should fight, exploit, or concede.
