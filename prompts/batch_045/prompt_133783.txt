I write novels, and most mornings as a warm-up I build myself a Bongard problem — six boxes on the left that share some feature, six on the right that don't — because for me it's show-don't-tell in its purest form: if the rule is any good, a reader picks it up from the pictures and never has to be told. My gut about which of my own problems are actually any good is all over the place though, and I want to know what I'm going by.

- left convex, right concave: good problem
- small shape inside the big one vs outside: good
- even number of shapes vs odd: good, and I'm a little surprised it is
- total number of straight segments in the box is prime vs composite: cheating, even though it's exactly as countable as the last one
- exactly one shape touches the border of its box: good. But the logically identical version — zero shapes, or two or more, touch the border — I hate, and I can't say why, since its the same problem
- symmetric under a half turn: good, even though it takes a minute to see
- it's a triangle, OR a circle with a dot inside: bad, feels like two problems stapled together
- the two shapes overlap by more than half of the smaller one: good. Apex angle greater than 60 degrees: cheap. Both are just thresholds on a continuum
- looks like it's tipping over: I like it, even though I can't state it precisely, and even though I'd hate a rule like looks graceful
- same rule, two sets of examples: convex vs concave where the right side is all spiky stars — bad problem. The same rule where the right side includes a couple of smooth dented blobs that kill the obvious wrong guess — good problem. So it's not only the rule.

The one I keep flip-flopping on: every shape in the left box is different from every other one, on the right at least two match — a rule about something not happening.

So: is there one principle that gets all these, or do I have to pick between something baroque that saves every verdict and something simple that overturns one or two? Tell me which of my verdicts it can't save, and what it says about the case I go back and forth on and about one case I haven't thought of. Lead with the principle itself in the first sentence, then the reasoning.
