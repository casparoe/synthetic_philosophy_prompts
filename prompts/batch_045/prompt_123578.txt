I spent last evening arguing with an old university friend about whether taxation is theft. We got nowhere on the verdict, but I came away more interested in a move I kept catching him making, and, when I am honest, catching myself making in the opposite direction. I would like to know whether that move has a name; more than that, I would like it carved out precisely from the several things it resembles, including the ones I can already generate for myself.

Put abstractly, the move is this. A distribution of holdings that is thoroughly a product of law — contract enforcement, limited liability, planning permission, a land register, a currency, the rules of intestacy — gets treated as the natural or pre-political baseline, with the result that the tax imposed on top of it carries the entire burden of justification. What shaped the baseline is described as the background rather than as something anyone is doing to anyone. The description is not applied even-handedly. When a transfer runs in a direction my friend dislikes it is a taking, with agents and victims and a demand for reasons; when it runs the other way, or is old enough to have hardened into what people expect, it is part of the furniture of the world, and asking after its justification is treated as a category mistake. He will concede in one breath that, in any deep sense, nobody has pre-tax income, and in the next breath rank fiscal schemes by how far they depart from what people would have had anyway.

Three instances from the argument, in case the abstraction is doing too much work.

1. He said the distinctive thing about tax is that if you refuse, men with guns eventually come for you. I said the same is true if you occupy the field he calls his. He said that is different, that is the enforcement of a right, not a taking. I said the existence of the right was precisely what was in dispute, and this struck him as wilfully missing the point, though neither of us could say why.

2. On inheritance tax he said the state is stealing from the dead person's intentions. I said the power to dispose of property at death, and to have those intentions bind the living, is about as pure a creature of statute as one can find, so the taking is out of a pot the law itself placed there. He said that was sophistry, and then said the really objectionable thing was the rate.

3. After conceding the general point about baselines, he said tax is still unlike a fee or a fine, because it takes a share of whatever you have rather than a fixed sum: it tracks your holdings rather than any service rendered or wrong done. This intuition struck me as surviving the concession entirely intact, and I cannot tell whether it is a further, independent insight or the same move reappearing somewhere new.

What I am asking. Does the pattern have an established name, and if so, is it the same phenomenon as mine or merely adjacent to it? The candidates I can think of myself are: status quo bias, except that my friend is not remotely conservative — he wants the status quo changed in half a dozen directions — and in any case that work seems descriptive, whereas I am after something normative; the baseline problem in accounts of coercion; the slogan to the effect that there is no such thing as pre-tax income, which someone tells me comes from a book about ownership I have not read and cannot obtain; older Marxist-sounding talk of the naturalisation of the status quo; and motte-and-bailey, which I take it is not, since there is no strong claim being quietly swapped for a weak one but rather a single claim, with what shifts being which parts of the world count as actions at all.

I have been privately calling it baseline laundering, but I do not want a slogan to wave about at the next argument, and I would rather not be handed a reading list, since I have no library access and am writing nothing. What I want is the shape of the thing: a definition crisp enough to have genuine boundary cases; a verdict on whether any version of the asymmetry is actually defensible, that is, whether there is a principled reason to place the burden of justification on changes rather than on continuities, or on share-takings rather than on fee-takings, which does not already presuppose the baseline it is meant to defend; a diagnostic test I could apply to an argument to tell whether the move is being made, together with an account of where that test misfires, including the cases where someone is reasoning perfectly well and I would be wrong to accuse them of it; and finally, some view on whether my third instance is the same failure mode or a different one that merely travels alongside it. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and define the thing yourself, distinguishing it from each of the near misses above.
