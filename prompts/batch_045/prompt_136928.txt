So I've somehow ended up on the committee at our hospital that signs off on clinical ML tools (long story, I said yes before I understood what it involved), and at the same time I've been having a running argument with a friend in ML who thinks the whole "but does it actually understand" question is empty and that we should just talk about benchmark performance. So I went back and re-read that Dennett material on the two strange inversions — Darwin's, where purpose-looking structure gets explained without any prior purpose-haver, and Turing's, where comprehension turns out to be a late achievement assembled out of sub-personal machinery that doesn't comprehend anything at all — and I find both of them enormously persuasive, but I cannot get them to sit still next to the rest of what I believe. Here's roughly the pile, as best I can write it down:

1. Darwin's inversion: I never need a designer or a prior comprehender to explain purpose-y structure. The "reasons" we read off an organism are retrospective summaries of what selection did, not what did the work.
2. Turing's inversion: comprehension isn't a precondition of competence, it's what competence occasionally gets built into — and any comprehension you point to decomposes, when you actually look, into competences that don't comprehend.
3. Aboutness is cheap: given (1), the fact that some internal state is about stuff out in the world is fixed by the selection/optimisation history that installed it. No subject required, nothing spooky, no homomorphism to a homunculus.
4. Stance instrumentalism: whether I attribute belief, desire or understanding to something is settled by whether the attribution pays off predictively for me. There isn't some further fact of the matter about whether it "really" understands that the stance is latching onto.
5. My verdict on the thing I actually have to vote about: current LLMs are competent without comprehending. They don't understand what they output. And I want that as a claim about them, not about me and my explanatory convenience — I don't want it to collapse into "somewhat less understanding than a human," I think there's a line there and not just a gradient.
6. My own attempt to draw that line, which I've been calling the provenance condition: a system counts as comprehending only if it got its competence by being sensitive to the reasons why the thing was right, not merely by being reinforced for getting it right. I like this because it seems to respect (2) without making comprehension magic.
7. And the practical one I have to defend at the meeting on Tuesday: for anything that touches a patient, we keep a human who comprehends in the loop, and "the model beats the residents on the benchmark" is not by itself an answer to me.

Can you audit this for me? I'd like the genuine tensions ordered by how bad they are rather than all flagged as equally serious, and for each one: what exactly has to give, what the cheapest available repair is, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list. Please also tell me which of the apparent tensions actually dissolve on inspection — my friend says (1) and (4) can't both be true and I think she's wrong about that, and I have a nagging feeling that (2) and (7) are fine together too, but I honestly can't tell. And then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the mess at once. Two things I'd rather not get: a reading list, and "it depends what you mean by understanding" as a stopping point — I want you to pick a reading of it and then say what follows. One more worry I can't place: if (6) is right, isn't it kind of awkward that LLM training data is basically nothing but recorded human reason-giving, which would make them among the most reason-trained systems anyone has ever built? I don't know whether that's a problem for (6), for (5), or for something further back like (1) — tell me which.
