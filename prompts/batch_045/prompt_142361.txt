I have spent about ten years writing about the epistemic case for democracy, and for much of that time I have leaned on the Condorcet jury theorem in its textbook form: if each voter is more likely than not to be right and their errors are independent, the probability that a majority is right climbs toward one as the group grows. Four months ago I served on a twelve-person felony jury, and I have not been able to read that result the same way since.

The case was an assault with a weapon; the evidence was inconsistent testimony and one poor video. We split nine to three on the first ballot. I was one of the three. I changed my vote on the second afternoon, and I still cannot tell whether I was persuaded by something the foreman said or worn down by two days in a small room with eleven people who were already sure.

Next month I am to give twenty minutes of testimony to our state's criminal rules advisory committee, which has a draft rule that would permit six-person juries in non-capital felony trials. The case for it is cost, backlog, and the difficulty of seating and holding twelve people through a two-week trial. The chair tells me the constitutional floor is settled and is not on the table; what the committee wants from me is whether anything is epistemically lost by halving the jury.

My testimony is not a simple defence of twelve, and I would rather it did not sound like one. The argument runs like this. Taken naively, the theorem says little in favour of twelve: at a per-juror accuracy of 0.6, and using odd sizes to dodge ties, majority accuracy is roughly 0.75 at eleven and roughly 0.68 at five — a loss of about seven points, real but much smaller than the rhetoric suggests, because of diminishing returns. But the independence assumption is false of juries in the most obvious way: jurors talk to each other for two days, and talking is exactly the mechanism that makes their errors correlated. If residual errors are equicorrelated at ρ, the effective size of a group of n is n/(1 + (n−1)ρ): at ρ = 0.2, eleven jurors behave like about 3.7 independent ones and five like about 2.8; at ρ = 0.4, like about 2.2 and 1.9. The gap between twelve and six nearly disappears, and I intend to say plainly that I do not know whether ρ in a real jury room is 0.1 or 0.6. So there is a trade-off the theorem does not model — deliberation raises each juror's competence and destroys their independence at the same time, and nothing in the theorem says where on that curve a jury ought to sit. My conclusion is that the committee should not decide this on accuracy grounds at all; it cannot, because the parameters are unmeasurable. It should decide on the shape of the distribution of outcomes: a smaller panel is a rougher instrument, with more variable verdicts, more deadlocks, and results more sensitive to which particular six people happened to be seated; and on whether the panel is likely to contain at least one person whose doubt is not correlated with everyone else's. Whether the institution preserves holdouts. I was one, and I am not confident I survived. If they do cut to six, my minimum recommendations are element-by-element verdict forms, so that it is explicit which propositions are being aggregated — a majority on each element and a majority on the verdict can come apart — and some formal standing for a declared minority of two, so that dissent is not purely a matter of stamina.

The room matters more than the argument, I think. The committee is: a presiding judge of twenty-five years who believes juries work and that academics model the one thing juries do not do (we deliberate, he will say, we do not walk in and vote); a second judge who is an enthusiast for empirical legal studies and will want effect sizes; a senior public defender who will say that the target is not accuracy but the allocation of the risk of error under a burden of proof, and that my model assumes a fact of the matter that contested trials often do not have; a victims'-rights advocate who will care about finality and about hung juries as a second injury; a professor of evidence who will point out that a verdict is a composite judgment over elements; a law-and-economics professor who has in fact heard of the jury theorem and dislikes it, and who will want to know where my p and my ρ come from and why, if the theorem is right, we are not using twenty-four; a state legislator who thinks twelve is a number inherited from people who counted in dozens; and a court administrator who mostly listens.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defence. Give me the eight or so objections I should expect, in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased the way that particular person would put it — I want to hear their vocabularies, not yours — and for each one a two-minute reply together with a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which one or two things I should concede in my first two minutes, before anyone can say I buried them. Tell me which single reply has to land if the testimony is to move anyone at all. And give me the question I am hoping nobody asks. Two constraints: do not hand me citations, studies, or a reading list — I cannot use them in twenty minutes of oral testimony and this committee stops listening the moment an academic starts citing papers, so I would rather have the objections framed conceptually. And if any of the numbers in my sketch are wrong, say so before you rely on them; the arithmetic is the part I am least able to check standing up.
