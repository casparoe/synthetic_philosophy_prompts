I teach a short optional module on practical rationality to final-year joint-honours students in economics and philosophy, and I have always used the sunk cost fallacy as a twenty-minute warm-up on the way to something else. This year I want to give it a full session, because the more I look at it the more the standard treatment seems to me to conceal where the real disagreement lies. The textbook definition — a cost is sunk when it is unrecoverable whatever you now do, therefore a rational chooser leaves it out of the calculation — is normally presented as though the only open question were whether people conform to it, when most of what is interesting, it seems to me, lies in what that definition is even measuring, and in whether the cases in which people appear to conform least are cases to which the definition applies at all.

What I would like from you is a decision tree I can hand out, taking a student from the single commitment everyone in the room will grant — that in a stripped-down case, where the past expenditure has no causal bearing on future payoffs, no evidential bearing on how good the project is, no audience, and no further decisions hanging on it, it cannot count in favour of continuing — down to a named leaf. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of isms: give me the branch questions first, say how many combinations of answers they admit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and tell me which combinations are unoccupied and, for each of those, whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Each node should be a question about a concrete case, phrased so that someone who has never heard the labels can answer it: what separates the branches should be the structure of the case, not the vocabulary.

The cases I want covered are these, and you should feel free to add: the half-built telescope (£40m already spent on a mirror, £15m more to finish, expected scientific value of the finished instrument £10m, nothing recoverable if abandoned); the same telescope where the extra £15m is being given by an anonymous donor and no one will ever learn of the decision, so there is no audience and no future choice to protect, only the question of whether the mirror gets used; the string quartet that has played together for twenty-two years, where every member could earn more elsewhere and the sound itself would not survive a substitution, so the shared history looks either like a cost that has been incurred or like part of the good being produced; the half-built reactor you did not commission but inherited from a predecessor, so that no plan, commitment or reputation of yours is attached to the spending; and the case where the agent suspects her judgement now is worse than it was when she began, and continues partly in deference to the person who started. Leaves should include at minimum: the strict view that there are no exceptions; a view on which apparent sunk-cost reasoning is really rationality given information the experimenter failed to hold fixed; a view on which it is about what continuing signals or commits one to in the eyes of others; a view on which it is a device for self-government against one's own future weakness or shifting tastes; a view on which the past investment is partly constitutive of the good aimed at rather than a mere means to it, and so is not sunk at all; and the view that the experimental literature has never shown a fallacy, because in every setting something else was going on. Where the tree cannot separate two leaves, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction; tell me which single question does most of the work and which is nearly inert; and name each leaf descriptively, adding the usual label only where you are confident of it — do not spend the answer trying to pin down who defended what.

Close with a short note on where this tree is most likely to be wrong: which branch question you would expect to collapse under pressure, which leaf you had to stretch or partly invent in order to fit it in, and what empirical finding or argument would make you redraw the split at the top rather than merely add a branch lower down. Please write in British English.
