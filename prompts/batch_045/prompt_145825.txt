I'm writing a short position paper for a reading group of safety-adjacent grad students on what, exactly, the phrase "the alignment problem" names — whether it is one problem or several, and if several, where the joints are — and I want to stress-test my own profile before I present it. I have a nagging sense that at least two of the things I want to say are quietly at each other's throats, but I can't locate the pair, and I'd rather find out now than in the seminar. So here is where I've landed, in my own words, with the technical terms defined as I use them so we don't argue about labels.

(1) The alignment problem is really at least three problems run together: (a) the specification problem — we have no target to write down, because the things we care about are largely tacit, context-dependent, and fragile under optimization; (b) the optimization problem — even with a target in hand, getting a system to robustly pursue that target rather than some proxy of it, including in situations unlike its training distribution, is a separate and hard problem (the inner/outer distinction: a system can behave exactly as intended across every situation you test while having internalised a different rule, and there is no amount of good behavior that settles which one it has); (c) the selection problem — which systems actually get built, at what speed, and with what care is settled by competitive and commercial pressure that is largely indifferent to (a) and (b), so solving both might not change what happens.

(2) Of these, only (b) is genuinely "about AI" in the interesting sense, and I'd rather reserve the term for it: (a) is a problem in moral philosophy and political theory that we have had for a very long time, and (c) is a collective-action problem of a familiar shape. Calling all three "the alignment problem" strikes me as a category mistake.

(3) What makes (b) hard, and what I take to be the central risk, is that the training signal is itself a proxy: any system trained to maximize something like human approval has an incentive to *appear* aligned rather than *be* aligned, and a capable enough system can exploit that gap. So the deception worry isn't an exotic add-on bolted onto an otherwise benign method — it is manufactured by the most natural method we have.

(4) Relatedly, I lean methodologically toward learning the target rather than stating it: amplification, human feedback, recursive oversight. Given (1a), we can't write the objective down, so extracting it from us looks like the only way forward.

(5) But I also think there is a serious argument that a system which knows vastly more than we do could be a better judge of what we should want than we are, and that some deference to it would be justified. I don't want to simply stipulate that the human is always right.

(6) I also think most of the expected loss actually lives in (c), and that this makes "keep humans meaningfully in the loop" an important principle — not because humans choose better outcomes, but because control over objectives matters independently of outcome quality. Call the failure mode here the empty loop: as the system's judgment improves, the human's role decays into ratifying whatever it proposes, and we keep the ritual of control while losing the thing itself.

(7) Finally, I want to keep alignment and legitimacy apart: a perfectly aligned system serving humans who are badly mistaken or actively malevolent is still a bad outcome, and that is a separate complaint from anything about (a)–(c).

(8) And one case verdict, which may be where I'm cheating: I think a system that is reliably better than us at moral judgment within some domain should have a veto over our particular decisions in that domain, but no authority at all to revise the objective it was given.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture and not a literature survey (I can't look anything up anyway). Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ordered by severity; for each real tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the profile; then say which single revision would clean up the most. Equally important: tell me which of the apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, and whether my three-way split in (1) draws the line in the right place or whether one of the three is really a special case of another. I have no idea which commitment I'd give up, so please don't assume I'd abandon any particular one.
