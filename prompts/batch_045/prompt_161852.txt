I'm a backend engineer, about ten years in, mostly Python and Go, and an embarrassing amount of my job is legacy-code archeology: digging through git history and ancient design docs trying to work out *why* some module exists before I delete it. No philosophy background beyond some Dawkins and Dennett I read in college and a lot of lurking on alignment-adjacent forums.

I got pulled into a thread the other day about whether it's even coherent to say a trained model has a "function", and someone pasted in a handout from a philosophy-of-biology reading group contrasting two accounts: an "etiological" one and a "causal-role" one. Paraphrasing crudely from memory, the handout said something like: on the etiological account a trait's function is the effect it was *selected for* — the heart's function is pumping blood because pumping blood is why hearts got kept around — while on the causal-role account a trait's function is just whatever it currently contributes to some larger system's capacities, which (the handout noted, as an objection) seems to make the heart's thumping noise just as much a function as the pumping. My immediate reaction was that the first one is just `git blame` with extra steps — "the function is whatever effect caused this thing to be here" — and the second is just "read the call graph and see what breaks if you rip it out". But people in the thread kept insisting there was something more, especially around the words *normativity* and *malfunction*, and I genuinely couldn't tell whether they were onto something deep or just being vague in a way that sounds deep.

It matters to me concretely, because I have three things in my own codebase I can't classify. (1) There's a `normalize_prices()` helper that was written in 2016 to strip currency symbols off a vendor feed; that vendor is long gone, but the function also happens to dedupe rows, and three downstream services now silently depend on the dedupe. Is deduping "its function" now? (2) There's a whole retry/backoff module whose entire job is keeping other modules working when they'd otherwise fail — a second-order part, like a garbage collector or the test suite itself. (3) There's a codegen step that regenerates the build config, and a config that regenerates the codegen step; the system builds its own successor, and in that case "what it does right now" and "why it's still here" seem to collapse into the same thing. A guy in the thread also floated a third option I haven't stopped thinking about: the function of a thing is just whatever the person who wrote it intended, or whatever the current maintainer would defend as its point if you asked. That felt more natural to me than either of the two in the handout.

So: restate the etiological vs. causal-role debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — call graphs, dependency direction, git history and blame, specs vs. implementation, "would break if deleted", refactors, regressions, dead code that tests keep alive, self-hosting compilers, intentional authorship — and run all three of my cases plus the maintainer-intention variant through both accounts. Then I want the honest part: what translated cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no expression at all in a software-engineering vocabulary. Specifically, is the thing philosophers call malfunction or normativity — a heart that *fails* to pump, not merely one that doesn't — actually sayable in my terms, or is my vocabulary missing a concept? And is the bit about self-reproducing systems a real convergence between the two accounts, or am I conflating two things they keep apart? I'd also like your read on whether my `git blame` theory is basically right and the disagreement is smaller than it looks, or whether it's missing something.
