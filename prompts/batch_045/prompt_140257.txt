I am in my first year of a PhD and I am drafting the claims section of my first paper, a seed-variance study of a small transformer trained on modular addition. My advisor is away for another ten days, and I would rather put a worked-out position in front of her than send a vague email, because she is exacting about one word in particular, representation, which she has struck out of three of my drafts already. I would like you to reconstruct the rule she appears to be running from her past verdicts, tell me what it commits her to in the case I am about to describe, and tell me what I can safely write.

Her verdicts so far are these. In a one-layer transformer trained on a plus b modulo 113, the embedding of the sum lies on a circle, rotating that embedding by k shifts the output by k, and the effect holds for sums we never used when we calibrated the rotation; she allowed that the model represents the sum as an angle on a circle, and put the words as an angle into the draft herself. In a network trained to predict legal moves in a board game, a probe recovers the board occupancy from the activations and interventions on the recovered directions change the predictions the way occupancy requires, again on held-out positions; she allowed that the model represents the board. In a language model, a probe recovers the gender of a document's author with high accuracy, but steering along the recovered direction changes nothing about the continuations and nothing in training had anything to do with authorship; she refused, on the grounds that this is information sitting in the activations rather than something the model represents. In another language model, ablating a direction shifts outputs in a way that correlates with whether the sentence is in the passive voice, but the effect is noisy, vanishes under a redesigned intervention, and is better explained as a length or surprisal signal; she refused again, saying that a confound is not a representation. Finally, in a small multilayer perceptron, the variable of interest is not linearly decodable at all, yet a smooth nonlinear readout recovers it and intervening along the gradient of that readout steers behaviour exactly as predicted on held-out inputs; she allowed it, remarking that representations need not be linear.

Here is the new case. We trained the same architecture on the same modular-addition task from forty random seeds. All forty reach essentially the same loss and agree with one another on every input we test. Twelve of them show the ring: circular order in the embedding space, distances that track the differences between sums, steering that generalizes. The other twenty-eight show nothing you would call an angle: the embeddings fall into clusters with no circular order, and the distances between cluster centroids carry no information about how far apart the sums are. Yet probes recover the sum almost perfectly in these models too, steering along the probe direction moves the output by the right amount, and this generalizes to held-out sums just as it does in the ring models. So the question is whether she will let us write one sentence covering all forty, of the form every one of the forty models learns a representation of the sum, or only the twelve.
