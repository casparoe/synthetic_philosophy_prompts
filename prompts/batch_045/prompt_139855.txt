I am preparing an application for a six-month single-investigator junior research fellowship — no lab, roughly twenty hours a week, and I am comfortable with undergraduate statistical mechanics, elementary information theory, and writing my own simulations. I need a two-page research proposal, and what I want is for a half-formed idea of mine to be turned into something a selection committee would actually fund.

The idea. The standard way of exorcising Maxwell's demon locates the compensatory cost in the demon's memory: measurement can in principle be carried out reversibly, but the demon must eventually clear its record, and clearing it dissipates. I want to contest the framing rather than the result. That framing treats the demon as a device with a memory that is written, held, and then reset, as though the only interesting event in the life of a bit were its deletion. But a demon operating continuously on a streaming gas can be charged for at least three different things: acquiring a record, keeping that record distinguishable from thermal noise while it is still being used, and disposing of it. The second of these is the one I never see treated as a separate item, and in the proposal I want it given a name and an operational definition — I have been calling it the retention cost, but I would rather the proposal define it properly than inherit my label. My suspicion is that once the memory is finite and noisy, retention rather than erasure is the binding cost, and that the quantity the second law is really tracking is not how much information the demon has but the throughput of a noisy channel: the rate at which usable correlations can be moved from the gas into the control of the trapdoor.

The working model I have in mind, which the proposal should adopt and motivate: a discrete-time Szilard-type setup in which one molecule arrives per step, the demon has an n-bit memory whose bits flip independently with some fixed probability per step, and at each step the demon chooses which protocol to run on the trapdoor given what its memory currently says. Crucially, the demon is permitted to let bits decay rather than pay to erase them, so erasure becomes a special case of a more general choice among refreshing, overwriting, and abandoning a record.

Three distinctions I want the proposal to keep apart throughout, and to argue are genuinely distinct rather than three names for one thing: the Shannon entropy of the demon's memory; the mutual information between the memory and the incoming molecule; and the work-value of a bit, meaning the expected work extractable from it given the class of protocols the demon is permitted to run. The central question is which of these appears in the correct bound on extractable power.

There is one further wrinkle I want addressed rather than smoothed over. Because the demon's own memory decays, the demon's record is systematically stale, and it may need to track the reliability of its own bits in order to use them well. Tracking reliability is itself information that must be stored in the same decaying memory, which invites a regress. I would like the proposal to say whether that regress is real, whether it terminates, and whether the second law can be read as the principle that cuts it off.

What the proposal should contain, in this order:

1. A precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer — ideally a trichotomy along the lines of: retention cost is reducible to erasure cost in every physically realizable protocol; retention cost is irreducible and yields a genuinely new finite-memory bound; or the answer is model-relative, in which case the interesting result is a lesson about which modeling choices carry the weight.

2. Why the existing settlements leave the question open. I mean the erasure-based resolution, the mutual-information budget or accounting version, and the fluctuation-theorem treatments. Characterize these as positions and arguments; do not cite anything, and do not write a literature review — I will supply references myself.

3. The method, month by month across six months, and for each part what is derived analytically, what is only simulated, and what is left as a conjecture. Include the parameter regimes worth sweeping and what a simulation would have to show to be evidence rather than illustration.

4. What the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them. I want the erasure-based answer to be able to win: do not write this as though my suspicion were already established.

5. An honest failure section: say what would count as the project having failed, and in particular whether the risk that every sharp bound depends on arbitrary choices of decay model or coarse-graining is fatal or merely a limitation to report.

6. A scope assessment for six months of one person's part-time work, including what should be cut, and an explicit statement of why the project stays entirely within the classical setting rather than moving to quantum probes, entangled ancillas, or anything involving discord.

7. A closing paragraph on why this is philosophy rather than a simulation exercise: specifically, what the result would show about whether the claim that information is physical is a substantive identity or a bookkeeping convenience, and about whether informational formulations of the second law are laws of nature or constraints on the situation of an agent embedded in the system it acts on.

Write it in the register of a grant application: prose throughout, no bullet lists of headings in the final document, and a length that would fit two single-spaced pages.
