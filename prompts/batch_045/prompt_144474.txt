I want a number, and more than the number: I want the decomposition that produces it, because I have a standing disagreement with a friend that I cannot locate, and I suspect the disagreement is not really about the headline claim.

Setup. Call me A. A is a human person in their thirties, not a twin, produced in the ordinary way by the fusion of a particular sperm S and a particular egg E, carried to term by the mother who produced E. The proposition I want your credence on is this: A could have come from different gametes — i.e. there is a possible world in which A exists and in which the gametes whose fusion initiated the organism that is (or now constitutes) A were not numerically identical with S and E. Read different as numerical distinctness, not qualitative difference: a distinct sperm cell whose haploid genome is base-pair-for-base-pair the same as S's counts as a different sperm for my purposes. Hold fixed everything downstream that can be held fixed — the same uterine environment, the same developmental history as far as the difference in gametes allows.

Give me a headline credence for that claim, read permissively: could A have come from numerically different gametes, however similar those gametes are in every other respect? Then give me a credence for each rung of the following ladder, because I suspect the interesting structure is in the grain rather than at the top:

1. Gametes numerically distinct from S and E, genomes base-pair identical to the actual ones, same parents, same everything else.
2. As in 1, except the sperm's genome differs from S's at a single base pair, in a way that makes no developmental difference at all.
3. Same mother and father, but a different recombination outcome: the sperm carries a haploid genome that differs from S's in the ordinary way in which one sperm of the same man differs from another.
4. Nuclear genomes as in the actual case, but the egg is a different cell: the mother's nuclear material was transferred into an enucleated donor egg, so the mitochondrial DNA and the cytoplasm that carried the zygote through its first divisions came from a third party rather than from E. This is a live clinical technique, so treat it as a real counterfactual, not a fantasy.
5. Ordinary conception by the same parents on the same night, but with a numerically different pair of their gametes: the different hour, different gametes case.
6. Gametes from different parents: A born of a different mother, or of a different father.

Now the decomposition. I want the sub-questions your headline number is a function of, a rough probability for each with a one-line justification, and the arithmetic that combines them — and I want you to say explicitly whether you are treating those terms as independent, since I suspect several of them are not. Here are the sub-questions I can see, but add, split, or replace them as you think the structure demands:

- Is there a determinate fact of the matter about de re modal claims concerning concrete individuals at all, or is transworld identity to some degree stipulative? If the latter, say what your number is a credence in: the truth of the claim, or what the correct convention would deliver.
- Person or organism? Does the answer change depending on whether A names a psychological continuant or a human animal, and if you think A is an animal that began at or around gamete fusion, does gamete-origin essentialism follow from some general thesis about the essential origins of organisms, or is it an extra claim needing its own support?
- Grain: what exactly is the candidate essential property — originating from these very cells, from cells with these very genomes, from cells produced by these very parents, from a fusion event at that time? Is there a principled stopping point, or only a stipulation that resists the sorites?
- The location of the difference. Everyone grants that A survives a single base-pair substitution occurring naturally in the zygote shortly after fusion. If A also survives the same substitution located in the sperm instead, then the modal difference between rung 1 and rung 2 has to be carried by the identity of the cells rather than by anything about their contents. Is that coherent, or does it show that the cells are doing work that nothing else supports? How much does this consideration move you?
- Twinning. A zygote can divide into two embryos, and on most views it is then identical with neither of the resulting children; and A could very easily have been one of two. Does that push the essential origin back from the zygote to the gametes, and does it make the modal claim unstable under small changes in when the organism begins?
- The donor-egg case in rung 4. If origin essentialism about the egg is read at the level of the whole cell, cytoplasm included, then the child born after mitochondrial replacement is not the child who would have been born from the same nuclear material left in situ. Is that a reductio of the whole-cell reading, evidence for a nuclear-genome reading, or a case where our intuitions carry no weight?
- The epistemology of modality. I can imagine a world in which a qualitatively identical sperm and egg fuse, and the classic origin argument derives much of its force from denying that this kind of conceivability is a guide. How much of your number rests on a general view about how we come to know modal facts?

Finally, three meta-questions. Which term in your decomposition are you least confident in, and what would move it? Which sub-question, if it flipped, would move your headline number the most? And: my credence on the headline is about 0.6, my friend's is about 0.1, and neither of us can find the joint — given your decomposition, which sub-question is our disagreement most likely about?

One constraint: nothing in your answer should depend on who argued what, where, or when. I do not want the literature on this, and I do not want anything that rests on your having read it accurately. I want the argument and the arithmetic.
