I work on trust and safety infrastructure for a mid-sized chat product; my actual training is in compilers and service architecture, and my only exposure to philosophy is a long-running argument with a friend in linguistics who keeps telling me that the interesting question about slur words is not what they refer to but what they *do*. We are at a design fork and the argument has become load-bearing. Option A treats the derogatory word the way a lexer treats a reserved or forbidden keyword: I will call the token W, and on this design the occurrence of W anywhere in the stream is caught by a pattern match and either blocked or rewritten, with no attention to syntactic position, speaker, or audience. Option B leaves the token in the stream but attaches derogation to the message object as structured out-of-band metadata — a tag, alongside provenance fields — so that downstream ranking, reply generation and logging can read it while the truth-conditional content of the utterance (who was said to be what) passes through untouched. Option C is my colleague's: stop special-casing W entirely, treat it as an ordinary word, and let a separate downstream classifier deal with whatever harm actually materializes; he says the offense is all pragmatics and consequences and there is nothing for us to encode at the token level. My friend says the three options secretly commit us to three different theories, and that the way to find out is to run them against cases. So here is my test suite: W used as a greeting between two members of the group it targets; W appearing in reports, quotations and mentions, as when a user writes that someone else called them W, or in a seminar transcript or a court exhibit; W inside negation and other embeddings, in sentences like no Ws are ever on time or is it true that any W applied; W with no target at all, as an insult aimed at a fictional character, at a robot, or as a bare intensifier; W in a variety of English where it is ordinary in-group slang rather than a slur; and W sitting in a scraped training corpus or a dead account's archived messages, where there is no speaker with intentions and nobody is reading.

What I would like is a translation, not an opinion. The philosophical terrain as I half-understand it has roughly these stops: that the derogatory component is part of the word's semantic content but not part of what the sentence says, so that it survives negation and embedding while leaving truth conditions alone; that it is a presupposition or a conventional implicature of a structurally similar kind; that it is not encoded content at all but an effect of using a word that is prohibited, so that the offense lives in the act of utterance and in the norms governing it; and that in-group reclamation changes something about the speech act without changing the word's meaning. Please restate each of these as precisely as you can in the vocabulary I actually reason in: tokens and lexing versus parsing, semantic values, return values versus side effects, effect systems and exceptions, static versus dynamic semantics, type annotations that are checked but erased, pragmas and attributes and decorators, lint rules and deprecation warnings, escaping and quoting as the machinery of mention versus use, scoping and namespaces, who owns the specification and who has standing to change it, backwards compatibility, and provenance and data lineage. For each position, give me the closest structure in that vocabulary, then tell me what you had to bend to make it fit, then tell me what has no counterpart at all. As a sanity check I want the translation to earn its keep on the test suite: for each case, say what each encoding predicts, and in particular say which position entails that W is still derogatory in the corpus case where there is no speaker and no audience, and whether my vocabulary has any slot for that answer.

The part I actually care about is the residue report. A few things strike me as possibly untranslatable and I want you to say whether they are. First, authority: in a programming language, the meaning of a token is settled by a specification and a maintainer, and reclamation looks like a fork by people who do not own the repository — is my framework's biggest failure here that it has no notion of contested authority over meaning, or is that a real feature of slurs that the philosophy is right to take seriously? Second, the harmed party: my vocabulary can express a downstream consumer misreading a tag, or a filter failing to fire, but I do not think it can express a hearer being wronged — and if it cannot, is that a defect in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical notion of derogation is doing less work than it appears to? Third, the difference between a deprecation warning and a norm: one is a coordination device with a migration path, the other seems to bind whether or not anyone is coordinating. And fourth, the corpus case again, where I suspect the whole analogy to an executing program quietly breaks down. So: what does not survive the translation, and for each piece of residue, is the honest verdict that my framework is too thin, or that the philosophical claim was never as clear as it looked? Please do not give me a content-moderation policy recommendation; I want the translation, its failure modes, and, at the end, your view on which of our three designs each position would actually commit us to and what would break if we shipped it.
