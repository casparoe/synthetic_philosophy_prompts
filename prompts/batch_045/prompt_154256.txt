I'm a recently retired GP, and for the past year I've been going to a philosophy reading group at my local library — mostly retired people, plus two students who do most of the talking. We've been working through free will, and I've come away with the suspicion that the debate is mostly about a question I don't actually care about. Nearly everything we've looked at seems to be asking whether people can be blamed or punished, or whether anyone could have done otherwise in exactly the circumstances they were in. Neither of those is what keeps me awake when I wonder whether my life has been mine. So I want to ask about the thing I do care about, and whether it has a name.

Here is roughly its shape. Last spring I was trying to decide whether to take a job abroad, and I noticed that I kept trying to work out what I was going to decide by adding up how much I cared about each consideration — and that from the inside this felt not like predicting a settled fact about myself, but like doing something that had not yet come out one way or the other. A friend who knows me well said, oh, you'll obviously go, you always do this sort of thing. What bothered me wasn't that the prediction might be false, and it certainly wasn't that it was unflattering; it was the suggestion that the matter was already settled before I went through the process of weighing it. I'd have been just as bothered had he been right. What makes me think I'm after something specific rather than merely confused is how local the worry is. Other people predicting my unreflective reactions doesn't trouble me at all: if my wife says I'll bristle when my brother raises politics, I take that as useful information and feel no less free for it. It is predictions about what I'll conclude after weighing reasons that grate. And it isn't ignorance I'm after. Another friend suggested I simply refrain from guessing my own decisions, which missed the point entirely: not knowing doesn't help if the answer is already fixed by everything about me — my character, my values, the reasons I have so far. I want the answer not to be fixed by all of that. But I don't want the dice-roll version either: if someone told me my deliberation terminates in a coin flip somewhere in my head, I'd take that to be no better than the prediction — worse, in fact.

The bits of vocabulary I've picked up feel close but wrong. Whatever people mean by being responsive to reasons seems too easy to satisfy: a machine that reliably adjusted its output to the reasons fed into it would qualify, and it would also be perfectly predictable in advance to anyone who knew the reasons and the machine, which is exactly the feature I find deflating. Being the source of one's action, if I've understood it, seems to concern where an action comes from rather than whether it was going to come out that way, so it looks compatible with everything being fixed. And the ability to do otherwise doesn't capture it: I'm not claiming I would have chosen differently in identical circumstances, I'm claiming that at the point of deciding the matter was genuinely not yet settled by everything there was to be said. My suspicion is that whatever view gives me what I want will turn out to be one of the ones that says the ordinary picture can't be had — but I can't tell whether the thing I want is just that picture restated or something narrower that the other side could also offer.

So my question: does what I'm describing have an established name, or is there a named argument that nothing could satisfy it? I don't want a label handed to me on its own. I want to know exactly what the thing is, which established notions it sits nearest to, how it differs from each of them, and where its edges lie — in particular whether it collapses into incoherence, into the demand for alternative possibilities, or into randomness, once stated carefully enough. If it is a genuine position, what would have to be true of an agent for it to hold; if it isn't, what should I call it and how should I define it so that it stays distinct from the neighbouring views? Please answer in British English, and please argue it out rather than pointing me at books or papers — our group has no access beyond what happens to be on the shelf, so references would be no use to me.
