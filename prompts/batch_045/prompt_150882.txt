I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I'm working on a piece about the Sleeping Beauty problem, where the standoff between thirders and halfers has now persisted for more than two decades of published argument with neither side budging. Having interviewed people on both sides, my hypothesis is that the dispute isn't really about the coin: it's about whether a piece of self-locating information — that one is awake now, or that it is now Monday — is the kind of thing one Bayesian-conditionalizes on at all, which is why the double-halfer position (staying at one-half even after learning it is Monday) seems to require a non-standard update rule for centred propositions, while Lewis-style halfers update only on the Monday news and thirders treat the awakening itself as evidence. A related suspicion: the influential betting arguments settle nothing, because the choice between scoring per awakening and scoring per experiment already builds in the sampling assumption under dispute, so the real fight is about whether 'now' can be treated as a random draw from a reference class whose size depends on the hypothesis. Is that the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work — a genuine substantive disagreement about the reference class, a verbal one about the word 'credence', or two different questions (how the coin landed versus how frequently awakenings like this one are Heads-awakenings) filed under a single heading? I'd also like your verdict on a case I've been using as a probe: suppose that on tails a single physically distinct duplicate of Beauty is created and each copy is interviewed exactly once, so that the sampling is over subjects rather than over times; if the disagreement is genuinely about self-locating belief, this variant ought to force convergence, and if it doesn't, I want to know what that reveals about the structure of the dispute. Finally, given whatever diagnosis you settle on, what would count as progress — a general update rule for centred information, a decision-theoretic result about which scoring rules are admissible, or an argument that the question should be dissolved rather than answered?
