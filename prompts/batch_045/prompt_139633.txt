I serve on a small grantmaking committee that divides a fixed annual budget between programmes serving two very different populations — one with high baseline income, good health infrastructure and a wide range of available substitutes, the other much poorer, with basic services that are cheap to expand but where a given expenditure produces a very different pattern of gains. We agree that we want to compare benefits across people rather than just count them, and we agree that ordinal, separately-scaled utility functions per person are not enough. We disagree about what to do next, and the disagreement has crystallised into two rival accounts of what an interpersonal comparison even is. Before our next meeting I would like a careful, unsentimental comparison of the two, written for someone who will then have to use one of them.

The first account is the imaginative one: I extend my own concerns by imaginatively occupying each person's position in turn — their history, temperament, attachments, adaptation level and prospects — and my attitudes toward the outcomes of those imagined positions, formed under a veil about which one is actually me, supply a single interpersonally comparable welfare scale. Comparability on this view is something discovered about the structure of a (suitably extended, suitably impartial) agent's attitudes; its characteristic failure modes are imaginative poverty, affective forecasting error, and the awkward fact that people adapt to their circumstances so that the same objective condition can register very differently from inside two lives. The second account is the money-metric one: rather than looking for a common felt magnitude, convert each person's welfare change into a common currency using their own market behaviour — for each person, price the change by the sum of money that, at a fixed reference price vector, would compensate them for it, or that they would pay for it — and compare those sums. Comparability on this view is not a discovery about feeling but a by-product of a shared numéraire plus each person's own trade-offs; its characteristic failure modes are reference-dependence and income effects, including the familiar phenomenon that compensation-measured and equivalence-measured valuations of the same two policies can rank them in opposite directions, so that the resulting social ordering depends on the path along which you move through the space of policies. A third position, which one of my colleagues keeps pushing, is that this shows the money-metric comparisons are best understood as partly stipulated: we fix a reference price vector and a path not because they are metaphysically privileged but because pricing everyone's gains at the same reference prices is procedurally fair, and we then treat the resulting scale as a convention we adopt for decision-making rather than as a measurement we have made.

I want you to compare these two accounts, and the stipulationist gloss on the second, along lines that matter for someone who has to choose between them next month. What does each take interpersonal comparability to consist in, and does that difference actually show up in the recommendations, or is it a metaphysical dispute with no downstream consequences? What informational requirement does each deliver: can it rank people by how well off they are in absolute terms, can it compare the sizes of gains and losses across people, or can it do both — and which of those do the four jobs we actually need (aggregating totals, identifying the worst off for prioritarian weighting, judging when one group's loss is offset by another's gain, and saying anything coherent about inequality) each require? Where exactly does each break, and is the failure symmetric: does the imaginative account escape path-dependence only by burying an equally arbitrary choice about whose imaginative standpoint is used, how much detail is imported, and how adaptation is handled? If you think the two are not really rivals but answers to different questions, say what the two questions are and which one a committee in our position is actually asking. Finish by recommending which account we should adopt, what would have to be true for you to reverse that recommendation, and what the cheapest evidence we could gather over the next year — about our own disagreements, or about the populations we fund — would most efficiently discriminate between them.
