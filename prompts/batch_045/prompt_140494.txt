I work in computational solid mechanics — mostly multiscale modeling, which in practice means I spend my days moving between a fine-scale description of a material (lattice or grain-scale, sometimes explicit atomistics) and a continuum finite-element model of the same structure, and arguing with colleagues about which one is "really" doing the explanatory work. I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group this term, and we recently got to the reduction and emergence literature. I came away with the sense that my colleagues and I use "emergent" to mean something quite different from what the philosophers mean, but also that several of the philosophers' distinctions don't obviously line up with anything I would ever use to decide which model to run on a given problem.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Restate the central positions in that debate, one by one, in the vocabulary I actually work in: representative volume elements and scale separation; homogenization as the procedure that buys me constitutive closure at the macro scale; the Cauchy–Born rule as a literal bridge from a lattice description to a continuum stored-energy function; effective moduli that are computed from microstructure but are not properties of any single grain; and, on the other side of the ledger, the regimes where that machinery fails — strain localization and shear banding, crack-tip fields, the size effect in quasibrittle fracture, anything where the length scale of the field I care about approaches the microstructural length scale so that no representative volume element exists and homogenization is simply not available. Assume I'm fluent in all of this; don't explain it back to me. The positions I want translated are: (i) reduction as the deduction of a higher-level theory from a lower-level one with the help of bridge laws; (ii) the multiple-realizability argument against type identities between higher- and lower-level kinds; (iii) the causal-exclusion argument, on which a complete lower-level causal story appears to leave nothing for the higher-level cause to do; and (iv) the claim that emergent macro-level behavior is autonomous because it is insensitive to microstructural detail. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what translates only if you distort it, and what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all — and please don't just relabel; if the only way to fit a claim into my terms is to quietly change what it asserts, I want that flagged as distortion rather than passed off as translation.

The residue report is the part I actually care about. Going one way: which of those four positions mark distinctions that show up in my working life, and which correspond to nothing I'd ever track — is it possible that when I say "that's emergent" I usually just mean "I don't yet have a homogenized model for it"? Going the other way: which of my distinctions have no counterpart in the philosophical debate? I'm thinking especially of the fact that a continuum law is often obtained identically from wildly different microstructures — my effective stiffness depends on a few statistics of the grain structure and not on its details — and of the fact that in the failure regimes the fine scale stops being ignorable in a way that no choice of vocabulary makes go away. Does that kind of robustness-to-micro-detail count as evidence for the reductionist or for the emergentist, or does the debate as it is standardly framed have no way to represent the thing that actually makes multiscale modeling either succeed or break down? And when the dust settles: does the residue point to a blind spot in how engineers talk about scales, or to "reduction" and "emergence" as the philosophers use them doing less work than they appear to?
