I have taught a graduate seminar on reference and singular thought for a number of years, and over that time I have kept — and periodically revised — a list of identity statements together with my own verdicts about which of them are informative. By informative I mean: a statement that a speaker can fully understand, using both of its terms with perfect competence, while still rationally treating as an open question, and about which she can learn something by coming to accept it. My verdicts no longer look to me like the output of any single principle, and my own attempts to impose one have all felt ad hoc. I would like to know whether they can be captured by one, and at what cost.

The list, with my verdicts.

1. *The evening star is the morning star.* Informative. Someone entirely competent with both expressions, and familiar with the sky, can wonder whether it is true; coming to accept it is an empirical discovery.

2. *The evening star is the evening star.* Empty. Knowable in advance by anyone who understands the term; someone who professed to doubt it would thereby betray that he had not understood it.

3. *Cicero is Tully.* Informative, and in the same way as (1): an a posteriori discovery, biographical rather than astronomical.

4. *Water is H₂O.* Informative — but if true, necessarily so. So informativeness cannot consist in learning a contingent fact about the world.

5. *The sum of the interior angles of a Euclidean triangle equals two right angles.* Informative for a student who has not seen the proof, and yet a priori: no observation, and no discovery about which things there are, settles it.

6. *Two labels from one briefing.* A police officer tells me in a single breath that the man I am looking for answers to 'Slim' and also to 'the Weasel'; I have no other information about him. For me, 'Slim is the Weasel' is as empty as (2). This is so despite the two terms being orthographically and phonologically distinct, and despite my not having known, before the briefing, whom either of them names.

7. *One encounter, two sortals.* I stand before a cast bronze statue, knowing nothing of how it was made. 'This statue is this lump of bronze,' with both demonstratives directed at the same object in the same perceptual episode, is informative: I learn something. No possible observation settles it, and I am inclined to call it a conceptual rather than an empirical discovery. Yet it is hard to see that I have two presentations of the object at all — one look, one object, two sortals. I take (6) and (7) to be the pair my list turns on, and I do not have a principled account of why they differ.

8. *Belief contexts, first version.* A subject sincerely assents to 'He can fly' when the name used is one, and sincerely dissents when it is the other, and would also deny the identity of the two. Here the failure of substitutivity in attitude ascriptions and the informativeness of the identity statement travel together.

9. *Belief contexts, second version.* A subject has encountered the same name in two quite separate corners of his life and would, if asked whether the two are one person, refuse to answer. The case seems to me the same as (8). But here there is no difference in his information about the referent that he could state without using the name itself — there is nothing, that is, that he believes of the one that he denies of the other, under any description available to him.

10. *One source, two channels.* A colleague is in a room, on the telephone, describing to me the one man in it; I am watching a monitor fed by a camera in that same room. I have no descriptive information about the man at all. 'The man you are watching is the man I am describing' is informative to me: I can check it, and until I do I do not know it. But the two terms differ neither in descriptive content (I have none) nor, as far as I can tell, in the causal route by which they are anchored — both are anchored by perception of the same object, his and the camera's. All that differs is which perceiver's link fixes which term. If informativeness tracks something about how a subject is related to a referent, this case suggests the relation in question is finer-grained than either a description or a chain.

Two further cases I cannot settle. First: 'I am this human animal,' said by an ordinary adult while looking in a mirror. Part of me hears it as empty in the way (2) is; part of me hears it as informative in the way (7) is. Second: I was once told, in a single breath, that the author of certain letters signed himself both 'A' and 'B'; I have entirely forgotten this, but I retain both names and a good deal of information about the man. Is 'A is B' informative for me now, and is its informativeness the same phenomenon as in (1), or merely my ignorance of what I was once told? I take the answer here to depend on whether the relevant notion is a property of the terms or of a subject's current informational state, and I do not know which to say.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can test it against cases I have not thought of — not a survey of the familiar answers to the puzzle about identity statements, and not an account of who has defended what. Specifically:

(a) the principle itself, with an indication of which clause is forced by which case on my list, and especially what distinguishes (6) from (7);

(b) an explicit statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and why I should abandon those verdicts rather than add a clause to save them;

(c) whether informativeness, on your account, is a property of the sentence, of the pair of terms, or of the sentence together with a subject and the information she has — and, on the last option, whether my verdicts in (1) and (10) survive the relativization;

(d) whether the substitution failures in (8) and (9) are the same phenomenon as the informativeness in (1) through (5), or whether a single account must treat them as separate and say where the boundary falls;

(e) verdicts on my two unsettled cases; and

(f) one prediction of your own: a case of a kind my list does not contain, with what your principle says about it.

If no single principle is available, say so plainly, and say what the phenomenon should be split into and where the dividing lines lie.
