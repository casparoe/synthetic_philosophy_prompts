The question I set reads as follows, verbatim:

"A species of intelligent molluscs, the Veretians, have complete and reliable census records. Kav, a Veretian philosopher, knows that roughly 6 × 10^10 Veretians have lived before her, so that her birth rank is about 6 × 10^10, and she knows her rank to be roughly typical of living Veretians. Veretian social science has narrowed the future of her civilization to two hypotheses, which for this question you should treat as exhaustive and as equally supported by all evidence that does not involve anthropic reasoning: Near (the Veretians die out after a total of about 10^11 Veretians have ever lived) and Far (they settle the galaxy and a total of about 10^16 Veretians ever live). Kav adopts the self-sampling assumption (SSA): she should reason as if she were a random sample drawn uniformly from the class of all observers in her universe, a class she currently takes to comprise the Veretians alone. (a) Set out Kav's doomsday argument and state precisely which assumption does the work; compute her posterior odds on Near versus Far. (b) The self-indication assumption (SIA) holds that, in assessing hypotheses, you should weight each hypothesis in proportion to the number of observers it says exist, on the ground that your finding yourself to exist is itself more likely the more observers there are. Explain how a defender of SIA responds to Kav's argument, and compute what SIA yields here. Is the SSA/SIA dispute a dispute about priors or about sampling? (c) Veretian cosmologists then establish, to Kav's satisfaction, that the universe contains a further 10^20 observers who are not Veretians: comparably minded, in regions from which no signal can ever reach her, and — crucially — believed to number the same under Near as under Far. Does this discovery change Kav's conclusion, and does the answer depend on how the reference class is drawn? Show your work."

The answer I'm grading reads as follows, verbatim:

"(a) The argument turns entirely on SSA. Without it there is no link between Kav's birth rank and how many Veretians will ever exist: the fact that she is the 6 × 10^10th Veretian is just a fact about her, and nothing follows from it about the total. With SSA the link appears, because her rank is then a random draw from the positions the hypothesis makes available. Under Near, her chance of finding herself at rank 6 × 10^10 is 1/10^11; under Far, it is 1/10^16. The likelihood ratio is therefore (1/10^11)/(1/10^16) = 10^5 in favour of Near, and with prior odds of 1:1 the posterior odds are 10^5:1 on Near, i.e. a probability of about 0.99999. Two things are worth flagging. First, the shift enters as a likelihood ratio, not as a prior, so the argument is not answered merely by pointing out that Kav may have had optimistic prior odds: whatever the prior odds are, the argument multiplies them by the same factor. Second, the conclusion is comparative and not catastrophic in the everyday sense: Near still allows about 4 × 10^10 further Veretians, and nothing in the argument requires the population to have been growing exponentially, or at all.

(b) SIA says that hypotheses are confirmed by your own existence in proportion to how many observers they contain. Here it boosts Far over Near by a factor of 10^16/10^11 = 10^5 before any sampling is done, which is exactly the inverse of the factor SSA produces, so the two cancel and the posterior returns to the prior odds of 1:1. I think the right way to understand this is that SIA is simply SSA applied to a wider reference class — the class of all possible observers rather than the class of all actual ones — so that the famous SSA/SIA disagreement is really the reference class problem turning up again under a new name, and 'accepting SIA' is the same kind of move as widening the class from Veretians to all observers in the universe. On that reading the debate is not any deeper than the question of which class you sample from.

(c) The discovery of the extra 10^20 observers does not touch the argument. Under SSA, Kav's probability of occupying the position she occupies is 1/N, where N is the number of Veretians who will ever live, because the positions she might have occupied are Veretian positions; the non-Veretian observers are not among the positions she could have found herself in, so N is unchanged at 10^11 or 10^16 and the likelihood ratio stays at 10^5. Nor are those observers really in the same reference class in the first place: reference classes are fixed by causal and informational contact, and by hypothesis these observers lie beyond any possible signal exchange with Kav. So her posterior stays where it was, at 10^5:1 on Near."
