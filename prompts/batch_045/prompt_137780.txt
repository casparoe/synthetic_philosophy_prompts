Hi — I'm running a 90-minute evening session of a small reading group, about 12 people (mostly cognitive-science grad students, a few programmers, one historian who wandered in and never left). Nobody in the room has met Bongard problems before, and I want to keep it that way for the first hour: no definition up front, no "these are called Bongard problems", no lecture. Just two panels of little hand-drawn figures and the question "which side does this new one belong on?" I'd rather the word "rule" not even appear on the handout.

What I want them to feel in their hands, not be told, is that twelve examples (six a side) don't pin anything down in an interesting sense — that what does the selecting is a shared, mostly unstated prior about which predicates are natural, and that the "oh — now I see it" click is not evidence you've recovered the author's intended distinction. Secondary thing I'd like them to run into by themselves: the space of candidate distinctions is a *choice*, and if you only ever consider properties of individual figures, a whole family of distinctions about relations *between* the figures on a panel stays invisible.

Can you design me a runnable activity for that? Things I'd like baked in:

- Practicalities: 12 people, teams of 3, 90 minutes, one room, no projector, no laptops. Materials should be stationery-shop stuff — index cards, coloured markers, envelopes, printed sheets. Nothing that needs charging.
- A "vocabulary card" handed out first: a short fixed list of legal primitives any distinction has to be built from (shape, fill, size, count, and relations like touching / overlapping / contained-in / separate), maybe 8–10 items. The reason I want it fixed is that afterwards we can genuinely ask "how many distinctions expressible in this little language fit these twelve cards?" and argue to an answer instead of hand-waving.
- Round 1: each team gets a panel pair plus a few loose probe cards. They have to (a) place the probes and (b) write down *three* seperate distinctions consistent with the training cards, not one. Rank them by which they'd bet on.
- Then I reveal a pre-made set of maybe five extra probe cards, and each team has to commit, in writing, to a side for each probe *under each of their three distinctions*. If their own distinctions disagree with each other, they should find that out by doing it, not by me announcing it. Tell me how to build that probe deck so it reliably splits the kinds of distinctions people actually generate.
- Round 2: teams author a panel pair for another team, sealed in an envelope, with a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money (chocolate is fine). Here's the bit I care most about: I want authors rewarded when solvers *fail*, but penalised — call it a "collision" — when solvers name a different distinction that fits all twelve cards and the author can't produce a card, within the vocabulary, that seperates it from their own. My hope is that this forces authors to appeal to something outside the data (simplicity, naturalness, "what a person would think of") in order to defend their puzzle, and that's the moment the debrief names. Sanity-check whether that incentive actually produces the behaviour I want or whether it just makes everyone write easy puzzles.
- Somewhere in there I want one planted panel pair whose distinction is configural/relational — e.g. on one side no two figures are exactly alike in the vocabulary, on the other at least two are — slipped in without comment as just another problem. I want to see who gets stuck and how they describe being stuck.

Give me a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact wording for each handout, the payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the specific points (underdetermination by the data; the prior doing the work; the hypothesis space as a choice; the click not being evidence).

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I can already guess at a few — someone draws a distinction so baroque nothing could refute it, someone "solves" by vibes and can't articulate anything, someone builds their puzzle on their own drawing style (shaky hand, pen pressure) rather than on the vocabulary, and the physical cutouts end up coarser than the printed vocabulary so people cheat with arrangement on the table. But give me your list and how to handle each.

And be honest about which feature of the real thing the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief. My worry is that because everything is pre-symbolised on cards, the activity tests *selection among already-formulated hypotheses* and misses the perceptual work of constructing the description in the first place — which is arguably where Bongard problems actually bite. If that's right, say so, and tell me what the facilitator has to add so nobody walks out thinking they've understood the whole thing.
