I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences days, and I run a small Sunday-evening study group out of my living room: seven or eight people, mostly working programmers, one biologist, one person who took a single probability course and remembers Bayes' rule and nothing else. We have a whiteboard and ninety minutes. A few months ago I got into an ugly thread in the comments under a post that invoked "Aumann" as a norm of disagreement, and I realized I couldn't explain, to my own satisfaction, why the standard blog-level move — rationalists with shared priors can't agree to disagree, so when someone disagrees with you, update toward them — doesn't actually follow, or what precisely it is that the theorem does say. So next month I'm running a session on Aumann's agreement theorem, and what I want from you is a plan for teaching it, not the lecture itself.

Please take the role of a formal epistemologist and decision theorist who has taught this material both to graduate students and to general-audience meetups. Think carefully about the pedagogy rather than about displaying the proof: I want the order in which the components have to be introduced and what depends on what, one worked example that carries the whole thing, a map of the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with or form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, a check that tells me whether the idea landed, and an explicit list of what I should decline to cover in ninety minutes. Do not write out the explanation itself, and do not restate the theorem at me as though that were the plan.

A few constraints, some of which I learned the hard way. The room will arrive with the folk version — two Bayesians with the same prior who share all their evidence must agree — and I think that is nearly right but wrong in a way that matters, since the theorem gets agreement from common knowledge of the posteriors alone, without anyone handing over the evidence behind them, so the sequence should be built to break that reading at the right moment rather than at the start. The carrying example has to be a single binary proposition with a prior the whole room can genuinely assent to sharing, not a political or contested empirical claim, and it needs two private signals whose likelihood ratios are clean enough to compute on the board; it must be arranged so that the naive "just split the difference" shortcut, averaging the two credences, gives a visibly wrong number, because in the symmetric setup I tried last time the averaging heuristic accidentally produced the right answer and I lost the room; and it should be free of anything that lets people argue about whether the prior is really common or about whether the evidence was soft or ambiguous. One member is a committed average-your-credences person and will defend it, one will try to drag the session into prediction markets and the no-trade point, and one will ask whether the theorem says anything at all about who is better calibrated or more informed — I'd like each of those absorbed into the design rather than treated as an interruption.

Please reply in the following sections, in this order:

- Assumptions I can safely make about this room (~100 words): what to treat as known, and what not to.
- Dependency map (~300 words): the ordered sequence of ideas, each with a sentence on why it has to come after the ones before it.
- The carrying example (~350 words): the example worked through numerically, why it has the features it has, what a bad version of it would look like, and one alternative you rejected and why.
- Misconception ledger (~400 words): a table, one row per misconception, with columns for where in the sequence it will surface and the move that defuses it.
- The check (~150 words): one question or short closing task whose answer distinguishes someone who has the idea from someone who has only picked up the slogan, including what a wrong-but-confident answer would sound like.
- What to refuse to cover (~150 words): including at least the common-prior idealization, the many-agent case, and whatever you think is the single most tempting thing for me to overreach into.
