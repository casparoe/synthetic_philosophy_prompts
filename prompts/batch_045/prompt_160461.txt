I'm in the second year of a philosophy PhD, and this term I've been going to a fortnightly workshop run by a senior philosopher in my department, Dr. Hallam, who keeps a ledger on the whiteboard: at the start of every session she writes up a sentence and makes us file it under "analytic," "synthetic," or "won't say," and once filed it stays filed. Over six sessions the ledger has come to this: (1) "Every bachelor is unmarried" — analytic. (2) "Every vixen is a female fox" — analytic. (3) "Whatever is a brother is male" — analytic. (4) "No number is both even and odd" — analytic, with the remark that she cannot describe an experience that would make her give it up. (5) "Every square is a four-sided figure" — analytic, "boringly so." (6) "Water is H₂O" — synthetic, though she insisted it is necessary if true and knowable only a posteriori, that conflating necessity with analyticity is the error she most wants to stamp out, and — when someone pressed her — that how firmly we hold a claim is not what analyticity consists in, "though I don't think it's irrelevant either." (7) "An action is right if and only if it maximizes happiness" — synthetic, "even if it were true it wouldn't be true in virtue of what anyone means." (8) "Everything green is extended" — "won't say," the only use of that label all term, on the ground that her giving it up last is no reason to call it analytic and her inability to define "green" is no reason to call it synthetic. We've spent the term on the mid-century assault on the distinction and the replies to it, but she has never told us which she accepts.

The case I want to bring next week is "Whoever has promised to do something thereby has a reason to do it." I need to know where it will land, because a chapter of my thesis turns on it: if it comes out analytic I can dispose of it in a paragraph, if synthetic I owe it a full section, and if "won't say" I should probably restructure the argument to avoid leaning on it at all. I've been going back and forth between two readings of the ledger — that she is testing whether the predicate is somehow already contained in the subject concept, and that she is testing whether the sentence can be turned into a logical truth by substituting synonyms — but I suspect there are other rules that fit all eight entries equally well. I'm also worried that entry (7) settles my case in advance, though I don't think it does: promising looks like a practice with constitutive rules in a way that "right" doesn't, even though I can't say why in a form that would survive the room.

So: what will she say about the promise sentence, and what rule does the ledger suggest she is actually running? Please set out the candidate rules that are consistent with all eight verdicts, say what each predicts about my case, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and tell me which single further sentence I should put to her to separate the surviving rules from one another. I had also been considering bringing "Every whale is a mammal" instead, on the theory that it exposes something about the "held come what may" idea — is that more diagnostic than the promise case, or less?
