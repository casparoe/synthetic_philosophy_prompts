I keep going back and forth about conscription and I can't work out what exactly a country is signing up for when it keeps it. Maybe you can help me unpack it.

The package I have in mind is the Finnish/Swiss/Swedish one, roughly: the state legally requires a big chunk of each birth cohort to spend months in uniform for a token daily allowance; refusing outright can land you in prison, and the conscientious-objector alternative in Finland is set at twelve months, which as far as I can tell is longer than what most conscripts actually do, so the pacifist typically serves more time than the person who was willing to carry a rifle. After training you stay in the reserve for decades, get called back for refreshers, and have a pre-assigned wartime job. In the Swiss version the whole thing is explicitly a militia: the army is meant to be the civilian population under arms rather than a separate profession. And it's not just soldiers — households get civil-defence pamphlets, buildings have shelters, the whole society is treated as part of the defence system.

Two things puzzle me. First, this is the one dangerous public job we staff by compulsion. Nobody drafts firefighters or deep-sea rescue crews; we pay people to do those. Second, the old justification seems to have evaporated: everyone used to say these countries need a people's army because they're neutral and stand alone, but Finland joined NATO in 2023 and Sweden in 2024, and Sweden had actually scrapped conscription and then brought it back. So the neutrality story can't be doing the work anymore, yet the practice survived it — which makes me think the practice was never really about neutrality in the first place.

I also read some blogger distinguishing two very different justifications: conscription as a cheap way to buy bodies (a fiscal argument) versus conscription as a way to make citizens (a formative one), and claiming that the militia-plus-reserve model only makes sense on the second, because if all you wanted was soldiers you'd just pay professionals. Someone else framed it as a tax paid in risk instead of money, which would make it about the most regressive tax there is. Both framings sound half-convincing to me and I can't tell whether they're compatible.

So: what does this bundle of practices actually presuppose about the state, citizenship, or obligation? Which bits are strictly required and which are just historically associated with it — could a fairly thin liberal view get you peacetime conscription plus a lifelong reserve obligation, or does that need a thicker republican story about civic virtue and the armed citizen? Is it coherent on its own terms that the objector serves longer, that in Finland and Switzerland it's mostly men, and that civilians are simultaneously treated as participants with duties and as people the state exists to protect? And what would actually have to change if you swapped the implicit theory for a professional-army one — what would you lose besides the bodies?
