I teach a first-year seminar called "Persons and Personhood," and we spend a fortnight on the relational or communitarian conceptions of personhood developed in the Ubuntu literature: the family of views on which a human being is not simply born a person but becomes one by being woven into a network of relationships, so that what makes someone a person is not an intrinsic psychological property carried around inside them but the relations they stand in and the standing they have within a community. This year I have been pressing a distinction that my students find abstract, and I want to test it against a literary case. Call the two versions the constitutive reading and the recognitive reading. On the constitutive reading, relations partly make the person: sever them and you damage or diminish the person, but the relations themselves do the metaphysical work. On the recognitive reading, what does the work is being acknowledged and incorporated by the community; personhood is a status the community confers, much as it confers citizenship or standing. The two come apart in a case I have started calling the boycott problem: when a dominant group refuses to enter into or acknowledge relations with some population — under slavery, under apartheid, under caste exclusion, under organized indifference — the recognitive reading appears to entail that those people are not persons, or are lesser persons, which is both morally appalling and surely the reverse of what relational theorists meant to say; the constitutive reading has more room, since relations among the excluded can still partly constitute them, but then it owes us an account of why the excluded group's own relations should be morally weightless for the wider society that refuses them. My students can state both horns now; what they cannot yet do is say which one we should accept, or what accepting it would cost.

The case I want to put to them is Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, together with Mark Romanek's 2010 film of it; I am screening it for a film-and-philosophy evening attached to the seminar and then circulating a short reading. Assume I know the plot: Hailsham and its guardians and its emphasis on art, Madame and her mysterious collection, Miss Lucy's indiscretion, the Cottages and the search for possible originals, the rumour that a couple genuinely in love can have their donations deferred, Kathy's years as a carer, the visit to Madame and Miss Emily and the discovery that the rumour was never true, the completions, the fence at Norfolk and the lost things caught in it. What I want is a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical distinction seriously and refuses to reduce either to the other. Roughly: the Hailsham students stand in relations as dense as any in literature — loyalty, rivalry, love, the long bodily intimacy of caring for one another through the donations — while being deliberately held at the edge of the wider moral community, which relates to them purely as a resource and cultivates a studied inattention to them. That makes the book something close to a designed experiment for the boycott problem. I also want you to sit with the scenes where the machinery is visible: the gallery, where the point of the art is to demonstrate to the outside world that the students have inner lives — which is evidence of exactly the intrinsic-property sort a relationalist should regard as beside the point, and may mean the novel is quietly siding with a rival view; the deferral rumour, where Kathy and Tommy stake their claim to a future on the quality of their love for each other, as though being genuinely in relation were the thing that entitled you to more life, and where the rumour turns out to be false; and Kathy's narration itself, which addresses a reader, assumes a shared we with them, and confides in a way that no one in her world confides in her — so that the only recognition on offer in the book may be the reader's, and the form of the novel may be doing the philosophical work rather than illustrating it.

Two further things I would like handled honestly rather than rhetorically. First, the dark side of the relational picture: Hailsham's communal ethos — the premium on producing art, on being kind, on not making a fuss — is also the mechanism of the students' extraordinary docility, the reason nobody runs and nobody riots and everyone reports for donation. If personhood is what you acquire by being incorporated into a community, then incorporation is also a technology of subjection, and I suspect that the boycott problem and this conformism worry are the same worry seen from two directions: a view that makes personhood hostage to the community will fail its victims either by letting the community withhold it or by letting the community dictate its terms. Say whether the novel supports that suspicion or whether I am reading my own thesis into it. Second, say plainly where the reading breaks down: where the book is doing something else entirely — an appeal to species membership, or to the capacity to suffer, or to what art is for — and where a careful critic has to concede that the fit is loose or that I am over-reading a scene. I would rather give my students a reading with its failures marked than a tidy one. Close by saying what, if anything, the novel settles or reframes that the journal literature on relational personhood does not, since that is the claim I would have to defend if I put this in front of colleagues.
