I'm a recently retired emergency physician, and in retirement I've been reading my way through the debate about whether the world has become less violent. Over roughly thirty-five years in the same city's trauma service I watched the same injuries stop killing people. A penetrating abdominal wound that in the early 1980s we would have lost maybe half the time is now very often survivable: faster transport, ultrasound in the resuscitation bay, damage-control surgery, blood products on hand, better intensive care. Nobody in that room became less violent; the victim just stopped dying. And since so much of the optimistic case rests on counts of violent death — homicide rates, battle deaths, executions, rates of death by interpersonal violence in the archaeological record — it seems to me that medical progress may be doing some of the work that the optimists attribute to civilization. The same pattern shows up in war: across the last century or so the ratio of wounded to killed climbed enormously, so a battle of unchanged ferocity produces a much smaller pile of dead, and if you measure violence by deaths you will find a decline even if the shooting, shelling, and intent are exactly as before.

I keep trying to find out whether this specific thing has a name and a literature, and I keep being handed things that aren't it. It isn't the familiar per-capita-versus-absolute-totals dispute — my worry is about what counts as one unit in the numerator, not about what we divide by. It isn't exactly the ordinary complaint that concepts of harm have been expanding, so that things which used to be ordinary discipline, roughhousing, or household authority now get classified as violence and push the numbers up; that is the mirror image of my case, and I'd like to know precisely how the two differ, whether one is the same mechanism running in reverse, and whether they might in principle cancel so that nobody can say which way the true trend goes. It also isn't quite the standard epidemiological worry about diagnostic or case-definition drift, because in my case the definition of the event hasn't been tinkered with: the act is the same act, and only its downstream consequences have changed.

So: does this pattern — the same amount of force directed at persons getting recorded as less violence because its consequences have been mitigated — have an established name of its own? Is it best treated as one species of the reclassification family, the kind of thing where moving cases between statistical buckets improves both buckets without anything in the world improving? If there is no settled term, coin one, define it tightly, and give me its boundary cases, because I want to raise this at a reading group and I'd rather be corrected in advance than on the night. Would body armour count as an instance? Faster ambulance response? A change in how police record an incident? A murderer switching from knives to a method that is more lethal? A famine or a blockade imposed by force, where better logistics save the victims — is my concept over-generous if that counts? More than the label, I want the carving. Is the quantity 'amount of violence in the world' a quantity about acts — force intentionally directed at a person — or about outcomes, the harm that actually lands? If the honest measure is incidents or intentions rather than deaths, does the decline thesis survive being re-run on that measure, or does it quietly become unfalsifiable? And is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is getting less violent at all, or only a fact of the matter relative to a measure someone has chosen?
