Hey, I'm a TA on a first-year course on practical rationality — I run one 75-minute small-group session a week, about 16 students, mostly 18 or 19, a mix of philosophy majors and people who are only there because it filled a requirement. In two weeks I've got the session on weakness of will, and I really don't want to put up slides about discount curves and have everyone nod along. I'd rather they walk into the thing themselves.

So: design me a runnable activity — a game, a staged sequence of choices, whatever — where the students run into their own time-inconsistency before I've named it. Constraints: one 75-minute slot, basically no budget (I can get a bag of cheap chocolates or hand out participation points, no real money), a projector and a whiteboard, and phones are a nightmare so I'd rather not need them. The stakes have to be real enough that the choices aren't hypothetical: ideally the delay is something they actually sit through in the room, and there's something small they can actually gain or loose. Also, several of them have definately seen a "would you rather have £10 now or £15 in a week" thing on tiktok, so a plain sooner-vs-later menu won't surprise anybody, and a couple of the sharper ones will announce in advance that they'd never be so silly.

Two things I'd specifically like built in. First, a second-order layer: they predict what they'll do later, or they can spend part of their own payoff (points, chocolates) to buy some restriction on their own future choice — and then they find out whether they were right about themselves. Some will spot what's coming and try to preempt it, some won't, and I want that difference to show up in what happens rather than in me explaining it afterwards. Second, if you can manage it, I'd love at least one round to run on something aversive — a boring or mildly unpleasant task they can do now or later, with the longer version as the cost of delay — because procrastination is where they actually recognise themselves, and I suspect the pattern there isn't just the reward case with a minus sign.

What I want them to feel, roughly in order of importance: that the flip isn't a discovery that the smaller-sooner option is better, but that "now" is doing something special, since the same pair of options at different distances gets opposite answers and they should catch themselves doing it; that there's a real difference between gritting your teeth and rearranging things so you can't choose, and they should get to try both and see which survives contact with the moment of choice; and that it is genuinely not obvious whether the person who reaches for the smaller-sooner thing is being irrational or just has different preferences at that moment. I want them leaving the room arguing about whether the patient self or the tempted self speaks for them.

Give me the setup and materials, the rules and payoff scheme with no money involved, a minute-by-minute timeline, what the facilitator does and pointedly does not say, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific bits of the theory and actually leaves the "so was that weakness of will or not?" question open. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — the ones who treat it as a puzzle to beat, the ones who try to renegotiate with me at the moment of choice, the ones who precommit and then resent it — and how to handle each. Be honest about which part of the idea the activity just can't stage and I'll have to say out loud. Oh, and I run the same session twice that day for two groups who share a group chat, so tell me how to keep the second lot from being spoiled.
