In nine days I defend the third chapter of my dissertation. Forty minutes of presentation, then roughly seventy minutes of questions from a committee of four whose members disagree with each other more than any of them disagrees with me. I want to walk in with the question period already mapped out: the objections I will actually get, from whom, in whose vocabulary, what each one costs me, and what I say back inside ninety seconds. Please build me that prep sheet.

The chapter: its title is Epistemic Foundations Without the Fixed Point. The thesis is that in epistemic game theory, common belief in rationality is standardly treated as an assumption you stipulate in the model, and that it should instead be treated as an achievement that no finite agent ever completes. This is not a verbal reclassification. Games differ in how much of the belief hierarchy their prescriptions depend on. Call a game tail-insensitive when, for every margin of error, there is some finite depth such that any belief hierarchy that agrees with common belief in rationality up to that depth yields a prescription within that margin of the fixed-point prescription. Call it tail-sensitive otherwise. My claim is that the games the foundations program leans on hardest are the tail-sensitive ones, and that in those games the finite approximations do not converge toward the fixed-point prediction but point the opposite way. The exhibit is Rubinstein's 1989 electronic mail game: for any positive probability that a message fails, the unique Bayesian Nash equilibrium has both players taking the safe action no matter how many confirmations have been exchanged. I read the number of confirmations as depth of almost-common-knowledge, and the result as a proof that in this game depth is not a perturbation of the fixed point but its negation.

The constructive half, which I am much less sure of, replaces the fixed point with a depth-indexed rule. Delete only as far as the depth where the expected gain from one more round of deletion falls below the expected loss from a mistaken deletion at that depth. Then the Diminishing-Support Postulate: each further level of a higher-order belief draws on evidence at most a fixed fraction of the strength of the level beneath it, because evidence for level n-plus-one is evidence about the other player's evidence rather than evidence about the world. From the two together I conjecture, without proving, a logarithmic bound on optimal deletion depth, and I use it to predict, from rationality alone and without positing altruism or confusion, that players in a hundred-node centipede will pass for a handful of rounds and then take. That is the result I want on the record.

The committee. The chair is a microeconomic theorist who has spent his career on interactive epistemology in the Aumann-style type-space framework and regards the program as essentially finished; he writes in theorems and wants to know where mine is, and he has already told me in writing that he does not think the email game is doing the work I think it is. The second reader is a formal epistemologist who works on higher-order evidence, peer disagreement, and imprecise credences; she is sympathetic to the anti-fixed-point mood and will press me to say whether I mean belief or knowledge, full or graded, and whether rationality is a normative constraint on the model or a premise inside it, and she will want everything restated in terms of credal states. The third reader is an experimental economist who runs centipede and guessing-game sessions and fits the data with noisy best-response models; he thinks epistemic game theory is unfalsifiable, and he will want a number, an experimental design, and an answer to what would make me give the conjecture up. The external examiner is a logician in a computer science department working on epistemic logic and distributed systems; he will ask whether my complaint about achievement is anything more than the observation that common knowledge is not compact and that the relevant operator has no finite iteration that reaches its fixed point, and whether depth-limited deletion is even well defined when the order of deletion matters.

What I need from you: the eight or nine objections I should expect, ordered by how likely each is multiplied by how much damage it does, each labelled with the reader who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it. For each: the concession I should make in the first sentence, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which two things to concede in the first five minutes of the talk so that the committee spends its energy on the constructive half rather than on step two. Tell me the single reply that has to land if the chapter is to survive, and which objections I should deliberately hand to the formal epistemologist to answer for me rather than answering myself. Finally: my own candidate for the question I am hoping nobody asks is this — if the Diminishing-Support Postulate is true of the players, does it not also apply to my own confidence in the chapter, and does the postulate not then eat the argument that asserts it? Tell me whether that is really the worst question in the room, and give me a line to use if it comes anyway.
