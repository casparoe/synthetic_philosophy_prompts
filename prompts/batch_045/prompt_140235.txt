I teach an upper-level seminar on value and choice — 16 students, 80 minutes, one session. They've already done the standard material on incommensurability, so they have the vocabulary but they don't feel it: to them everything still collapses into either "the two options are exactly equally good" or "you just don't know your own preferences well enough." I want an activity where the difference shows up in what people do, not in what I say afterwards. So: no mini-lecture up front, and nobody hears words like "incommensurable," "on a par," or "hard choice" until the debrief. The options and the rules should be built so that they run into the distinction rather than being told about it.

The mechanism I keep coming back to but can't quite build is a "small improvement" move. Roughly: everyone commits to a pairwise choice first; then, only after they've committed, I tell them the option they rejected has been improved slightly on the dimension it was already strong on — a little more money, a slightly better teacher, whatever — and they get one chance to switch, free or nearly free. My hunch is that this gives a clean behavioural signature: where the two options really were equally good on some shared scale, a tiny improvement settles it instantly and nobody agonizes; where the choice was a genuinely hard one, the tiny improvement does something odd — it neither settles the choice nor leaves the person indifferent — and students can feel that even though they can't articulate it. I'm honestly not sure the hunch is right, or that free switching is the best way to cash it out, so tell me plainly if the mechanism is confused and rebuild or replace it if so.

One thing I do want kept: a second beat where the choice turns out not to be arbitrary. Something in the setup should get them to notice that a hard choice isn't a coin flip — that after committing there's a difference between "I picked this one" and "this one is now the one I'm behind," and that this difference doesn't exist in the equal-goodness case. Arrange the rules so they notice it themselves; I don't want to supply it in the debrief.

Practical constraints: no real money, no prizes anyone would actually buy, under ten minutes of setup, and it has to work both in pairs and with the room as a whole. Real but small stakes and actual commitments are encouraged — I'd rather they be on the hook for something than merely imagine it.
