Hey — I need something I can actually use at work, not an essay.

I'm moderation lead for a neighbourhood assembly (Northgate Ward, about 40k people; we meet twice a month and get 20–40 written submissions per meeting on whatever's on the agenda: a zoning variance, a supervised consumption site, school start times, the police budget line, a payday lender's permit renewal, a bike lane). My job, in roughly ninety seconds a submission with no research allowed, is to sort each one into one of two buckets:

- Bucket R ("reasons"): the assembly owes this a written answer in our report — we have to say what we made of the consideration and how we weighed it.
- Bucket C ("convictions"): it gets printed in the record in full, we confirm we read it, but we owe it no answer about its content.

Getting it wrong costs real things. Over-using C is how we end up in front of the council on a discrimination complaint, and two neighbourhoods basically stopped showing up last year after we did it badly. Over-using R means our reports become unreadable, we spend the meeting writing theology instead of policy, and the city solicitor gets twitchy about the assembly taking positions on doctrine. So it matters, it happens constantly, and I rule on the spot with a three-person panel (me, a clerk, one rotating resident) that has to sign off. None of us has philosophy training. I did one undergrad course and remember almost nothing except that there's a big literature on "public reason" and whether religious arguments belong in politics, and that it mostly argues with itself.

So: build me the procedure. Ordered tests I can run inside ninety seconds, what counts as a yes or a no at each step, where it terminates in C, and where it hands off to a human because the question is really a values call. Give me a laminated-card version too, plus a short "don't do this" list. And I need a one-sentence script I can read back to a submitter that doesn't sound like "your kind of reason doesn't count here."

Here are the distinctions I keep tripping over, which the procedure has to handle (I've made up my own labels, sorry):

1. Detachable vs. welded. Sometimes you can strip out the God-talk and the argument still stands on its own — the shelter says the noise from the venue next door will push its residents out, and the bit about their faith obliging them to protect the residents is doing motivational work, not argumentative work. Sometimes pulling the religious premise collapses the whole thing. Also: "just translate it for them" is a labour question, not a solution — if I do the translating, am I editing someone's reason into a different one and then answering my own version?

2. The letterhead problem. Two submissions with identical sentences and identical empirical backing, one from a tenants' union and one signed by fourteen churches on letterhead. Does the source move it between buckets? I don't think it should, but I can't say why.

3. Borrowed-secular claims. The submission makes a checkable factual claim, but the person holds it for religious reasons (the material in the curriculum is false because it contradicts Genesis; the shelter will lose its licence). My instinct is that a claim being false or unpopular shouldn't push it toward C — that's just censoring by another name — but that its being checkable should pull it toward R. Except then we're spending meeting time "answering" stuff that's flatly wrong.

4. Testimony, not argument. Someone writes that they have to say this because they'll answer to God for staying silent. That isn't a consideration for the policy at all, it's a statement about the speaker. Which bucket, and does the answer change if fifty people send the same one?

5. Comprehensive doctrines that aren't religious. We get just as many from a deep-ecology guy (the river has standing), a Marxist, an anti-natalist, and a guy who cites Ayn Rand at length. The procedure can't be secretly just anti-religion, because if it is, it will get lawyered within a month.

6. The floating middle. "Human dignity," "the common good," "natural law," "the sanctity of life" — historically religious vocabulary that now just floats around in policy writing. Religious or not?

Then run it on these, showing your work at each step:

(a) Opposing the supervised consumption site: the body is a temple, the city would be complicit in sin, and they'll be praying about it — plus one sentence about needles in the park behind the library.

(b) A faith-run homeless shelter opposing a late-night venue next door: thirty years on that block, the noise will push their residents out, and their faith obliges them to protect the people in their care.

(c) A coalition of Black churches and a tenants' union opposing the payday lender's permit renewal, in Jubilee-and-usury language: the land isn't a commodity, debt has to be released, a lender charging these rates is devouring the poor.

(d) The letterhead case: a pastor's submission on school start times that cites sleep-science numbers and nothing else — and then the same submission with the church letterhead and a closing line about stewardship of children's bodies.

I'm a practising Catholic and I agree with a lot of the religious submissions, which is exactly why I don't trust my own gut on this. So I want at least one of those run to come out in a way I'll hate and will be tempted to overrule — tell me honestly which one it is and why, and whether my wanting to overrule it is a reason to change the procedure or a reason to trust it. My guess is (c), but you tell me.

Finally: where will this misfire? What should we do on the spot when someone is in the room and can restate their own reason if asked, versus a written submission where nobody can? And say plainly what you think the procedure cannot settle, so I can stop pretending otherwise in the report.
