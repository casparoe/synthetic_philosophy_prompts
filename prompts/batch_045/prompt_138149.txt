I write a column on science and ideas, and I am trying to write something narrower than the usual piece about whether consciousness is an illusion. I want the specification. Before I give any view of that kind space in a newspaper, I want to know what I am entitled to demand of it: what a theory that treats conscious experience as a kind of user interface — a system's simplified, self-directed rendering of its own activity, which presents itself as a show laid before someone but is not a report of an inner show — would have to be able to do in order to count as adequate.

Let me fix the purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory has to be good enough for two jobs. First, adjudicative: a reader should be able to use it to decide how much weight to give a first-person avowal, including avowals from systems that are not human. Second, diagnostic: it should tell a reader what they are doing when they consult their own case and come back with something rich, apparently private, and apparently ineffable. If those two jobs pull the requirements in different directions, say so explicitly; that would itself be the column.

Below are the demands I have collected from reading around and from conversations with people who work on this. I do not trust the list. Some of it I suspect is parochial — the preferences of one research programme dressed up as constraints on any theory — and some of it I suspect is incoherent when taken together. What I want from you is the list, repaired: for each requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it; which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable or merely local preferences; which pair or subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied, so that the list itself becomes the result; and which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise popular candidates. Add the requirement you think belongs on the list and that nobody thinks to ask for.

The demands I have collected:

1. The itemized audit. No blanket verdicts. The theory must go feature by feature through what experience seems to have — felt quality, ineffability, apparent intrinsicality, privacy, unity at a time, temporal thickness, the apparent audience or self to whom it is presented — and say of each whether it is part of the illusion, part of the underlying activity, or a confusion, and whether these verdicts are separable or all-or-nothing.

2. Explain the compulsion. It is not enough to say the verdict is mistaken; the theory owes an account of why the mistake is practically incorrigible — why being told about the interface does not dissolve it, why the knowledge argument and the explanatory-gesture cases have the grip they do.

3. Stop the regress. If the user is itself part of the display, the theory must say how the regress halts and what, if anything, is doing the consuming.

4. Make a prediction I could check. Concretely: if a subject were fitted with a real-time readout of the system's own confidence and uncertainty signals, would the apparent stream get richer or thinner, and why? A theory that can accommodate both outcomes has not said anything.

5. Say what would count against it.

6. Be consistent with the sparseness findings: change blindness, inattentional blindness, blindsight, confabulation following hemispheric disconnection, and patients whose behaviour suggests unawareness while their neural response to commands suggests otherwise.

7. Handle the avowal case. A system trained to emit fluent first-person reports of experience, with no plausible claim to the architecture the theory relies on, produces the sentence that it is like something to be it. The theory must say something determinate here, and must not answer in a way that collapses into plain behaviourism — or must explain why that collapse is not a cost.

8. Not collide with ethics. It must distinguish denying that experience has the properties it seems to have from denying that reported pain matters, and it must not hand a reader a licence to discount suffering.

9. Declare its asymmetry. For the adjudicative purpose, it must say whether false positives or false negatives are the worse error, and for whom.

10. Be statable in eight hundred words for a general reader, without machinery that evaporates on contact with one.

11. Apply to the theorist's own case without exemption.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered: requirements 2 and 3 pull against each other, because anything that explains convincingly why the illusion is compulsory seems to reintroduce, at the level of the account rather than the object, exactly what the theory denied — and if that is right, the interesting finding is not which theory wins but that the specification has no satisfying member. If you think the impasse lies elsewhere, tell me where.

At the end, a short scorecard only — a paragraph or a small table — on three or four of the main contenders, including the illusionist or interface picture, higher-order or self-representational accounts, and global-workspace or report-bottleneck accounts. Keep the weight on the criteria; that is where the thinking is, and that is what I am writing about. And do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists: I do my own sourcing, and I want the argument carried by the cases rather than by who said what.
