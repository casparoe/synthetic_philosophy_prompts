I help coordinate a federation of about a dozen small worker co-operatives, and our committee has recently got badly stuck on a proposal we have started calling "coordination without a market": instead of each co-op buying and selling on the open market, each would send a central scheduling group a list of what it needs and what it can make, the group would set notional transfer prices and nudge them up wherever demand exceeds supply and down wherever there is a surplus, and this would repeat until the plan balanced. One member says this is the Lange-style simulation of a market and that the whole question was settled decades ago; another says it is exactly what Mises and Hayek argued cannot be done, because the relevant knowledge is local, fleeting and partly inarticulable, and because people will not report it truthfully when what they receive depends on what they say.

I have a study day coming up with about twenty of our members — few of them economists, ages from early twenties to late sixties, two hours including a break — and I do not want to settle this by lecturing at them. Design an activity for that session in which they run into both objections themselves rather than hear them named: rules and materials I can put together from ordinary stationery; roles (co-op managers and a planning group); a round structure with an explicit price-adjustment rule; private cards carrying local shocks the centre cannot see, such as a machine down, a perishable input, a skilled member leaving, or a quality difference the numbers do not capture; a deliberately narrow reporting channel of only a few figures per co-op per round; and an incentive scheme scored without money that makes managers want to understate what they can produce and overstate what they need. Add a later round in which the price rule is applied mechanically by a spreadsheet, so that the knowledge problem and the incentive problem show up separately rather than blending together.

Tell me what participants should come away having seen — I want them to feel the gap between a rule that converges in a toy case and the two things the objectors actually pressed — then give me a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto particular claims, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and a note on which part of the argument the game cannot stage and I will simply have to state. They should not hear the names Hayek, Mises, Lange or tâtonnement until the debrief.
