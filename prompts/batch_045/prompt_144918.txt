You are a professional philosopher who works on social ontology and the game-theoretic structure of conventions, and who has advised universities and professional bodies on conduct policy. Think carefully before you write: begin with a short private pass in which you enumerate candidate principles and the cases that break each of them, then commit to one and draft.

I sit on the four-person conduct committee of an international learned society — roughly four thousand members, an annual conference in a different host city each year, and a fellowship that sends about twenty doctoral students to institutions abroad for a term. We owe the council a one-page guidance insert on everyday local conventions: tipping, queue discipline, informal street-crossing, and the like. The committee is split, and the discussion of this question that I have seen in public has never been much help: it is all the when-in-Rome rule on one side and accusations of moral tourism on the other, with no attention to what kind of convention is in play.

Here is the distinction I have been pushing in committee — I have not seen it drawn in print anywhere, so treat it as stipulated rather than received. Call a convention (a) coordinative when its point is only that everyone do the same thing, so that any departure makes the practice worse for others — queue order, which side of the pavement you keep; (b) extractive when a visitor's noncompliance shifts a cost onto an identifiable local third party — the server whose pooled share shrinks, the cloakroom attendant who is reprimanded when a foreign visitor leaves nothing; (c) locally dispreferred when it is an equilibrium locals themselves would exit if they could coordinate on exiting — a city's tipping rate ratcheting upward, or non-queueing that is stable only because everyone expects everyone else to push in — and where a visitor's compliance is itself part of what holds the equilibrium in place.

Three real incidents from last year are on the table. A fellow who holds that tipping is a bad institution declined to tip for three weeks in a host city where wages are set on the assumption of tips; the council has heard that one server was docked over it. Another fellow insisted on holding a queue at a registration desk and was simply swept aside each time, delaying the four other members of her lab group who were relying on her to collect their badges. A third waited at empty crossings, in a city where crossing with the crowd is near universal and unenforced, and ended up separated from her group and late to two sessions.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate, addressed to the committee and no longer than about six hundred words: a bottom line in the first two sentences; a recommendation among full compliance with local convention, compliance only where noncompliance imposes costs on identifiable others, and no duty of compliance beyond non-interference and private mitigation; an explicit ruling on locally dispreferred conventions, including what our fellows should do when they cannot tell which kind of convention they are facing; and one concrete implementation step with a date. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side its best formulation rather than a straw version — in particular, take seriously the thought that a visitor who refuses to comply is making a point at a local person's expense, and that a visitor's model of a strange city's equilibrium is usually worse than the locals' revealed behaviour. No citations and no suggestions for further reading; the memo stands on argument alone.

End with a final line in the form \answer{LABEL}, where LABEL is one of FULL-COMPLIANCE, COST-BASED, or NON-INTERFERENCE-ONLY, followed by a colon and at most five words naming any carve-out.
