I'm on the pest management advisory committee for the university arboretum — 40 acres, a big chunk of it a documented collection, including an allée of ash planted in the 1920s as a war memorial. County extension confirmed last month that the ashes are infested with emerald ash borer. Without treatment they tell us to expect most of them dead inside five years. We have maybe three weeks before the trunk-injection window closes for the season, and the committee has to hand the provost a recommendation we can all sign. There are four of us: the grounds superintendent, an entomologist from the ag school, a bioethicist the provost stuck on as a community rep, and me (I'm landscape architecture, I do the collections side).

The options on the table: systemic insecticide by trunk injection, repeated every couple of years for as long as we want the trees; releasing the state-approved parasitic wasps that lay eggs in the borer larvae and then persist on their own; pre-emptive removal of the ashes and replanting with non-host species over five years; or doing nothing and letting the allée go as a designed object. Complicating it: the campus signed a pollinator pledge a couple years back that bars neonicotinoids on university land, there are student hives about 300 yards away, and a nature preschool uses the grove three mornings a week.

I spent about three hours arguing with a friend about this last night and we got absolutely nowhere, which is why I'd rather just hand the committee something written. Her position, roughly: this is gardening, insects are almost certainly not sentient in any sense that matters, and all the moral language is camouflage for the fact that we like the trees and want to keep them. My position was that the *means* matter independently of the body count — that injecting a tree and releasing a self-perpetuating predator are different kinds of act even if they end in the same number of dead larvae, mostly because one of them we can stop and the other we can't, and one we do once and the other we set running forever. Then she made two points I couldn't answer. First, if the grubs matter, the university is already killing insects on a scale that dwarfs this — mowing, the mouse traps in the dorms, the ag school's own work — and nobody loses any sleep over any of it, so our sudden scrupulousness here is just aesthetic. Second, she said the wasp option is the *worst* of the four, not the kindest: the chemical kills quickly and in one season, whereas the wasps make a slow, ongoing, permanent thing out of it, they'll spill onto native wood-borers we didn't intend to hit, and we'll have created a population we can't recall if we change our minds. That one has been stuck in my head.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate, the kind of thing the provost could act on. I need a bottom line — pick one, or a sequenced package with actual dates — not a menu. It should say what we owe the beekeepers, the preschool and the public given we're using land they use, and it should say what would count as evidence to reverse course at the spring review. It should not just punt to "we don't know whether insects are sentient" — tell us how much of the decision actually turns on that, and what follows if nobody can settle it. We're not going to go chase down studies, so don't build anything on numbers you can't stand behind; reason from what a grounds crew actually deals with. And be honest about the strongest thing on whichever side loses, including my friend's inconsistency point if that's the one that bites — I'd rather hand over a memo that names the thing I couldn't answer than one that pretends it's settled. Keep it to something I can paste into the packet.
