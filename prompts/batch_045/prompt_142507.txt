I'm putting together a short paper — around 3000 words, for a graduate seminar on philosophy of action — on the toxin puzzle, and I've got a set of notes that I'd like to have torn apart before I commit to writing actual prose. What I want is a close critical read of the argument as it stands, not a summary of the puzzle and not a map of the secondary literature: I'm not looking for references or further reading, just for your reaction to whether the moves I'm making work. A friend I discussed it with said the whole thing collapses into the familiar slogan that you can't intend to do what you know you won't do; I think that's too quick, but I can't tell whether my central move is a genuine advance on that slogan or just a more elaborate way of asserting it. For reference, the case I'm working from is the standard one: an eccentric benefactor pays you a very large sum at noon on condition that, at the moment of payment, you have formed the intention to drink, before the day is out, a toxin that will make you severely but harmlessly ill for about a day; once you have the money you have no remaining reason to drink and every reason not to, so the question is whether you can collect.

The notes below are the skeleton. The core idea is that the case is hard because it exploits a double role that intention normally plays. Call the first the executive role: an intention to do A initiates and sustains A-ing, coordinates sub-plans, and triggers reconsideration when the agent's reasons for A-ing lapse. Call the second the settledness role: to intend to A is to have closed deliberation about whether to A, and that closure does independent work — it licenses others' predictions about you, supports your own self-conception, and is what you express when you say you're going to A. Normally the two roles travel together; the toxin case, I want to say, pays for the second while our test for genuineness runs through the first, and the apparent impossibility of collecting is the felt collision of those two.

1. The reframing I want to defend. The fatal ingredient, I claim, is not foreseen non-performance but foreseen decisive future reason against performance. Contrast case: a terminally ill novelist who knows she will be dead before she can act settles, now, that her unpublished manuscripts are to be burned. We treat that as a genuine intention despite her certain knowledge that she will never execute it. So the slogan my friend offered is false as stated, and the toxin case must be doing its work somewhere else — in the fact that the agent foresees that at action time her reasons will have flipped, not merely that at action time she won't act. I also want to say what this does to the usual formulations: it relocates the puzzle from the metaphysics of what intentions are to the rationality of commitments that are known in advance to be reason-resistant.

2. The positive proposal: graded resolution. Intentions, on my view, come in degrees of how much future reconsideration has been ruled out, and the degree you can achieve is indexed to the expected stability of your reasons. To make this testable I introduce a probabilistic variant: same offer, except that after the intention is formed a chance process with known probability p will, at midnight, compel you to execute exactly what you intended at noon. As p rises from zero toward one, I predict a smooth increase in how readily the agent can intend — no sharp threshold, and the felt impossibility in the original case is the limit case at p = 0, amplified by how salient the aversive act is.

3. A second variant, which I'll call the inverted toxin: you are paid at noon for having formed the intention not to drink a potion that is delightful, harmless, and freely available to you that evening, and which after noon you will have every reason to drink. This removes the aversiveness while keeping the structure of no reason at action time to comply. My account predicts it should be just as hard as the original. If intuitive difficulty drops sharply instead, then aversiveness is doing real work and the double-role diagnosis is carrying less weight than I claim.

4. What I take to follow. If the two roles can dissociate, then in cases where the reward is keyed to a mental state — the recursive decide-to-decide cases, predictors who read intentions rather than acts, offers to pay for believing or for intending — what the agent produces is not the state that normally settles what she will do, and the principle that you can always intend to A when you have decisive reason to intend to A has to be given up or restricted.

5. Worries I can already see. (i) Maybe the two-role split is just a relabeling of the puzzle rather than a diagnosis of it. (ii) Maybe degrees of resolution is incoherent because intentions are all-or-nothing — you've either settled it or you haven't — and the thing that really varies is strength of desire or weight of reasons. (iii) Maybe the dying novelist isn't really a case of intending at all, but of wanting, or of requesting. (iv) And, most worryingly, maybe the probabilistic variant changes the subject: once p > 0 the intention has expected executive work to do, so the executive role is back in play and the case no longer isolates the settledness role at all.

What I'd like from you, concretely: (a) whether the reframing in 1 is genuinely a step forward or merely a restatement of the standard framing, and if it's the latter, what the standard framing would have to be committed to for my version to add something; (b) which of the two variants is the more serious threat to the account, and — given that I've already conceded (iv) for the probabilistic one — whether that concession guts the argument or whether I can absorb it; (c) whether graded resolution can be restated so that it survives the all-or-nothing objection, perhaps by relocating the gradation somewhere other than in the intention itself; and (d) the strongest single objection you'd put in a referee report, aimed at whichever step you take to be weakest, written out as one paragraph. If the two-role split strikes you as having some consequence for paying-for-mental-states cases beyond the ones I list in 4, I'd like to hear that too.
