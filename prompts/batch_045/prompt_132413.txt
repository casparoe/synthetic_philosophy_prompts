I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a book set in a mid-sized city in a country I made up, where basically nothing moves through a government office without an envelope or, in the rural chapters, a goat. The chapter I'm stuck on is written from inside the head of a woman who pays a clerk to release her late father's land-title file, which has been sitting in a cabinet for eleven months, and whether she's doing something bad is the difference between the chapter being a confession and it being a grievance. So this isn't idle for me.

At a residency last month two other writers got into it at dinner and I have been chewing on it ever since, because it is more or less the argument my protagonist is having with herself. Bea is from Minnesota and spent two years with a rule-of-law NGO; Tunde grew up in Lagos and lives in Berlin now. Roughly, as I remember it:

(1) Bea: it's corruption, full stop. The clerk is selling a decision that isn't his to sell. He holds an office, the office is a trust, the rule is the rule, and whether the price arrives as cash or livestock is local color.

(2) Tunde: the office-as-trust picture is a recent and fairly provincial ideal — impersonal bureaucratic fairness, a couple of centuries old and mostly at home in a handful of rich countries. Across most of the world and most of history, an officeholder is expected to be a patron: refusing a gift insults the giver, and refusing a kinsman is its own moral failure. You aren't describing a universal wrong, you're describing Northwestern Europe and then grading everyone else against it.

(3) Bea: two replies. First, "they don't experience it as wrong" isn't a defense — every society that lives with this has a thick slang vocabulary for the payment and real fury at the man who demands it; people experience it as a shakedown. Second, the harm isn't exotic: the family that can't pay stays untitled for another year, and a scarce public good gets allocated to whoever has money instead of by the rule.

(4) Tunde: concedes some of that but splits the cases. Paying to get something you are *already legally owed* is extortion — the wrong is the official's, not the payer's, and calling it corruption muddles it with theft. And in a thin, unreliable state, where the file will never move otherwise, paying looks less like betraying a trust and more like buying a service on a market. There's no functioning trust there to betray. Also: in my chapter, once the clerk takes the money he actually works the file — three offices, two weeks, stays late. If he delivers, where exactly is the corrupt part?

(5) Bea: "no trust to betray" proves too much — it makes the weaker institution the bigger excuse, which is backwards, since this is often what made it weak. And a bribe that works isn't less corrupt, it's a bribe with results. The line that does the work is excludability: a patron who gets his district a borehole delivers something nobody is excluded from, and that's representation with bad aesthetics; my clerk hands a title deed to one family that another family was equally entitled to. That's the sale of an office. Separately, look at the practice from far enough out and it's a tollbooth everyone erects and everyone pays — collectively self-inflicted, which is a reason to hate it, not a reason to excuse the individual payment.

(6) Tunde: the excludability line wobbles. The borehole gets sited in the patron's own village and routed to the people who voted right — a "public good" that's as targeted as the title deed, just bigger. And in the US a donation buys access and a lobbying firm drafts the rule, and it's all legal: on your own account, is that corrupt or not? If the concept convicts a goat in my city and acquits a check in Ohio, maybe the concept is doing something other than moral work. Last thing, and this is the bit that's actually breaking my chapter: in the town I've written, the gift is offered in front of the giver's family, and refusing it humiliates him publicly. Saying no is genuinely cruel. So paying and refusing both leave a residue.

(7) Bea: on the US stuff — fine, some of that is corrupt in substance even where the law shrugs, but that shows the legal concept gets captured by the people with lawyers, not that the moral concept is parochial. The residue you're pointing at is real, but it's a reason to be careful about blame, not a reason to retire the word.

I have to write the scene this week and I honestly can't tell whos winning. Please go through it move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say why, and then tell me who is more right overall. Then — the part I actually need — say what *both* of them are missing. My hunch is that they're quietly using one word for two different jobs, a juridical one and a moral one, and that the residue in (6) isn't a cute edge case but the whole difficulty; but tell me if that's definately just a novelist's excuse for not deciding, because I'd rather know than be flattered. End with a short note on how confident you are in what you've claimed and single out the one point you're least sure of — I want to know which part of the verdict is safe to lean on when I'm writing dialogue. American English, please.
