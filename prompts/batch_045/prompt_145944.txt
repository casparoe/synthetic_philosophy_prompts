I'm stuck on a passage in Hobbes's Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematicks (1656), the bit where he is arguing that geometry is a demonstrative science and then, weirdly, that civil philosophy is too. I have to paraphrase because my PDF is a scanned old edition and I don't trust my eyes on the spelling, so here is my rough paraphrase of the passage:

In every subject, you only get genuine science if you already know the causes, or the generation and construction, of the thing you are studying; where the causes are known, demonstration is possible, but where you have to start from the effects and go hunting for the causes, you can't demonstrate anything. Geometry is therefore demonstrable, because we ourselves draw and describe the lines and figures we reason about; and civil philosophy is demonstrable for teh same reason, because we ourselves make the commonwealth.

What I can't work out is what Hobbes thinks the real source of the certainty is. Is it that we physically draw the figures, so the production is under our control? Or is it that we get to lay down the definitions, so the objects are whatever we say they are? Or is it something about construction in general, namely that a constructed thing has a known cause while a natural thing does not?

Here is where I need help, and I should say my study group came up with a gloss that might be somthing, but I can't tell if it is brilliant or BS. Half-jokingly, my TA suggested that for Hobbes a line is not a static object at all but the trace of a body in motion, and motion is the one thing we experience from the inside as produced by our own endeavor or will. On that reading, when we draw a figure we are causing the generating motion by our own voluntary effort, and that is why we know its cause from the inside, as it were. Call this the voluntary-motion reading. I have no idea whether Hobbes actually holds it, but I want to test it as a hypothesis.
