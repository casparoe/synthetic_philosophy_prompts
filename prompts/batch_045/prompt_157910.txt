I record a small, slightly scruffy fortnightly podcast on moral philosophy, and Sunday's guest is Dr Yusuf Adeyemi, who defends the normative-power account of promising: the thought that in promising you exercise a power to change what you owe, so that breaking a promise is a bare wrong that isn't just disappointed expectations, wasted reliance, or a dent in a useful social practice. He's good, but he also has one move he uses for basically every objection — 'that's a story about why promising is useful, not about what the obligation is' — and I'd like a set of questions that makes that move progressively harder to get away with over the course of the hour.

Could you draft me eight to ten? I send the questions over in advance so he'll have prepped answers, which means softballs are useless; I need each one to press on a real case and then have a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give.

Things I specifically want covered:

- The gratuitous promise: I promise to do something I was going to do anyway, or that makes no difference at all to the promisee, and then I don't do it. Why is anything wrong? Follow up on whatever he says about having given your word.
- The forgotten promise: the promisee has completely forgotten I ever promised, no reliance, no expectation either way. Does the duty survive — and here's the bit I want squeezed — if only the promisee can release me and she's forgotten the promise exists, am I stuck forever, or can I release myself? That seems to cut against the directed-duty picture whichever way he goes.
- A promise made jointly to two people where one of them releases me and the other refuses to. Halved, extinguished, or fully intact? The follow-up should ask what his answer tells us about what a directed duty even is.
- Promises whose content turns out to be wrong or mildly bad. If the power really does change what I owe, does promising ever make a bad act obligatory? And if he says the duty is only pro tanto, ask him for a case where it actually changes what he most has reason to do.
- Promisees who can't play the practice game at all — a toddler, someone with late-stage dementia. And, if there's room, promises to the dead.
- The overhearer: I promise A, B overhears and reorganises her life around it. Does B have any standing to complain when I don't perform?
- Duress by a third party: I'm threatened into promising, but not by the promisee, who's done nothing wrong. Void, voidable, or binding until she lets me off?

End with one question about what sort of thing would actually count as evidence against the view. If he falls back on 'that's reliance-thinking in disguise' anywhere, I want the follow-up ready. Nothing generic please — no 'what got you into philosophy'.
