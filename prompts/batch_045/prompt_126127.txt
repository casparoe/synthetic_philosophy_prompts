hey — I want a diagnosis of a debate that's gone stale, not a verdict on who's right.

I'm in a small reading group that's spent about a year on whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading someone (saying something true, arranged so the hearer ends up believing something false). Three positions crystallized around month two and nobody has moved since:

1. Equivalence: there's no morally interesting difference. What matters is deliberately getting someone to believe something false. Whether you do that by asserting a falsehood or by arranging what they'll infer is a fact about mechanism, not about wrongness.
2. Specialness: asserting what you believe to be false is its own wrong, over and above the deception. You asked the hearer to take your word for something and abused that. Misleading at least leaves them their own inference.
3. The reversalists: in a lot of real cases misleading is worse, because it's engineered to be deniable — you get the effect of the lie without ever being on the hook for it.

What's bugging me is that on cases we agree far more than the framing suggests. Everyone thinks lying to the murderer at the door is fine. Everyone thinks quietly implying a false delivery date to close a sale is bad. Everyone thinks a politician who words something so that supporters hear a falsehood is doing something objectionable. And still 1 and 2 haven't converged at all.

My hypothesis, which I want kicked around: this isn't one debate, it's roughly three things sharing a heading.

(a) A thin question about whether there's a wrong-making feature distinctive of false assertion. My guess is 1 and 2 basically agree here in the abstract and only diverge because the cases used as data smuggle in extra baggage: institutional roles, whether the hearer has any claim to the information, how much of the inferential work is left to them, whether anyone could ever prove what happened.

(b) A question about which category should do the work in blame and in rule design. Here I think there's a structural point that's underused in the discussion: a lie leaves an auditable trace (you said p, p is false, here's the recording), while misleading leaves none — which looks like a large part of why legal systems criminalize one and handle the other through disclosure duties. That's an institutional fact wearing the costume of a moral intuition.

(c) A bit that might just be verbal: whether 'lying' is defined by what's asserted or by what's communicated. If it's the latter, half of position 1 collapses into 2 by stipulation.

I also want to float a test I made up, call it the escalation test. If the hearer presses you — are you telling me that p? — would you say yes? If yes, your misleading was a lie with the assertion outsourced. If no, you're exploiting the gap between what you said and what you let them conclude. Positions 1 and 2 seem to me to come apart sharply on which of those two agents is worse, and I suspect that gap is where the real disagreement lives.

So: substantive or verbal? Are the sides answering different questions under one name? Which of the moving parts — the inference-work point, the deniability point, the auditable-trace point, the rights-to-the-truth bedrock — is load-bearing, and which is decoration? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding about what hearers actually infer, a change of question? Or is this one where no progress should be expected, and why?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the diagnosis, in one line — then give me the reasoning. No preamble and dont restate my question back at me.
