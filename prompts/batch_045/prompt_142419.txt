Hey — a thought experiment I've been chewing on and can't quite get striaght, pls answer in British English.

Set-up (you can take it as given, no citations needed): a lot of people who worry about wild animal suffering assume that if wild lives are on balance bad — short, full of starvation, parasitism, cold, most juveniles dying before they get anywhere — then destroying habitat is, other things equal, a good thing, because it cancels a whole future stream of bad lives. Call this the *subtraction* picture: suffering scales with habitat, roughly hectares × years, so less habitat means less suffering, and the animals who never get born aren't around to be harmed by it.

The rival — call it the *leakage* picture — says that's too container-shaped a way to think. Animals aren't sitting in a habitat like soup in a bowl; regional populations are regulated by density-dependent stuff (dispersal, competition for territories, disease, predation pressure), so when you clear a patch you don't necessarily delete the animals, you squeeze them. Survivors pile into what's left, get pushed out into sink habitat where they die anyway, and the emptied ground gets recolonised — often by generalists and edge-tolerant species with high reproductive rates and colossal juvenile mortality — so the community you end up with may churn through just as many short miserable lives per hectare as the one you bulldozed, or more.

Compare these two pictures properly. In particular:

- Which quantity is actually the right one to track in the accounting: the standing population, the annual birth cohort, the number of deaths, or some suffering-weighted integral over time? Do the two pictures disagree about *which number matters*, or only about what happens to it when habitat goes?
- Sketch in words (no real maths needed) the shape of the "welfare yield curve": total future suffering in a landscape as a function of how much habitat is left. Roughly linear? Flattening out? Could it be hump-shaped, so that an *intermediate* amount of habitat produces the most suffering of all? What in each picture drives the shape?
- Is there a version of the subtraction picture that survives the leakage objection — say, clearing that's permanent and sealed (a reservoir, a car park, a mine) versus clearing that reverts to scrub, pasture or plantation?
- And the awkward bit: if the leakage picture is roughly right, does that make habitat destruction *actively worse* than the naive subtractionist thinks, or does it just make it a useless lever — plenty of harm done for no welfare gain — without actually flipping the sign?

Say where you land if you can, but I'd rather see the disagreement located precisely than hedged to death.
