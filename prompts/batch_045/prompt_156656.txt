So I somehow let myself get talked onto the board of the Hallam Trust about a year ago — I write historical fiction, three novels set in the 1790s and one in 1913, which I think is why they wanted me — and it's turned into something I'm not equipped for. The Trust is a modest endowment left by a novelist, Octavia Hallam, 1889 to 1962, and it funds two things: a prize for a debut novel set before 1900, and a small reissue series for neglected fiction of the same period. Hallam herself is the whole problem. On one thing she was genuinely ahead of her time: she wrote about unmarried mothers and domestic violence in ways that got two of her books banned in the thirties. On another she was thoroughly of her time and then some: from the early forties until she died she published essay after essay arguing that refugees and migrants from eastern europe were unassimilable, that there should be literacy tests and a hard cap on naturalisation, and that people saying otherwise were sentimentalists who'd never had to live next door to it. She never retracted any of it, and this is the bit that split our last meeting — she was still publishing versions of it in the mid-fifties, by which point the camps and the displaced-persons crisis were common knowledge and a fair number of writers who'd held exactly those views in the thirties had publicly reversed themselves. So the usual move, that she couldn't have known any better, doesn't sit quite right, and our chair, who is lovely but who says things like the past is a foreign country, keeps saying it anyway.

We have to put something to the AGM in the first week of June, and the board is in roughly three camps. One wants to keep the name and staple a short historical note onto the prize and the front of each reissue. One wants to drop Hallam's name from both programmes and fund them out of the general endowment, on the ground that honouring somebody is something we do now, not something that happened in 1962, and that a note nobody reads is just varnish. One — me, sort of — thinks both of those are answering the wrong question first, because what we actually need is a rule we can apply to the next case, and we already know there are two more named bequests heading our way, one of whose namesakes is almost certainly worse than Hallam. At the last meeting somebody circulated a draft standard I've started calling the reachable-dissent rule: we count a historical figure as culpable for a view only if people in her own time and place, with no more information than she had, publicly argued the contrary. It looks beautifully non-presentist, because it never asks her to anticipate us. But a friend of mine who does intellectual history — we argued about this for two hours over a bad bottle of wine — pointed out something I can't shake: the dissenters we count as relevant are just the ones we now think were right, so the test quietly measures whether she agreed with the ancestors we happen to admire, and it also means she'd be assessed differently if she'd been born fifteen years later or lived in a different city. Her own alternative, which I only half understood and which is why I'm writing to you, is that this was never a question about Hallam's soul at all but about what our money is for, now, and that once you see that the whole argument about what she could have known looks like a detour.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate to the other eight members. It should recommend one concrete course of action on the two programmes — keep, rename, or something I haven't thought of — with a timeline and who is supposed to do what before June, and it should also state the general rule we'd be adopting for the bequests coming next year, since the whole point is that we'll be doing this again. Please take the rename-it-now position seriously rather than batting it away: the version of it I find hardest is that a prize is a continuing speech act in the present tense and that a context statement nobody reads is a way of feeling like you've done something. One more thing that's specific to my end of this and that I'd like you to use rather than ignore: the prize guidelines tell applicants that characters should be true to their period, and every year we get manuscripts where true to their period turns out to mean a protagonist saying things the author clearly takes to be simply true. Whatever we decide about Hallam will be read by those applicants as guidance on exactly that, so if your rule has implications for how a novelist in 2026 ought to render a character who is wrong by our lights, say what they are. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — and lead with what you think we should actually do. About a thousand words, and don't hedge into both sides are reasonable, I've had enough of that from the rest of the board.
