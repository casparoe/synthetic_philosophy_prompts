i've been reading a bit on my own about how record-keeping is a power thing — no classes, just whatever i can find — and the part that keeps bugging me is who ends up in the files at all, and what the gaps do to what people later are able to know. i made up two ideas while thinking about it and i want to see if they actually hold up on a real case instead of just sounding clever in my head.

first one i call the list trap: for someone on the margins, the only way to get protection or recognition is to get written down, but getting written down is exactly what makes you findable later. so the same file that saves you is the file that can be used on you, and you can't have one side without the other. second one is that there seem to be loud silences and quiet ones. a loud silence is when stuff is destroyed in public — everyone sees the fire, so we at least know something is missing and roughly what shape it had. a quiet silence is when nothing was ever written in the first place and nobody notices there's a hole. my worry is that the loud ones get all the attention (memorials, books about what was lost) and the quiet ones stay invisible, so even our sense of what's been lost is skewed.

the case i want to try them on is Magnus Hirschfeld's Institute for Sexual Science in Berlin. from what i've picked up: opened 1919, first sexology institute anywhere, run as a charity, gave free or cheap medical and legal help to gay and trans people. Hirschfeld coined the word transvestite and pushed the Berlin police to issue papers letting people wear clothes that didn't match the sex they were assigned at birth, which protected them from being arrested for public nuisance — and the Institute was the body that assessed people for those papers. it charged for them, something like 150 marks for a court report and 50 for a paper, and it didn't give them to everyone; apparently it depended partly on how well someone passed in public, and a trans woman named Paula Huhn was refused in 1929. Prussia let people change first names from 1920 but only to a short list of neutral ones. then in early May 1933 Nazi student groups raided the place, hauled out the library and the files and burned them in the street; Hirschfeld was out of the country and never came back. if i've got any of that wrong just say so quietly and keep going, and please don't tell me what to read next — i have no library access, so work from what you already know.

what i'd like: run the who-gets-recorded-and-what-gets-left-out frame over this properly. who are the keepers of the record here, who is being recorded, what are the actual terms of getting in the file, and what does the frame predict should have happened. then use my two ideas on it and tell me honestly whether they do any work or whether they're just the same point in worse words. and then go the other direction: what does the case do to the frame? it looks to me like an odd fit, because the people building this archive weren't the state — they were a marginal, half-funded charity trying to make a stigmatised group legible on purpose, and being legible was what kept people out of jail. so is this a case the theory confirms, one it never thought about, or one that shows it's only ever about archives held from above? also tell me where it just goes quiet and I shouldn't force it to speak. and one counterfactual i can't stop thinking about: if the files had been left alone and were sitting in an archive today, what exactly would we have, and would it be a good source — or would it only ever have been a record of the people who could pass well enough to get in?
