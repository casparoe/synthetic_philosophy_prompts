I teach a small upper-level seminar on decision theory and formal epistemology (third-years and a couple of MA students, about ten people) and I'm writing one question for the take-home final. They all know Bayes' theorem, conditionalization and expected-utility maximisation, and we did the standard result that the expected value of free information is never negative -- we just call it Good's theorem in the course, no citation needed, I don't want them naming any papers. The proof they saw in class is the one-liner: write the value of using the signal as the sum, over possible results r, of P(r) times the maximum expected utility of an act computed with the posterior P(s|r), then swap max and sum to get that this is at least the maximum expected utility computed with the prior.

The problem is that last year every single answer was the same two moves: free information can't hurt because you can always just ignore it, and here is the inequality again. So I want a question that breaks that. Here's the case I've been playing with. Bea has a condition that comes in two strains, A and B; her credence that she has strain A is 0.8. Two treatments: surgery (utility 10 if A, 0 if B) and a drug regimen (utility 2 if A, 9 if B). Before deciding she can take a free test that prints + or - , with P(+|A) = 0.8, P(-|A) = 0.2, P(+|B) = 0.4, P(-|B) = 0.6. Bea is not a perfect Bayesian: she has a stable, well-documented disposition I'll call prior-forgetting -- after seeing a result R she sets her credence to P(R|A) / (P(R|A) + P(R|B)), i.e. as if her prior had been 50/50. She can't change this and she knows she has it. Before the test runs she can either sign a binding directive committing her to a treatment (or to a rule mapping results to treatments) whatever the printout says, or simply decline the test and never see it. Numbers are invented and not medically realistic -- tell me if that matters. What I want is a question with three parts: (a) compute her expected utility if she declines, if she takes the test and then chooses on her prior-forgetting credences, and if she signs a directive first; show that the test is worth exactly 0 to a perfect Bayesian here but strictly negative to Bea, say how much she should be willing to pay not to see the printout, and in one sentence say why the numbers come out that way; (b) name exactly which step of the proof above fails for Bea and why, and then assess the you-can-always-ignore-it objection -- does it work for her, and what does it presuppose about what is under an agent's control; (c) is Bea irrational to decline the test, is she irrational to have the disposition, and what does the case show about Good's theorem -- refuted, in need of explicit extra premises, or fine but doing less work than people think, and in particular whether it can be used as an independent argument that you ought to conditionalize or that you ought to want information. 25 marks total, about 45 minutes, calculators fine.
