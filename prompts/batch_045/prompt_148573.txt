I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas — and I'm stuck on a piece about whether it makes any sense to say an AI system did something "for a reason." Which has dragged me into this business of free-floating rationales: explanations that genuinely make sense of why something was done, that would genuinely justify it, but that aren't anybody's reasons. Rationales with the reasoner left out. I notice I have really firm gut reactions about individual cases and absolutely no idea what I think in general, so I want you to help me dig out the principle.

Please take this seriously and work through it carefully rather than firing off a tour of the usual positions. Act as a professional philosopher who specialises in metaethics and the theory of practical reason. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate principles before you commit to one, and be explicit about where you're guessing. No reading lists, no paper titles, no citations — I'm working offline and I just want the argument. Somewhere under 800 words.

My verdicts, case by case:

1. A thermostat switches the furnace on. I won't say it had a reason, but "because the temperature fell below the setpoint" is a perfectly good rationale, and it's the kind of thing that would justify the switch if anyone were making it. Rationale: yes. Reason: no.

2. My heart beats faster on a hill. Same shape — a real functional rationale, not a reason. I'm confident about this one and about #1; nothing is owed to the heart and nothing is at stake for it.

3. In 1963 some engineers at a turbine plant write a memo explaining why they specified a titanium fastener at one joint. Nobody alive ever reads it. Fifty years later a new team independently works out the same thing and specifies titanium. My verdict: the memo was right, it was normative the whole time — someone should have used titanium there, and it would have been a mistake not to — even during the decades when the rationale was sitting in a drawer and no agent had any reason at all. I feel this surprisingly strongly.

4. My grandmother asked me to scatter her ashes on a particular fell, and then she died. The reason doesn't evaporate when she does, but it's not her reason anymore — she's not a reasoner now. It's mine, inherited.

5. A firm has a reason to hedge its currency exposure. Everyone who understood the hedge leaves and is replaced by people who don't. I still want to say the firm has the reason, and I don't want to reduce that to "well, some new employee will eventually have a reason."

6. The non-identity case — the one where which people exist depends on which policy we pick. We pick the policy that leads to different people existing, people whose lives are good but somewhat worse than the lives other people would have had. My verdict: there's a reason against doing that, even though nobody is made worse off and so it isn't a reason to benefit anyone. This is the one I'd least like to give up.

7. Here's the one I keep flip-flopping on. A dispatch program reroutes power and prints a line citing the overload on line 4. The consideration it cites really does justify the reroute by the system's own operating standards; the rationale is correct, publicly checkable, and generated by a process that tracked it. But I balk at saying the program had a reason. And then I notice I said yes in #3 to a rationale sitting in a drawer, and yes in #5 to a firm — and I can't say what the firm has that the program doesn't. A goal state? A standpoint? Interests? Somebody who could be worse off?

My own stab, which I don't trust: call a rationale an *orphan* when its addressee slot is permanently empty — nothing could ever take it up. Orphans are true and explanatory but not normative, and that's why #1 and #2 are out. A rationale becomes a reason when there is some role that could take it up, even if nobody currently occupies the role — which is why #3 and #5 come out as reasons, addressed to whoever maintains the turbine and to whoever speaks for the firm. But I can already see the trouble: if a mere role is enough, the dispatch program has one too, and #7 flips to yes. And if the role has to come with a standpoint or the possibility of being better or worse off, then #6 collapses, because the people who'd exist aren't anybody's standpoint yet.

So: can my addressee test be repaired, or is there a better principle behind these verdicts? Tell me precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I have to drop — including #7, if I do. Then give me your account's verdict on two cases I haven't listed: (a) an ecosystem or a species — does the species have a reason to keep its range contiguous, or is that an orphan rationale; and (b) a future system that reads our archived memos and inherits the projects, with no human left who could take up the rationale.

Finish with a single machine-readable line, the last line of your answer, beginning "Verdict:" and stating the principle in fifteen words or fewer.
