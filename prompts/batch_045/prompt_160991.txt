I work on a small internal governance group that has to write the value-specification section of a frontier model's deployment spec, and our group has drifted into using one analogy for everything. We say that an aligned system should be neutral among competing conceptions of the good in roughly the way a liberal state is supposed to be: it should not take sides on what makes a life go well, it should supply the framework within which individuals and communities pursue their own ends, and the only commitments it may enforce are the ones required to keep that framework standing. The analogy carries our working conclusion, which is that we never have to answer "whose values?" substantively. Aggregate only over what everyone could be given reason to accept, restrict the model's shared layer to that thin overlap, push the contested thick material downstream into per-user or per-deployment configuration, and the question dissolves into procedure.

I want that analogy audited rather than endorsed or dismissed. Please do the following.

Set out the correspondence explicitly: for each element of the liberal-state picture — the demos, territory and borders, citizenship, elections and other authorization mechanisms, the monopoly on legitimate coercion, the coercion-justification link that neutrality is supposed to answer, constitutional essentials versus ordinary legislation, public reason, the public/private distinction, the assumption that citizens can revise their own ends — say what, if anything, plays that role for a deployed model, and mark each mapping as clean, strained, or broken, with the reason in one line. Then identify the features the analogy quietly imports: things true of the source that the target does not have, and that are doing work in the argument rather than sitting inert. For this particular use I suspect the load-bearing ones include these, but tell me if I have the emphasis wrong:

- A state's demos is bounded and persists across generations, and its authorization comes from that demos. A model's affected set is not a demos: it includes people who never opted in, people in jurisdictions with no input into our process, future people, and arguably nonhuman animals. Neutrality was a requirement about justifying coercion to those subject to it; that structure has no obvious counterpart when "being subject to" means being foreseeably affected by an output.
- A model's outputs feed back into the values it later aggregates. State neutrality is supposed to leave the distribution of conceptions of the good to the citizens; a system that is neutral with respect to current输入 can still systematically reshape what people come to want, so neutrality here may fail to conserve the very thing it is supposed to leave alone.
- A state must give one answer over one territory; a model can disaggregate. If each user can have their own value-profile, the framework/ends distinction that neutrality runs on may not have a fixed reference, and "letting users choose" may itself be a thick default rather than the absence of one.
- The thin-overlap fallback may not be thin, may be empty or unstable, and may simply be the values of whoever we happened to sample. Neutrality among conceptions of the good is not the same as the intersection of the conceptions of the good actually on offer.
- Neutrality and aggregation pull against each other. Voting-style aggregation produces winners and losers; neutrality is meant to avoid producing either. Our spec currently tries to do both, and I want to know whether that is incoherent or merely in tension.

Then audit the conclusion. Does "we never have to answer whose values" survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If it does not, say exactly which premise the analogy was carrying.

I also want you to adjudicate, using the audit, between four live options for the spec, rather than just describing them:

1. Thin neutrality: shared layer restricted to the overlap, everything thick pushed downstream.
2. Stake-weighted aggregation of thick values, where weight tracks how much a party is foreseeably affected rather than whether they are a user.
3. Meta-aggregation: don't aggregate the values at all, aggregate the aggregation rule, chosen under a veil of ignorance about which value-profile one will end up having — a constitutional-level move that borrows the impartiality intuitions behind both Rawlsian and contractarian arguments.
4. Radical disaggregation: per-user value-profiles with a minimal shared floor.

Option 3 is our own invention and is the one I care about most. Does the liberal-neutrality analogy actually support moving the choice up to the constitutional level, or does that move presuppose a determinate demos at exactly the point where the analogy was supposed to relieve us of needing one?

Work two concrete cases through whichever option you think strongest. First: a user asks the model for ordinary dietary and consumer advice, where the model must pick a default about how much moral weight nonhuman animals get. Second: a manager asks the model to help draft a workplace policy that will bind employees who never chose and never used the system. For each, say what the neutrality analogy prescribes, where the prescription runs out, and what your preferred option does instead.

Finally, propose a better analogy if there is one — something that captures what is actually being done to affected parties without importing a bounded demos and a coercion monopoly. Candidates we have floated are a common carrier, a public utility, a professional fiduciary such as a physician or trustee, and a standards body; take these seriously or replace them, but say what each would license that neutrality does not, and what it loses.

I don't want citations or a literature review — I want the argument, and I want it anchored to mechanisms we actually have available: system-level defaults, per-user personalization, jurisdiction-specific deployments, access tiers, and an internal oversight board.
