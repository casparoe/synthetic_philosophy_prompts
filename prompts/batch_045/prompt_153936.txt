I am writing a novel whose central scene is a hearing, and before I can stage it I need to know what reasoning has to stand behind it.

The novel's world contains a standing body — a Permanent Commission on the Use of Force — which publishes Opinions on historical interventions. Each Opinion returns two verdicts: one on lawfulness, understood as whether the force was authorised by the Security Council, and one on justification, understood as whether, all things considered, it ought to have been done. I have drafted five Opinions as the background against which my characters argue. Now I have to draft the sixth, on the invented intervention the novel turns on, and I want it to be an Opinion my readers cannot dismiss as arbitrary: it has to follow from the five, and before I write it I want to know what exactly it is following from. I am not asking for the correct doctrine of humanitarian intervention; I am asking what doctrine my Commission has been running without quite saying so.

The five, as drafted:

1. Kosovo, 1999. A North Atlantic alliance bombed a sovereign state for some eleven weeks with no authorising resolution, to stop the killing and expulsion of a province's Albanian population. The alliance did not attempt to remove the head of state, claimed no territory, and afterwards the province was placed under United Nations administration with the Council's blessing. My Commission holds it unlawful but justified.

2. Iraq, 2003. Two states and their allies invaded without authorisation, on the strength of a weapons programme that was not there. The government was overthrown, its leader captured and later executed, an insurgency followed, and foreign forces remained for most of a decade. My Commission holds it unlawful and not justified.

3. Libya, 2011. The Council authorised force to protect civilians threatened by their own government. The alliance's air campaign continued until the government had fallen and the head of state was dead; no ground occupation followed; the country broke into militias and rival authorities and the violence spread across its borders. My Commission holds it lawful in its inception, unlawful and not justified as conducted.

4. East Pakistan, 1971. A neighbour intervened after a military crackdown that drove some ten million people across its border. It fought alongside an insurgency that had already declared independence; a new state was formed; the intervener withdrew within months and took nothing. My Commission holds it unlawful but justified.

5. Cambodia, 1978–79. A neighbour invaded and overthrew a regime that had destroyed a very large part of its own population, and the killing stopped. But the intervener installed a client government and remained for roughly a decade against a continuing insurgency, and the Council never authorised any of it. My Commission holds it unlawful and not justified.

The sixth case is of my own invention. Six states belonging to a regional union intervene in a neighbouring state, Tessaria, where after a disputed election and an uprising in the western province of Vardene the national army has begun killing and expelling the Vardene population: perhaps fifty thousand dead in three months, four hundred thousand refugees into two of the intervening states. The Council, with no veto cast and three permanent members abstaining, authorises member states to use force to protect civilians in Vardene and to enforce a ban on military flights over it; the resolution says nothing about the removal of the government. Nine weeks of air strikes destroy the government's air defences and much of its command structure, and Vardene insurgents, armed and advised by officers of the union, take the provincial capital. Without any further resolution, those forces advance on the national capital; the government collapses; the president is detained and eventually handed to an international court. Forces of the union occupy the capital and an interim administration is installed in which Vardene politicians and advisers from the union are dominant. That was seven years ago. The mass killing stopped within a month of the strikes and has not resumed. Elections were held in the fourth year; seventy states recognise the interim government; the Council has never recognised it and has never condemned the intervention; a rump government holds two provinces and a low-intensity insurgency continues; and two of the intervening states hold mining concessions in Vardene, granted in the second year at rates well below market. The protective mandate was renewed twice and then allowed to lapse. The General Assembly has twice called for withdrawal.

What I would like from you:

Predict both verdicts, on lawfulness and on justification, and say which rule produces them.

Set out the rules that fit all five precedents equally well but come apart on the sixth. I can make out at least four candidates: an authorisation rule, on which the Council's resolution is constitutive for lawfulness and justification turns on atrocity, last resort and proportionality; a mandate-integrity rule, on which the fatal fault is the conversion of a protective mandate into the removal of a government, so that the Council cannot be taken to have authorised what it did not authorise; a long-horizon consequentialist rule, on which justification is a matter of net killing avoided over a decade or more and everything else is evidence at best; and an acceptance rule, on which what matters is whether the population in whose name force was used accepts it — which raises the question my Commission has never answered, whether the relevant population is the protected province or the state as a whole. There may be a fifth about mixed motives and the mining concessions; if it earns its place, give it one.

If the five genuinely underdetermine the sixth, tell me plainly: identify the closest analogue among the precedents for each option, and state what principle the Commission would be committing itself to whichever way it rules. And give me one further case — a real one, or one I can invent — whose verdict would let a careful reader tell the surviving rules apart, together with which verdict goes with which rule.

Finally, if more than one Opinion is defensible, tell me which makes the better hearing. I would rather have a Commission that holds together than a conveniently dramatic one, but where both hold together I will take the drama.
