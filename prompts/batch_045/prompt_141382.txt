I am two-thirds of the way through a novel set in a failing seaside town, and a chapter has stalled on a problem I can no longer fake my way through. Two characters — Celia, a retired district nurse, and Frank, her brother-in-law, who is losing his sight — have what is recognisably the same conversation twice, once in early summer and once in November, and I need the reader to feel that the single consideration that made Celia's silence the right thing in June is the very consideration that makes it wrong, or at least not called for, in November — without my ever saying so, because if a character explains it the chapter dies. A philosopher friend tells me the dispute this belongs to has two named sides: those who hold that a feature which is a reason in one case may be no reason at all, or a reason pointing the other way, in another, and that this need not be because some further feature has been added; and those who hold that there is always some further feature, or failing that a further clause, that accounts for the difference. What I need is a case that actually earns the first claim rather than merely illustrating it, together with the argument showing that it does.

The constraints, if you would build one for me: small and realistic — a kitchen, a phone call, a hospital corridor; nobody at risk of dying, no trolleys, nothing a general reader would read as a contrivance. Both versions must be stageable with the same two characters in the same room, differing in at most a handful of lines of dialogue and no more than one circumstance of the surrounding situation. The consequences must be held fixed across the two versions, so that nothing is settled by what happens downstream and the disagreement cannot be smuggled in as a disagreement about outcomes. And no character may say the words principle, defeasible, holism or reason; whatever work the case does has to be doable in the bones of the scene. Please also do not hand me one of the pairs that turns up in every introduction to this debate — I have read them, and so has my editor.

Then, after the scene: name the feature that flips; set out what someone on the first side says about it and why that verdict follows from their view rather than being stipulated by you; give the strongest reply available to someone on the second side, including the reply that keeps adding hedges to the principle until it fits the case, and tell me honestly whether that hedge move is legitimate or whether it has quietly become unfalsifiable. I would also like a note on which single element of your construction is doing the work, and what remains if you take it out — specifically, whether what remains collapses into an ordinary case of one consideration being outweighed by another, which both sides already handle without difficulty. If you think my constraints cannot be jointly satisfied, say which one has to give and why, rather than quietly breaking one of them. And finally, in one sentence, tell me what the reader should feel at the seam between the two versions: if the case works philosophically but is inert on the page, I cannot use it.
