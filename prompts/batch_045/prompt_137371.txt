I co-teach a twelve-person upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and I have one seventy-five-minute slot to spend on the objection that epiphenomenalism is self-stultifying: if the having of an experience plays no causal role in producing my judgment that I have it, then it looks as though I would issue exactly the same judgment whether or not the experience occurred, and my claim to know my own conscious life starts to look either unjustified or a matter of luck. My students all concede the argument in the abstract and none of them believe it. They have read the standard version, they have met the steam-whistle comparison, and their response is invariably that of course their pain is what makes them say "ouch" — so the premise must be wrong somewhere and nothing has been shown. I want to change the order of exposure: have them collide with the structure first, in a setup whose rules they can inspect, and only then tell them what they were colliding with. No one should hear the label until after the debrief begins.

The rough design I keep circling, which I want you to develop, improve, or replace if you have something better, is a two-phase game with small stakes (points, counters, or chips — nothing real) built around hidden stimuli and reports. In the first phase, a participant undergoes something they are asked to report on, but their report is actually generated by a mechanism that never consults what they experienced; the catch is that the mechanism is perfectly reliable, because it reads the same upstream state that produces the experience. In the second phase, that shared upstream link is quietly severed for one participant, so the mechanism keeps producing confident reports while tracking nothing at all. What I want the participants to feel in their bones is the difference between two claims that students routinely run together: that there is no causal path from experience to judgment, and that experience and judgment merely share a common cause. The second is meant to be a genuine reply rather than a restatement of the problem, and I want the game rigged so that the group discovers on its own which of the two is actually doing the damage — ideally by arguing about whether a given report was "still right" once they learn how it was produced. There should also be a betting round, after the mechanism is revealed, in which participants stake points on the truth of reports they or others made, so that the difference between how a judgment is produced and whether it is justified shows up as something they have to act on rather than discuss.

Please give me a runnable artifact: the rules and materials, a timeline with minute markers, the payoff scheme, the exact wording for the reveal, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the argument and says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my bet is that someone will rig the stimuli, or that the group will treat the whole thing as a puzzle about the mechanism rather than about themselves). Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words — I assume it cannot manufacture the felt certainty of a first-person report, only stage a surrogate. And commit to something: tell me which horn you think really does the work — the exclusion of the experience from the causal chain, the counterfactual insensitivity of the judgment, or the loss of any distinctive first-person method — and why the others are alarming mainly in the telling, so that I know which one to press in the debrief and which to let go.
