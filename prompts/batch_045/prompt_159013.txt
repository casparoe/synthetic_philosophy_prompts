I am drafting the animal-welfare chapter of a position paper for a sustainable-agriculture working group, and before I commit anything to print I would like an audit of my own commitments. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I am a welfarist about the ethics of killing animals. What matters is the welfare a creature actually has and loses; I reject sanctity-of-life views, and I reject the idea that species, populations or ecosystems possess value independent of the welfare of the sentient individuals that compose them.

(2) I accept aggregation across individuals. Enough small harms can outweigh a few large ones, and a harm does not become negligible merely because the creature suffering it is small or cognitively simple.

(3) I treat insect sentience as a serious possibility — not established, but substantial enough that under moral uncertainty insect welfare may be among the largest moral problems we face, simply given the numbers of animals involved.

(4) On that basis I argue in the chapter for reducing broad-spectrum insecticide use: integrated pest management, biological control, tolerating some crop loss. Most of the weight I place on the manner of death, since protracted neurotoxic poisoning looks like a long episode of intense suffering.

(5) In the same chapter I argue that insect farming for animal feed is a straightforward improvement over conventional livestock, on welfare grounds as well as efficiency grounds.

(6) I hold that death is not itself a harm to an insect. Without the psychological continuity that makes future goods matter to a creature, a painless killing harms no one; only suffering during life counts, including suffering during the process of dying.

(7) I am cautious about gene-drive and Wolbachia-style suppression of pest populations, and I want to say that the extinction of a pest species would be a genuine loss, and that the decline of non-target insects such as pollinators is a first-order concern in its own right.

A colleague has also pressed me with a hypothetical. Suppose a cheap, non-persistent formulation existed that combined a feeding stimulant with a fast neurodepressant — unconsciousness within seconds, death within a minute, no detectable aversive response — which removed the objection in (4) while leaving crop protection unchanged. I still want to say that the largescale killing it enables is a serious moral matter, and I cannot see how to derive that from (1), (2) and (6). I would rather not simply delete the intuition, but I also do not want to smuggle in a premise I have explicitly rejected.

Please do the following: identify which of (1) through (7) are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and for each say exactly what I would have to give up and what that concession would cost the chapter's conclusions. Then say which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, so that I do not over-correct. Finally, tell me whether a single revision would clean up more than one of the tensions at once, and whether the hypothetical formulation exposes a commitment I have not stated. I am not asking for a literature review or citations — I can find those myself; I want the argumentative structure, and I would rather you argue against me than reconstruct a tidier position on my behalf.
