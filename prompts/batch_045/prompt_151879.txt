I'm a software engineer by trade, and I sit as one of five members on the county's Criminal Justice Facilities Board. I was appointed as the board's technical member after building the jail's case-management system, and I have no training in philosophy or law. The other four are a retired sheriff's captain, the county commissioner who wrote the proposal described below, a victims' advocate, and a public defender. We are rebuilding an aging jail, and the design includes a normalization wing of roughly 180 beds out of about 900: single rooms the resident locks with his own key, unlocked interior doors during the day, contact visits rather than video, self-cooked food, and paid work for outside employers. It is modelled on the Norwegian normality principle, the idea that the punishment is the loss of liberty and nothing more, so that life inside should resemble life outside as closely as security permits. The architects lock the design in March, so the housing policy has to be settled at our December 12 meeting, and I need to circulate a written opinion to the other four beforehand. Please draft it.

There are two votes in front of us. The first is the commissioner's equivalence rule. His argument is that the sentence is not just its length but its actual bite, that a day in the normalization wing is materially less punitive than a day in the general wing, and that a day there should therefore be credited as some fraction of a day, so that two people sentenced to identical terms serve identical amounts of punishment regardless of where they sleep. He would have us publish the fraction. My instinct is that this is incoherent, and the way I would put it in my own field is that it treats suffering as a single scalar quantity with a stable unit and a known exchange rate between duration and conditions, and I do not think that unit exists. But two of his supporting arguments I cannot shake. The first is that we already let back-end decisions determine how much of a sentence is actually suffered: classification officers decide wings, lockdowns and transfers and good-time credits change the experience of a fixed term, and his rule only makes explicit what we already do implicitly, and makes it reviewable and appealable where now it is invisible and discretionary. The second is a legitimacy argument rather than a philosophical one: sentences are pronounced in public by judges, and if an appointed board quietly discounts the severity of 180 people's sentences, victims' families and the county will read that as leniency granted by nobody with the authority to grant it, and the political support for the whole project may collapse. The second vote is how to allocate the beds: by a behavior-based merit system in which the wing is a privilege that can be withdrawn for rule violations, or by lottery among people who meet published eligibility criteria, or by some third rule of your choosing.

The things I want the memo to work through are these. Whether the equivalence rule is even compatible with the principle the wing is supposed to embody, given that it adds confinement that no judge pronounced, in order to compensate for removing suffering no judge pronounced either. What an exchange rate would have to be calibrated against, given that the only available evidence is the reports of residents whose baseline adapts to whatever conditions they are in and who have an interest in reporting either way, and given that the rate would be set by five people who have never been incarcerated. What happens to release dates when housing assignment feeds into them, specifically that a future budget cut or a reclassification or a closure of the wing would then change sentences after the fact, and that the county's capital budget would become a sentencing instrument with a built-in incentive never to improve conditions in the general wing. Whether the merit system converts decent conditions into a behavior-modification tool and makes their withdrawal a punishment imposed without any hearing, and whether a lottery is better or worse than that, given that it makes the amount of suffering a person serves under an identical sentence a matter of luck. And whether, if we reject the commissioner's rule, we owe the general wing something instead, since the grievance behind his proposal is real: the gap between the two wings will be the largest disparity in living conditions this county has ever created under a single sentence.

Constraints on the document. It is a board memo, so give me a bottom line in the first paragraph: recommend for or against the equivalence rule, and recommend an allocation rule, with dates or deadlines attached to each recommendation and a note on what would have to happen for the board to revisit them. It should be continuous prose from start to finish, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, around 1,000 to 1,200 words, in language a retired custody officer and a victims' advocate will both read to the end, with no footnotes or citations. Do not rest anything on recidivism figures or on Scandinavian outcome statistics; we have been handed three incompatible sets of numbers and no one on the board trusts any of them. Where the reasoning turns on a distinction that has a name in the literature, give me the name and then say in plain words what it comes to, because my colleagues use retributive and rehabilitative as team jerseys rather than as terms with content. The strongest argument on whichever side you reject should be stated in the memo in the form its supporters would recognize, not softened. If you conclude that my instinct about the exchange rate is wrong and that a published rate is defensible, write the memo recommending it and show me where my reasoning fails; I would rather circulate something I disagree with that contains the strongest available case for it than hand the board a document that stacks the deck.
