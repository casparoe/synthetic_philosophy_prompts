I've been reading popular books about chaos and determinism on my own — I have no formal background in any of this, I work in freight logistics and read this stuff at night — and I keep hitting the same wall. Every account I find says some version of "a deterministic system can still be unpredictable in practice," and then stops there, as if "in principle" were the end of the conversation. I want to know whether "in principle" survives being made concrete, and I think the only honest way to answer that is with a number and the arithmetic behind it.

So here is the concrete version, which my friend and I have been arguing about for weeks. Fix a location — say the official temperature station at Chicago Midway — and a start date. The target questions are a batch of a hundred yes/no questions of the form "will the daily maximum temperature at that station on day D exceed 25 °C?", for the hundred consecutive days beginning exactly ten years after the start date. The predictor is Laplace's demon with two restrictions that I insist on, because without them the question is child's play: it lives inside the universe rather than outside it, so it may use at most the matter and free energy of the observable universe as its computer and must hand over all hundred answers before the first of those days arrives; and it may not disturb what it is predicting, meaning the rearranging and the waste heat of its computation must leave the temperature at that station within the range it would have had anyway. It does not need to know that its answers are right, only to be right. Give me your credence that such a predictor gets at least ninety of the hundred correct. Mine is about 0.55; my friend's is about 0.15, on the grounds that the whole setup is incoherent and that the "exact state" of a quantum world is not the sort of thing that settles whether it hit 25 °C on a particular afternoon. I should also say that I suspect some of our disagreement is verbal, so if you think my question as posed is ambiguous, restate it in the sharpest form you can and tell me how much of the spread between 0.55 and 0.15 you would attribute to us simply not meaning the same thing. In particular I have started separating three grades — the world's later macroscopic facts being uniquely fixed by its earlier ones; an unconstrained predictor outside the universe being able to derive them; and an in-the-world, resource-limited, self-including predictor being able to deliver them on time — and I would like to know which grade your number is about, and which grade each of your sub-questions attaches to.

What I actually want is the derivation, not the number. Decompose the question into the terms you think carry the weight, give each a rough probability and one line of justification, then combine them and say whether straight multiplication is legitimate here or whether the terms are entangled in a way that would double-count. The terms I have in mind, which you should use, drop, or add to as you see fit: whether the fundamental dynamics are deterministic in the first place, as opposed to irreducibly stochastic or to there being no single true dynamics for us to appeal to; whether, even granting a deterministic microdynamics, a macroscopic predicate like "the daily maximum exceeded 25 °C" has a determinate truth value at all, given the vagueness in things like what counts as the day's maximum and where the station's measurement ends; whether the map from the initial state to the answer is the kind of thing that can be evaluated by any physical process at all, or whether a dynamics can be perfectly well defined and still fail to be computable in the relevant sense; whether the available resources — matter, energy, and above all time before the deadline — suffice for a computation of that size, given that the system being simulated includes the simulator; and a term I never see discussed, which is the demon's data problem: everything it can know at the start is confined to its own past light cone, so predicting Midway's weather in ten years requires reconstructing things it cannot see, like the interior of the Sun, from surface evidence via what looks to me like an ill-posed inverse problem. I would also like a second number for a different question about the same atmosphere — say, the annual count of days above 25 °C at that station in the year beginning on day D, or the regional seasonal mean — because I have a hunch that predictability attaches to the question rather than to the system, so that some observables in a chaotic system stay answerable long after others have gone, and I want to see how much of your 0.55 is really a fact about the dynamics and how much is a fact about the coarseness of what I asked. Finally, tell me which single term your total is most sensitive to — the one that, if it were settled at 0 or settled at 1, would move your number farthest — and what kind of thing would settle it: a theorem, an experiment, a conceptual argument, or nothing we could ever get our hands on.
