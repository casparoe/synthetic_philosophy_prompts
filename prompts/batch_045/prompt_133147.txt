I'm writing a short paper, and putting together a reading-group session to go with it, on whether a system of rules can settle the conditions under which it amends itself — the self-amendment problem from Nomic, and, on the jurisprudence side, the question whether something like Hart's rule of recognition can be amended without leaving an unpaid debt behind. I need a worked example to open with and then pick apart, and I would rather it be set in an institution a reader recognises than on a game board.

So I want you to design one: a short set of bylaws — a housing co-operative, a condo association, a union local, an open-source project's charter, whicheveryou can make work — together with a single proposed amendment, engineered so that all of the following hold at once.

1. The amendment is valid: it meets every requirement the bylaws impose on amendments as those bylaws stood immediately before it was adopted, and the votes are properly counted.
2. The amendment is invalid: it fails a requirement that the bylaws impose immediately after it is adopted — the rules it brings into being are rules it does not itself satisfy.
3. Nothing in the system settles which of 1 and 2 is the correct verdict. In particular, if you apply the post-adoption test to the amendment itself, the amendment falls, and its falling restores the pre-adoption test, which it passes. I want genuine indeterminacy here, not a clash between two live provisions that a judge could resolve by weighing them against one another.
4. The system is otherwise in perfect working order. For every ordinary question a member might ask — who may sublet, what counts as a quorum, how the board is elected — the bylaws return one determinate answer, and the indeterminacy is confined to the standing of this one amendment.

The obvious ways of producing this are cheats, so let me rule them out.

- No self-reference. No provision may name itself, refer to its own validity, or be Liar-shaped in the manner of a bylaw that declares itself void if it turns out to be valid. The trouble has to arise from the interaction of several provisions, each entirely sensible in isolation, plus one ordinary amendment.
- No explicit retroactivity. The amendment may not purport to apply to itself or to amendments already made; the backward pull has to fall out of how the provisions are framed, not be written in.
- No contradiction within a single snapshot of the bylaws. At no point may the document contain two provisions that flatly conflict. What I am after is a sequencing or time-index problem, not a conflict between coexisting norms.
- No vagueness and no misconduct. Everything must turn on clear rule text and correctly counted votes. Nothing may hinge on a contested reading of an ambiguous word, and nobody may act in bad faith.
- Something must be at stake. The amendment has to be one a real faction in this institution would want for its own sake, and its awkward feature has to be the price of getting what that faction wants rather than a gratuitous puzzle. Show why they would still prefer to propose it.
- Short. No more than six provisions in the initial set, and the amendment in a single sentence. It has to be legible on one slide.

What I want back: the initial bylaws in the wording you would actually put on that slide; the amendment; then the walkthrough — check the amendment against each provision as the bylaws stand before adoption, then as they stand after, and lay out the oscillation explicitly rather than gesturing at it. After that, do four things. Identify the single feature of the drafting that is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed or reworded: in particular, show what a drafter's repair — making the new requirement expressly prospective, say — would cost the members who wanted the amendment. Give the strongest reply available to someone who denies there is a paradox at all, for instance someone who holds that what the members or officials in fact treat as the rule of recognition settles the matter as a plain social fact, so that all that remains is a question about what people will accept. And then tell me honestly whether the case smuggles its verdict in anywhere, or whether 1 through 4 really do fall out of the provisions you have written.

My own instinct is that the work will have to be done by a requirement that becomes stricter in the very act that unlocks something the members want, but if that cannot be made to yield genuine indeterminacy without one of the cheats above, say so and use whatever does work.

Format: lead with a single sentence giving your bottom line — whether a case meeting all of these constraints can in fact be built, and, in a phrase, what the case is — then give the bylaws and everything else. No preamble, no restatement of what I have asked for.
