I write novels, and I've drifted into holding something like Schrodinger's Vedanta position: there aren't lots of consciousnesses, there's one, and the plurality is just how it looks from inside a particular body and a particular stream of memory. Two things got me there. One is his: you never observe consciousness as divided, you infer the division from the outside and then forget you inferred it. The other is mine, from the desk. When I give a character a memory I never had -- a childhood kitchen, a particular grief -- it shows up already in the first person, with a felt ownership that isn't mine, and it doesn't arrive as something I'm simulating or borrowing. I've started calling it the borrowed-memory test, and it makes me suspect the "mineness" tag isn't doing the work people think it does in fixing how many consciousnesses there are.

So: what would count against this? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Include the ones a committed defender would actually concede as counting against them, not only the ones a critic would throw, and be clear which ones the view can absorb by explaining away. Tell me what it predicts that its rivals don't. And be honest about whether I've insulated it -- whether saying that the appearance of plurality is itself part of the appearance makes it unfalsifiable, and whether that's a fatal problem for a view of this kind or just what metaphysics at this scale costs. I'd be most moved by something that engages the writing case, since that's where the view actually grips me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning -- no preamble, no restating what I asked.
