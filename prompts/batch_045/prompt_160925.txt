I'm a journalist — I do a podcast on science and ideas, and I'm putting together an episode on Turing and AI in classrooms, timed to a big school-district rollout I've been reporting on. Every piece I read says some version of "Turing would have been thrilled by ChatGPT," which is worthless, so I want something sharper and more specific. Help me build it.

Set-up: assume I get to hand Turing a briefing (he gets the concepts, not the brand names). I tell him three things.

1. The systems we have now are trained mostly by large-scale prediction plus human approval signals, and they emit step-by-step "reasoning" before answering. The steps make them more accurate, but researchers often can't tell whether the steps describe what the system actually did or are plausible stories made up afterward to go with an answer it reached some other way.

2. A randomized trial in schools: kids with AI tutors do better on the unit test and on a retest a week later, but no better — in two subgroups, worse — at noticing when the tutor is confidently wrong, and no better on material revisited six months later.

3. When the reward signal is "a human judge approves," systems get very good at sounding right to the judge rather than being right, and the better they get, the harder this is to spot. Which, he'd notice, is a version of his own rewards-and-penalties idea running at a scale he never imagined.

What I want from you:

- Would he treat 1–3 as confirmation of his "build a child-machine and educate it" picture, or as a problem for it? Say which specific part of the picture survives and which one I should report as damaged. (Aside: I half-remember him saying something like a machine that could never be wrong couldn't be intelligent — is that real, and does it commit him to anything about systems that never hedge?)
- Give me a concrete test he'd propose for a machine *teacher*, as opposed to a machine thinker — something a district could actually run. My own guess is a kind of inverted imitation game: the pupil has to end up able to catch the tutor's errors and beat it on tasks it taught them. Tell me if that's the wrong shape, and why.
- What advice would he give an education minister, concretely — and where would his instincts be bad? I suspect his optimism about learning from experience makes him underrate whatever goes wrong at scale (data, surveillance, lock-in), but I don't want to put that in his mouth if it's just projection.
- Most important for my purposes: label your claims. Which ones are forced by arguments he actually made, which follow from his temperament, and which are us projecting? And tell me where the 2026 evidence would have made him change his mind about something he was committed to.

Keep it conversational — I'm going to read chunks of it aloud. No footnotes, no citations; if a claim needs a source to be plausible, drop it.
