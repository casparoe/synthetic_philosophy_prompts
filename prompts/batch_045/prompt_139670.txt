**Question 3 (of 4).** A conservator's set of 100 colour swatches, numbered 1 to 100, runs in a smooth gradient from unmistakably green (1) to unmistakably grey (100). Consider the predicate 'is green' as applied to these swatches. Assume:

(i) In any admissible precisification of 'green', the swatches that come out green form an initial segment of the series: there is a single cutoff k such that swatches 1 to k are green and k+1 to 100 are not.
(ii) The archive notes establish that the cutoff is at an odd-numbered swatch and lies between swatch 30 and swatch 70 inclusive.

Stick to the two theories from the lectures: the *ignorance theory* (vagueness is ignorance of a sharp boundary; every sentence about a swatch is determinately true or determinately false) and the *precisification theory* (a sentence is true iff true on every admissible precisification, false iff false on every one, and neither true nor false if it varies). No need to discuss degree theories or contextualism.

(a) For each sentence, say what each theory says: determinately true, determinately false, or indeterminate.
 1. Swatch 20 is green.
 2. Swatch 50 is green.
 3. There is a cutoff, and it lies between swatch 30 and swatch 70.
 4. The cutoff is at swatch 50.
 5. The cutoff is at swatch 47.

(b) Consider the tolerance conditional 'if swatch n is green then swatch n+1 is green', read truth-functionally and evaluated inside each precisification in the usual way. Does the answer depend on which n you pick? Say what each theory says for n = 49 and for n = 50, and say how each theory diagnoses the sorites argument that starts from 'swatch 1 is green' and ends with 'swatch 100 is green'.

(c) Is it determinate that swatch 47 is a borderline case? Which theory, if either, has the harder time with that question?

Show enough reasoning that we can see where your verdicts come from.

---

**Student's answer:**

Both theories agree that the swatches at the bottom of the series are determinately green and the ones at the top determinately aren't, and both agree that there is a cutoff. (1) is determinately true on either theory, since every admissible precisification puts the cutoff at swatch 30 or later. (3) is determinately true too, since by stipulation every admissible precisification has a single cutoff in the right range. (2) is where they come apart, in the expected way: the ignorance theory says swatch 50 is either determinately green or determinately not, and our archival evidence doesn't tell us which; the precisification theory says it's indeterminate, because precisiifications with the cutoff at 49 make it not green and ones with the cutoff at 51 make it green. (4) is determinately false on both: 50 is even, so no admissible precisification puts the cutoff there, and the ignorance theory agrees that the real cutoff is some odd number in the range.

On (5) I don't think there's much of a dispute left. Since we know the cutoff is an odd number between 30 and 70, the ignorance theory says 'the cutoff is at 47' has a determinate truth value that we can't discover; and the precisification theory can say the same thing in its own vocabulary — the sentence is true on some admissible precisifications and not on others, and since we can't tell which precisification is the right one, we can't tell whether it's true. So the two views are really the same view about the location of the cutoff, dressed up differently: there is a fact of the matter and we are ignorant of it. The disagreement is only about how we describe that ignorance.

(b) For n = 49 the tolerance conditional is indeterminate on the precisification theory: a precisification that cuts at 49 falsifies it, most of the others verify it. So it isn't true, and the sorites argument therefore has a premise that isn't true and is unsound. The same holds for any n in the middle of the range. On the ignorance theory the conditional is, like every other sentence here, determinately true or determinately false, and the sorites fails because at least one instance of it is in fact false — that's just where the sharp boundary lies.

(c) The precisification theory handles higher-order vagueness better. It can define 'determinately' as truth on every admissible precisification and then iterate: borderline is not determinately green and not determinately not-green, borderline-borderline is defined the same way one level up, and so on. That gives it an unlimited hierarchy of higher-order vagueness for free, which is a decisive advantage over the ignorance theory, which has to model higher-order vagueness as iterated ignorance and gets tangled up trying to say where the boundaries of our own ignorance lie.

One caveat: because the precisification theory abandons bivalence for borderline sentences, it also has to give up the law of excluded middle. 'Either swatch 50 is green or it isn't' is, on that view, neither true nor false, since neither disjunct is. That's the price of the gap, and it's why the view needs a non-classical logic. But it's a price worth paying to avoid saying that there's a sharp cutoff sitting somewhere that we could never in principle find.
