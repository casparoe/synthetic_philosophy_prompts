Novelist here, and I keep getting stuck on a line my friend repeats whenever we argue about this. He's writing a pop-philosophy book about happiness, and his line is that pleasure is the currency of wellbeing: experiences are like different goods, pleasure is what they all convert into, and once converted you can add them up and score an afternoon, or a life, by the total. He leans on it to say something I resist — that the three or four things I'd call the pleasures of writing a novel are really one thing in different denominations, and that a day's work can be ranked by how much of it there was.

They don't feel like one thing to me. There's the click when a plot problem solves itself, which is sudden and slightly electric. There's the hum of a sentence I go back and reread for no reason at all. There's the slump of having got the day's pages done. And then there's the case I can't fit into his picture at all: an afternoon spent reading a brutal editorial letter. Miserable start to finish, not pleasant at any moment, and I still count it among the best afternoons of those two years — and not merely as medicine. I'd want that afternoon again for it's own sake, even though nothing in it felt good.

Can you audit the money analogy? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — what plays the role of goods, of prices, of exchange rates, of a bank, of a currency being real rather than just a way of talking — then where it breaks, and whether the conclusion he wants from it survives once the breaks are on the table. Please give me: Mapping (short), Where it holds (~100 words), Where it breaks (~200 words, and tell me which breaks actually matter for his conclusion and which are just decoration), What the analogy smuggles in (~100 words), A better analogy if there is one (~75 words), Verdict (~100 words).
