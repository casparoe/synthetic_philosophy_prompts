I should be grateful for your help with a problem that has been nagging at me since I took up philosophy four years ago, having retired after forty years as a hydrologist and water-resources engineer. I still write expert reports, for solicitors and occasionally for inquiries, and a good part of my working life has consisted in saying — and being paid to say — that this failure caused that flood. My professional instinct is that causation is a matter of continuous physical process: water, energy, momentum, a chain of events one could in principle instrument. An absence has no energy and no momentum, and on the face of it cannot push anything. Yet my reports are full of absences. I should like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind the verdicts I actually give, or whether I have been equivocating for decades.

Below are the cases with the verdicts I give before I begin theorising. I have invented the details, but they are the shapes of situation I have met repeatedly.

1. I promised to water a colleague's tomato seedlings in her greenhouse during the July heatwave. I forgot. They died of desiccation. Verdict: I caused their deaths. I will not give this up.

2. The same, except that the seedlings were already infected with a wilt that was lethal irrespective of watering. Verdict: my omission caused them to die on the Tuesday rather than the Friday; it did not cause their deaths. Before I knew about the fungus I would have said otherwise, and I am not convinced that this is merely a change of evidence rather than a change of verdict.

3. A night-duty operator did not open a spillway, because a contractor had mis-set the alarm threshold three years earlier and no alarm sounded. The reservoir overtopped and the town downstream was flooded. Verdict: I would write that her omission caused the flood, and that the contractor's omission was a background condition. I cannot say whether "background condition" is a weaker causal claim or not a causal claim at all.

4. A sluice controller's firmware failed to issue the open command because of a coding fault. No human was in the loop. The marsh was not flushed and the reedbeds died. Verdict: unhesitatingly, the fault caused the dieback. I record this because I am entirely comfortable with an absence here — I treat it as a machine having failed — and entirely uncomfortable with the human cases, which troubles me.

5. A pumping station's backup generator had been left unserviceable through a maintenance omission. A fire destroyed the mains transformer and the generator with it. Verdict: the maintenance omission did not cause the pumping failure, even though but for it the generator would have run. Had the fire taken only the mains, I should have said without pausing that the omission was the cause. My verdict thus seems to turn on whether the safeguard would in fact have been called upon, not on whether it was in place.

6. A levee required a hundred sheet-piles. The ganger ordered ninety-nine. The breach began precisely where the hundredth would have stood. Verdict: the omission caused the breach, notwithstanding that a single pile is a negligible fraction of the defence. I suspect I hold this verdict partly for a bad reason: it is where the engineer points.

7. I did not write to my MP about flood-defence maintenance, and neither did ten million others. Verdict: my omission caused nothing. I am tempted to add that the electorate's collective failure did cause something, and I cannot say what that amounts to.

8. The pair I cannot reconcile. (a) In 1962 a catchment board considered planting a shelterbelt on a slope and minuted a decision not to. In 2011 the slope failed and buried three houses; an arboriculturalist colleague assures me the root cohesion would have held it. Verdict: the 1962 decision caused the 2011 failure. (b) In 2013 a storm surge flooded a town for which no sea wall had ever been proposed. Verdict: I refuse to say that the absence of a sea wall caused that flooding. The only difference I can find is that in the first case someone considered the measure and declined it, and in the second nobody did — and I dislike that, because what a board minuted in 1962 cannot, it seems to me, be among the causes of a hillside failing in 2011. Had the town rejected a sea wall on cost grounds in 1970, I should be much more inclined to call its absence a cause, and I cannot see why a fifty-year-old vote should make a difference to what the water did.

9. The case on which I oscillate. The duty officer did not telephone the downstream warden, because the shared dashboard showed a green tick indicating that the automatic alert had gone out. The alert had in fact failed silently and the tick was stale. On Mondays I say her omission was a cause: she was the last safeguard and she did not act. On Thursdays I say it was not: she had excellent reason to think acting unnecessary, and the cause was the telemetry failure. I cannot settle which of these is my considered view.

What I want is the principle, if there is one. Please state it precisely enough to be applied, and say which of my cases motivates each of its clauses. In particular:

- Which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which of them you think I ought to overturn. I would rather give up a verdict than keep a principle that only fits by being baroque, but I should like to be told the price of each.
- Whether the pattern I have described is a pattern of causal judgements at all, or whether I have been tracking blame, responsibility, or explanatory salience and calling it causation. If the latter, tell me what I should put in an expert report instead.
- Whether a principle is available on which what people expected, planned, promised, or were employed to do is irrelevant to whether their omission was a cause. I should prefer such a principle, on the ground that causation should be the sort of thing a hillside does not care about; but if my own verdicts in cases 1, 3, 8 and 9 foreclose it, say so plainly.
- What the principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: first, an omission attributed to a nine-person committee in which no individual member was tasked with the action; and second, an omission that merely permits an already-begun harmful process to continue, as where an operator does not close a valve on a leak that started before his shift. I notice I find "he allowed the tank to empty" much easier to write than "he caused the tank to empty", and I should like to know whether that ease is principled.

I am not asking for a bibliography or for the names of the positions I have stumbled into; I can find those myself. I want the principle, the verdicts it forces, and the verdicts it costs me.
