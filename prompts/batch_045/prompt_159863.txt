I'm reworking the diachronic-rationality section of a paper I've been sitting on forever (I also teach this material to sophmores in the spring), and I keep running into the same wall. The line I want to break is the complacent one: that full sophistication — knowing exactly how your own tastes will move and choosing by backward induction — already immunizes you against money pumps, so that "diachronic rationality" adds nothing to synchronic rationality plus foresight. My own sketch was a conference hotel thing: colleague has a cheap room booked, an app offers her an upgrade Monday, offers to swap back Wednesday, and so on. But my sophisticate just declines the first offer and stops, and nothing bad happens to her, so the case does nothing. I need you to build one where the sophisticate actually loses money.

Specs, and I'm picky about these: (1) no synchronic defect anywhere — at every stage her preferences over final outcomes are complete, transitive, and monotone in money, where money is the one thing she never revalues, so more cash is strictly better at every stage and by the same intensity; (2) the preference change has to be endogenous, produced by her own earlier choice (habituation, contrast, the "I've had enough of it" revaluation), not an exogenous mood swing or a surprise; (3) she is fully sophisticated and never misinformed — she predicts her future preferences and future choices perfectly and maximizes by backward induction, so the usual "she didn't see the reversal coming" pump is off the table; (4) the exploiter is epistemically ordinary: no private information, no coercion, no enforceable contracts, no commitment devices available to either side, and no better read on her tastes than she has herself — the only lever is which trades to offer, when, and in what order, and every trade she accepts has to be one she genuinely prefers at that moment given what she foresees; (5) finite and small — three decisions at most, pump terminates, total loss under two hundred dollars, no leaning on infinite extraction; (6) realistic and classroom-usable, the sort of thing a grad student would actually run into — a room, a registration, a subscription, a resale platform — no demons, no predictors, one paragraph. Then run the three planning dispositions (myopic, sophisticated, and what people call resolute choice) through it with explicit dollar intensities on the non-monetary comparisons so I can check each trade really is an improvement at the time: the sophisticated chooser has to come out strictly worse than if she had never traded at all, and the resolute chooser has to come out strictly better than the sophisticated one without help from any external commitment device — just by executing a plan. If you can't hit both verdicts, say which one fails and why rather than quietly relaxing a spec.

Be committed about it: give me one case, not a menu of options; commit to whether each spec is met; and commit to a verdict on what the case shows, including the strongest objection you can see to your own reading of it. One thing I especially want you to be honest about is standpoint: exploitation has to be worse-than-doing-nothing for somebody, but I suspect it can't be worse for every stage, since whoever makes the last trade thinks that trade was worth it.
