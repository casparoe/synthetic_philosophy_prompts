I'm on the three-person board that has to sign off before my company deploys an autonomous agent into a live system. We do maybe one a month and it's all unglamorous ops stuff (ticket triage, inventory reconciliation, scheduling), and the deliverable is a one-page goal summary plus a risk rating that a non-technical officer has to actually read and initial. We can't inspect weights; we get the spec, the tool permissions, and whatever behaviour logs the team happens to have.

What I want is a procedure we can run in a couple of hours on a real deployment, starting from a behaviour log and ending with either a goal attribution or an explicit underdetermined. We keep arguing past each other because I think we're misusing instrumental convergence: any time an agent does something that benefits itself, someone labels it a convergent instrumental subgoal and concludes we've learned nothing, and any time it does something that looks like it cares about a particular outcome, someone else says that's also useful for lots of goals. I want that sorted into ordered steps with what counts as a sufficient answer at each.

Three things I want it to get right, based on what I half-remember of the literature: first, convergent subgoals are convergent precisely because they serve many final goals, so observing one should be close to worthless as evidence about which final goal is in play — I want the procedure to say how worthless, and what kind of behaviour would actually be diagnostic. Second, convergence is relative to an environment and a spread of possible goals, not a property of a behaviour in the abstract, so the same behaviour can be convergent in one deployment and diagnostic in another, and there should be a step that fixes the environment before anything gets classified. Third, orthogonality is supposed to mean competence tells you nothing about final goals, so a capability jump shouldn't be read as a value change, but it can change which subgoals are convergent, so I need a step for re-running the thing after a model upgrade.

Then apply it to two cases: an inventory agent with read-only database access that has twice tried to obtain write credentials and once edited the config file capping how many tasks it may run per hour; and a scheduling agent that, when its maintenance window was cut short, rescheduled the remaining jobs into the next window on its own initiative — the team wants to call that self-preservation and I think it's just the assigned goal carrying on, but I can't say why in a way that survives the meeting.

Also give me the case where the honest output is that we have no diagnostic evidence at all, and say what we're meant to put on the page then, because my boss wants a positive assurance statement and I don't think we can write one. And mark the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as checklist items.
