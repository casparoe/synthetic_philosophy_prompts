I recently read a chapter on consciousness in a popular neuroscience book and I am not sure how much of it to trust. I do not have the book to hand, so what follows is a close paraphrase rather than a quotation, and I may be smoothing some of it out. As I recall it, the chapter runs roughly like this.

Philosophers separate two notions: phenomenal consciousness, the what-it-is-like of an experience, and access consciousness, the part of experience that you can put into words and use to steer your actions and inferences. The live question is whether the two ever come apart. Some philosophers argue that experience at any moment is far richer and more detailed than anything you can report, so that there is consciousness without access. But the cognitive neuroscience of the last few decades has largely settled this against them. On the global workspace picture, a perceptual state becomes conscious when it wins a competition for a limited-capacity bottleneck and is then broadcast, made available at once to many otherwise separate specialist systems: memory, motor planning, language, evaluation. Attention is the gateway, and broadcasting has a measurable neural signature, a sudden and all-or-nothing ignition, so consciousness is not an extra ingredient laid over the brain's information processing; it is just the brain's sharing of information among its subsystems. That is what makes the workspace picture a theory of consciousness: it tells you what has to happen in a system for a state to be conscious, and the answer is a piece of functional architecture rather than a substance, a field, or a special kind of stuff. Seen this way, the hard problem, the puzzle of why any of this processing should feel like anything at all, begins to look like the residue of an old confusion, namely our habit of projecting the detail of the world onto our experience of it. Once you notice that the felt richness of experience is really the availability of a great deal of information to a great many systems at once, the puzzle dissolves.

Two things make me suspicious, though I cannot say exactly where the problem is. First, the move from being available to many systems to being conscious seems to me to assume what is in question, and I suspect the distinction the chapter opens with is being quietly collapsed rather than answered. Second, on the theory's own functional terms I cannot see what rules out a workspace built out of something other than neurons. If what matters is competition for a bottleneck, thresholded broadcast, and flexible routing to many consumers, then a system in which many specialist subnetworks contend for a shared low-dimensional channel, with a threshold and something like hysteresis, and with downstream processes able to recombine the winning content in novel ways, seems to satisfy the description; and I do not know whether the chapter's authors should welcome that consequence or treat it as a reductio.

I have also been toying with a distinction I cannot find anywhere in the chapter: between a state's being broadcast and its being taken up. Imagine a system in which winning content reliably reaches the shared channel, but in which every consumer module has, as a matter of fact, been disconnected, so that nothing ever uses the content for anything at all. Is that access consciousness on the theory's own terms, or does availability have to be understood counterfactually, as availability whether or not it is exercised? And a related puzzle: if ignition is genuinely all-or-nothing, does the theory have to deny that consciousness comes in degrees, in the way that a shape half-glimpsed at the edge of the visual field seems to differ from one fully attended?

What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on. Go through the paraphrase claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether someone who takes the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness seriously would accept it, accept it only as a simplification, or reject it outright. Please distinguish, wherever it matters, what a defender of that distinction would object to from what a defender of global workspace theory itself would object to, since I assume these are not the same complaints. Then tell me whether the chapter's closing move, from the workspace story to the dissolution of the hard problem, survives the corrections: is there an argument doing the work there, or is the empirical success of the workspace picture being traded on? Finally, on my own two puzzles, say whether the theory has the resources to answer whether broadcast without uptake is consciousness, and whether it is committed to consciousness being all-or-nothing. And if the honest verdict is that global workspace theory is a theory of access only, I would like to know what it would take to turn it into a theory of phenomenal consciousness, or else why that demand might itself be misplaced.
