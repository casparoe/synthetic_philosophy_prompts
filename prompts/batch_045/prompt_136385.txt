I'm organizing the closing reading for a one-day workshop on searches for extraterrestrial technosignatures, and I'd like to commission one poem on the Fermi paradox: that we look out at a galaxy old enough and large enough to have been crossed many times over, and find nothing we recognize as another technology.

What I don't want is a tour of the catalogue of solutions. I've heard the jokes about zookeepers and lighthouses, and a poem that winks its way through a list of hypotheses will die in the room. I'd rather the poem be built around a single speculative thought I've been turning over: that the silence may not be measuring how many technological civilizations exist, but how the ones that do exist spend a bounded budget of noticeability.

The idea, put plainly: there is presumably some ceiling on what a civilization can put into the sky — radiated power, volume filled, centuries of a beacon kept lit — and it can spend that ceiling either as one intense flare, visible across the galaxy for a short while, or as a long faint emission that never carries very far. On that picture the two properties a survey needs — that a civilization be loud, and that it still be loud at the moment we happen to look — pull against each other, and any snapshot is biased toward exactly the strategies that don't last. The silence then stops being evidence about the number of civilizations and becomes evidence about how the ones there are allocated themselves. I know the reply I'd get from the workshop: nothing forces loudness and duration to trade off; a civilization might expand and simply keep going, loud and durable at once. Please let that objection stand somewhere near the end. I want the poem to close in the uncertainty rather than settle it, and honestly that is where the field is.

I'd also like the trade-off to be structural rather than stated. My suggestion: five stanzas, each carrying the same total number of syllables, or near enough that the equality is felt rather than counted, with the budget spent in fewer and longer lines at the start and in more and shorter lines as the poem goes on — the same quantity of light spread thinner and longer, so that a reader feels the dimming and the lengthening at once. If you can think of a better way to make the conservation do formal work — a refrain that keeps its length while its vocabulary thins, say — please use it; the structural constraint matters more to me than the scheme I've proposed.

Some constraints. Free verse, no regular rhyme scheme. No ventriloquized aliens: the poem should not pretend to know what another civilization would say. Keep the astronomy clean — no invented numbers, no confident colonization timescales, no named researchers or papers; convey scale comparatively if you need it at all. Around 30 to 45 lines. It has to survive being read aloud to a room of people who work on this, and then being reread on the page by someone who has never heard the word "technosignature."
