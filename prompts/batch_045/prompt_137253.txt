Right, so I run this little fortnightly reading group with three mates from my old philosophy department — we're all a bit rusty, me especially — and last week we completely failed to get anywhere on the question of whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg. We know the classic Kripkean line, where you imagine the very same parents producing a child from different gametes and feel pulled both ways at once, and then someone says "well, isn't this just verbal?" and the whole thing fizzles out. So I want to try a different tack. Could you act as a professional metaphysician who specialises in modality and personal identity, brainstorm for a moment about what the strongest version of each side actually looks like before you start writing, and then produce a dialogue of roughly 1,000–1,400 words between two philosophers — call them Bea and Tariq, or whoever you like — one defending the view that a human being's origin in a particular pair of gametes is essential to it, the other defending the view that the same person could have originated from a numerically different sperm and egg. Both of them should be genuinely good at this: no strawmen, no one saying "surely it's obvious that", and if at the end a reader can tell who's meant to have won, you've done it wrong.

The thing I want them to be arguing about is a specific constructed case rather than the bare intuition, and here's the case I've been doodling: suppose a clinic can do what I'll call germline reconstitution. In the first variant, a couple's later conception — a different sperm, a different egg, from a later cycle — has its nuclear genome edited at the zygote stage so that it is a base-pair-for-base-pair match with the genome that their existing daughter Alice actually developed from; the mitochondria and the cytoplasm are of course from the new egg, so the cell is a different physical object with a different maternal lineage, and yet the developmental programme it runs is the one Alice ran. In the second variant — the mirror image, and this is the bit I want them to really chew on — Alice's own actual sperm and egg are used, but the zygote's genome is edited so that it differs from Alice's in a handful of places, enough to change her eye colour and to give her a late-onset cardiac condition she doesn't in fact have. Both children grow up; both are, in your story, adults. Bea has to say which of these two, if either, is Alice, and — crucially — she has to locate the essence somewhere specific: in the gametes themselves, in the nuclear genome, in the mitochondrial or cytoplasmic contribution, or in the zygote's total molecular state at syngamy. She's not allowed to stop at "the origin is essential, full stop", and she has to show that wherever she locates it, her view doesn't quietly turn into Tariq's. Tariq, meanwhile, owes a positive account of what makes it true or false that Alice could have had different gametes — counterpart theory, closest-continuer stuff, a developmental-programme account, whatever he likes — and he has to apply that account to the fact that the genome-matched child is psychologically and bodily just like Alice, not just assert that "could" is a slippery word.

There are a few things I'd like them to get through if you can fit them in naturally: how the answer bears on monozygotic twinning, where one zygote's total state becomes two people and the essence, if it was in that state, seems to have nowhere to live; the awkward fact that gametes are themselves the output of a recombination lottery, so "a different sperm" is already a modal claim about a chancy mechanism that produces genetically unique cells; and whether any of this settles anything about prudential concern, i.e. whether Alice should care about the genome-matched child's future in the way she cares about her own. Neither character may say the dispute is merely verbal, or that identity is up to us, or that the answer is "just intuitive", and neither may use unanalysed talk of the closest possible world as a way of changing the subject.

Format-wise: prose dialogue with the odd bit of stage direction, no bullet points inside the conversation, and please don't have anyone quote or cite anything real — a couple of my group have no library access and it would just be showing off. Write it in British English, keep the tone of two specialists who like each other and have been round this loop before, and let them actually respond to each other's moves rather than delivering alternating monologues. They should converge on at least one substantive claim about the case — something they'd both be willing to write down — and then I'd like it to end with each of them identifying the exact sentence where they diverge, plus one further variant of the case, not yet discussed, that would genuinely move them to change sides.
