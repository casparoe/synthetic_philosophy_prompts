So I've been going back and forth on this for ages and I want to know whether my gut on a bunch of cases actually adds up to anything.

The setup: a community remembers something about its own past, and the archival record says something different. Sometimes I think the memory should give way, sometimes I think it should be left the hell alone, and I can't work out what I'm tracking.

1. Alpine village with an annual festival and a plaque commemorating how its militia held the bridge against an invading army in 1478. An archivist turns up pay records showing the bridge was actually held by mercenaries hired by a neighbouring city, and that the village militia arrived late and then mostly ran. My verdict: keep the festival, keep the reenactment, change the plaque. Which is odd, because the reenactment is the most literally false part of the whole thing and I'm totally unbothered by it.

2. My family always said my grandfather was in the resistance. The paperwork says he was a railway clerk who signed a loyalty oath in 1943, and who on one occasion used his staff pass to move two families across the border. Verdict: we should stop saying he was in the resistance. But we should go on telling the story about the two families, and I actually think telling that one matters more than getting the label right.

3. A country whose textbooks describe the independence movement as unified and nonviolent. The record: two factions, one of which took money from a neighbouring empire and had some of the other faction's leaders killed. Verdict: the textbooks have to change, the national holiday can stay, and the statues of the guy who took the money — that's the one I keep flip-flopping on.

4. A town remembers being abandoned by the central government during a catastrophic flood: the relief barges never came. The record: the barges were sent, and the town's own council turned them away because they didn't want the price controls that came attached to the relief. Verdict: this memory should give way, loudly and publicly. It feels completely different from 1 and 3 and I want to know why.

5. A religious community's oral tradition says its shrine has stood there since the beginning of things. Archaeology dates the current structure to around 1400. Verdict: nothing to correct here. It feels like the archaeology isn't even answering the same question the memory is answering.

6. Colonial police archive describes a 1921 gathering in the district as a riot instigated by three named men. The community's oral history says it was a peaceful tax protest and the police fired first. Verdict: trust the memory over the record — and not merely because the record is self-serving. I'd still trust it if the archivist were scrupulous and the paperwork immaculate.

7. A massacre survivor, ninety now, names the unit she says burned her village. The unit's muster rolls put it two hundred kilometres away that week. Verdict: her testimony stays standing as testimony, but the memorial shouldn't name that unit. And I don't want to say she's wrong about her own experience — I want something more specific than that.

8. The pair I suspect breaks whatever it is I believe. A town that invented its founding legend wholesale in 1890 as a tourism stunt, where everybody now knows it's invented — I'm fine with that, it's basically civic cosplay. Versus a four-hundred-year-old memory that is almost certainly a garbled version of a real event — I'm weirdly protective of that one. Judged by reliability alone the ranking should be the other way round.

9. And the version of this that I think might be the real crux: the same community's memory, except now it's the only evidence that a crime happened at all, and it's the descendants of the people who did it who are demanding that the record get to decide.

Some other things that seem like they should matter but I can't place: whether the community was ever allowed near the archive (records destroyed, or kept in a language the community was forbidden to write in); how old the memory is; who exactly is asking for the correction; whether the community has much else going for it.

My tentative principle, which I'm shure is wrong somewhere: the record wins on who did what to whom, memory wins on what it was like and what it means, and memory loses its protection the moment it starts being used as a reciept — when it's doing the work of "you did this to us" or "you owe us." That's meant to explain why 4 collapses and 1 doesn't. Commemoration should then be judged as a practice rather than as a bundle of claims, which I thought explained the reenactment-vs-plaque thing until I noticed that a plaque is a practice too.

So: is there a principle that gets these, or gets close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? What does it say about the statues in 3 and about case 9? And does it have anything to say about the situation where the record and the memory disagree but nobody alive has any stake in either — the purely academic case? (I got into this because I'm on a small heritage committee and we are literally arguing about a plaque.)
