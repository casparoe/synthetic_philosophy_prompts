ive agreed to give a short talk to my forecasting meetup in a few weeks — about twenty people, mostly hobbyist geopolitics forecasters plus two historians who I already know will hate it — and before I write the thing I want to know whether the position ive ended up in actually hangs together. I have a feeling it doesn't but I cant locate the crack. Here's roughly where ive landed:

1. The outside view should dominate. Set up your reference class, take its base rate, and let the specifics of the case move you only a little. The inside view is where overconfidence lives.

2. But the reference class isn't handed to you by history. Picking one is a judgment about which causal features matter — wether you file the case under "empires" or "multinational states with a contested frontier" or "polities with one overstretched hegemon" is a theoretical decision, and two honest people can pick different classes without either of them misapplying the method. So the "data" part of the outside view is thinner than its reputation. I've started calling the bad version of this "reference-class laundering": you pick the class whose base rate happens to match what your inside-view model already said, and the outside view adds nothing while providing rhetorical cover.

3. For empire duration specifically I basically dont trust the base rate at all, because the number of cases is an artifact of how we carve up the past — is Rome one empire or four, do the Achaemenids and the Ottomans belong in the same bin, does the count move when you switch from reigns to dynasties to states? The denominator is a convention. Call it the individuation problem. But this seems to mean the outside view doesn't even apply to the one question I actually care about.

4. At the same time I want to say a good forecaster in, say, 1988 should have had something like 5–10% on the Soviet Union simply not existing five years later, even though no great-power reference class supports a number anywhere near that. Unprecedented things happen and I dont want a method that structurally cant see them coming.

5. I also think "I dont know, no forecast" is a legitimate and badly underused output — if you have neither a reference class nor a story about the mechanism, you should refuse to give numbers even when people push you.

6. When a case is driven by a technology thats changing fast, the historical record is obsolete and the inside view should win instead.

7. And I use historical analogies constantly — most of my reading is narrative history and my best calls have come from "this is 1914 in slow motion" style thinking — even though I also think you should never forecast off a single analogy.

8. I think probabilities about one-off events are perfectly meaningful, even though my whole method is built on counting how often things happened in the past.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and whats the cheapest fix? Rank the tensions by severity instead of flagging all of them as equally bad, and for each real one tell me which commitment has to go and what giving it up costs me. For the ones that only look inconsistent, tell me why the appearance arises and what distinction dissolves it. I dont want reading recommendations or citations — I want to know if the package is stable enough to say out loud.
