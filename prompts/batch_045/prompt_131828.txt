I'm a software engineer by training with no formal philosophy background, so I'd be grateful for a careful answer rather than a sophisticated one.

Last month an engineer on my team built an internal deployment tool on her own initiative, mostly over about five weekends. It now saves the company a considerable amount of money each quarter, and management announced a bonus for her and put her name alone on the announcement. One colleague, who grew up in Norway and invokes Janteloven fairly often, objected to both halves of that. Her objection was a single sentence: nobody is self-made. She took it to imply that the bonus should be divided evenly across the team and that the announcement should name the group rather than the person. Two features of the case strike me as relevant. First, the win was not hers alone in any ordinary causal sense: our infrastructure group had already built the systems the tool plugs into, and the tool only became urgent because a competitor shipped a serious bug three months earlier. Second, she would probably not have produced it at most other companies, and she says so herself.

I don't think the sentence can be doing just one job as she used it, and I'd like it taken apart. In engineering we routinely separate three things that I suspect are being run together here: credit, meaning the bookkeeping of who gets named and rewarded, which looks like a matter of incentives and signalling; desert, meaning what a person has actually earned through effort and choice; and status, meaning how much attention or deference the rest of us owe them. So far as I can tell, nobody is self-made might be a claim about causes, about luck, about who the created value belongs to, or about what attitude we should take toward the successful, and those would support very different conclusions about the bonus, the announcement, and the general habit of singling people out.

Please set out exactly five distinct things the sentence could be claiming, ordered from the weakest and most trivially true to the strongest and most contentious. For each, say what it commits someone to, whether you think it is true, which of credit, desert and status it actually bears on, and therefore whether it supports splitting the bonus, anonymising the credit, both, or neither. If one of the five is what someone in her position is usually reaching for, say so in that item.

Please respond as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction, no conclusion and no headings.
