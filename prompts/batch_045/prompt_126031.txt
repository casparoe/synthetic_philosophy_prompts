I'm running a philosophy-in-film night for the undergrad ethics society I help out with, and the next one is Spider-Man 2 (the 2004 Raimi one with Doc Ock). I picked it partly bc it's fun, and partly bc I can't stop arguing with a friend who has gone fairly hardcore effective-altruist and keeps needling me about how little I give — I give some, but I also just bought a bike, and I'd like a principled answer about whether I'm a bad person or merely a normal one before we do the film. So I want to read it through the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and I want the reading to be doing real work rather than being an excuse to watch a superhero movie.

The bit of the debate I find hardest, and which the movie seems almost built for, is something I've started calling elastic demandingness: consequentialism's demands aren't flat, they scale with your marginal cost of complying, and Peter's marginal cost of saving people is about as close to zero as a human being's can get — he's strong, he heals, he can cross the city in minutes, and the worst risks are ones he's already opted into. So if the objection is really about how much is being asked, it should be at its loudest for him, and yet basically nobody watching thinks he should hang the suit up for good. That makes me suspect the "it asks too much" version is the least interesting form of the complaint, and that what's actually load-bearing is either (a) that the demand has no ceiling — there's no point at which Peter has done enough, no moment at which the rest of his life is simply his own — or (b) that the demand is impersonally additive, so his own projects (the physics degree, MJ, the job, just having a Tuesday) never count for anything in their own right, which is the integrity / moral-saints side of it. I'd like you to work out which of those the film is actually dramatising, and whether it's consistent about it. I'm also oddly stuck on whether the power-and-responsibility slogan is even a consequentialist principle at all: it could be a claim about role duties or about virtue or about what a decent person does with gifts, and if it isn't consequentialist then the film isn't straightforwardly evidence for or against the objection.

Concretely, here's what I want from the reading. Does the film frame Peter quitting as a moral failure or as a legitimate limit, given that his burnout is staged as much by his own evasiveness — lying to MJ, dodging Aunt May — as by exhaustion? Is his return a vindication of the open-ended demand, or does the film quietly change the subject: the train sequence where the passengers take hold of the ropes and share the load, Aunt May's speech about there being a hero in everyone — that all looks closer to a fair-share or distributed-duty picture, where the demand is real but isn't meant to land entirely on one guy. And please be honest about where the reading breaks down, because I don't want to oversell it: the genre can't actually let him quit, super-villains aren't a standing feature of our world so the stakes are rigged, and Doc Ock is a warning about obsession with a project rather than about moral demandingness. One more thing I'd like you to settle: does the film preserve anything supererogatory whatsoever, or does having the powers convert every act of heroism into duty? Give me three or four scenes to build the discussion around, say where a card-carrying consequentialist would push back on your reading, and end with whether the film contributes anything to the argument that the papers don't. No need for citations or a reading list — I'll be talking to 19-year-olds who have read exactly one essay on the drowning child.
