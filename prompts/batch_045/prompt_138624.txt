I'm a high school student working through the absent-minded driver problem on my own, and one step keeps going in circles for me. The setup I'm using: a driver will pass two indistinguishable intersections and cannot recall whether he has already passed the first. Exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 1. Before setting out, committing to "continue" with probability 2/3 maximizes expected payoff (4/3); but once he is at an intersection, conditioning on being at an intersection seems to give him a 3/5 chance of being at the first one and a 2/5 chance of being at the second, which makes continuing look strictly better than exiting.

My confusion is that "what should I do right now?" appears to depend entirely on an assumption about my other self. If I suppose the copy of me at the other intersection still follows the original plan (exits with probability 1/3), then continuing and exiting both work out to exactly 1.6 — precise indifference. If instead I suppose the later copy will do the sensible thing and exit at the second intersection for sure, then continuing is worth 3/5·4 + 2/5·1 = 2.8 against 2/5·4 = 1.6 for exiting, so I strictly prefer to continue — and then never exit at all, which is worse than the plan I started with.

Two questions. First: which of these ways of evaluating a deviation is the correct one, what exactly goes wrong in the other, and is the 3/5 itself even the right probability to be using when the driver cannot tell the two situations apart? Second: the exact indifference in the first case surprised me. Is it an artifact of the particular payoffs 0, 4, and 1, or is something about the structure forcing it (I redid the calculation with 1, 5, and 2 and got indifference again, but I can't tell whether that means anything)? Please keep the answer conceptual — I don't need the history or the literature on the puzzle, just the reasoning — and please write it in American English.
