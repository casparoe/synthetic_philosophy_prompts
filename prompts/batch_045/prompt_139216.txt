Hi — my flatmate sent me the transcript of a podcast episode about online polarisation and I can't work out how much of it I should actually believe. I'd always used "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" as two names for the same thing, and the host says that's precisely the mistake. He says the distinction comes from a philosophy paper by some American academic whose name I've forgotten. Here's roughly what he says (paraphrased from memory, so some of the mess is probably mine):

An epistemic bubble is what happens when your information diet gets filtered — by your mates, by an algorithm, by whichever paper you happen to buy — so you only ever encounter one side of things. The filtering is invisible to you by design, which is the worrying part. But bubbles are structurally fragile: nobody has touched your general trust in outsiders, so all it takes is someone shoving the missing evidence in front of you and the thing bursts. That's why "just show them the facts" sometimes works.

An echo chamber is a different and much nastier beast. It's a social structure that actively discredits everyone outside it — members are taught in advance that any outside source is corrupt, biased or malicious. Since that distrust is now built into their belief system, giving them counter-evidence makes things worse: they file it as further proof that the outside is lying. So echo chambers are sticky where bubbles are brittle. And there's no cheap fix: getting out means cutting ties with the community and rebuilding your trust from zero, which is why deprogramming people can take years.

He also says a couple of other things I'm less sure about: that the mechanism is basically confirmation bias plus homophily, just scaled up by social media; that you can tell which one you're in by asking whether the group keeps repeating the same claims back at itself, which is where the "echo" bit comes from; and that there's a third structure he calls a "jurisdiction chamber" — a group that never calls outsiders liars, it just holds that outsiders lack standing to speak on this topic at all. Because the disagreement gets read as overstepping rather than as evidence, exposure to it still reinforces the group, but nothing as crude as global distrust is involved.

I'm suspicious of the "just show them the facts" line, because I've tried that on my uncle and it did precisely nothing — though the host would presumably say that just means my uncle is in an echo chamber rather than a bubble, so I don't know if it's evidence against anything.
