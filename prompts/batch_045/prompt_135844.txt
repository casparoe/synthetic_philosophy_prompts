I am a professor of philosophy and, for the last two years, the chair of this university's Committee on Commemoration and Historical Context. The committee hears petitions about what the campus publicly honors: named buildings, named professorships and lecture series, portraits, and passages in the common curriculum that petitioners want removed, recontextualized, or annotated with an editorial note. We meet monthly and dispose of twenty to thirty items a year. Our recommendations are public, they go to the president, they are read both by the students who filed the petition and by the alumni trustees who fund the things being renamed, and they are leaked to the student paper with some regularity. We decide from a dossier: the petition, the response from the unit that owns the item, and whatever a general reference work tells us. We have no research budget, no outside consultants, and no time for archival work. Every item has to be settled in one meeting and defended in two paragraphs of plain prose, by a committee that includes a student, a librarian, and a trustee who have never had any of this machinery explained to them.

What we lack is a procedure. Right now the word presentism functions in our meetings as a conversation-stopper in both directions: it is used to defeat petitions (that is merely presentism) and to defeat defenses (that is merely the standards of his time). I want something better than that, and I want it as a procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a yes or a no, and where it stops because the remaining question is one of values and must be handed to the committee with a clear flag rather than disguised as a finding.

Four things the procedure has to get right, because they are where our arguments actually stall.

First, triage by what the utterance is doing. An explanation, a verdict on an agent, a decision about what to teach, and an act of honoring are not, I take it, governed by the same norm; honoring seems to me constitutively a present practice done for present reasons, so that calling a commemoration presentist may be a category mistake rather than an objection. Relatedly, the procedure must distinguish a claim that is presentist but harmless from one that is false but not presentist, and say what we do with each.

Second, the availability of a standard. When we say an agent could not have known better, we owe an account of could not: expressible in the vocabulary available to them; actually articulated by someone within their reach; feasible for someone in their position; or survivable by someone in their position. I want the rungs distinguished, and I want a rule for which rung is demanded by which kind of question.

Third, the choice of comparison class and time window. Whether an agent was typical depends on whom you compare them to and over what interval, and both are trivially gerrymandered in either direction. I want a window rule and an account of how it gets gamed, plus something I have come to rely on informally: a dissent test, on which no one could have known better fails as soon as the dossier shows contemporaries who said the thing.

Fourth, the fork between using what only we know in order to explain and using it in order to condemn, together with two failure modes I want named and policed: the slide on which an accurate frequency claim (his views were common for his class and decade) does the work of an excuse whether or not it was offered as one, and the asymmetry whereby we notice anachronism in condemnation but not in praise, so that calling a thinker a proto-feminist passes unremarked while calling her a misogynist does not. I also want the procedure to face the awkward fact that our standpoint is itself downstream of the history we are judging, and that the anti-presentist norm we would be applying is one of ours. Where the procedure bottoms out in that regress, say so and stop.

Run it on three cases with the facts as stipulated here.

One: Hartwell Hall, a dormitory named for Elias Hartwell (1811–1879), a shipping and insurance magnate who in 1858 published a pamphlet arguing that emancipation would ruin the sugar islands and that people of African descent were unfit for self-government. The dossier establishes that abolitionist pamphlets were on sale in his city that year and that two of his business partners subscribed to an abolition society. The petition asks that the name come off; the committee's working draft is a plaque reading that Hartwell's views, indefensible to us, were those of most merchants of his class and time. Is the plaque, the petition, or neither the presentist object?

Two: Aletheia Vance's On the Education of Citizens (1792), required in the sophomore sequence, which argues that a woman's education should be confined to household management. The dossier establishes that Vance ran a school in which girls were taught Latin and double-entry bookkeeping, and that she corresponded with a contemporary who published an argument for equal education. The petition asks that the text be dropped. I want to keep it, and my draft note is that Vance was a pioneer of women's education whose theory lagged behind her practice. I have a stake in this one, and I suspect my note may be the presentist object rather than the petition; if that is where the procedure lands, say so plainly.

Three: the Crane Lectures, endowed in 1919 by Josiah Crane (1845–1921), a manufacturer. The dossier establishes that everything he did was lawful and locally praised — libraries, a hospital wing — and that reports published by his foundation were quoted approvingly by eugenicist campaigners in the decade after his death, with no connection to him and without his having sought or foreseen it. The petition argues that the lectures now function as an honor to a man whose work fed a later harm. I want to know whether the procedure recognizes retrospective injury of this shape at all and, if it does, what would have to be true of the causal chain for it to count.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire on the cases we actually get, which of our questions it is simply the wrong instrument for, and which of its steps are judgment calls wearing procedural costume. Please do not give me further reading or a bibliography; whatever we decide, we have to decide in the room, from the dossier and from reasoning.
