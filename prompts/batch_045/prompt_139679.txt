I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the days when the sequences were still being posted, and the habit I picked up there — before asking which answer is right, ask what work the answer does — has soured me a little on the statue and the clay. The standard moves are familiar enough: the statue and the portion of clay that currently constitutes it occupy exactly the same region at exactly the same time, yet seem to differ, because the statue cannot survive being squashed into a ball while the clay can, so they have different persistence conditions and so, by Leibniz's law, are not identical; constitution is then offered as a relation that is not identity. Then come the familiar satellites — coincident objects, temporal parts and the view that persisting things are four-dimensional sums of instantaneous stages, the view that there are really no statues at all but only matter arranged statue-wise, the problem of the many and other vagueness-driven embarrassments, and the thought that gradual piece-by-piece replacement raises the same question in slow motion.

What I want is a piece of sustained parody that puts this bookkeeping to work in a setting where the distinctions would have to be cashed out. Please write the terms and conditions of a household contents insurance policy offered by a fictional insurer to owners of a consumer product I'll call the Forma: a block of programmable matter, about the size of a carry-on bag, that can be instructed to rearrange itself into a statue, a chair, a lamp, and back again, and which its owners routinely reshape several times a week. Because payouts depend on what got destroyed and what merely got reconfigured, the underwriters cannot avoid taking positions on material constitution, and the fine print should quietly take them.

The humour has to come from the metaphysics rather than from wordplay or from anyone saying 'it's all just semantics' or insisting that we taboo 'exists' — that joke has been made to death and I don't want it. The laughs should come from the structure of the positions: from a premium that scales with how many coincident candidate insured things the policyholder elects to schedule, from exclusions that have to anticipate further candidates nobody has proposed yet, from coverage computed over temporal stages so that a claim must specify which slice of the thing was lost, from an optional rider for the customer who maintains that there are no statues, from a rule about what happens if the portion of matter becomes a living organism, from a threshold on how much gradual replacement per day counts as continuity, and from a schedule for prorating losses across borderline cases. The reductio I'd like to arrive at is the actuarial one: an industry that has built an entire pricing structure on distinctions that make no difference to any observable outcome, and which eventually discovers a way to charge for them anyway.

Please target the positions and the style of reasoning, not any person; the insurer and its staff are invented, and I'd rather you named the moves than the authors, since I want this to land for readers who have never opened a metaphysics paper. My housemate, who writes compilers and has no philosophy, should be able to follow the whole thing, while someone who actually works on material constitution should find the satire fair rather than a strawman — exaggerate what the views really say, don't invent commitments for them. Keep it deadpan throughout, and let it stay legible as irony without anyone winking at the reader.

Sections, in this order:

1. The Policy — the numbered clauses of the terms and conditions, prefixed with a short header giving the insurer's name and the product, roughly 700–900 words.
2. Commentary — 250–350 words on the choices you made: which clause is pinning which real doctrine, where you think the exaggeration is fair, and where you think a defender of the constitution view could reasonably object that you've misrepresented them.
