You are a professional philosopher of mathematics who also works on the philosophy of physics. Please think carefully before answering: first sketch, privately, two or three candidate diagnoses of the argument below, price what each one costs, then commit to the one you think is best and say why the others lose. Answer in American English, in continuous prose, aiming for about 500 words.

I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately been going to a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and one of the other members, a set-theoretic realist, has backed me into a corner with the following argument. Every step looks acceptable to me on its own, and yet I cannot accept where it ends up.

(1) In Hilbert's Hotel every room is occupied, and a new guest is nevertheless accommodated by moving the occupant of room n to room n+1 for every n. If this is a genuine reductio of an actually infinite hotel, then nothing in the story depends on the rooms being made of brick: replace the rooms and guests with any countably infinite collection of concrete objects arranged in an omega-sequence, and the same manoeuvre works. So the absurdity, if there is one, attaches to the structure, not to the matter.

(2) The structure involved is exhausted by the successor map. Its only non-trivial ingredient is the arithmetical fact that n ↦ n+1 takes natural numbers to natural numbers, is injective, and never takes the value 1. That is a fact of pure arithmetic; no physical, causal or temporal feature of hotels is doing any work in it.

(3) So the finitist's complaint is not really about hotels, space or matter at all. It is a complaint about treating the natural numbers as a single completed totality over which such a map is defined. Finitism about the physical world turns out to require finitism about arithmetic, and conversely, a completed number sequence already contains the hotel.

(4) But if one holds the classical Aristotelian line, on which the numbers are only potentially infinite, always extendable and never completed, then there is no such thing as all the natural numbers, and hence no determinate fact of the matter about which maps on the number sequence exist. In particular, that the successor map pairs off all the rooms with all but one would be neither true nor false.

(5) It follows that the modest finitist cannot so much as formulate the Hilbert's Hotel objection: on her own view, the premise it needs has no truth value. And if she helps herself to a completed sequence in order to state the objection, she has conceded the actual infinite, at which point the hotel is merely surprising rather than absurd, since a set's being equinumerous with a proper subset is just what it is for it to be infinite.

(6) Therefore the moderate position is unstable: no actual infinite, but the potential infinite is respectable. It collapses either into full acceptance of completed infinite totalities or into an inability to say anything at all about the infinite.

(7) And the argument generalizes beyond arithmetic. Anyone who states classical electrodynamics or general relativity is already quantifying over an infinity of space-time points, so unless she accepts actual infinities she cannot state her own theory, let alone object to anyone else's.

I would most like to keep two things: the intuition that the hotel story describes something that could not happen to a real hotel, and the claim that the number sequence is only potentially infinite. I would also rather not surrender the right to write down field equations over a continuum. Which step do you reject? Explain why that step looked sound, what exactly rejecting it costs me, and which of the claims I want to keep it forces me to weaken. If instead the argument is sound and something has to go, tell me which of my commitments is cheapest to surrender.
