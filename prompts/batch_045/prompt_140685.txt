I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and next week I'm leading a seminar session on the units of selection. I'd rather anchor the session in a real episode than in the usual stylized examples, so I want to use the 2010 exchange in Nature over the evolution of eusociality: the paper by Nowak, Tarnita and E. O. Wilson (Nature 466, 1057–1062), which argued that kin selection theory built on inclusive fitness has serious limitations in explaining eusociality and that "standard natural selection theory in the context of precise models of population structure" is the simpler and superior approach, together with the published reply in which a large group of evolutionary biologists defended inclusive fitness as a fully general partitioning of natural selection into its direct and indirect components. I already know the surrounding background — the haplodiploidy hypothesis and the 3/4 relatedness of sisters, its decline once diploid termites, ambrosia beetles, sponge-dwelling shrimp and mole-rats were taken into account, Hamilton's rule, the equivalence of inclusive-fitness and neighbour-modulated accounting, and Darwin's own move of treating the whole colony as the unit — so please spend as little time as possible recapping it and as much as possible on the analysis. (A lab mate of mine insists the entire episode is purely notational and that nothing substantive was ever at stake; I'm not convinced, and part of what I want is material to settle that.)

What I'd like is a mapping that runs in both directions. In one direction: lay out what the candidate units actually are in this case — the allele, the individual insect, the family, the colony, the local population — and for each say what the units-of-selection framework takes the relevant players, options and payoffs to be, and what would have counted as evidence for selection acting at that level. Which features of the episode does that apparatus genuinely illuminate, and which does it misdescribe or fail to reach: the fact that the two camps could agree on predictions while disagreeing about explanation, the empirical claim that eusociality has arisen only in monogamous lineages, the role of modelling tractability, the disciplinary and rhetorical stakes, and above all the question of whether a eusocial colony is a group of organisms or an organism of a new kind? In the other direction, treat the episode as a test of the debate itself. Does a dispute in which each side accuses the other of misdescribing its assumptions, while granting that the alternative bookkeeping gets the right numbers, support the deflationary view that the choice of unit is only a choice of accounting, or does it show that the choice carries substantive commitments the deflationary reading papers over?

Two further things I want pressed, both of which strike me as where the case is least standard. First: both frameworks take population structure — who interacts with whom, and hence the relatedness coefficients — as an input, but in the transition to eusociality the traits under selection are the very traits that generate that structure: whether offspring disperse or stay, whether a foundress mates once or many times, how colonies reproduce. (Their critics made a nearby point when they observed that the Nowak–Tarnita–Wilson model fixes monogamy and places dispersal and helping under the control of a single locus.) So the boundaries of the group are partly endogenous to the character whose evolution is being explained. Does the units-of-selection literature have resources for a case in which the unit is constituted by the trait under selection, or is this a place where the framework is silent — and if it is silent, please say so rather than manufacturing an answer. Second, consider a stylized lineage in which the number of mates per queen rises with colony size, so that within-colony relatedness falls precisely as the colony becomes more functionally integrated and more colony-like in the superorganism sense. Along such a trajectory, is there a determinate point at which the correct description shifts from selection among individuals, mediated by relatedness, to selection among colonies, or is that question ill-posed? And does the answer depend on whether the colony has something like a germ-line/soma division of reproductive labour — that is, on whether what I am calling a group here is really an individual?
