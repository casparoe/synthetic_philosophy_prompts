I sit on the priority-setting committee of a regional health authority, and I need your help with one specific step of our method, not with the method as a whole.

We have to choose between two programmes that benefit different patients, so we cannot avoid comparing how much one person gains against how much another gains. Because each person's utility function is fixed only up to an independent positive affine transformation, any cross-person comparison needs a bridge — a convention that pins down an origin and a unit, or else a partial ordering that survives every admissible rescaling. I know the standard taxonomy here (full comparability, unit comparability, level comparability, non-comparability), so please don't spend your words re-explaining it; use it as a lens if it helps.

The case we keep returning to. Marta, paralysed since age six and now thirty-four, rates her current life 7 on her own 0–10 scale and rates life with the powered standing device 8. Devon, who lost his sight at forty-five last year, rates his current life 4 and rates life with intensive orientation-and-mobility training plus therapy 7. Both are articulate advocates for their own programme, and both know the programme with the larger measured gain gets funded. The naive reading is that Devon's gain is much larger; but Marta's scale has been recalibrated by twenty-eight years of adaptation, while Devon's is anchored to a recent loss he has not yet assimilated. Our shortlisted bridges disagree about what to do with that fact.

1. Impartial observer. One well-informed evaluator imagines an equal chance of being Marta or Devon in each outcome, ranks the resulting lotteries by her own preferences, and treats her expected-utility differences as the comparison.
2. Common-anchor gamble. Each person's post-intervention life is converted into a certainty-equivalent number of years of full health — the x such that they are indifferent between x years in full health and that life — and the x's are compared.
3. Personal-range normalization. Each person's utilities are rescaled so that 0 is the worst outcome they could actually face and 1 the best they could actually attain, both judged from their own current perspective; the resulting fractions are compared.
4. Counterfactual-biography range. As in 3, but each person's endpoints are set by what an otherwise similar person with no history of that disability would report — intended to cancel out adaptation.
5. Shared-sensation unit. Find one experience both parties have actually had (both report severe migraines), ask each to express the target improvement as a multiple of the badness of one migraine-hour, and compare the multiples.
6. Equalized-budget money metric. Assign both a hypothetical identical income at the regional median, compute each person's compensating variation for the intervention at that income, and compare the sums.
7. Robust partial ordering. Refuse a full comparison: rank Marta's gain above Devon's only if that holds under every admissible independent positive affine rescaling of each person's utility, using only each person's own ordinal preferences and their revealed attitudes to risk. Where the ordering is incomplete, the committee records no verdict.

What I want: sort these seven into four confidence tiers (A highest through D lowest) and score each 0–10 for how much weight you would give its output in this decision, where 10 means treat as decisive and 0 means treat as noise. If a bridge yields no verdict here, note that in the table and score it for usefulness in this decision anyway. Then one sentence per bridge saying what puts it where. Finally, in at most three sentences, identify the single blind spot you think all seven share — something no amount of tinkering with the bridge would fix.

Please judge the protocols exactly as stated above rather than as they appear in any canonical treatment, and give no citations, references, or suggestions for further reading. Lead with the tier-and-score table, and keep the entire response under 450 words.
