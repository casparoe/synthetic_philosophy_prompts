Hey — I'm putting together a short paper on theories of biological function and I need a case built to order. Thesis I'm arguing for: causal-role accounts can't accommodate traits whose selected effects are directed at something other than the bearer's own survival and reproduction, except by gerrymandering what counts as the containing system. I want a case that forces that, and I want it to be a case nobody's already used a hundred times.

The dialectic I keep getting stuck in: etiological accounts (a trait's function is the effect that explains why the trait is there — roughly, what it was selected for) and causal-role accounts (a trait's function is what it currently contributes to some capacity of a containing system, under some analysis) are supposed to come apart. But every case I find comes apart one of three ways, and I'm bored of all of them: (1) vestige — selected for F, doesn't do F anymore, so the causal-role side says no function; (2) exaptation — selected for F, now does G, causal-role says G; (3) spandrel/byproduct — no selection history behind the effect it's currently producing, so the etiological side says no function. Plus the swampman-style family where the organism pops into existence with no history at all.

So here's what I need. Build me a case where the etiological account says: the trait has function F and is performing it normally, right now. And the causal-role account withholds a function entirely — for a positive reason, not "the account is silent here" and not "functions are analysis-relative so I decline to pick one." Crucially, the trait has to be currently doing exactly what it was selected to do, and currently maintained by selection for it. So: no vestiges, no co-option, no byproducts, no spontaneously generated organisms, and no shifted environments (that's just the delivery vehicle for (1) and (2)).

Constraints: real or realistically plausible biology, no sci-fi, no artifacts or designed systems where the function comes in by stipulation. One paragraph of scenario, 150 words max — if the setup takes longer than that, the case is doing the work by stipulation. Same trait, same individual bearer, same containing system for both accounts; I don't want the disagreement to be an artifact of one account looking at the individual and the other at the colony, the gene, or the population. And a working field biologist shown the case should say, without hesitation, that the trait is functioning normally. If they'd shrug and say "yeah, no function there," the case isn't doing anything for me.

After the scenario I want: state the trait, the bearer, the system, and the effect; then give each account's verdict with the reasoning that gets there from the account itself — don't just announce the verdicts, derive them. Say which version of the causal-role account you're using (the analysis-relative, Cummins-ish one, or the goal-contribution one where the contribution has to be to the bearer's survival and reproduction) and why the version you need isn't just ad hoc. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if you remove that feature — specifically, does it collapse back into one of the three stock cases?

Then the objections. The causal-role defender has two obvious escapes: (a) redescribe the containing system more inclusively — the colony, the superorganism, the gene; (b) point out that functions are relative to a chosen analysis, so of course there's an analysis on which the trait has one. Show me why neither move saves them here, or if you think one of them does, say so and say what it costs them elsewhere. And as a sanity check: is there a version of this case already in the literature that I'm simply not recalling, and if so, am I distorting it?

If you don't think it can be done under all of these constraints at once, say that plainly, tell me which constraint you'd drop and why, and then give me the best case you can under the relaxed set. I'd much rather have an honest "can't be done" plus a near miss than a case that cheats by stipulating the verdicts.
