Hey — I'm the undergrad student rep on our Campus Food and Wellness Committee (one vote out of nine) and I'm also taking a philosophy seminar right now on autonomy, paternalism and the state: we did Mill's harm principle early on and then a pile of contemporary stuff on nudging, manipulation, and whether interfering with someone can ever *promote* their autonomy rather than just override it. Our next meeting is Thursday and I'm supposed to circulate a written recommendation beforehand, and I've been staring at a blank doc for two days. Can you draft the memo for me?

Here's the case. Our dining contractor, Verdant, runs the mobile order-ahead app that about 60% of meal-plan transactions now go through. Right now the combo auto-fills as fries plus a regular fountain drink; swapping to the side salad or to water takes two taps and doesn't persist, so it resets every order. A student group called Two Taps has proposed something different from what the committee already voted down last year (5–4) — making the salad the default. Their proposal is a 'streak prompt': if a student has ordered the same default combo five days running, the app shows one screen saying, roughly, 'you've had fries and a regular soda with this order five days in a row — keep it, or switch?', with two identical buttons, left/right order randomized each time it fires, no calorie counts, no health messaging, no imagery, one tap either way. It fires at most once a week, and if you dismiss it three times it never fires again on your account. Verdant will build it, but only on condition that they can run it as a randomized rollout for eight weeks so they can measure whether it changes anything and sell it to other campuses; our IRB has already declined to review it on the grounds that it's 'quality improvement,' not research.

The committee is split roughly in half and both halves sound right to me. Two Taps and their faculty allies argue this isn't paternalism at all, because it doesn't favour any outcome: it's a nudge to choose rather than a nudge to choose X, its whole aim is that the choice be more genuinely the student's own instead of a habit running on rails, and the dismiss-three-times clause respects anyone who wants to be left alone. They also point out that the status quo is already a nudge — someone chose to make fries and soda the one-tap option — so we aren't deciding whether to nudge, only which way. The other side, led by Dr. Okonjo in nutrition and Prof. Halvorsen in philosophy (not my seminar, thank god), says this is worse than the salad default, not better: it's *manipulation* rather than paternalism, it works by manufacturing a moment of doubt in people who were perfectly content, and the streak trigger encodes the insulting judgment that a choice someone keeps making isn't really theirs. Their sharpest point, which I can't shake, is the washout objection: if reflecting didn't move people off fries, Verdant wouldn't pay to build it and Two Taps wouldn't have proposed it — so 'we take no view on what you pick' is a fiction, and the proposal is a bet about students' own good wearing procedural clothing, i.e. exactly the paternalism the committee rejected last year.

Two more things are nagging me. First, who actually eats five days running at the same station: first-years, athletes, and students on the biggest meal plans — the people who *can't* opt out, since on-campus residents are required to buy a plan and the nearest grocery store is a 25-minute bus ride, so the 'just dismiss it' exit sits inside an app they have no alternative to. Second, Verdant's contract gives it control of the app's UX, and if we say that interrupting students' habits for their own good is fine, I don't know what we say when Verdant starts running the same streak prompt to upsell dessert or smoothies for its own margin — which they've already hinted at.

What I need is a memo to the committee, around 800–1000 words, with the recommendation up front: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if it's a pilot, for how long and what we'd have to see to renew or kill it (I'm thinking eight weeks of spring term with a report back at week 10, but argue me out of it if that's wrong). It has to give reasons Two Taps could actually reply to, and it has to give the losing side the strongest version of itself rather than knocking it down — Halvorsen will be in the room and he'll notice. My own half-baked thought, if it's any use: I keep coming back to the gap between intervening on a *single* choice and intervening on a *pattern*, and between two different ideas of what makes an interference paternalistic — that someone else's judgment about your good gets substituted for yours, versus that your behaviour gets moved by a route that bypasses your judgment — which seem to come apart in this case in a way they don't for the plain salad default. But I can't turn that into anything nine people could vote on. Please don't pad it with citations, a bibliography or suggested reading — I need the argument itself, not sources to chase.
