I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy — I work in software quality assurance and read about this material in the evenings — so if I have hold of the wrong end of the argument, tell me so before you write anything. I edit the back page of a small monthly magazine read mostly by translators and localization engineers, and for next month's issue I want a piece of sustained parody rather than an essay.

What I would like is a document that reads like an official standard: something along the lines of Standard Operating Procedure 41-B, Certification of a Field Manual for a Previously Uncontacted Language, issued by a fictional bureau, laid out in numbered clauses with cross-references, a definitions section, and at least one appendix. Around 700 to 900 words, in the deadpan voice of a compliance manual that has no doubt about its own authority.

The situation the Standard has to regulate: a team of auditors must certify that the manual rendering the native expression "gavagai" as "rabbit" is the correct one. As I understand the argument, they cannot do this, and not because the natives are hard to read or the evidence is thin. Rival manuals that render the same expression as "undetached rabbit part," as a temporal stage of a rabbit, or as whatever single thing all rabbits share in fit the evidence exactly as well, provided the manual's translations of the surrounding apparatus — identity, sameness, counting, plural endings, "that one again" — are adjusted in step. So the Standard should keep trying to close, clause by clause, and each time should close on nothing.

Where I want the comedy to come from:

The criteria the Standard lays down should be the real ones people reach for, and should fail for the real reason. Recording the natives' assent and dissent to the sentence under stimulation. Pointing and asking. Asking whether this is the same gavagai as that one. Appealing to simplicity, to charity, to what a native would obviously mean. The failure must not be that the natives are unforthcoming or the instruments crude. It should be that every question the auditors can put has to be put in a language whose manual is the very thing under certification, and that a rival manual predicts every answer just as well once it adjusts its plumbing.

The tone should be bureaucratic confidence rather than bafflement. The bureau must never worry, never confess ignorance, never drift into wondering whether the natives really have a language or minds of their own. It goes on issuing certificates. The joke is that the certification is empty while the paperwork is impeccable — a clause that cross-references itself, an appendix of compensating adjustments that grows each time a rival manual is accommodated, a dispute-resolution clause that certifies both of two incompatible manuals and bills the project for both.

At least one clause or footnote should let slip, without anyone drawing the moral, that the bureau's own English is in the same position: that the Standard is also available in a version in which its own terms have been reinterpreted part-wise or stage-wise, and that both versions carry the same certification mark.

Please include one short quotation from a fictional fieldworker who has been disciplined for insisting that his manual is the right one, and whose objection is precisely the one the Standard has no reply to.

Two things I would ask you not to do. Do not let the piece become a joke about rabbits, or about fieldworkers being muddled: the rabbit should be visibly a placeholder, and by the end a reader should be able to see that the same certification problem would arise for any term of any language, including his own. And do not let the Standard's failure be a failure of evidence or of nerve — no clause should imply that more data, a better instrument, a longer stay, or a cleverer question would settle the matter.

It should be legible to a working translator who has never heard of Quine, and fair enough that someone who has would not wince. As I understand the position, it is not skepticism about what the natives mean and not the claim that translation is impossible in practice, and the piece should not be readable as either.
