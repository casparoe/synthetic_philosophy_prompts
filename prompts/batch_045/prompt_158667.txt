hey — I'm stuck on a paper about aggregation in Scanlon-style contractualism and I want to stop and write down what I'd even count as a solution before going back to the literature and getting sucked in again.

So: what does a contractualist way of handling aggregation actually have to do to count as adequate? I mean a principled story about cases where a big cost to one person has to be set against small benefits to many (or the other way round), inside the contractualist framing where the test is whether some individual could reasonably reject the principle — not whether total welfare goes up. Fix the purpose as this: it has to be a principle you could hand to an institution (a triage committee, a transport authority, an ethics review board) and have them actually decide a case, with the justification framed in terms no individual could reasonably reject. Not just "an interesting position in a debate."

Give me the full spec. For each requirement, the case or consideration behind it, and whether it is genuinely non-negotiable for the view or just something one camp wants. I especially want to know which demands follow from contractualism itself and which are smuggled in from elsewhere: from consequentialism, from a prior commitment to the ordering being complete and transitive, from wanting the principle to be usable by actual committees under time pressure, and so on.

Cases I want any candidate to handle, and ones I keep coming back to:

- the plain one: one person loses a limb, ten thousand people avoid something trivial. Give your cleanest version.
- dilution: same total benefit, spread over more and more people, so each person's share shrinks toward nothing. At what point should aggregating stop working, if ever? And is the thing being added up a sum of benefit sizes or a count of people who have a complaint? Those come apart and I think the spec has to say which one it is using.
- the shrapnel case: one person loses something enormous. Can her loss be broken up into many smaller losses and set against many small benefits to many people? If the answer is no, why not — and does that depend on how fine-grained we make "a complaint"? If the answer is yes, hasnt aggregation just come back in through the back door?
- the same-person-over-time version: the many small benefits and the one big cost all land on the same person at different times (lots of small pleasures now, one serious injury later). Is that structurally a different case from the many-person case, or the same one? I dont trust my intuition here.
- the reverse direction: one person gets a large benefit, many people each lose a little.
- mixed-motive / role-rotation: the people who benefit are also the ones imposing the risk, and anyone might end up on either side. Does that change what the principle needs to do?
- the conditional-existence one: the many are people who will only exist if the principle permits aggregating.

Then the part I care most about: flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidates and which requirement is really a prejudice of one camp dressed up as neutral.

Whatever you take Scanlon's own move to be — the bit where you compare how individuals fare across candidate principles rather than summing — I want a spec that an answer has to meet regardless of whether that move survives.

Short scorecard at the very end is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria: (a) straight aggregation done inside contractualism, (b) the individualist style restriction where you compare the loser against each winner one at a time, (c) threshold or tie-breaker views where aggregation only gets to decide among principles that already survived individual objections, (d) ex ante or randomizing versions that aggregate expected complaints, (e) anything else that deserves a row.
