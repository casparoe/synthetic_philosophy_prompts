I'm finishing a chapter on hypocrisy and the conditions on blame, and I've come to think the usual framing — can a hypocrite blame? — is too coarse to be answered, because our actual practices don't answer it the same way twice. So I want to work from the other end: take the practices as given and reconstruct the theory of standing they commit us to, then ask whether that theory holds together.

Here is the pattern I keep circling. Under the equitable clean-hands rule, a plaintiff whose own blameworthy conduct was bound up in the very transaction they are suing on is refused equitable relief altogether; but the refusal is all-or-nothing, it has to be raised by the defendant, and the misconduct has to be connected to the subject matter of the suit rather than a general blot on the plaintiff's character. Move across the courthouse and the same kind of fault behaves completely differently: in tort, the plaintiff's own carelessness doesn't bar the claim at all, it discounts the recovery proportionally, and in criminal proceedings the complainant's own criminal record is irrelevant to whether the accused is guilty, surfacing at most as a question about credibility. Move out of law into the international sphere and it shifts again: the post-war tribunals insisted that one belligerent's commission of the same crime does nothing to excuse another's, tu quoque is not a defence to liability, and yet in diplomatic argument the reply does demonstrably something — it lowers the critic's standing, and everyone involved behaves as though it does, while insisting in the same breath that it shouldn't.

The thing I can't bring into focus is whether there is one principled story here or three unrelated ones. Notice that equity's rule is essentially relational: the misconduct that disqualifies you is misconduct in the very transaction, and generally toward the very party you are suing, which makes it look less like a doctrine about hypocrisy and more like a doctrine about what you may demand from someone you have wronged. That would explain why the objection bites hardest when a state denounces another state's treatment of that state's own citizens and bites least, or not at all, when I denounce you for what you did to me — even though in both cases the speaker does the very thing they are condemning. But if the implicit view is relational, the practice of shaming in international human-rights institutions is left unexplained, and so is the fact that the same people who discount a hypocritical state's blame treat the hypocrite's advice, warning, or prediction as entirely in order.

So let me add the variant I've been toying with, because it is the case that makes me think the practice is underdetermined. Suppose a tribunal adopted what I'll call the would-have proviso: a complainant lacks standing if, placed in the defendant's circumstances, they would have done what the defendant did, as the tribunal best can tell — with the result that someone who attempted the same wrong and failed through bad luck loses standing, while someone who committed it and self-reports retains it. That reverses the actual doctrine on both halves, since the actual doctrine cares about what you did in this transaction, not about what you would have done, and it doesn't much care whether you were caught or came forward. I take the proviso to presuppose a dispositional or commitment-based view of standing, where what matters is what the norm actually has by way of a grip on you, whereas the doctrine we have looks backward at conduct and complicity. I want to know whether that's right, whether the backward-looking rule strictly presupposes a complicity view or would sit equally well under several, and in particular whether the practices rule out the dispositional view or merely fail to implement it.

A second wrinkle, in the same territory. We are distinctly unimpressed by the blame of someone who refrained only because they never had the opportunity — you didn't defraud anyone because nobody ever left you alone with the accounts — and yet the international system runs on small states shaming great powers for exactly the failures they themselves would never have had the capacity to commit, and we don't treat that as a standing problem. Does that asymmetry get explained by whatever theory you favour, or does it show that the practice is tracking something other than standing, like the epistemic value of the critic's testimony or the strategic effects of letting them speak?

What I'd like from you: reconstruct the implicit theory of standing that this cluster of practices — equitable, tortious, criminal, diplomatic, and the informal norms of moral address — comes closest to presupposing, and keep separate what the practices strictly require from what merely fits them comfortably. Say where several theories would license the same practice, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined and my chapter can't lean on it as evidence. Say which part of the pattern, if any, is an accident of institutional history rather than a commitment. Then tell me what would actually have to change in doctrine and in ordinary moral address if the backward-looking test were replaced by something like the would-have proviso, including what it would do to the evidential position of a tribunal and to the blamer who has simply never been tempted. If it helps, distinguish standing to blame from standing to advise, to demand an accounting, to impose sanctions, and to resent, since I suspect the practices distribute these separately and the conflation is what has made the literature hard to read.

I don't need the literature named or a reading list — I have that part covered. Lead with a single sentence giving me your bottom line — what the practices actually presuppose, in one breath — and then argue for it.
