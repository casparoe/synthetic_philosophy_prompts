I'm a journalist — I mostly cover science and ideas — and I'm finishing a long piece on whether an ant colony can have a mind. You know the setup: the Aunt Hillary bit from Hofstadters Gödel, Escher, Bach, where you end up having an actual conversation with a hill of ants and each of her 'concepts' is supposedly some team of ants that lights up. I've interviewed two people for the piece who are clearly talking past each other, and I'd much rather end it with something they could both sign than with another round of he-said-she-said. So: draft me the joint statement.

Camp one thinks the colony is a genuine cognitive agent — the ants are doing roughly what neurons do, colony-level states are where the real beliefs and desires live, and it isn't a metaphor that the colony decides where to move the nest. Camp two thinks that's all projection: there are ants, there are local interactions, there's chemistry on the ground, and nothing in the setup picks out a single thing that has a point of view, or that would be the one doing the minding. (Both agree there's no ant in charge — nobody is defending a little queen sitting in a control room.)

What I want, concretely:

1. A numbered set of agreed claims, the stuff neither side disputes. Include the biology: stigmergy, pheromone fields doing real computational work, no central controller, workers dying off and being replaced while the colony itself persists for years, colonies that fission, colonies spread across several nests at once, ants sometimes working at cross-purposes. Correct me if I've got any of that wrong. Then the agreed claims about what it would even take for something to have a mind.

2. A table of the disagreements, each one phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. I don't want a straw version of either view — I want to email this to both of them and see if they'll put their names on it.

3. The cruxes, and for each one a case or test both sides would accept as bearing on it, plus what each predicts in advance, written down before the fact.

Some cruxes I think are in there, but use your own if mine are bad:

- Integration. Camp two's strongest move, as I understand it, is that no part of a colony has access to the colony's overall state; every ant only ever samples its immediate surroundings, so there's nothing playing the role a global workspace or an integrated conscious state is supposed to play. Camp one's reply, which I half believe, is that the pheromone field and the nest architecture *are* the shared medium — a memory written outside anyone's head that any ant can read from and write to. So the crux is whether a shared external medium counts as integration, or whether it just relocates the mind into the environment. Is there an experiment that would tell these apart, or is this the place where the whole thing goes verbal?

- The overwrite test. This one I made up and I want you to take it seriously: overnight, you swap the colony's nest floor and surrounding substrate for chemically identical but clean material, ants and queen untouched. The chemical record — trail strengths, gradient fields, whatever history is sitting in the ground — is gone. Both camps: has something been damaged here, or is the colony simply in a different world? Compare erasing a person's notebook, giving them amnesia, and bulldozing their house. Is there a version of this where camp one should predict a specific, measurable deficit and camp two shouldn't, or the reverse?

- Vocabulary. Individual ants can't count, and you can't *tell* a colony anything — you can only arrange situations for it. So is the colony's representational repertoire essentially closed, a handful of chemical channels with nothing recombinable, or is there a productivity in trail geometry that does the work a language of thought is meant to do? If it's closed, does that settle the question, or does it just show the colony is a very stupid mind?

- Unity over time and space. A mind that can be in two nests at once, split in half, absorb another colony, lose most of its workers and keep going — is that a mind with unusual boundaries, or a sign that we were never tracking a single thing to begin with? Note that nothing here has the tidy answer you get for an animal.

Also: tell me which parts of this dispute you think are purely verbal and why, and flag anywhere the two camps are using 'memory', 'decision', or 'concept' to mean different things. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I can't check them and my editor cuts them anyway. Plain language where you can; it's going in front of two smart people who aren't philosophers. And if your honest conclusion is that they can't sign anything substantial, say that instead, and tell me what the piece should conclude instead.
