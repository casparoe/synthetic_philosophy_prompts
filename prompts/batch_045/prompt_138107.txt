hey — I'm a novelist and I need someone to think out loud at me before I do something I definitely can't undo. Please don't give me sources, citations or a reading list; I have a lawyer for the paperwork and I've read enough op-eds. I want the reasoning, in plain words.

Seven years ago my friend Ines (also a writer, we met in a workshop in our twenties) was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's. A year later, still completely sharp, she asked me to be the one who decides. The law where we live lets you set this up in advance — from what I understand a named person has to confirm at the time that the conditions the person wrote down have actually arrived, and it was me she named. Her condition wasn't "when I'm in pain" or "when I can't walk". It was: when I no longer recognise my daughters. And she gave a reason. She said the part of her that could love them and worry about them was what she meant by herself, and that once that was gone, whatever was left in the chair wasn't the kind of thing that could be wronged by being killed or benefited by being kept going.

She also said something I keep going back to. She'd watched her own mother go through it. She said: if that woman is smiling, don't take the smile for an answer. A smile is just what's left over when you delete the person who made the promise. It's not me talking, and you shouldn't treat it as if it were.

O.k. So that was then. Now: Ines hasn't recognised either daughter in about eight months. She lives in a care home, and by every report and by what I see, she's content. She makes jam in the home's kitchen, she hums, she's worked out a sort of flirtation with the man who comes to fix the boiler. When something upsets her she's distressed for a few minutes and then can't remember what it was. She's not in pain. She laughs at things.

The daughters are split. One says that isn't my mother any more, do what she asked. The other says my mother is sitting right there, she laughed at my joke on Tuesday, and you would be killing a woman who wants to be alive, whatever a piece of paper says. The staff stay out of it.

Here's the part that's mine and that I can't get an angle on. I've been going twice a week for two years. I turned down a residency. I'm a year late with a book that's already been paid for, and my agent has stopped being polite about it. And I make my living out of endings that feel earned. When I picture doing what Ines asked, part of what I feel is relief — and part of it is the shape of the thing, the promise kept, the letter, the last visit. That's the least trustworthy part of me, I know that. If it were only relief, I'd know what to think. If it were only loyalty, I'd know what to think. What I can't settle is whether my sense that this is the right ending is a reason, or just a plot.

So two questions. First, the one Ines set up for me: is the contented woman in that chair the person who made the promise, in the way that matters? I don't mean is she the same human body, obviously she is. I mean: does her happiness now count for her the way your happiness counts for you, or is it more like somebody else's happiness, happening to somebody sitting in her body? I've gone back and forth until both answers feel like word games.

Second: given that I can't trust my own motives, what should I actually do — sign, refuse, refuse but keep visiting, or hand the decision to somebody else? And if I hand it over, is that honesty or is it running away? Tell me what you'd do if you were me rather than giving me both sides, but do tell me where you think I'm kidding myself.
