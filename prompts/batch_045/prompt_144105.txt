Hi — I'm on the naming committee at a small atrocity-remembrance museum (we write the wall texts and the annual statement) and we've boxed ourselves in, so I wanted an outside read before the next meeting.

Our record so far: Srebrenica 1995 — genocide. Herero and Nama 1904–08 — genocide. Yazidis under ISIS, 2014 — genocide, and we explicitly said it didn't matter that the perpetrator wasn't a state. Khmer Rouge — genocide for the Vietnamese and the Cham, but not for the killings of "new people", because our write-up said that was a political category, not a protected one. Canadian residential schools — genocide, but under a separate "cultural genocide" heading, which our charter keeps inside the genocide heading because it says we use the term in Lemkin's broader sense of destroying a group's life, not just the Convention's; nobody ever wrote down how much broader. Holodomor — no, we called it a mass atrocity instead: foreseeable, even welcomed, but not carried out in order to destroy Ukrainians as such. 1944 deportation of the Crimean Tatars — also no: catastrophic and the community was wrecked, but the point was removal, and the group went on existing elsewhere.

New case, and it isn't historical. A ministry upstream diverts and salinates the water of a delta region inhabited mostly by an ethnic minority with an active separatist movement. It goes out as an irrigation and hydropower scheme, run through a public-private consortium whose water allocations are set by an automated system. Ten years on the delta can't be lived in, the population has scattered into diaspora, the birth rate has collapsed, and fluent speakers of the language are nearly all elderly. Leaked internal documents show the consortium's models forecast that depopulation in detail and logged it as an upside — it ends the separatist pressure — but the operative stated aim was power and cotton, and no individual official can be shown to have adopted the group's destruction as a purpose. Nobody disputes the group is an ethnic minority.

Do we put genocide on the wall? Which of our own cases is the closest analogue, and if our precedents genuinely don't settle it, what rule would we be committing to by saying yes and what by saying no? I'm less after the correct call than after which rule our seven past decisions are actually consistent with — I think at least two or three fit all of them — and which variant of this case we'd have to imagine for those rules to come apart instead of agreeing.
