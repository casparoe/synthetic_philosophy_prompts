You're a professional political philosopher who's spent a decade or so on memory politics, transitional justice and the ethics of commemoration; brainstorm privately for a minute about where the *real* disagreement sits, then write carefully and don't hedge everything into mush.

The setup: two camps have been arguing past each other for a year and a half about whether there is a duty to remember historical wrongs. Call them the Remembrance camp (roughly Margalit-flavoured: we owe it to the victims and to those we stand in thick relations with to remember, forgetting is an insult distinct from forgiving, and an unremembered wrong is a wrong that keeps succeeding) and the Amnesty camp (roughly Rieff-flavoured in spirit: a community that keeps its founding wounds open can't hold together or have a shared future, letting a grievance go cold is not the same as denying it, and a duty to remember risks binding the living to the dead). I'm naming those two only as orientation — don't do textual exegesis and don't quote anybody.

The bit I actually care about, so please fold these in rather than reaching for the familiar moves:

- a distinction between hot memory (the wrong stays affectively live; the community keeps being wounded by it) and cold memory (the wrong is retained propositionally, available to reasoning and to institutions, but no longer hurts). Which of these does the duty, if there is one, actually demand? I recon this is where they genuinely split.
- the sleeping-archive problem: a society funds a perfect, searchable, well-maintained record — transcripts, recordings, an interactive AI testimony system, monuments — and nobody consults it, nobody alive could recite anything from it, but anyone could at any time. Has the duty (if any) been discharged? Is that custodianship or is it abandonment? Both camps have to answer it and I want both answers.
- asymmetry: is the duty different for descendants of victims than for descendants of perpetrators, and does it change character when the last person with living memory of the event dies?
- the memory-budget point: attention and public time are finite, so any duty to remember is really a rule for allocating scarce attention — which makes forgetting either an omission or a decision, and I want to know which each side says it is.
