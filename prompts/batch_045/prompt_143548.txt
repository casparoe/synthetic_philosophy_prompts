I am trying to settle a recurring disagreement with a colleague about whether Malthusian population theory was, in the familiar phrase, right but early: correct in its mechanism, merely wrong about when that mechanism would bite. She puts the probability somewhere near 0.6; I am closer to 0.15. I would like your own number, but I care much more about the decomposition that produces it than about the number itself.

Take the proposition to be this: Malthus's central causal claim — that, absent a preventive check on fertility, population pressure tends to push a population's standard of living back toward subsistence rather than generating sustained growth in per-capita income — is a correct description of the mechanism that governs human populations, and the two centuries of rising incomes since 1798 represent a mistiming of that mechanism's predicted onset rather than a refutation of it.

Please give me a credence in that proposition, decomposed. The terms I would like you to assign probabilities to, adding or substituting any you think are doing more work:

1. That the theory's core, read as the arithmetic of population's capacity to grow faster than the food supply, is doing genuine empirical work at all, rather than being close to true by construction — something that holds of any population with a positive growth capacity in any finite environment — in which case the claim may be unfalsifiable rather than early.
2. That the theory is best read as scoped to a pre-industrial regime in which the escape had not yet occurred, and whether that scoping makes it supported by the long stretch of roughly flat pre-modern living standards or instead immunised against the evidence that would otherwise count against it.
3. That the reversal in the sign of the income–fertility relationship in modern societies — rising prosperity is now associated with falling rather than rising fertility — is a change in a background parameter the theory was always entitled to leave open, as opposed to a failure of the mechanism as stated.
4. That the relevant resource constraint is best modelled as comparatively fixed, so that population pressure produces scarcity, rather than as one that expands endogenously when population pressure rises, so that pressure itself induces the technical changes that avert scarcity — and, on the second reading, whether the pressure is a cause of subsistence crises or of their avoidance.
5. That a preventive check belongs inside the theory from the start, and hence whether the demographic transition is something the theory predicts, something it merely accommodates after the fact, or something it survives only by being restated.
6. That a claim about a long-run regime can be tested at all on the evidence available to us, given that the apparent escape from the mechanism has occurred once, in a single historically entangled case, with no independent reruns — and how much that underdetermination should by itself suppress confidence.

Then combine the terms into a single number, tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, and tell me which one piece of evidence, if it were available, would move that number the most and in which direction. Be explicit about what you would count as the question's being resolved, since right but early has a way of arranging things so that no observation could ever settle it; if you think the proposition is best split into several better-defined ones, say so and give a number for each.

Finally: given the two figures of 0.6 and 0.15, say where you think my colleague and I actually disagree — which of the sub-questions above, or which one that neither of us has articulated, is most likely carrying the gap. Please do not answer that it depends on what one means by Malthusianism; if the reading matters to you, state the reading your number is conditional on and tell me how far it moves across the alternatives.
