last night me and my friend got into a three-hour argument about Parfit's teletransporter (Reasons and Persons, 1984) and got nowhere. His line is that the branching version settles everything: identity isn't what matters in survival, both output people are equally as much me as I'd have been in the non-branching case, there's nothing further to work out. I dont think that's an answer so much as a refusal to ask the next question, and I think I finally see what the next question is: everyone treats what matters as a single relation that either holds or doesn't, when it might come in degrees that don't have to add up to one across branches — and, separately, nobody asks which practical attitudes are supposed to track it. Fear of death, prudential discounting, promising, debt, blame, ownership: those could come apart, and the standard cases only ever test the first two.

The cases I want to build the paper around aren't the usual fission ones: a staggered scan (the reconstruction is assembled from scans taken years apart, so the intermediate psychology was never instantiated in anyone), a lossy scan (missing stretches filled in by inference from population-level patterns rather than from my brain), and a cold backup (the scan is stored, never sent, and the replica is built thirty years after the original dies). Then I want to push the practical side: if I promise to meet you tomorrow and then branch, do both of us owe you the meeting, and can one of us skip it on the grounds that the other is handling it.

Write me a two-page prospectus for a one-semester senior thesis, roughly 8000 words. Don't just take my side — give the strongest version of my friend's position and say where my framing is weakest, I'd rather find that out now. No citations, I'll fill those in myself.

Sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: Question (one paragraph, about 120 words, put it so it could come out either way); Why it's still open (about 200 words); Method (about 250 words — what I'd actually do, week by week, and why that rather than just arguing intuitions about more cases); What a result looks like and what would mean the project failed (about 150 words); Scope risks (about 100 words — what I should cut if this is too big for one semester).
