Every week he puts up a case and asks two questions: "is this fact a reason to do the thing, a reason not to, or not a reason at all?" and "if you don't do it, is there anything left over that you owe anyone?" Here's his track record from the term so far.

1. I promised my flatmate I'd help him move on Saturday, then something much more important came up. Ruling: the promise is a reason to help, it can be outweighed, you're allowed to do the more important thing — but you owe him something. 2. Going to a party because it'll be fun: the fun is a reason to go. But a bunch of people are going to an event where the whole point is watching some guy get publicly humiliated, and they're going because they'll enjoy it. Ruling: here the fun is a reason AGAINST going, and he got really insistent that this isn't just a small reason-for that gets outweighed by something bigger — the fact that you'd enjoy it is itself part of what's wrong with going.

3. Telling your mate that his girlfriend is cheating on him will really hurt him, and he'd asked you ages ago to always tell him hard truths. Ruling: the fact that it'll hurt him is, in this case, a reason FOR telling him — not merely a cost that gets outweighed. Something about the hurt being a measure of how much it matters to him.

4. A friend guilt-trips you into promising you'll back him up in a lie to his parents. Ruling: the fact that you promised gives you no reason whatsoever, and if you don't back him up you owe him nothing on that score. (He did add that you might owe him an apology for saying "I promise" in the first place, which I didn't really get.)

5. There's a silly rule in our department handbook saying undergrads can't use the good coffee machine. Ruling: the fact that something is against a rule is normally a reason not to do it, but since nobody can name a single purpose this rule serves, it gives you no reason at all — not even a tiny one.
