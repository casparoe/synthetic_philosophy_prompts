Write a short dialogue between a Buddhist ethicist and an act-consequentialist advising a retreat center on how to run a small alms-distribution program. The Buddhist holds that an action's moral quality is settled by the intention from which it springs—whether it expresses loving-kindness and non-attachment rather than craving or aversion—and that this remains true even when two actions have identical effects on suffering. The consequentialist holds that the right action is simply the one that maximizes welfare, and that intentions matter only as causes of further consequences. To keep the exchange from settling into slogans, they must address a wrinkle: the center tries distributing alms by an impersonal algorithm that reliably relieves the most measurable suffering, but the recipients, on learning that no one actually cared about them, feel humiliated and some of the benefit is lost; by contrast, monks who give with genuine loving-kindness produce less measurable welfare but leave the recipients feeling respected. Have the Buddhist argue that the humiliation and the respect are not merely extra consequences to be entered into the consequentialist ledger, and have the consequentialist reply by saying whether the intention behind the action is a distinct moral concern or only a causally important input to the outcome. Both characters should be equally competent: the Buddhist should engage the claim that the relief of suffering is what ultimately matters, and the consequentialist should engage the claim that a mental state can be partly constitutive of the good rather than merely productive of it. The dialogue should end with each stating the precise point at which they take the other's framework either to ignore something real or to count it twice.
