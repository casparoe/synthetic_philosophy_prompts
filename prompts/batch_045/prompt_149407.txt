I have written up my position on why promises bind for a graduate seminar and I would like an audit of it rather than a survey of the literature. The commitments, as I currently hold them, are these:

1. Promising is a normative power, and the reason it generates is content-independent: it runs to doing the promised thing as such, and is not exhausted by — nor reducible to — the reasons supplied independently by the promisee's expectations, her reliance, or the general value of people keeping their promises.

2. What is created is a directed duty: it is owed to the promisee, breaching it wrongs her in particular, and she holds a standing power to release me from it.

3. That power is personal and lapses with her. Once the promisee is dead, no one else — not her estate, not her family, not the person the promise concerned — can release me.

4. Wrongful content nullifies outright. A promise to do something immoral generates no obligation at all, as opposed to an obligation that exists and is then defeated or outweighed.

5. Coercion nullifies as well, but only where the threat comes from the promisee. If a third party forces me to promise you something you had a right to anyway, the promise does bind.

6. Nullity is not to be extended from wrongfulness to triviality. A promise about something of very slight importance still generates a genuine obligation; there is no de minimis threshold below which promising yields mere considerations rather than duties.

7. Obligation survives zero impact. It remains in force, and breaking it remains wrong, in the case where the promisee has since died, no one else knows of the promise, and no one's expectations, interests or reliance are affected either way.

8. Promising requires intention, and intention fixes the content. One cannot promise accidentally; and if I say that I promise to be there at six while privately intending only to try, then trying is all I owe, however reasonable the promisee's contrary understanding.

Some of these pairs look to me as though they must collide, but I cannot tell which collisions are real. Please go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against one another and which apparent conflicts dissolve once the right distinction is drawn — in particular, say where an argument that looks like a reduction from one commitment to another is simply invalid. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, state what would have to be given up and what that costs; and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most. If any commitment is best abandoned only in its strong form — as a claim about what obligations exist rather than about what there is reason to do, or the reverse — say so explicitly. Please answer in British English.
