Hi. I sit on the review panel for a new interdisciplinary call at our foundation (working title something like "Origins of Mind and Society" — the scope is roughly: how you get cognition, cooperation and culture out of stuff that has none of it). We see about forty proposals a cycle, from physicists, developmental psychologists, anthropologists, ML people, and at least one person per round who has discovered that everything is really a resonance. My job is to attach a one-page "explanatory adequacy" note to each proposal, and I have maybe twenty minutes per proposal, no way to ask proposers for clarifications (it's blind), and a panel chair who is a cell biologist and will visibly loose patience if I say anything that sounds like jargon. Each proposal is around 1.5M over four years, and I have to be able to defend the note if a rejected PI complains.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run, not an essay. The ideas to operationalize are Dennett's: cranes versus skyhooks, and the complaint about greedy reductionism — the move where you "explain" something by jumping straight to the bottom level and skipping the middle ones. My panel basically agrees with both when I describe them in plain words, and both are useless to me as stated, because nobody writes a proposal that says "and then a skyhook happens."

So: give me an ordered set of gates, each with (a) the question to ask of the proposal text as written, (b) what counts as a sufficient answer, (c) where I stop with a no, and (d) where I hand the decision to the panel because it's a values call rather than a factual one — risk appetite, whether we're funding techniques or answers, that kind of thing. Verdicts should come from a short fixed list I can put in a dropdown, and the output should include the one objection I'd have to raise if the PI appealed.

Two bits of vocabulary I've half-invented and been using on my own — keep them if they're worth keeping, tell me plainly if they're confused:

- "Ghost crane": a proposal that looks like a mechanism because it's written in mechanism language, but whose moving parts are credited with exactly the competence that was supposed to be explained, just at a smaller scale, an earlier time, or a lower level. My rough test is that you can substitute the word for the thing-to-be-explained into the description of the part without anything changing.
- "Handoff debt": every crane has to be handed off from the level below it — somewhere you have to say how the dumber parts do the work. Declared debt (they say roughly who will pay it and when) I'm happy to fund against. Hidden debt is where I think greedy reductionism actually lives.

Other tests I've been fiddling with and can't get to behave: a look-ahead test (does the mechanism need the end state specified in advance in order to work at all?), an unplugging test (if the crane is removed, does the capacity degrade gradually or does the whole thing vanish?), and some rule about what the lower-level story predicts that the upper-level story doesn't — if the answer is "nothing", why go down there? Consolidate these, drop them, or replace them as you see fit, but be explicit about which distinctions have to survive for the procedure to still be tracking the real idea and which ones are only conveniences.

Then run it properly on three proposals (treat these as one-page summaries):

1. A physicist proposes a new variational principle — call it "coherence maximization" — that governs open systems, and claims it explains both the origin of life and the evolution of cooperation, with a proposed experimental signature in thermodynamic efficiency.
2. A group proposes to explain a large, well-documented social trend (your pick: the long decline in interpersonal violence, or the fertility transition) with a story about a neuromodulator, plus a design that measures proxies for it in a modern population.
3. One from my own lab, which is why I'm asking: we want money to model children's word learning as Bayesian inference over a hypothesis space of candidate meanings. I think it's a crane. I suspect your procedure is going to tell me the hypothesis space is a ghost crane and that the actual explanatory work is somewhere we haven't done it. If that's what it says, say so, and say what a version of the project that passes would look like.

Also show me the procedure declining to give a verdict: e.g. a proposal that just wants to build a large descriptive model of collective behaviour from video data and claims to explain nothing. I don't want to force those into the frame.

Finally, be honest about where the procedure misfires. I'm most worried about (i) genuinely new phenomena where nobody yet knows what the levels are, (ii) proposals that are right but badly written, and (iii) me using "skyhook" as a fancy way of saying "I definately don't like this guy."
