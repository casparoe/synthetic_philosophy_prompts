I would like a reading of Borges's short story Funes the Memorious — the one about the Uruguayan boy who, after a fall from a horse, acquires a perfect, exhaustively detailed memory and is, precisely because of it, described as barely capable of thought — through the lens of Bongard problems: the twelve-panel visual puzzles in which six figures instantiate a concept and six violate it, and the solver must induce the concept from those examples alone.

The connection I want tested is this. A Bongard problem is formally and radically underdetermined: infinitely many predicates separate the left-hand panels from the right-hand ones, including gerrymandered ones of the grue variety. It is solvable at all, or so I want to argue, only because the setter and the solver share a stock of natural concepts and a similarity metric. Funes looks like the limiting case of a learner with all the data and none of that shared bias: call it a Funes machine, a system that returns a hypothesis extensionally consistent with the twelve panels — a lookup table over the positive examples, every detail retained and nothing compressed — but chosen with no preference for the natural, the simple, or the projectible.

I would like the reading to address, or to explain why it cannot settle, the following:

1. Does the story support the compression picture on which forgetting differences is constitutive of having a concept, or something narrower — that Funes lacks a shared similarity metric rather than a simplicity preference? Is his failure best described as a failure to generalize, or as a failure to have the same concepts as anyone else, making him ungradeable rather than wrong?
2. Bongard problems are often cited as cases where people succeed in seconds while systems trained on vastly more data fail. If that is right, what does the story imply about the direction of the explanation: that more retention is worse, that the needed inductive bias is a kind of convention, or that the relevant capacity cannot be characterised as inference from the panels at all?
3. Where does the story resist this reading? Say so plainly if the tale is doing something Romantic or existential rather than epistemological, if Funes's memory is presented as perception-like rather than as stored data, or if the machine analogy distorts it.
4. What, if anything, does the story add to the standing arguments about underdetermination, projectibility, and simplicity priors that those arguments lack on their own?
