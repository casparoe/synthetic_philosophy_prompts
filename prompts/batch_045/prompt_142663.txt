I am writing the chapter of my dissertation in which the standards of adequacy for an account of mental causation get fixed, and I want the specification settled before I begin scoring candidate views. I have read enough of the surrounding literature to recognise the standard moves, so please do not survey it, attribute positions to authors, or hand me a reading list: I want requirements, each with the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, together with your judgement about which of the things people demand in this area are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp.

The purpose against which adequacy is to be measured is fixed, and I would like the list held to it. The theory must vindicate the claim that an agent's reasons can explain her action in a way that could bear on moral responsibility: not merely predict the action, not merely redescribe it in mental vocabulary, and not merely rationalise it after the fact. An account that secures causation for the mental only by making mental properties causally idle summaries of neural goings-on fails for this purpose, and I would rather that failure be recorded as a requirement on the list than silently assumed.

Here is the pile of demands I have collected. For each, tell me whether it is a genuine desideratum, a demand that presupposes the answer against one family of views, or a preference of a camp; rewrite the genuine ones into a form that does not beg the question; and add the requirements I have missed.

1. The theory must respect the causal closure of the physical, or else say exactly which formulation of closure it rejects and why the formulation its opponents use is stronger than anything physics supports. My suspicion is that the premise usually written down (roughly, that every physical event with a cause has a physical cause) is weaker than what the exclusion reasoning needs, and that the difference becomes visible if microphysics turns out to be indeterministic.
2. It may not buy room for the mental by appealing to indeterminacy at the micro-level without saying why the resulting probabilities are not themselves fixed by prior physical goings-on.
3. It must not let the exclusion reasoning generalise: if the argument drains causal work from the mental, it seems equally to drain it from temperature, from the brittleness of glass, and from the special sciences generally, and a view that stops the argument only at the mental looks ad hoc.
4. It must not make the causal status of the mental hostage to how complete physics happens to be at a given time; a theory that predicts mental causation is refuted by further neuroscientific detail is, I think, disqualified.
5. It must respect a set of distinctions I take to be non-negotiable candidates: causation by a property versus causation by a particular event; causation versus causal explanation; difference-making versus production; symmetric overdetermination versus preemption; and the causal relevance of a state versus the agent's exercising control in acting on it.
6. It must handle the case in which the mental cause and its physical realiser come apart in time: a long-standing intention is cited as the cause of an action performed months later, though nothing that realised the intention when it was formed still exists. Say what the causal relation relates, and what the requirement rules out.

Two further cases are of my own construction, and I want every candidate scored on them. The first: a single belief, with one content, is realised twice over inside one nervous system, each realiser sufficient on its own for the behaviour and one of them actually pre-empting the other. Does the mental cause survive, and does your answer differ if the two realisers had different contents? The second: the state that does the causal work is subpersonal, in the sense that it is not a state the agent could cite as her reason even on ideal reflection. If a theory allows such a state to be the cause of action, I want to know whether it has thereby given up the thing my chapter needs, and whether that is a cost or a discovery.

Finally, three things I most want from you. First: is any subset of the retained desiderata jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which one would you drop and what does dropping it cost — I suspect the list itself is a result here. Second: which single requirement does the most exclusionary work? Third: mark which requirements hold only relative to the responsibility-focused purpose I have fixed, and say where the list forces a decision about what causation itself is, since I suspect much of the dispute is a fight about that question conducted at one remove. Keep any scoring of the live candidate families to a short paragraph each, described by their structural claim rather than by name.
