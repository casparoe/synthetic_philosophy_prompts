I've been flip-flopping on this for almost two years now and I've stopped wanting a verdict — I want to know what kind of fight it even is.

Setup: vague predicates, "tall", "heap", "red". Two stories I keep switching between.

Story one: the cut-off is sharp. There's some exact height in micrometers where "tall" stops applying, nothing about our use of the word settles where it is, and we can never find out. It feels right because classical logic and bivalence come out untouched and the sense that reality has no joints here gets relocated into our ignorance. It feels wrong because "there is a fact of the matter that nobody could ever know" is doing an awful lot of work, and because it turns the location of the line into a wild coincidence with what creatures with our eyes and memories can discriminate.

Story two: there are many admissible sharpenings, and a borderline sentence comes out true on some and false on others, so it's neither true nor false. It feels right because it takes at face value what it's actually like to be faced with a borderline case — you don't feel ignorant, you feel like there's nothing to be ignorant about. It feels wrong because it doesn't explain why we don't know which sharpening is the right one, and because the line between borderline and definately borderline looks just as sharp as the line it was supposed to get rid of, one level up.

Why I suspect it's verbal: I can't find a single piece of conversational data that seperates them. Both predict that nobody asserts "he's tall" of the guy in the middle, both predict we'll never locate the boundary, both predict tolerance inferences feel fine in practice. The only places they diverge are formal — which patterns of inference count as valid, what happens when you stack "definitely" on top of "definitely" — and metaphysical — whether there's a fact of the matter at all. So maybe they're answering different questions: one modelling what fixes reference, one modelling validity and what speakers are entitled to assert.

Two things break that story for me, though.

First: suppose you build something that has seen every use of "tall" there's ever been, can compare any two people's heights exactly, and has no working-memory limits. Could it know where the cut-off is? If yes, then story one's ignorance claim is a contingent, anthropological claim about creatures like us — a strange thing for a semantic theory to contain, and it means the dispute has a hidden empirical question sitting inside it. If no, then "we can never know" isn't about us at all, it's about the nature of meaning, and the fight with story two is squarely about whether meaning can determine a boundary no agent could in principle detect. I genuinely can't tell which one story one is committed to, and I suspect that ambiguity is doing alot of the work in keeping the debate alive.

Second: a guy I argue with about this floated a third option we started calling the drift view — there is a cut-off, but it isn't fixed; each new use of the word nudges it, so there's no fact about where it is now, only about where it was after some particular run of use. This felt like it dissolved the whole thing for about a week, until I noticed that the drift itself could be sharp or vague, and then it looked like I'd just relaunched the original argument with extra steps.

So what is this actually? A verbal dispute about what a semantic theory is for? A clash about what counts as data — whether a theory of meaning owes us an account of what it feels like to hit a borderline case, or only an account of validity and reference? A hidden empirical question about cognition in disguise? Or just two bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given whatever you think it is, what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a distinction I'm missing, an experiment with a non-human reasoner, changing the question, or nothing at all? Push back on my framing if my framing is the problem — I'd rather that than an answer to the question as I've set it up.
