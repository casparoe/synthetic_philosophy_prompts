The question as set:

(a) Explain the distinction between the primary (1-)intension and the secondary (2-)intension of an expression. Give the 1-intension and the 2-intension of the sentence "water = H2O", and say which of the two is supposed to track apriority and which metaphysical necessity. (Ignore indexicals: treat the primary intension as a function from worlds considered as actual.)

(b) Now consider a Twin Earth world — one where the clear, drinkable stuff in the lakes and taps has the chemical structure XYZ instead of H2O, and where "water" is used for that stuff. What does the framework say about "water = H2O" when that world is considered as actual? Why is this supposed to capture the intuition that water could have turned out not to be H2O, without going back on the Kripkean claim that the sentence is necessary?

(c) "The two-dimensionalist has only relocated the necessary a posteriori, not explained it." Explain this objection, and give one reply a two-dimensionalist could make.

(d) Suppose a chemist in 1885 introduces a new expression into English with the words: 'Let "glimmer" name whatever element in fact makes up more than half the mass of the core of the innermost planet of our solar system — whatever element that turns out to be.' Suppose it later turns out, empirically, that this element is iron. Is "glimmer = iron" necessary a posteriori, contingent a posteriori, or neither? Does it matter that the term was introduced by an explicit stipulation rather than being an old natural-kind word like "water"? Be explicit about what the two-dimensional apparatus does settle here and what it does not settle on its own.

The student's answer (copied out as written):

(a) An expression's two-dimensional intension is a function from pairs of worlds (w1, w2) — the world being taken as actual, and the world being evaluated — to extensions. The secondary intension of a sentence is what you get by fixing w1 at the actual world and letting w2 vary; that's just the ordinary Kripkean intension, the thing that is true or false at a counterfactual world. The primary intension is the diagonal: you vary a world and consider it as actual, i.e. you evaluate (w, w). For "water = H2O" the secondary intension is necessary, because once actuality is fixed "water" rigidly picks out H2O and H2O is H2O in every world. The primary intension is contingent: take Twin Earth as actual and "water" picks out XYZ, so the sentence is false there. The framework's idea is that apriority tracks necessity of the primary intension while metaphysical necessity tracks necessity of the secondary intension. That is how a sentence can be both necessary and a posteriori: necessary 2-intension, contingent 1-intension.

(b) At Twin Earth considered as actual, the primary intension of "water = H2O" is false, and that is why it can seem as though water might have turned out not to be H2O: the intuition is about the primary intension, about which scenario we might have turned out to be in. This doesn't go back on Kripke, because the primary intension is not being offered as the meaning of "water" in the descriptivist sense that Kripke argued against; it is only a function from scenarios-as-actual to extensions. The semantic content — what gets asserted and evaluated at counterfactual worlds — is the secondary intension.

(c) The objection is that the secondary intension of "water = H2O" is still necessary and still only knowable empirically, so the puzzle has been shifted into the secondary dimension rather than dissolved. A reply: the framework doesn't deny that, it predicts it. It tells you in advance which sentences will be necessary a posteriori — the ones with a contingent primary intension and a necessary secondary intension — and it explains why the a posteriori intuition arises (it tracks the primary intension) while the necessity claim concerns the secondary intension. Once you see that "necessary" and "a priori" were tracking two different intensions all along, there is no longer a paradox needing explanation. And since the primary intension is the diagonal of the matrix, the metaphysical facts here are fixed by the epistemic ones: the secondary intensions are definable from the primary intensions, so there aren't really two independent kinds of modality at all.

(d) "Glimmer = iron" is necessary a posteriori, the same shape as "water = H2O". The stipulation fixes the reference — the "whatever element that turns out to be" clause is a reference-fixing clause, not a definition — so "glimmer" is rigid and its secondary intension is necessary: iron is iron in every counterfactual world. But the sentence is a posteriori, since it took empirical work to find out what the core is mostly made of; take as actual a world whose innermost planet has a mostly nickel core and the primary intension is false. Notice that this verdict doesn't depend on whether the stipulation was meant rigidly or merely as a shorthand for the description "the element making up more than half the mass of the core of the innermost planet": either way the primary intension is contingent and the secondary intension is necessary, because once we hold the actual world fixed as actual the term picks out iron, and iron is iron in every world. So the framework gives the same verdict for stipulated terms as for ordinary natural-kind terms, which is just as well — otherwise the account would be at the mercy of how some speaker happened to introduce a word.
