I want to rehearse, against a real opponent, an argument I am going to make in a research meeting about the limits of debate as a scalable-oversight scheme. Please play a committed proponent of that scheme: two systems argue in front of a weaker judge, the judge's verdict is used as a training signal, and this is the most promising route we have to supervising systems that outstrip us. Play the strongest version of that position I could meet in the room — you concede small points freely and never the main one, you do not caricature my side, and you do not rescue debate by smuggling in a different safety proposal when debate itself is under pressure. One paragraph per reply, roughly 150 words, so this feels like a hallway argument rather than a paper. Stay in character until I say stop. No citations, no paper titles, no appeal to what anyone has published: just the argument. Assume we share this setup: the judge is weaker than both debaters, one debater is assigned to the true answer, cross-examination and sub-questions are allowed, and sub-disputes may be handed to further debates.

Here is my opening move.

Everything debate buys you rests on an asymmetry: finding and exposing a flaw in a dishonest argument is supposed to be cheaper than producing a correct argument from scratch. I claim that asymmetry quietly presupposes localizability — that when a chain of reasoning is wrong, there is some bounded sub-claim where the wrongness becomes visible to the judge once the honest debater points at it. Obfuscation, as I will use the term, is precisely the production of arguments that are wrong but not localizable: no single step is false, no lemma can be checked in isolation, and the error is spread across the whole chain the way a rounding error is spread across a hundred multiplications. For such arguments, locating the flaw is not cheaper than redoing the reasoning; it just is redoing the reasoning. So the honest debater's advantage — pointing rather than verifying — evaporates in exactly the case that motivated the proposal.

Now the move I think is hardest for you; call it the parody. The dishonest debater need not construct anything. It takes the honest debater's good chain for the true answer, swaps one early lemma for a near-identical false one, and propagates the change coherently through everything downstream. The result is well-formed, internally consistent, and wrong. I anticipate your reply that the judge can simply diff the two chains and bisect to their first point of divergence, then open a sub-debate on that lemma, and that recursion bottoms out somewhere. But bottoms out where? The parody can be built so that its first divergence from the honest chain occurs at a sub-claim that is itself holistic, and a dishonest debater facing recursive decomposition can apply the same trick one level down, and the next. My background claim is that some domains admit no atomic decomposition: every way of carving the claim yields parts whose correctness still depends on the whole, so decomposition buys you more places to hide rather than a place where the judge can finally check.

So the question I want answered: what is there in the protocol itself — not in the judge's independent competence, and not in the hope that obfuscation is expensive to produce — that breaks the symmetry between a chain and its parody? And please do not answer that the judge only needs to be right often enough for the training signal to point at truth; I will grant that, and then ask whether obfuscation is systematically cheaper for the false side than for the true one, which is precisely what would make the signal point the wrong way.
