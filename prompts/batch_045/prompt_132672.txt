For the better part of a decade the argument about "side effects" and impact measures has run in place, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on it.

The question is whether an agent can be penalized for side effects — changes to the world that its objective did not ask for and that we would disvalue — without first specifying, more or less completely, which states of the world are valuable. One camp says no: a side effect is just disvalue that failed to make it into the objective, and any penalty we can actually define will either be a value judgment under another name or a crude proxy that degrades precisely where it matters. The other camp says yes, or partly yes: define impact in terms of what an action does to the ability to achieve goals — the attainable-utility and relative-reachability families, empowerment- and option-value-style measures, penalties on divergence from a baseline policy's distribution over reachable states — and you get a quantity that provably tracks how much the world was changed, without ever taking a stand on which changes are bad.

The exchange I keep encountering has a fixed shape. The second camp produces a measure; the first observes that the measure's verdicts are sensitive to design choices that are value judgments in disguise; the second patches the particular counterexample; the first re-derives the objection one level up. As far as I can tell, nobody has been moved in either direction by this, and I want to know what kind of standoff it is.

Here is my own attempt to make the objection exact, which you should feel free to reject. First, I count four places where a value commitment can hide: the baseline policy (a no-op? what would have happened anyway? the principal's default behavior?); the goal set that the measure averages or ranges over, including how goals are represented; whose ability to achieve goals is being measured (the agent's, a reference agent's, the principal's under uncertainty); and the aggregation over goals, states, and time. Second, I think there is a substantive second-order value embedded in all of these measures, which their defenders rarely flag. Consider an agent told to secure a set of documents. It can lock the safe with the key, preserving the set of states anyone can later reach, or weld the safe shut, permanently destroying a large region of that set. Impact measures of the reachability kind prefer the key. But now suppose the safe contains evidence the principal is about to destroy, or that the goal the principal would in fact have had tomorrow is one they would repudiate on reflection. Then option preservation is not obviously the right thing, and the measure's preference is coming from a commitment — a neutrality among unidentified future goals, an open-future or option-value principle — and not from value-freeness. Third, I have a rough argument that the disagreement may be structurally unresolvable, which I'll call the squeeze. Let G be the set of goals the measure is defined relative to. If G is narrow — the objectives we already know about — then the penalty is a function of the objective we already specified and adds nothing beyond a reweighting of it: redundant. If G is broad — every goal some principal could have, or every computable goal — then the changes in reachable-state sets induced by any nontrivial action swamp the differences between the actions available, the penalty tends toward a constant across them, and it can no longer rank actions: degenerate. Only a narrow band of intermediate goal sets yields a useful penalty, and constructing that band is exactly the value judgment supposedly being avoided.

So my questions. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what "side effect" and "impact" mean, or two groups answering different questions under one name — one asking whether a formal quantity with certain invariance properties exists, the other asking whether penalizing it would reduce the risk from systems anyone will actually build? To the extent it is substantive, where does it live: in a factual or empirical bet (about what future systems will be like — whether they will be goal-directed at all, whether their objectives will be separable from the world's default dynamics, whether hand-designed penalties survive capability generalization), in a difference about what counts as a legitimate datum (gridworld demonstrations versus out-of-distribution behavior in deployment-like settings), or in a bedrock difference about which failure mode is worth insuring against? My own hypothesis is that the empirical and strategic disagreement is doing most of the work and the trading of measures is epiphenomenal, with perhaps a genuine verbal component layered on top; tell me if that is wrong and, if it is mixed, which component dominates.

Tell me also whether the squeeze argument is sound, whether it could be made into an actual formal result rather than a hand-wave about swamping, and whether establishing it would settle anything or would simply be absorbed as one more move in the cycle. Finally: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — the impossibility result, if it can be had; an empirical demonstration one way or the other; a reframing of the question into something like reversibility, corrigibility, or uncertainty over the principal's goals; or nothing at all, because the question is well-posed only in the middle range between a fully specified and an entirely unspecified value? I would rather have an honest argument for the last option, with reasons, than an optimistic research agenda.
