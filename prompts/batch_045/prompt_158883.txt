Write a sonnet on Newton's bucket argument in which the form itself does the arguing. The octave should stage the asymmetry the argument turns on: at the start the vessel spins and the water does not, the relative motion is at its greatest, and still the surface lies flat; by the end water and vessel turn together, there is no motion between them at all, and the surface has climbed the sides. So the concavity cannot be tracking motion relative to the bucket. Let the volta bring in the relationist reply: what does the work is rotation with respect to the rest of the matter there is, the fixed stars above all, not the sides of the vessel.

Then let a case of my own devising carry the sestet: annihilate the stars, and let the walls of the vessel dissolve as well, so that all that remains anywhere is a single sphere of water, turning. Ask in the poem whether that water still swells at its equator into an oblate lens, or whether, with nothing left to turn against, it must hang exactly round. The closing couplet should state what each side is committed to here without deciding between them: on one reading the bulge survives, because there is still something for the water to rotate with respect to even when nothing else exists; on the other, a lone sphere has no rotation to have, and a bulge with nothing to be bulged relative to has not yet been given a sense. Do not name Newton or either of the two positions anywhere in the poem. A reader who knows the debate should be able to tell which position each half of the poem is serving, and a reader who does not should still take it as a poem about a vessel of water.
