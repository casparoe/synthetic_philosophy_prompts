I'm a retired hospital chaplain, and four years ago our college's board of trustees put me on its new Committee on Archives and Digital Access — I suspect because I was the only person willing to say out loud that some of what we're being asked to approve is about dead people rather than about documents. The case now in front of us: the college holds the literary estate of the writer Miriam Hale, who taught here for a single year in the 1980s, liked the place, and left us her papers when she died in 2019 at seventy-one. The archive is unusually rich — roughly forty years of diaries, something like twelve thousand letters, several unfinished manuscripts, and several hundred hours of recorded interviews and public readings. A small company that builds what it calls constrained persona systems has offered, at a donor's expense, to build a chat interface over the whole collection, and it has proposed two modes. In Reading Room mode the system answers only with sentences that occur verbatim in the papers, each one footnoted to its source and date, and when nothing in the archive answers it declines by displaying a line from her 1994 diary to the effect that she has no idea what to say to that. In Correspondence mode it speaks in the first person, may combine and reword, may answer questions she was never asked and respond to things that happened after her death, and displays a standing banner saying that it is a model and not her. Our IT office has told us flatly that the second mode is the only one anyone will actually use, and that the first is a curiosity. Her niece, who is her literary executor, supports the whole project and has written that she would find it a comfort to be able to ask her aunt a question. Her son has written twice to say his mother would have loathed it, and there is a transcript of a 2016 interview in which, asked whether she would like to be reconstructed in some form after her death, she laughed and said, more or less, good God no, let the dead stay dead, and let people read the books if they want me.

We are split three and three, and it has fallen to me to draft the memo we will circulate before next month's meeting. What I keep circling around is something I read years ago in a philosophical dialogue — a man imagines conversing with Einstein's brain, kept alive in a jar, and is then pressed on whether a book thick enough, one containing an answer to every question the man might ask, would itself be a person, or would merely seem to be. I've never got that out of my head, and it is why I don't think we are deciding a question about software. My own half-formed thought, which I can't finish, is this: a genuine conversational partner can be wrong in ways its makers never intended — it can misunderstand you, lose patience, say that it doesn't know, change the subject, refuse to answer — whereas a database can only be wrong by accident, and the better our system gets at being a partner, the more it will have to be capable of exactly those failures. That makes me unsure which design is the safer one. It also makes me unsure whether it is a point in favour of Reading Room mode that every sentence really is hers, or a point against it: her words are on offer with nobody left who means them, and a footnote to a diary page tells you where a sentence came from but not who is speaking.

So please draft the memo. I need a definite recommendation — authorise one mode, or both, or neither — with a time frame attached (an outright no, a one-year pilot under named conditions, or something else, and in any case a date at which we revisit the decision), and with reasons our IT office, her executor and her son could each actually engage with rather than being told they have misunderstood the metaphysics. Any conditions should be the sort of thing someone could check: something the vendor can be held to, something we can verify before launch, something that would trigger shutting a mode down. Give the strongest version of the losing side's case, not a polite nod to it — I want the people who disagree with the recommendation to recognise their own argument in the memo and think it has been stated better than they stated it themselves. Please write it in plain words: no names of philosophers, no schools of thought, no references of any kind, since two of our members stop reading the moment a name is dropped and the rest of us have no way to check a citation anyway; if some famous argument bears on this, describe it in a sentence. And at the end, add a short passage I can read aloud at the meeting saying where the memo's argument is most likely to be wrong: what we do not know, what new fact about the archive or about how people actually use the thing should make us reverse course, and which of the memo's own claims you would abandon first.
