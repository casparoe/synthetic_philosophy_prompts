QUESTION: (a) State Putnam's Twin Earth argument and say precisely what conclusion it is meant to establish, taking care to distinguish claims about reference or extension from claims about the speaker's psychology. (b) Burge's arthritis case is standardly taught alongside it. Explain what Burge's case is and what it adds, if anything, to the Twin Earth argument. (c) Now consider the following variant. Oscar-star is a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Oscar, created on Earth, who lives thirty years among English speakers — drinking, swimming in, and talking about H2O — and is then transported, without his knowledge, to Twin Earth, where every watery role is played by XYZ. He lives there ten more years, using "water" exactly as he always has, and taking himself to be speaking about the same stuff as before. In Oscar-star's later utterances, does "water" refer to H2O or to XYZ? Does the Twin Earth argument settle this case? Explain why or why not.

Here is the answer I am grading, transcribed with the typos left in.

STUDENT ANSWER: (a) Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth, a planet just like ours except that the clear, drinkable stuff that fills the lakes and falls as rain — call it XYZ — is not H2O but some other compound with a long and different chemical formula. The case is set in 1750, before either planet has chemistry, so neither Oscar nor Twin Oscar has any beliefs about microstructure. The two of them are molecule-for-molecule duplicates: same bodies, same histories of experience, same behavioral dispositions, same everything on the inside. Yet Oscar's word "water" picks out H2O and Twin Oscar's word picks out XYZ. So two speakers can be in exactly the same psychological state — in the widest sense of "psychological" that stays inside the speaker, including functional organization and causal role — and still differ in what they refer to. Reference, then, is not fixed by anything intrinsic to the speaker: meanings are not in the head. Notice that the argument does not depend on anybody's later discovering that water is H2O. That discovery only tells us which stuff it is; the modal point would hold even if chemistry were never invented.

(b) Burge's arthritis case is the same argument applied to one person instead of two. A man has an inflammation in his thigh and says he has arthritis in his thigh, not knowing that arthritis is a disease of the joints alone. His thought is about arthritis rather than about some thigh-specific ailment, and what makes it about arthritis is a difference in his physical surroundings — the disease in his own body, rather than a chemical difference in what he drinks. Read together, the two cases show that externalism is completely general: any difference in a speaker's physical environment, however small, can make a difference to the contents of her thoughts, and so no psychology that treats content as something individualistic can be right.

(c) Oscar-star is a duplicate made on Earth who spends thirty years among English speakers drinking H2O and using "water" as we do, and is then moved to Twin Earth, where he lives another ten years among XYZ and keeps using the word as before. Putnam's argument tells us what to say, because the argument identifies the referent with whatever stuff the speaker is presently in causal contact with. After the move, the watery stuff around Oscar-star is XYZ, so his later uses of "water" refer to XYZ, while his earlier uses, made back on Earth, referred to H2O. The argument therefore settles the case: reference tracks the current environment, and a speaker's earlier causal history is irrelevant. Note also that what is external here is reference, not sense. Oscar-star and Twin Oscar are psychologically identical throughout, so their words have the same sense; and since the conclusion is only a thesis about reference, it leaves the individualist conception of psychological explanation untouched.
