In the reading group I run on AI governance, one move gets made almost every week. Someone describes the situation — a handful of frontier labs, each able to accelerate by spending more compute, hiring more aggressively, or shipping with less internal caution; large first-mover advantages; no body with global jurisdiction; only partial national regulation and export controls — and then says: this is just a prisoner's dilemma, or equivalently a tragedy of the commons. The conclusion is then treated as following more or less immediately: nothing short of an enforcer with real punitive power can help, and in the absence of a credible global enforcer we should expect the dynamic to run to its bad end and plan accordingly. In these circles the dynamic also has a name, Moloch, and I have started to suspect that the name arrives with the remedy and the pessimism already attached to it, doing argumentative work that nobody has checked.

Before we build anything on this, I want the analogy audited properly, and I want the audit done on the specific case rather than on race to the bottom in the abstract.

Please give me, first, the mapping made explicit — a correspondence table is fine: who the players are, what counts as a strategy, what plays the role of cooperation and what plays the role of defection, what the payoff ordering is supposed to be and where those payoffs are supposed to come from, what plays the role of the commons, what plays the role of the shadow of the future if the game is iterated, and — since the name is doing work too — what plays the role of Moloch. Where the mapping cannot be filled in without stipulation, say so rather than stipulating silently.

Then the places it breaks. Four worry me, and I would like each assessed both for whether it is real and for whether it matters for the use the analogy is being put to.

The first is what I have been calling the missing defector. In the prisoner's dilemma the engine is a dominant strategy: whatever the other does, defecting is better, so two rational agents land somewhere both regret. But in the case as described it is not clear that anyone is defecting. There may be no discrete cooperative move available to a lab at all — only a continuous margin of investment and caution, where slowing down means losing position to whoever does not. If defection is not an option anyone declines to take, the central mechanism of the dilemma is not what is generating the outcome.

The second is a distinction I want tested rather than assumed: traps that operate within a decision versus traps that operate across a population of agents by differential survival, growth, or replication. The prisoner's dilemma is a model of the first kind — fixed agents choosing among strategies. A great deal of what we call a race may be of the second kind: nobody chooses to be outcompeted, the labs that keep pace persist and the cautious ones do not, and the bad outcome is produced by selection over agents rather than by choice within one. If the case is largely of the second kind, then punishment-and-enforcement remedies address the wrong mechanism, and the unit of intervention is not the agent's choice but the gradient that decides which agents persist. I want to know whether that distinction survives scrutiny, and if it does, what it implies about remedies.

The third is the shape of the stakes. The prisoner's dilemma is symmetric and its payoff matrix is common knowledge. Here the actors differ enormously in resources, in how much they stand to gain from being first, and in how uncertain they are about what the technology will do; and some of the relevant harms have the structure of a one-taker curse, where an action need only be taken once by one actor to impose the risk on everyone. If that is right, the situation may be closer to a different game entirely than to a dilemma, and the remedy implied by the different game may not be an enforcer at all.

The fourth is recursion. The suggested remedy is a sovereign, but the sovereign is itself a player: states compete, regulators are captured, and any enforcement layer faces its own version of the same dynamic at the level above. I want to know whether the analogy has quietly imported its own solution by picturing the trap as a commons with a herder — an agent with intentions, standing outside the game — when the whole point was meant to be that no one is running it.

Finally, the verdict on the conclusions. Does the claim that only a punitive enforcer can help survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? Does the pessimism survive, or does it depend on assuming the mechanism is a dilemma when it may be something else? Which of the four breaks above are load-bearing and which are cosmetic? And if the prisoner's dilemma is the wrong source domain, is there a better analogy — drawn from evolutionary dynamics, arms races, or some other well-understood structure — that keeps whatever is genuinely right about the Moloch framing while licensing a more defensible conclusion about what to do?
