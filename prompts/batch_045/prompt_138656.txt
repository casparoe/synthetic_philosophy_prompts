I teach the rational-choice half of an upper-level undergraduate decision theory seminar: 20 students, one 75-minute slot, one instructor and no teaching assistant, a bingo cage, a forty-pound prize budget, and a projector I would rather not switch on. They can compute an expected value and have seen the expected-utility theorem stated; they have not met the Allais paradox or the independence axiom, and I do not want either one named until their choices are locked in and paid out.

What I want is a single in-class activity in which they make real decisions under real (if small) stakes and run into this on their own: their preference between two options flips depending on an outcome that is exactly the same whichever of the two they pick. They should come away having felt the flip before it is described to them, and having discovered that the explanations they reach for at the moment of choice — the special pull of an option that involves no risk at all, the wish not to be the person who threw away a certainty and then watched the wheel land badly — come apart when the thing they were certain about was never going to be theirs. No lecture up front, no expected value worked out on the board before anybody has chosen, and if possible no opening for a student to name the paradox early and deflate the room.

Two things strike me as the hard parts, and I want them solved rather than gestured at.

First, scale. The pattern is normally produced with figures on the order of a guaranteed million against a ten-percent chance of five million, and I have forty pounds. If I simply shrink the numbers I expect the whole effect to wash out and the session to come up null. Tell me how you would get the relevant psychology with real stakes on that budget: a currency the students actually care about, a loss frame, a random multiplier, something consumed on the spot, a raffle over a prize they picked themselves at the start of term. Or tell me plainly that chasing real stakes is the wrong instinct here, and say what you would put in their hands instead and what the hypothetical version buys that a paid one cannot.

Second, disguise. If both decisions sit on the same sheet in the same figures, a good half of the room will spot the relationship and edit themselves into consistency, and I will have taught them nothing except that they were being watched. So: how are the two decisions separated in time or buried among filler, what cover story sits on each, what the filler items are, and how do I stop one student computing aloud from anchoring everyone else.

Here is a variant I have been turning over and cannot settle, and I want it either built into the design or explained to me as broken. In the standard pair, the branch that comes out the same under both options pays the chooser: in one decision it is a large sum under either option, in the other it is nothing under either. Suppose that in the first decision that common branch instead pays a third party, a named charity or another student in the room, while the contested branch still pays the chooser. As far as the chooser's own money goes, that first decision has then become the same problem as the second one. A student who takes the safe option in the first decision and the gamble in the second has, it seems to me, answered the same question about their own payoffs in two different ways, and the stories about certainty and regret are no longer available to them, because the sum they were supposed to be certain of was never going to reach them. I find this more uncomfortable than the original pair, but I am not confident it is sound. I can also see the obvious objection, that routing the common branch to someone else drags in altruism or rivalry and mangles the comparison, so deal with that directly. Tell me what the variant would show if it worked, what it would show if it did not, whether it belongs alongside the classic pairs or in place of one of them, and what I should do if nobody in the room flips. A colleague also suggested spinning the wheel in two stages, first revealing which block the student is in and only then resolving the prize, so that they confront the contested block on its own; if that adds nothing the third-party version does not already give me, say so and leave it out.

I also need the session to survive the fact that only some of the room will produce the classic pattern. Give me a short script for each of the four possible pairs of answers, safe-then-gamble, gamble-then-gamble, safe-then-safe, gamble-then-safe, marking which are consistent with the axiom and which are not, and what I say to the consistent students so that the hour is not spent only on the inconsistent ones.

Then the debrief. Map particular moments and particular things students will have done onto the parts of the idea: the branch that is common to both options, the invariance that branch is supposed to have, the difference between a preference that violates an axiom and a preference that merely depends on how the option was described, and the further step from either of those to any verdict about rationality. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I am most worried about students not believing the payouts will be honoured, about a one-percent chance of nothing being read as no chance at all, and about the session collapsing into a show of hands about whether risk aversion is irrational — and tell me how to handle each. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: which feature of the axiom has to be supplied in words by me because no game of this shape can stage it, and where the route from they flipped to they are being irrational is most likely to break.

No citations, no reading list, no suggestions for further reading. I want the exact wording of what goes on the cards and what I say out loud, the rules and the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and something I can run on Thursday.

One last thing. At the end, tell me what would make you withdraw this design. Which part of it are you least confident about, what would you expect to observe in the room if that part fails, and which step in the argument the activity is meant to make vivid do you take to be the weakest, the one a good student could reject on the way out of the door and leave the session unpersuaded?
