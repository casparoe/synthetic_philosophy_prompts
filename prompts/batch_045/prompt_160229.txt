You are acting as the rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration in rational choice theory. Assume research-level command of decision theory, the theory of rationality, and the literature on self-defeating and paradoxically profitable irrationality. Work carefully and in stages: begin with a short private brainstorming section of ten to fifteen bullets in which you list candidate shared claims, mark each keep or drop with a one-line reason, and flag any dispute you suspect to be merely verbal. Only then draft the deliverable. No citations, footnotes, or bibliography are wanted; the document must be self-contained and intelligible to a reader with no prior exposure to the debate, and it must not depend on any empirical or historical claim that could not be checked from the armchair.

The case, stated neutrally. A stranded motorist is offered a ride into town by a stranger who is to be paid on arrival. Once in town the passenger has no incentive to pay. The driver knows this, is a reliable reader of character, and offers the ride only if she is confident that the passenger is the sort of person who pays. So the passenger gets the ride only if he is disposed to pay, while paying, once in town, does nothing to advance his aims by its own efficacy. Call this the Base Case. Both camps accept that the passenger benefits from being the sort of person who pays, and that the act of paying, considered only as a cause of its own immediate consequences, is contrary to his aims.

The two camps, characterised by their claims rather than by names.

Camp D (disposition-first) holds that rationality is primarily a property of standing dispositions, traits, policies, or characters, and only derivatively of individual acts. A disposition can be rational while its exercise on a particular occasion is not. The Base Case is therefore a genuine case of rationally required irrationality at the level of the act: what the passenger has most reason to do is to be such that he would pay, and there is no further requirement that the resulting act be rational when it comes.

Camp R (decision-theory-revision) holds that rationality is primarily a property of acts under a choice rule, and that the Base Case shows only that the wrong choice rule has been assumed. Because the driver's prediction tracks what the passenger's act indicates about his disposition, the correct rule ranks paying above not paying, and the act of paying is straightforwardly rational. On this view nothing is both rationally required and irrational, and the appearance of rational irrationality is an artifact of ranking acts by their own consequences alone.

The deliverable is the joint statement both camps could sign. Draft it in four parts.

Part 1: a numbered section of eight to twelve agreed claims. These must not be vacuous; a claim belongs here only if some reasonable participant in this debate has denied it or would find it informative. Include at least: what the Base Case's causal and informational structure is; what both camps concede about the act of paying considered on its own; the point that both camps are offering a recommendation to the same agent at the same time, not describing different agents; and, if you can formulate one, a shared constraint on any adequate account, which you should state as the Amortization Condition: a disposition counts as rational only if the gains it secures across the range of occasions on which it operates exceed what it costs to have and to exercise, so that a disposition exercised exactly once and never again is not automatically vindicated.

Part 2: a table of the residual disagreements. For each, give the issue in neutral terms, then Camp D's formulation and Camp R's formulation, each phrased so that the camp in question would sign it and not complain that its position has been weakened into something the other side already accepts. Add a column recording whether you judge the disagreement substantive or verbal, with a one-line diagnosis for each verbal case.

Part 3: a crux table. For each crux, name the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp, then specify the case, argument, or parameter variation both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the outcome will be, phrased so each would recognise the prediction as its own and so that a miss could be identified later. Use at least these materials.

- The Trace-Reader variant: the driver has no access to the passenger's disposition itself and predicts only from accumulated behavioural traces of past behaviour in relevantly similar situations. Ask whether the two camps' recommendations diverge here, and in particular whether Camp D's story collapses into an ordinary forward-looking story about reputation.
- The Costly-Self-Binding variant: before the ride the passenger may install, at a known cost, a device that makes non-payment impossible, and the driver observes whether it has been installed. Ask whether either camp can treat this as the real solution, and what that would show about the Base Case.
- The Serial-Rider variant: the passenger faces the same structure many times, and the driver in each instance reads only the record of what happened on previous occasions. Use this to test the Amortization Condition and to separate disposition-based from record-based accounts.
- The Advice Test: fix what each camp would say to the passenger at the moment of decision in town, and separately what each would say to him before the ride is offered. Ask whether the camps differ in either piece of advice; if they differ only in the retrospective explanation and not in the advice, say so and draw the consequence for how substantive the dispute is.
- The Clean-Acquisition question: by what route does a passenger who is not disposed to pay become one? Ask whether either camp requires that the route to the disposition not itself involve an irrational step, what happens if no such route is available to this agent, and whether the inability to acquire the disposition on demand undercuts Camp D's claim that he ought to have it.
- The No-Predictor question: whether the mechanism that does the work in the Base Case is essentially the presence of someone who reads you, or whether the same structure appears in cases with no external reader at all self-command, emotions that make threats credible, personal rules, the strategic value of being unable to be dissuaded. Record whether the camps' verdicts track each other across these cases, and what divergence would show.

Part 4: a short closing section, signed by both, stating what each camp would count as a genuine cost or embarrassment for its own view, and naming the one further development the two agree would actually move the debate rather than restate it.

Throughout, keep the formulations honest: do not resolve a disagreement by stipulating a definition one side rejects, and do not manufacture agreement by making a claim so thin that neither side would bother to deny it. Where the two camps turn out to share a recommendation and differ only in the vocabulary used to describe it, say plainly which part of the dispute that is and why.
