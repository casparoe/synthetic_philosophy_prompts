I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I'm in a small monthly reading group where we take one paper a month and argue about it. This month we did the extended mind — the famous Otto-with-a-notebook case and the claim that under the right conditions something outside your skull can literally be part of your mind, so that what is written in the notebook is a belief or memory of yours rather than merely something you read. I disagreed with almost everyone in the room, but when I tried afterwards to write down the rule I was implicitly applying, I couldn't find one, and I've come to suspect I have two different rules that I switch between depending on which case I'm looking at. Two ground rules before the cases: please don't give me citations, bibliographies, or a survey of who said what — we have that side covered, and I want the argument rather than the sources. And please define any term of art in plain language, because I'm the least read person in the group.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can state them. (1) The notes file on my laptop that I've been appending to for years, that I consult automatically, and that has never once been wrong: yes, that is part of my memory. (2) My phone's contact list, for numbers I used to know by heart: yes, same thing. (3) My calendar, which pushes an alert that makes me leave for the airport: yes, but it feels like a slightly different kind of yes than the first two — it's about what I'm going to do rather than what was the case. (4) My password manager, whose entries I can only read after a fingerprint scan and none of which I could recite: I said yes in the meeting, my friend said no, and I've since become unsure. (5) A notes app that now runs an on-device model which rewrites, reorders, and summarizes my entries and occasionally inserts a sentence I never wrote, which I usually leave in: I said no, but I couldn't explain why a model's contribution differs in kind from a friend correcting my notebook. (6) A long chat thread with a language model from six weeks ago — I remember neither what I typed nor what came back, but I scroll it and treat the conclusions in it as moves I made: I genuinely go back and forth, and this is the case I most want a ruling on. (7) The CI pipeline: the build goes red and a bot tells me my change broke it. I said no, that's a tool and an alert. My friend says it's exactly the notebook — a standing state about how things are, coupled to me, that I act on without re-deriving. (8) A team runbook in a wiki, edited by twelve people, which I rely on and believe: yes-ish, but not in the way the notebook is, and I can't say what the difference is. (9) An append-only audit log I maintain for compliance and have literally never read back: no, and I'm confident. (10) A flaky test suite I don't trust, whose red I always re-run before shipping, and whose verdict reliably changes what I do: I said no, and I think that's right, but it embarrasses any test phrased in terms of what I automatically consult and act on. (11) Inline code completion — the IDE suggests a function body, I read it, understand it, and accept it: I said no, it wasn't my thought until I read it, but I'm not confident, and I notice I don't say the same thing about a sentence I dictate to a transcription tool.

The rough principle I came up with on the walk home was this: an external state counts as one of my mental states when (a) it is reliably available when I need it, (b) I go to it automatically rather than deciding to, (c) I would endorse it if I reflected, and (d) I put it there or can change it. I think clause (d) is doing the work in cases 5, 7, 8 and 11, and I suspect it is smuggling in an ownership intuition rather than a cognitive one. Please take that as a hypothesis to be tested, repaired, or replaced rather than as something to defend.
