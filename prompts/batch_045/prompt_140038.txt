I need one essay question for the take-home final in my philosophy of biology seminar (upper-level undergrads plus two MA students, 48 hours, 1500 word cap, this is one of two questions they choose between). The topic is the whole 'is DNA really a code / is genetic information literally information or just a metaphor' business, and I want something that can't be answered by lining up the three stock positions they've all read about and then pattern-matching. You know the three: the deflationary causal or correlational one where 'information' just means reliable statistical dependence between sequence and product; the selected-function or instructional one where the sequence counts as a representation because there's a consumer mechanism whose job is to read it; and the parity or causal-democracy one where the sequence is just one developmental cause among many and code talk smuggles in a privilege it hasn't earned.

So my plan is to hand them two or three invented inheritance systems with features that pull in different directions, so they have to work out which feature was actually load-bearing in each of those arguments. Rough sketches, which I'd like you to tighten up into something they can't wriggle out of on an ambiguity:

System K. A linear molecule built from four kinds of unit. It gets read in non-overlapping groups of three by a molecular machine, and each group causes the addition of one unit drawn from a set of twenty. Nothing in the chemistry dictates which of the twenty goes with which group of three — the correspondence could have been laid out differently and everything would still have worked. The correspondence is the same in every cell and at every life stage, and hasn't changed in a very long time. It isn't random though: it's arranged so that the commonest copying and reading mistakes either make no difference or swap in a chemically similar unit.

System W. Same molecule, same kind of machine, but three differences. (i) Which of the twenty a given group of three yields depends on which adapter molecules are in the cell at the time; different tissues carry different adapter sets, and the set can shift within an individual's lifetime in response to an environmental cue. (ii) The adapter set in the egg reflects the mother's environment, and an induced shift typically lasts two or three generations and then fades. (iii) The shifts aren't random with respect to fitness: the new readings tend to yield units that are useful in the sort of environment that triggered them.

System L. No linear read-out at all. What's transmitted is a pattern of chemical marks along the molecule, put in place by enzymes whose activity depends on which marks are already there rather than on the sequence underneath. The sequence is identical in both variants and doesn't predict which pattern you get. The pattern is transmitted with high fidelity across many generations and reliably switches the organism between two distinct adaptive phenotypes; wiping it and putting the other pattern in its place is possible and is heritable.

I'm telling the students explicitly that these are invented, that no marks accrue for dragging in facts about real biology (I know real life has things a bit like each of them, and I don't want them winning on that), and that naming papers earns nothing. Marks only for what they do with the cases.

The question I have in mind has three parts. K is mostly there as the control, so part (a) is short: for each of W and L, say whether you'd want to say it literally carries genetic information and whether there's a code there. Part (b) is where most of the thinking is, on W specifically: is the sequence the message and the adapter set the codebook, or is that the wrong way to carve it? What, if anything, makes one particular reading the correct one rather than a misreading? And if the codebook is itself transmitted, does that set off a regress of codebooks — and is the regress vicious? Part (c) is the synthesis: which of the features in play — arbitrariness of the correspondence, existence of a reader with the selected job of reading, combinatorial productivity, high-fidelity transmission of an adaptively relevant difference, the possibility of error — was actually doing the work in the arguments for taking genetic-information talk seriously, and what follows from that about whether the debate was ever really about DNA at all?

What I want back from you: the question itself with the system descriptions worded tightly, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with the marks for each and a one-line note on how people usually get each one wrong. The bit I care most about is that the scheme has to actually seperate an A from a B. For me a B is an answer that applies the three positions to the cases correctly but mechanically and never works out what was load-bearing; an A does something more, and I want the rubric to name exactly what that more is. Please dont let the model answer read like a literature survey either — it should be the answer I'd want from a student who'd never heard of any of this and just had to think.
