Hi — you're an expert here, so I'm trusting you: a professional philosopher of history who also understands how a newsroom actually works. Think carefully and go slowly, and before you write anything, spend some time brainstorming the failure modes — please don't just recite the standard summary of historical revisionism versus denialism, I can get that myself and it wouldn't help me on deadline.

I'm a journalist. I write a column and the occasional long feature on science, ideas, and how both get misused in public, and maybe three or four times a year I get pulled into history fights instead: a politician, a pundit, a professor or a textbook board says something about a mass atrocity — the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, Nanjing, those kinds of cases — and I have to decide what word to use for what they're doing, in print, about a day after I start. My news editor has to sign off, our lawyer occasionally glances at things when the stakes are high, and I am not a historian. So what I want from you is a decision procedure, not an essay.

The deliverable: an ordered set of questions I can run against a person's actual output — a speech, a book, a thread, a textbook paragraph — that lands me on one of a small number of verdicts, with real stop rules. Where does the procedure stop and tell me to write that this is denial? Where does it stop and tell me it isn't? And, importantly, where does it stop and say: this remaining question is a values call or a legal call and has to go to a human — my editor, our lawyer, an actual specialist I can phone?

Constraints it has to survive:

- I can't read minds and I'm not going to print an accusation about someones motives. If a step depends on guessing what the person really believes or wants, mark it as a step I'm not allowed to run.
- I'm working from a desk. Assume I can read what they wrote, find their other public writing, and get a rough sense of whether specialists accept the claims — but not much more than that.
- It has to be runnable in a few hours by a generalist, and defensible to my editor.
- Each verdict needs wording guidance: what do I actually write when the answer is "not denial but badly wrong", or "structurally denialist but I shouldn't use that word on this occasion".

Hard cases it has to handle. These are composites I made up, not real people:

1. The number fighter. They accept the event happened and that it was a crime, but argue the death toll was a fraction of the usual figure — a fifth of it, say — using old census or demographic records. Sometimes this is a credentialed historian publishing in peer-reviewed venues, sometimes it's a retired accountant with a website. What in the procedure separates those, and at what point does the number question have to be handed to a specialist because I simply cannot evaluate the demography?

2. What I've started privately calling denial by subtraction. Nothing is denied. The deaths happened, the camps or the death marches or the massacres are real. What disappears is the thing that made it an atrocity: the deaths get re-described as disease, chaos, famine, the fog of war, a communications breakdown; the perpetrators become people who failed to prevent something rather than people who did it. There's a sibling move where the same body count gets reassigned to a different cause or a different author, so the arithmetic is untouched but the responsibility dissolves. I need the procedure to catch these without me having to establish a state of mind.

3. The legal-category dodge. I don't deny a thing, I just think genocide is a narrow legal term from a treaty definition written in the late 1940s, and applying it to this case is retroactive law-making. Is that denial, is it a genuinely arguable claim about how far a concept extends, or is it a third thing that needs its own verdict?

4. The state that owns the word. Sometimes there's a law criminalizing denial of a particular atrocity and the government uses it against anyone who departs from the official account, credentialed scholars included. Sometimes it's the reverse: a government denies an atrocity its own predecessor committed and calls the historians liars. I do not want a procedure that defers to whoever has the law on their side, or to whoever my readers happen to sympathize with. What replaces that deference?

5. The merely-contrarian case, where someone is bad at history or addicted to being the only person who sees through things, with no ideological stake — and then the uncomfortable one: someone broadly on my side of a fight doing something structurally identical to case 2 about an atrocity committed by the side they don't like. If my procedure doesn't bite on my own side, it's worthless, so tell me whether it does.

One test I've found useful and want built in: an asymmetry check — does this person apply anything like the same ferocious standard of proof to atrocities committed by their own nation, party or side as they apply to the one they're attacking? That seems like the most reliable evidence I can actually gather from a desk. But be honest with me about how much weight it can bear and what it misfires on.

Also please:

- Run the finished procedure end-to-end on two of the composites above, including at least one where the verdict it produces is one I won't like. I'd rather recieve that news now than in a correction.
- Flag which questions are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. My editor will ask.
- Finish with what the procedure cannot do: the situations where it genuinely can't help and I should stop trying to mechanize this and just make a call.

Take your time, think it through properly, and be blunt where I'm being naive.
