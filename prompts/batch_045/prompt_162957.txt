I'm working on a piece about collective intelligence in social insects and I keep tripping over the Aunt Hillary bit from Hofstadter — the ant colony you're supposedly able to have a conversation with through a hole in a tree. My editor has circled every instance of "the colony decides" in my draft and written "does it??" in the margin, and honestly I've read the Ant Fugue twice now and im still not sure whether I'm being sold a metaphor with a pulse. I don't need citations or a reading list (I have a browser, and I'm not quoting you by name anyway); I want a number and I want to see the machinery that produces it.

Here's the proposition, stated as carefully as I can: take a mature colony of a few thousand ants of an ordinary species, every individual tagged and tracked so that we have the full interaction network. Consider the claim that there is a system composed of those ants whose states play the belief and desire roles — that it is literally true, and not merely a convenient façon de parler, that the colony believes the feeder on the east branch has gone dry and wants more protein, and that attributing those states is genuinely the best way to predict and explain what the colony does next. Give me your credence in that claim, for that colony, not colonies-in-general and not some engineered future system. Then decompose it, with a number and one line of justification per term and then the combination: how much rides on whether some functionalist or interpretationist account of the intentional is correct in the first place; conditional on that, whether the colony's dynamics actually instantiate the right functional organization or whether the honest model is a distributed optimization process with no unified state anywhere; whether the individuation conditions are met at all — is there a system with the right kind of causal integration, boundary and persistence, given that ants live about a year and colonies live for decades while the things we compare them to (neurons firing, thoughts occurring) sit at wildly different timescales, and whether that mismatch helps or hurts; whether the conversational test — you can talk to Aunt Hillary — is real evidence or just a stipulation dressed up as evidence; and whether the fact that the parts have divergent interests matters, since ant colonies have queen-worker and relatedness conflicts where brains, whatever else is true of them, have neurons with no interests of their own. My own guess is somewhere around 0.4 but I don't trust it because I can't tell which of those terms is doing the work.

The thing that actually got me stuck, and which you should treat as my stipulation and argue with if you want: suppose the colony turns out to have exactly one slow internal variable — a shared material store of liquid food that individuals both read from and write to, so that there is literally a single number the whole colony is tracking and updating, and everything else is just individuals bumping into each other and picking up cues. Does that raise your number or lower it? My gut says raise, the philosopher of mind I interviewed says it makes the colony look like a thermostat with extra steps, and she put the whole proposition at 0.2 while the myrmecologist I talked to put it at 0.8. So: your number, the decomposition, which term your number is most sensitive to, where my two interviewees most likely disagree, and what single piece of evidence would move you most — for instance, if someone handed you the complete lifetime interaction network for that colony, would that settle it, or would the disagreement still be sitting there untouched?
