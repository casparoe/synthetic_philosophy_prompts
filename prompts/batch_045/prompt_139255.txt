The context: I'm in a small reading group on Burke and Oakeshott with two friends — one of them a fairly hard-nosed effective altruist who thinks Chesterton's fence, as popularly used, is just status quo bias wearing a tweed jacket. We started from a real case at our own college, which requires junior fellows to dine in hall a fixed number of times each term; nobody can explain why, the Fellows' minutes from the relevant decade were destroyed in a flood, and a reforming faction wants the requirement deleted from the statutes. When I invoked Chesterton, my friend produced the following argument, which I think proves too much but which I cannot dismantle step by step. He is using Chesterton's version from The Thing (1929), the chapter on the drift from domesticity, where the more intelligent reformer says: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it," and where Chesterton goes on to say that "nobody has any business to destroy a social institution until he has really seen it as an historical institution," adding that "If he knows how it arose, and what purposes it was supposed to serve, he may really be able to say that they were bad purposes, or that they have since become bad purposes, or that they are purposes which are no longer served."

His reconstruction, with my own label for the key notion: call a practice *countervailing* when its point is to offset, buffer, compensate for, or mesh with some other practice or institution — a tax allowance whose function is to blunt a regulation, an administrative formality whose function is to slow down a process that another rule makes dangerously fast, a dining requirement whose function may have been to offset the isolation created by a now-abolished tutorial system. Then: (1) Chesterton's principle: you may not remove an institution unless you know what it is for, in the strong historical sense — how it arose and what purposes it was supposed to serve. (2) Most long-standing practices are at least partly countervailing, because practices co-adapt to one another over time. (3) If a practice is countervailing, you cannot know what it is for without knowing what it is a response to, i.e. without knowing the point of the practices it offsets. (4) So you may not remove any institution until you understand the institutions it countervails. (5) Those institutions are themselves fences; by (1)–(3) they may not be removed until understood, and understanding *them* requires understanding theirs, and so on. Since social practices are holistically interdependent, the understanding the principle demands is total: the whole inherited order, its history, and the practical knowledge deposited in it — Burke's stock of the species, Oakeshott's practical as opposed to technical knowledge. (6) Nobody has that. So by (1) nobody may ever remove anything: the principle is a blanket prohibition on reform. (7) But it plainly is not meant to be that: Chesterton explicitly allows destruction once you see the use, and Burkeans themselves want reforms — repeals, restorations, the undoing of what they regard as recent deformations. (8) So the principle is either a prohibition no one can ever satisfy — in which case it is action-guiding only by paralysing, and my friend says it is really just inertia rationalised — or it collapses into something weaker whose bite is unclear.

Where exactly does this go wrong, if it does? I want more than "holism doesn't require total understanding": I want to know which step carries the weight, why that step looked unobjectionable, and what each of the available repairs costs. If I have to choose, I would most like to keep (1) in something close to its strong Chestertonian form and to keep (7); I suspect the trouble is somewhere in (2)–(5), possibly an equivocation between knowing what a practice is *for* and knowing its full causal-historical explanation, but I can't make that precise enough to answer him. One tempting reply is his own: he says we should stop reading the fence as an epistemic condition on permission at all and read it instead as a decision rule for irreversible choices under uncertainty — a rule about option value and asymmetric losses, which needs no historical understanding and generates no regress. I find that too quick, because it seems to throw away the distinctively Burkean claim that there is reason deposited in inherited practice which no individual reasoner can reconstruct from scratch, and because on that reading the fence would tell us nothing we don't already get from ordinary expected-utility reasoning. So: name the step you reject (or explain why the argument is valid and the cost must be paid at (1) or (7) after all), and price the alternatives for someone who wants the fence to remain a genuine constraint and not a bumper sticker.
