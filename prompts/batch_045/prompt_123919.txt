so my ethics elective is doing utilitarianism right now and last week we watched spider-man 2 in class (the 2004 one with doc ock) and my teacher said anyone who wanted to could write the final paper on the movie instead of on one of the readings. i want to do that but i really dont want it to turn into "spider-man is a utilitarian, the end."

the part of the unit that actually got to me was the drowning-kid / peter singer style argument: if you can stop something awful from happening at small cost to yourself, you have to, and once you follow that all the way down it seems like you can basically never clock out, never spend money on yourself, never just have a weekend. my teacher called that the demandingness objection — the complaint that a moral theory which never lets you off the hook is leaving out something real about having your own life and people you love more than strangers.

so: read the movie through that. peter straight up quits for a while, he dumps the suit in a dumpster, his grades come back, he gets to just be a guy, and the movie clearly likes showing him happy — but it also frames that as something he has to be pulled out of. and the ending has him telling MJ he cant be with her and then her choosing it anyway, so he keeps the girl and the job. my read is the movie wants it both ways and doesnt notice.

but here are two things i actually want you to argue with instead of just agreeing to. first, i think the real problem with the demanding version isnt that it asks for too much at once, its that it never tells you when you are done — no off switch, no amount that counts as enough. does the movie give peter any version of an off switch, or does every attempt he makes to take one get treated as a failure in the films own terms? second, my own dumb distinction: a demand you volunteered for vs a demand you got drafted into. peter never chose the spider. does the movie ever notice that difference, and does it matter for how harsh we should be on him?

i want at least two scenes done properly, not just summarized, and i want you to say where the movie resists this reading and where it just cheats because its a superhero film. please dont just name-drop philosophers at me — the film has to carry its weight. end by telling me what, if anything, watching it gets you that the argument alone doesnt. its a high school paper, like 1000-1200 words, so keep it readable and dont give me a grad seminar thing.
