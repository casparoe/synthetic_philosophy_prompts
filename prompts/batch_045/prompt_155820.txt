Please don't just dump the textbook version of this debate — brainstorm first, sketch out the space of possible defeaters, then commit to a ranking and defend it. Think carefully, take your time.

Here's where I've landed and I want it stress-tested. I've become convinced that the heterogeneity objection to hedonism — you know the move, where you line up the pleasure of a cold drink on a hot day, the pleasure of finally cracking a proof, the pleasure of hearing your enemy got what was coming to them, and the pleasure of sex, and ask what on earth these have in common — is best answered by treating pleasantness as a single scalar dimension of experience. Call it *valence*, hedonic tone, felt goodness, whatever. My position: there is one common currency, and all the variety the critics point at is variety in the inputs — in what the state is about, what caused it, what it disposes you to do, what it's like in every other respect — not in the felt dimension itself. One dimension, many routes. So pleasures do share a property, qualitative hedonism gets its single currency back, and Mill-style talk of higher and lower pleasures has to be cashed out as talk about causes, consequences, or what the state is directed at, rather than about the feeling.

What I want: tell me what would count against this. Not "here are some objections" — I want the defeaters ranked by how much damage they'd actually do, fatal at the top, merely awkward at the bottom, and I want you to separate the ones a committed defender of *my* view should honestly concede as hits from the ones they can reasonably swat away. Please cover at least these, and add anything better:

- Recalcitrant cases. The silent or absorbed pleasures where introspection finds no distinctive feel at all (being lost in work, say). The mixed or bittersweet ones. Things that are positively valenced partly *because* they contain negative affect — being thrilled, scary movies, very spicy food. Relief versus pleasure: is scratching an itch really pleasant, or just the removal of something bad? Malicious pleasure. Pleasures you don't want. For each: does it break the single-dimension claim, or only my story about what the dimension is?
- The motivation gap. Cases where you're strongly pulled toward something you don't enjoy, and things you enjoy without being pulled toward them. Is my "dimension" secretly a desire or a functional role in disguise? Because if so I've quietly become an attitudinal theorist while insisting I'm a feeling theorist, and I'd count that as a real cost.
- Whether pleasantness is a property of experiences at all, or a way or manner in which experiences are had. If that alternative is right, is my claim a category mistake rather than a false empirical one?
- Cross-kind comparison. If you genuinely cannot say whether an hour of one kind of pleasure contains more of the dimension than an hour of another kind — and not because of sloppy introspection, but systematically — is that evidence there's no single scale, or only evidence about our access to it? Which would a defender concede?
- The empirical version: if it turned out there is no common currency in the brain but several dissociable systems with no shared scale, or that "valence" is partly an artifact of how researchers set up their tasks, what happens to the view? And the other direction: would a common neural currency actually *help* me, or is it irrelevant to the conceptual question?
- The insulation worry, which is what I most want you to be blunt about. If I define the dimension as whatever makes a state feel good, and "feels good" just means "is pleasant," have I made the claim empty? What distinguishes my position from the trivial truth that all pleasures are pleasant? Say whether you think it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind — maybe every view at this level of generality is like this, and maybe that's fine.

Also: what does my view predict that its rivals (pluralism about the pleasures, attitudinal theories, family-resemblance or disjunctivist treatments) don't? And for the record, personally I'd be most moved by the clean motivation/affect splits and by cases where introspection really can't find a common feel; I'd be least moved by armchair insistence that two pleasures just feel obviously, totally different, since I think introspection is unreliable under exactly those conditions. Tell me if you think that's the wrong ranking.

No fence-sitting at the end — give me a verdict on how exposed the view actually is, and if you think it survives, say why in one or two sentences.
