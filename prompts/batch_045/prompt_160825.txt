hey — I need help with a toast. My partners mum Ellen is retiring next saturday after thirty-eight years, first as a tool-and-die maker and then, for the last fifteen or so, as a union rep and negotiator. I got talked into giving the toast and Im a bit stuck, because everything I start writing turns into either a labour-history lecture or one of those "the robots are coming" things, and she would wince at both.

Ellen is a serious Marx reader — she was the person in her branch who actually brought the books to meetings — and her Marxism is quite specific, so the speech has to take an actual position instead of gesturing at one.

Two things she always insisted on:

1. Marx doesnt let you be a Luddite. She says the machines are not the enemy and that craft nostalgia is a trap — she has zero sentimentality about the old hand-tool shop, which she reckons was just as brutal, only slower. What you get out of Marx, on her reading, is that the same machinery which is used to lengthen and intensify the working day also creates the material possibility of shortening it, and you cant have the second without the first having been true already. I want the toast to actually say that in a way non-readers follow.

2. But she was just as scathing about the other direction — the "fully automated luxury communism" version, where you jump straight from automation to abundance and nobody has to organise anything. Her objection: the famous notebook passage about machines and the "general intellect" never matched any floor she ever worked on. Her line was that every machine has a person standing next to it with a grease gun — automation doesnt remove the work, it relocates it into setups, jigs, repair, cleaning, nights, and the endless fiddling to keep the thing running. She literally had a name for this: "the maintenance hand". And she used it to argue that Marxs own value story (machinery only passes on the value already embodied in it; living labour is what adds new value) means labour never actually disappears from production, it just gets harder to see and easier to underpay.

Then the thing that shaped her whole career: she never, ever opposed a machine. She opposed the contract. Her rule was that a piece of equipment isnt good or bad in the abstract; what matters is who gets the time it saves. She had a test she called "the two-hour question" — if a new machine saves two hours a shift, do those two hours end up in the workers pocket or on the companys books? She judged every agreement by that. She also thought Marxs send-up of the "compensation theory" (the idea that workers thrown out by machinery get smoothly reabsorbed elsewhere) was the funniest thing in the whole book, and she quoted it constantly: displaced workers come back only through the specific mechanisms of a specific deal, never by some automatic tendency.

The audience, which is the hard part:

- her old crew from the floor, about half of whom still think she gave away too much in the 1990s automation agreement. There's real bad feeling. The speech cant pretend its not there and it cant settle it either — she'd hate being defended in a way that makes them look stupid.
- her three grandchildren, early twenties, all in software, who will quietly bristle at anything that sounds like its about them personally.
- two Marx scholars from the local university who will catch every reference, and who will absolutely roll their eyes if the toast collapses into "technology is neutral, its all about how you use it." She thinks that slogan is wrong. So do they.
- assorted family who just want to cry a bit and eat.

Constraints: its a toast, so five or six minutes, warm, at least one genuinely funny moment, landing as affection rather than as a lecture or a stump speech. It cant say the machines took her job — they didn't, the plant is still running and she's retiring with her hands, which she's weirdly proud of. It cant say her work gave her dignity, because she thinks that's exactly the sentimental lie people tell about other peoples jobs. And it shouldn't name Marx more than once or twice, or explain who he was.

Last thing, which I cant figure out: I need some line about what shes actually handing on — an ending that isn't just "enjoy your retirement." I think it has to come out of the tension between the two strands above (machinery as promise vs. the maintenance hand) rather than papering over it. If you can get those two to pull together into one line without resolving them, that's the speech.
