Here is the phenomenon. In arguments about whether an actual infinite is possible, everything turns on the familiar hotel story: every room occupied, a new guest arrives, everyone shifts up one room, and there is space. What struck me is that the story treats being able to take in more as simply part of what it means for a collection to be infinite, when it seems really to be a claim about what the occupants already there can be made to do, and those look like different claims. Three things I think I have worked out for a hotel whose rooms are arranged in a single row with a first room. Taking in one new guest cannot be achieved by moving only finitely many existing guests: if just finitely many move, they must be rehoused in the rooms they themselves vacate, and there is one too many of them. Taking in infinitely many requires that some existing guests move unboundedly far, even though each individual guest may move only a finite distance. And if no guest may be moved more than k rooms from where they started, then at most k rooms can be freed, however cleverly the reassignment is arranged.

Does this have a name — the study of what a rearrangement costs the elements already present, as opposed to what the size of the collection permits? It is not just the notion of a set's being Dedekind-infinite, which seems too coarse: that is a yes-or-no matter, and all three of my observations are about degree and cost. It also feels adjacent to paradoxical decompositions and to supertasks, but neither seems to be about the burden placed on the existing elements. So: is there an established concept here, how exactly does the thing I have described differ from each of the neighbours, and if there is no established concept, coin one, define it, and give me its boundary cases. In particular I want to know whether my k-bound survives when the hotel has no first room — rooms indexed by all the integers rather than the positive ones — whether it is really a fact about discreteness, given that it seems to collapse entirely if rooms are indexed by the real numbers and a whole interval can be freed with everyone moving at most one unit, and how it generalises to rooms laid out in a two-dimensional grid, where infinitely many rooms can apparently be freed with everyone moving one step but only along a kind of frontier. I would also like to know whether any of this bears on the original metaphysical question, and whether it is correct that a hotel which is infinite and yet cannot take in a single new guest is not ruled out by the standard axioms of set theory unless one assumes the axiom of choice. A bare label will not satisfy me; I want to know exactly where the phenomenon begins and ends.
