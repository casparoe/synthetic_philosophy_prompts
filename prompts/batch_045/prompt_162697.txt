You are a professional philosopher who works on social ontology, natural kinds, and the methodology of the social sciences — someone who has thought hard about the age–period–cohort identification problem and about what it takes for a classification to be more than a convenient name. Please think carefully and at some length. Before you render a verdict, brainstorm briefly: enumerate the distinct claims that hide inside the sentence "generations are real" (claims about boundaries, about causal powers, about which predicates support induction, about kinds versus labels, about grain of resolution), and say which move below depends on which. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move.

At a dinner party I hosted on Saturday, two friends — call them Nadia and Elliot — got stuck on whether generational categories (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z) name anything real, and neither would budge. I genuinely couldn't referee it. Roughly, as I remember it:

(1) Nadia opened with: generations are not real. Her three points were: the boundaries are stipulated rather than discovered — different outfits draw the same named category with different birth years, and moving a boundary by a year shifts people wholesale, so nothing in the world corresponds to the cut; the traits attributed to a generation borrow their apparent solidity from two causes that are not cohort effects at all, namely age effects (twenty-two-year-olds do twenty-two-year-old things) and period effects (a recession, a war, or a smartphone arrives for everyone alive at once), so that in the standard decomposition cohort is the third term — the one you cannot cleanly identify; and variance within a birth-year band dwarfs variance between bands. Conclusion: a generation is a reification of a statistical residual.

(2) Elliot replied that vague boundaries don't refute kindhood — species, languages, mountains, and colors all have contested edges — and, more substantively, that cohorts have causal powers that persist. You are eighteen exactly once, and which calendar year that falls in determines which labor market, housing market, or conscription system you walk into; that is a trajectory-shaping fact that travels with you for life, not a snapshot of a current state. He also pointed out that some cohort effects are identified cleanly, using discontinuities: school-entry cutoffs and birth-date lotteries show that a gap of weeks in birth date produces measurable, lasting differences, which means the mechanism is demonstrably real at fine grain.

(3) Nadia conceded the causal-powers point but called it the wrong kind of support: a hurricane has causal powers and is not thereby a kind. What Elliot had shown, she said, is that cohorts are real exposure groups, the way "everyone who was in the room" is a real group — true, and not what anyone is arguing about. She added that "permanent and unrepeatable" is true of any accident of timing. The strong claim would be that cohort membership licenses induction and counterfactuals of the form "had you been born ten years earlier, you would think differently in specifiable ways." And meanwhile the ordinary generational label bundles a birth slice with media habits, a political valence, and an aesthetic — a cluster held together by a name, with no essence underneath.

(4) Elliot answered that clusters are still kinds when the clustered traits mutually reinforce one another, and then made what he took to be his best point: the label loops back. Once a generation is named, it is marketed to, legislated about, satirized, and invited to describe itself in those terms; people adjust to the classification. So the arbitrariness of the original boundary decays with use — a cut that was arbitrary when it was drawn becomes a real social fact a generation later, because institutions and selves have organized around it.

(5) Nadia said that this shows the label is causally efficacious, not that the generation is a kind: the map is doing work on the territory while remaining a bad map. And she offered what she called the diagnostic. There is, she said, no unique correct rule for updating a generation over time. Under a fixed-roster definition — a fixed band of birth years — membership is stable but the content drifts, because the oldest members of a cohort came of age in a completely different material world than the youngest. Under a fixed-anchor definition — those who were adolescents when event X happened — the content stays put and the membership changes. The two update rules disagree about who belongs, and nothing settles which rule is correct; that, she said, is the signature of a classification we project onto the world rather than carve out of it.

(6) Elliot's reply was that this is just pluralism, and pluralism is not antirealism: watersheds, counties, and ecological zones are all real carvings of the same landscape and they cross-cut, but nobody concludes from that that none of them is real. He also turned Nadia's own fine-grained point around: if weeks of birth date demonstrably matter, then coarse cohorts are not fabrications but low-resolution measurements of a real structure, the way "a mountain range" is a coarse but honest way of talking about something fine-grained. And he added that event-anchored cohorts sometimes predict better than calendar ones — that we should simply say there are many overlapping cohort structures and ask which one earns its keep for which purpose, rather than declaring the whole enterprise fictional.

Please adjudicate this. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and why. Be specific about where a move succeeds only by shifting the sense of "real," where a concession was made too cheaply or refused when it should have been granted, and where the two parties are talking past each other. Then say what both of them are missing — the consideration that, once stated, reframes the disagreement — and, finally, give an overall verdict: if one side is more right, say so and say what empirical finding would change your mind. Do not give me readings, citations, or a bibliography; I want the argumentative accounting itself.
