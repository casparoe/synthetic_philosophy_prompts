Two weekends from now I'm giving a 20-plus-10 talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on measuring well-being, and I have the slot right after lunch, which is the worst one. The organisers are two heterodox economists and a political theorist, and the room will be roughly six welfare and public econ people — the revealed-preference-and-GARP crowd, plus two people who run experience-sampling studies and think momentary self-report is the only game in town — five or six political philosophers working on adaptive preference, the capability approach and the ethics of paternalism, and two RL people who are on my side in principle but who will absolutely nitpick the technical claims. I'm a philosophy PhD student, I've been working this out with a friend in RL theory, and I keep loosing the thread on who the actual threat in that room is, so I'd like a prep sheet rather than a defence.

The thesis is that inverse reinforcement learning should replace revealed preference as the default way of attributing preferences from behaviour, and that philosophers of the paternalism-and-adaptive-preference sort should care because it gives them something their own methods don't. My argument runs in three steps. (1) The old complaint against revealed preference — that a given choice function is consistent with loads of preference orderings, and says nothing about what happens under a new budget set — is really a special case of the non-identifiability of a reward function from behaviour: infinitely many reward functions make the observed trajectories optimal, the textbook illustration being that you can add a potential-based shaping term and leave the optimal policy untouched, and exactly which transformations are available depends on whether you assume hard or soft optimality, which is itself a modelling choice. (2) IRL is still an advance, because the maximum-causal-entropy / soft-optimality fit is a real inductive principle for breaking that tie rather than an arbitrary restriction, and because what it hands back is a reward function over states and actions rather than an ordering over bundles, so it transfers: you drop the same reward into a new MDP with a different budget set or a different menu and get predictions out, which is precisely what revealed-preference recovery cannot do. (3) Then my own addition, which I've been calling reward laundering: much of what political philosophers call adaptive preference is just behaviour generated inside a constrained MDP — the cost of leaving, the legal barrier, the implicit threat — so you re-infer the reward under a counterfactual dynamics in which the constraints you have independent reason to call unjust have been relaxed, and what comes out is closer to a non-adaptive preference without ever asking anyone to introspect. Conclusion: extrapolated reward is a better welfare proxy than either revealed or stated preference.
