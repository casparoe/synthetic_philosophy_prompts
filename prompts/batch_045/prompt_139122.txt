I teach an upper-level seminar on reasons and normativity and I'm setting the final exam. I need one essay question on the wrong kind of reasons problem that cannot be answered by stating the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction and walking through the standard demon case. Assume the students have already met both; tell them in the question that the answer should be argument rather than literature review, that no citations are expected, and that they have 90 minutes.

The question should open with compressed stage-setting: fitting-attitude accounts treat facts about what there is reason to favor as what constitutes an object's having an evaluative property such as being funny or admirable, and such accounts therefore need to exclude facts that are reasons to have an attitude without bearing on whether the object has the property in question. Call this the exclusion problem. Say that the usual move is to separate reasons that bear on whether the attitude is correct or fitting from reasons that bear instead on what it would cost or gain you to have it.

Then present two cases that this formulation handles awkwardly.

Case A, deferred access: a poem has a funny-making feature that the reader can come to perceive only by first being amused, because being amused is what redirects her attention onto it. She is offered, as a reason to be amused now, the fact that amusement is her only route to appreciating the feature that makes the poem funny. The reason thus consists in the poem's really having the property, plus a fact about her psychology.

Case B, the friend's request: a friend asks you, as a friend, to find her mediocre poem funny. There is no threat, bribe, or reward, and you have no evidence that it is funny. But finding her work funny is partly constitutive of the friendship, and you have reason to sustain the friendship.

The question to put to students: for each case, is the reason offered a right-kind or a wrong-kind reason, and does the answer depend on whether the attitude would be held because of that reason? Then the crux. Can the distinction be stated so that it yields answers to both cases, does not appeal to the very value property the fitting-attitude account is trying to analyze, and is not merely a list of cases we have independently decided how to classify? If no statement does all three, say what the fitting-attitude theorist should give up instead.

Deliverables: the exam question as I would print it; a model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite stance rather than surveying options; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. Close with a single sentence naming what separates an A answer from a B answer.
