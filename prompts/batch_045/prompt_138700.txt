Ok so I need someone to pick apart an argument before a meeting on thursday and I'm too close to it. Short version: I'm on my county's pretrial services advisory committee, we use a risk score to help decide who gets released and who gets routed into a diversion program, and I'm the one who has to sign off on the fairness audit. A law student intern sent our committee a memo arguing the audit is pointless. I think the memo is wrong but I can't say where, and I also have skin in it because one of the things he dismisses is my idea.

Heres his argument, numbered the way I reconstructed it (I cleaned up the wording, not the logic):

(1) A risk score is fair only if it satisfies every fairness criterion that genuinely applies to it.
(2) Three criteria genuinely apply to any tool that sorts people into risk categories: (a) the score means the same thing in every group — among people assigned a given score, the same proportion actually go on to the outcome being predicted; (b) people who would in fact have the outcome have the same chance of being flagged in every group; (c) people who would not have the outcome have the same chance of being flagged in every group.
(3) Whenever the groups have different base rates, no imperfect score satisfies (a), (b) and (c) together. This is the standard impossibility result, and I take it as given.
(4) Our groups do have different base rates, and the tool is nowhere near a perfect predictor.
(5) So the tool is unfair — and so would be every possible replacement tool, and so is a human judge making the same kind of call, because the same math applies to any classifier at all.
(6) So the unfairness isn't manufactured by the algorithm; its inherited from the world. The algorithm is just the messenger.
(7) But then "unfair" can't be doing any work. A property that no possible decision procedure can have can't be what is wrong with this decision procedure. There is no such thing as a fair risk score.
(8) So there is nothing for a fairness audit to audit. No fairness constraint can rank candidate tools against each other, and the only questions worth asking are about the background rates and the world that produces them, not about the algorithm.

The part that set me off: the argument seems to prove way too much. If (8) is right, then "fair" does no work anywhere, including in the sentence "the background rates are unfair" — which is exactly the place he wants to send us in (8). He uses the criteria as real constraints in (5) and then concludes they have no discriminating content. That feels like sawing off the branch, but I can't turn it into a clean objection, and I suspect there's also a slide somewhere between (5) and (7) about what follows when a *conjunction* of criteria is unsatisfiable.

The bit where I have skin in the game is step (9), which he tacked on after I sent my suggestion around:

(9) Coarsening the score can't help either. My proposal was: keep one common set of cut points for everybody, collapse the score into four risk bands, and then require that *within each band* the rate at which people in that band actually go on to the outcome is the same across groups — so a "high risk" band means the same thing for everyone in it — while letting the bands themselves be filled differently across groups. His reply is just: a coarsened score is still a score, feed the banded score back into (3) and you get the very same trilemma with fewer distinct values. That reply feels too fast to me, like he's using "error rates" in two different senses (conditioning on the band versus conditioning on the outcome), but I can't get it straight whether my band rule actually escapes the impossibility result or just moves it somewhere I haven't looked, and what it costs me if it does escape.

One more wrinkle that may or may not matter: we don't use a single threshold. The score is used to rank people for a fixed number of slots in the diversion program, so what we care about is the ordering, not who clears a cut line. Tell me if that changes anything about which step breaks.
