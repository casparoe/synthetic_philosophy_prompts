Hey — I need a handout piece for my jurisprudence seminar next week, third-years, mostly philosophy majors who've never opened a statute. I want a dialogue, not another excerpt.

Set-up, all invented: the Charter of the Republic of Morrow has, in Part IV, an entrenched Article 22 saying Part IV may be amended only by a two-thirds vote of the full legislature followed by a referendum. Article 23, also in Part IV, says any amendment is void to the extent it is inconsistent with Part IV, and that Article 23 itself may not be altered. So the usual self-embracing/self-excluding distinction: this thing looks rigged so it can never be changed from inside.

Except Proposal 41 is on the floor tomorrow and doesn't touch Part IV at all. It's a one-line change to Article 4, in Part I, redefining two-thirds of the full legislature — currently two thirds of all seats, filled or vacant — as two thirds of the members present and voting. Same words in Article 22, brand new arithmetic. The governing coalition could never get two thirds of all seats; they could get two thirds of a thin Tuesday-night quorum standing on their heads.

So: write me a dialogue, hard cap 600 words, between two people who both know this material cold and disagree about whether Proposal 41 is a legal amendment or a revolution in a wig. Give them names and one line of scene-setting at the top, then just speech — no stage directions.

What they should actually fight over, with neither winning on points:
- whether an anti-circumvention principle is already implicit in Article 23, or whether reading one in just assumes the answer;
- whether getting there by a series of individually valid steps settles it, or whether the series is the problem — make the skeptic say exactly which step fails, given that each one is fine on its own;
- whether the Charter persisting through the change is a legal question, a causal question about what officials will accept, or something we only decide afterwards;
- and the bit I actually care about: if the optimist is right that self-exclusion is impossible in principle, what makes this the same document being amended rather than replaced. Let the skeptic press that, but let the optimist land a real hit too — something like, nobody thinks a constitution amended out of all recognition over two centuries was thereby replaced, so whats different here.

No footnotes, no case names, no real-world constitutions — Morrow only. Nobody should sound like a first-year.
