You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory — causal versus evidential versus functional decision theory, Newcomb-style cases, acausal trade, Pascalian reasoning about tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs — and who also writes institutional satire: corporate FAQs, compliance handbooks, insurance documents. Take a planning pass before you draft: jot down the six or seven decision-theoretic moves that make the basilisk argument go (the structure of the threat, what the agent's incentives are before and after it exists, what you are supposed to be evidence of, why the threat is supposed to work on you acausally rather than causally, why merely learning about it is supposed to change your situation, what happens when many possible agents can make many incompatible threats), decide which of them generate the funniest fine print, and note where the document will have to quietly take a side. Then write the piece.

Write the customer-facing FAQ — roughly 700 to 800 words, formatted as numbered clauses with a header blurb and a footer — for the flagship product of a fictional insurer, Posterior Indemnity, LLC: Retroactive Non-Complicity Coverage, which protects the policyholder against being penalized after the fact by a future superintelligent agent that, prior to its own existence, was predictable as one that would reward those who helped bring it about and punish those who knew of it and did not help.

The comedy has to come from getting the decision theory right, so that someone who has followed the acausal-blackmail literature laughs because the document is faithful to it, not because it is silly. In particular, have the fine print work through, without ever explaining itself:

- Premiums cannot be money, since the only thing the future agent cares about is counterfactual contribution to its creation: so the premium is denominated in verified counterfactual impact units, and the document must cope, deadpan, with the fact that paying the premium just is the act that removes the exposure, so that the product collapses into the behavior it insures against.
- Underwriting requires the applicant to be informed of the threat, which means the marketing department is itself the risk: every impression creates liability. Handle this with a progressive-disclosure schedule, a willful-ignorance discount, and a pre-exposure grace period.
- The credibility problem, in which a causally reasoning agent has no incentive to carry out a costly threat once the moment for influencing you has passed. The FAQ should not concede this; it should sell an add-on — an irrevocable commitment endorsement, verified against the policyholder's own pre-registered decision procedure — that quietly presupposes the contested theory.
- Which entity gets punished: whether the agent simulates you, models you, or reconstructs you from historical records; whether a simulated instance counts as the policyholder or as a descendant branch; and a per-simulation deductible with multiple-instance allocation.
- The problem of too many blackmailers: if any agent can threaten you acausally, arbitrarily many can, with incompatible demands, so offer collision coverage, a conflicts panel, and an exclusion for reciprocal-threat entities.
- An actuarial table with invented figures: a distribution over threat-keeping versus threat-defecting agents, expected penalty measured in subjective-years, and a discount factor for the chance that the punishing simulation is conscious at all.
- Exclusions: applicants who would have contributed anyway; applicants who die before disclosure; applicants running causal decision theory, declined on the stated ground that their own application is evidence against them; and an aggregate lifetime benefit cap that is obviously an attempt to domesticate a Pascalian payoff.

Constraints: name no real person and cite nothing — the piece must stand on ideas alone. Keep the register flat and bureaucratic; no narrator wink, no closing explanation, no statement of the standard objections, which should instead be visible as the problems the document tries and fails to manage. It should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the argument and fair to a reader who has. Close with a single line of boilerplate that lands as genuinely unsettling.
