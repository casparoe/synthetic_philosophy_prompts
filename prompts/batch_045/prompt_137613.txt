Here is the question as it was set to the students.

Question 3 (30 marks). Parfit's hitchhiker is stranded in the desert. A driver stops and offers to drive him to town if he promises to pay $1,000 on arrival. The ride is worth $10,000 to him. Once in town, paying costs him $1,000 and gets him nothing, since the ride is already over. The driver offers the ride only if she is confident that he will in fact pay, and she is a very reliable predictor of this; the hitchhiker knows that she is.

(a) [8 marks] Explain Parfit's distinction between a theory's being directly self-defeating and its being indirectly self-defeating, and his distinction between individual and collective self-defeat. State which of these the hitchhiker is invoked to illustrate, and why it illustrates that one rather than a neighbouring one.

(b) [12 marks] Now vary the case. Call this the Present-Intention Driver. The driver does not predict future behaviour at all. What she detects, perfectly, is whether the hitchhiker at the roadside now sincerely intends to pay on arrival; she gives him the ride if and only if he now has that intention. She forms no view about whether the intention will survive the drive, she never reads him again, and she cannot collect the money once they arrive. The hitchhiker knows all of this. Does this variant show that the lesson of the hitchhiker concerns what the agent can intend rather than what dispositions he must acquire, and hence that no "rational irrationality" is needed at all? Your answer should say exactly what work is being done by the assumption about what the driver detects, and should compare the case with Kavka's toxin puzzle, in which forming the intention is enough and the agent never has to act on it.

(c) [10 marks] Assess the following claim: "If it is rational for the hitchhiker to arrange to be someone who pays, then it is rational for him to pay." In answering, refer to Gauthier's constrained maximization and to the objection that a conception of rationality which recommends making oneself irrational is self-undermining.

Here is one student's answer, which I have to mark.

(a) A theory is directly self-defeating when following it makes things go worse for the agent than following some alternative theory would — or, in the collective case, worse for everyone, when everyone follows it. A theory is indirectly self-defeating when it tells the agent to bring it about that he will later act contrary to it: to give himself desires or dispositions whose exercise will lead him to do what the theory itself counts as irrational. The hitchhiker is a case of individual and indirect self-defeat. Individual rather than collective, because there is really only one agent with a genuine choice; the driver's behaviour is part of the fixed background, set by her prediction. Indirect rather than direct, because the self-interest theory does not tell the hitchhiker to pay. Once he is in town, paying would cost him $1,000 for nothing. What the theory tells him is to arrange to pay: to get himself a disposition to keep his promises, since without such a disposition he is never offered the ride in the first place. The theory therefore requires him to act against its own verdict later on. That is the "rational irrationality": rationality is on the side of causing yourself to have a disposition whose exercise is, at the moment of action, contrary to reason.

(b) The Present-Intention Driver does not really change the case, because the hitchhiker cannot form the intention the driver is looking for. He knows that he will reconsider once they arrive, so the most he can intend at the roadside is to pay unless he changes his mind, and no one reading his mind perfectly would count that as an intention to pay: she would read the intention he will actually act on, which is the intention not to pay. So he still has to acquire a genuine, stable disposition to keep his promise, and the fact that the driver reads only present intentions makes no difference. The case remains one in which he must turn himself into someone who will act against his own judgement when they get to town. Nor does the toxin puzzle help. In that puzzle the agent needs only to form the intention to drink the toxin, because the million is paid for the intention and he never has to drink; in the hitchhiker case the money actually has to change hands, so intending is not enough. That is why the hitchhiker is the harder case, and why the variant still requires the same rational irrationality.

(c) The objection that a conception of rationality which tells you to make yourself irrational is self-undermining is weaker than it looks. Once we see that what is rational is arranging to have the disposition, and that the rationality of the disposition and the rationality of the action it issues in are separate questions, there is no contradiction: rationality can coherently recommend that you later do what is, locally, against your interests. Indeed, if it is rational to arrange to be a payer, then it is rational to pay, and the hitchhiker who pays in town does what he has most reason to do, all things considered. Gauthier's constrained maximization is the general form of this point. The constrained maximizer is disposed to cooperate with whoever she deals with instead of maximizing case by case, and Gauthier argues that this disposition is rational because a population in which everyone has it does better than a population of straightforward maximizers; the argument shows that rationality cannot simply be a matter of maximizing. The disposition also has to be visible to others, which is Gauthier's translucency condition, and it must not be something you can shed when it becomes costly. So the hitchhiker should simply be a constrained maximizer about promises.
