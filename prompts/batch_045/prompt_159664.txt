You are a decision theorist who also teaches a film-and-philosophy seminar, so please answer as someone who is at home in both literatures and who is allergic to readings that flatten one of them into an illustration of the other. Take your time and think the mapping through before you start writing; I would rather have a careful reading than a fluent one.

The context is this. I teach a short unit on imperfect recall in an undergraduate decision theory course, and I want to close it with a screening rather than another problem set. The version of the absent-minded driver problem I give them is the standard textbook one: a driver on a highway comes to two indistinguishable exits, the second leads to the best outcome, the first to a merely tolerable one, and driving past both is worst of all; at the start he knows the layout perfectly, but when he is at an exit he cannot tell which exit he is at, and the notorious result is that the strategy that looks best to him when he forms the plan is not the one he would want to execute when he actually finds himself at an exit -- the verdict you get at the planning stage and the verdict you get at the action stage come apart. A colleague suggested Christopher Nolan's Memento as the film to show, and I am genuinely unsure whether it illuminates this or just gestures at it. My seminar last year had a fight about it: half thought it was simply a film about unreliable narration, and I could not settle it.

So here is what I want. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly -- a table is fine -- between the film's machinery and the formal problem: what plays the role of the two indistinguishable decision nodes, of the payoffs, of the signal that would (if you had it) tell you which node you are at, of the plan, and of the divergence between planning and acting. Leonard Shelby's system of Polaroids, notes, and tattoos looks to me like an attempt to manufacture exactly the piece of indexical information the driver lacks, and the scene where Natalie tests whether he can retain anything at all looks like someone auditing the channel through which that signal is supposed to reach his next self. But I want to know where the fit is illusory. In the canonical problem the driver's reasoning is intact and only his recall fails, whereas Leonard's difficulty may be less that he cannot tell which node he is at than that he cannot tell whether the note in front of him was written by himself, by a past self who was already deceived, or by Teddy -- which is not imperfect recall so much as an unreliable information source. And the ending, where he deliberately plants a false clue so that a future self who cannot check it will go and kill Teddy, looks to me like the exact inversion of the Ulysses-and-the-Sirens case that the decision theory literature leans on: there the plan binds a self against a temptation it can foresee, while here the binder lies to the bound. Is that right, or am I overreading?

Please also tell me honestly whether the film is usable in a decision theory class or whether it will mislead them, and if it is usable, which two or three scenes you would stop on and what you would say about each. I would like the reading to be fair in both directions: do not reduce the film to a puzzle with a solution, and do not stretch imperfect recall to cover a plot that is really about self-deception and grief. Give me roughly nine hundred words, and end by saying what, if anything, the film shows about imperfect recall that the formalism does not capture -- my own suspicion is that the formalism treats forgetfulness as a private, internal handicap, while the film insists that an agent who cannot remember is above all an agent someone else can exploit by controlling what reaches his next decision node, but I want you to tell me whether that suspicion survives contact with the formal problem.
