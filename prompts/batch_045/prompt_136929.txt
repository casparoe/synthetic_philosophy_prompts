I want a specification, not a survey: the requirements an account of individual obligation under the inefficacy objection has to meet, each with the case or consideration that is supposed to motivate it.

Fix the purpose first. The account is to do two jobs at once: first-personal deliberation — what should I do about my own emissions, my own meat purchases, my own vote, given that none of them will make a perceptible difference to anything — and third-personal assessment — what we may blame, criticise, or demand of others. Say whether one account can serve both jobs, or whether the two uses pull the requirements apart; I suspect they do, and I want that made explicit rather than smoothed over.

Below are the candidate requirements I keep coming back to. Tell me which are genuine desiderata, which are only the prejudices of one camp, and which are jointly unsatisfiable. Distinguish the non-negotiable ones from the optional ones, and keep each entry to a case plus a sentence of justification.

1. Nullity versus negligibility. Whether the account has to separate strict zero expected difference, under the agent's best model of the mechanism, from positive but imperceptible expected difference. My worry: since the agent is uncertain about the mechanism, and any nonzero credence that a threshold is close to her, or that her purchase is recorded in a demand-forecasting system that responds in lumps later, already makes expected difference positive, the inefficacy objection may only ever establish negligibility.
2. The unit of evaluation. Act-token, standing policy, annual budget, or whole life — and what turns on the choice, given that imperceptible contributions aggregate intrapersonally when the mechanism is continuous with total input, but do not when it is lumpy or threshold-shaped.
3. The baseline for abstention as replacement rather than omission: money not spent on beef is spent on chicken, the hour not spent voting is spent somehow; and offsetting is a different case from abating.
4. Overdetermination kept separate from thresholds: two independently sufficient presses, versus many contributions each insufficient on its own.
5. Open membership: what the account says when the set of contributors is not fixed independently of each contributor's own decision, since the standard fixed-number symmetric case makes pivotalness vanish in a way an open-number case may not.
6. Mediated effects — influence on other people's acts, on institutional records of demand, on the agent's own future motives — whether they count at all, and how they are to be bounded for an agent who cannot compute them.
7. Criterion versus decision procedure: whether the account must be usable by someone of ordinary epistemic limits, or whether stating the right-making features is enough.
8. Moral remainder: room left for criticising the agent who makes no difference but is glad she would have, and for the one who abstains for purely self-regarding reasons.

Finally, flag any pair of requirements you think cannot both be satisfied — that is the part I most want — and identify the single requirement that, if dropped, would let through the largest family of accounts. Score the familiar families by type rather than by author — expected-difference accounts, complicity or membership accounts, collective-duty and joint-action accounts — in a short table if it is useful, but keep the weight on the criteria. I will add the citations myself.
