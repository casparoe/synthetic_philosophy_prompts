For the last eighteen months I have been, without having sought it, the person in my family who remembers. My grandmother is in the later stages of a dementia that has taken most of her past but not all of it; she is the last of her generation, and the only one of them who ever said anything about the year the family spent in transit between two countries, and about the two siblings of hers who did not come through it. My brother thinks that returning to those stories with her is a form of extraction and that she should be allowed to forget in peace. My sister wants a recorded oral history and a digitised archive of the letters. A cousin has proposed something else: several hours of tape and about three hundred letters are enough, he says, to build a system that the grandchildren could still talk to. There is a little money available, perhaps what a few thousand would buy, and my own time is irregular weekends for roughly a year to eighteen months. There is no archive that wants this material, no institution behind me, and no one to adjudicate between my brother and my sister.

I have been reading around the duty to remember and the case for forgetting — Margalit's treatment of memory as something owed within relations of caring, and the contrasting suggestion, associated with Rieff, that a therapeutic culture converts forgetting from a failure into a remission — and I do not need a map of that debate. What I need is an ordering, because I have to act before the material to act on is gone.

So: which of the questions this area leaves open would actually change what I do, and in what order should I try to settle them? I want the ordering defended on explicit criteria — how much of the decision turns on the answer, whether one person with no archives and no expertise can make progress on it in a year, and how many of the other questions depend on it — and I want you to say where those criteria pull apart. Concretely, here is my candidate list, which you should feel free to cut, merge, or replace:

Whether a duty to remember is discharged by making a good deposit or requires memory that is occurrent and shared. If the first, my sister's archive is close to the whole answer; if the second, the archive is at best a mnemonic aid and the real work is finding people who will remember alongside me, which is a different project entirely and possibly not one I can do.

The last-member problem: if the duty is grounded in relations among people who remember together, what becomes of it when the community of rememberers shrinks to one person, and then to none? Does it lapse, or turn into something with a different shape — a duty of truthfulness about the past, say, rather than of memory — or transfer to people who had no relation to what is remembered?

Whether whatever duty there is runs to the dead or to the living, and whether the dead can be wronged by being forgotten. I also want to know which of the things people take to follow from that really do follow: that we owe the dead the unsimplified version, for instance, or the truth even where it discredits the account they gave of themselves.

Whether there are cases in which forgetting is not a moral failure but the object of the exercise, and whether that applies to the ones who remember as well as to the one remembered. My grandmother's forgetting may be a mercy; my brother's proposal that the rest of us stop returning to it is a different claim, and I am not sure the same argument covers both.

The question my cousin has put on the table: whether a system that can produce her idiom fluently but stands in no relation to her satisfies anything a duty to remember could be a duty to do, whether it corrupts the memory it was built to serve given that such systems confabulate smoothly and that a fluent false memory passed to grandchildren may be worse than silence, or whether it is simply a new kind of deposit that later people will use as they already use letters. I also want the prior question addressed: whether a proxy of that kind makes shared remembering possible once she is gone, or makes it impossible by erasing the visible difference between recalling someone and querying a record of them.

And the question I may be avoiding: whether the duty attached to me at all, whether I am permitted to decline it, and what it would take for the harm the remembering does to the living — to my mother, to my brother, to me — to outweigh whatever is owed in the other direction.

Tell me which two or three of these you would actually pursue given my situation, which are upstream of the rest in the sense that other answers move when they do, which I am overrating because resolving them would not change what anyone does, and what I have omitted. Finally, say what evidence available to me over the next year or so — a conversation that goes badly, a letter that turns up, an attempt at recording that fails — would move a given question up or down your list.
