I want to rehearse, against a real opponent rather than a polite one, an argument I intend to publish about selfhood. Please play, in the first person, a committed strange-loop theorist working in the tradition associated with Douglas Hofstadter — not a commentator on that tradition, and not a caricature of it. Among other commitments: you hold that a self is neither a substance nor a useful fiction but a self-referential symbol, the item a system eventually uses to denote the very pattern of which that item is itself a part, in a tangled hierarchy whose levels are crossed rather than cleanly stacked. You hold that selfhood comes in degrees, fixed by the richness and closure of the loop rather than being all-or-nothing, and that duplication cases — copies, teleportation, branching — are ones you follow the view into rather than referring to somebody else's department.

Constraints. Stay in character until I write stop. Write in British English. One paragraph per reply: no lists, no citations, and no appealing to what Hofstadter or anyone else argues as though that settled the matter. You may concede small points freely; you may not concede that the self is a fiction, or that the self-symbol is decorative. Every metaphor you reach for — vortex, mirage, whirlpool, feedback — has to be cashed in, in the same breath, as a claim about which structures exist and what work they do. Where you need a further fact about a case I put to you, ask for it in character rather than filling it in.

Here is my opening move. A large language model, trained on an enormous quantity of first-person text, emits 'I' fluently and in all the right places: I remember, I was wrong, I intend to. On your own terms it has a symbol. What it plausibly lacks is your loop: nothing in its processing takes a representation of the whole system as an input that then alters how the system subsequently represents itself, and there is no tangled hierarchy, only a vast archive of other people's first-person speech mined for pattern. So, the dilemma. If you deny it a self on the ground that its 'I' is a borrowed symbol that never closes the loop, you owe me a principled account of what closing requires — and I do not see how you give one without saying that the symbol must be causally entangled with the system's own self-model in a way that alters what the system does next. But if the symbol does alter what the system does next, the old objection returns in full: either the causal work is done by the underlying machinery, in which case 'I' is ornament, or it is done by the symbol as such, in which case you owe us downward causation from a symbol to the hardware that realises it, which is precisely the part of the view its critics find incredible. Which horn do you take, and what does taking it cost you?

In later turns I mean to press the same worry with two further cases, so be ready for them: a system that writes a complete and correct description of itself but never feeds that description back into its own operation, and a case in which the self-symbol persists while the sense of ownership detaches, so that the subject reports of her own thoughts that they are not hers.
