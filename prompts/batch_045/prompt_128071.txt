ok so I'm in this seminar on suffering-focused ethics / population ethics and we each have to circulate a short "where I stand" statement that someone else in the class gets to tear apart, and I'd rather find out now whether mine's a mess. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I accept the asymmetry: the absence of suffering is good even if there's nobody around who's better off for it, while the absence of happiness isn't bad unless someone's been deprived of it. But I'm not an antinatalist — I think it can be perfectly fine for people to have kids.

(2) Suffering is the only thing that's intrinsically bad. Happiness isn't intrinsically valuable; it's good only as a remedy, more like the settling of an unsatisfied need.

(3) Badness adds up straightforwardly across people: no diminishing weight, no priority to the worse off, no threshold where suffering suddenly jumps in badness. Roughly, more suffering = worse, and that's all there is to it.

(4) I think there's something like a saturation point for intensity: past a certain level, making something hurt more doesn't make the episode any worse for the person going through it, though I do think duration keeps adding up linearly. I take first-person reports from people with the very worst chronic pain at face value here, even though in general I think we're pretty bad at comparing intensities across people.

(5) I can't accept the trade-off cases. I don't think it's ok to torture one unwilling person in order to spare a gigantic number of people very mild headaches, and I don't think piling on more headaches eventually flips it. I don't have a principle that delivers this — I just can't swallow the trade.

(6) I think the worst off should come first: severe unremitting pain, treatment-resistant depression, animals in intensive farming — rather than mild suffering at enormous scale. Concretely, if I had money for either a program that cures 100,000 people of mild chronic itch or one that cures 10 people of the very worst kind of intractable pain, I'd fund the 10.

(7) I think reducing existential risk should be near the top of the priority list, and my reason is basically the sheer number of future beings at stake.

(8) I'm not a speciesist — if insects and other arthropods have valenced experience then their suffering counts, weighted by my credence that they do. But I'm fairly sceptical of "reduce wild invertebrate suffering" style interventions, because the evidence is thin and the ecological knock-on effects are unpredictable. People in my seminar keep telling me that's inconsistent of me and I don't think it is.

(9) Last one: I don't want to say that my own worst experiences were all-things-considered bad for me. There are a couple I'd keep if you offered me a pill that erased them. I think that's compatible with (2), but I can't quite say why.

Can you audit this for me? If there's a single revision that cleans up the most, tell me which. And for the ones you think dissolve, say what the dissolving move is, because I'll have to defend it out loud.
