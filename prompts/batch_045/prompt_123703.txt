I teach a graduate seminar on Berkeley's economic writings, and I have noticed that I hold firm verdicts about which arrangements are really money in the sense that concerns him — money as an instrument for setting a people to work, as against the store of metal that everyone mistakes for wealth — while being unable to bring those verdicts under a single principle. My own first formulation is this: an instrument is money only if (i) its acceptance rests on convention alone, none of its efficacy being owed to the stuff it is made of, and (ii) it is issued into circulation against goods actually produced or labour actually contracted for, so that more of it abroad means more industry, not more bullion.

Tested against the cases, that formulation strains:

- A guinea passing hand to hand in Dublin: money, and nothing about its being gold is what makes it so.
- The same guinea sealed in a strongbox for forty years: still money, though clause (ii) should have retired it.
- A banknote issued by discounting a linen merchant's bill of exchange: money, and paradigmatically so.
- The same note after the bank suspends payment in silver, with everyone still taking it: still money, and I am tempted to say more securely so than before.
- A setting-off of two merchants' mutual debts in a clearing house, with no instrument changing hands: not money — money cancelled rather than money used.
- A landowner's personal bond, endorsed round a market town where his acres are well known: I want to call it money and mistrust the wanting.
- A note issued against the mortgage of land, receivable in land at the holder's option — the device Berkeley proposed for an Irish national bank: money, and on a better footing than a note payable in silver.
- A token with no issuer and a fixed supply, taken by thousands of merchants, exchanged almost entirely for other tokens: this is the case I go back and forth on; my instinct is to withhold the name, and I cannot say why in the terms of my own principle.
- A pawn ticket for a coat, sold on at a discount: not money.

Please test my principle against these, repair it or replace it, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save. Then give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: the entries in a modern commercial bank's deposit ledger, and a unit in which every shop quotes its prices but which no shop will accept in payment. Lead with the principle, and keep the whole answer under 500 words.
