I've been reading about consequentialist cluelessness on and off for years — LessWrong threads, EA Forum posts, the actual philosophy papers — and the argument is sitting in almost exactly the spot it was in when I first noticed it. Side A: everything we do ripples out for centuries, our credences over long-run outcomes are close to noise, any expected-value comparison between two realistic options gets dominated by consequences we can't even gesture at, so "maximize expected value" isn't action-guiding for beings like us and the clued-in consequentialist should more or less shrug. Side B: you still have a credence distribution, you still maximize over it, if the noise is roughly symmetric it cancels and you act on the near-term evidence you actually have, and anyway the paralysis conclusion is way stronger than anything the cluelessness argument supports. Everyone has read everyone, everyone has heard the objections, and nobody has moved an inch. It has that specific feeling of a debate where both sides are doing something slightly different under the same name.

Two hypotheses I can't cleanly separate:

1. There are two different claims in the neighborhood and people keep proving one and concluding the other. Call them sign cluelessness (for any pair of options, we have no reliable view about which one has better long-run effects) and scale cluelessness (even granting a reliable sign, the magnitude estimates are so diffuse that the long-run term swamps everything else and the decision is effectively made by the tails, where our credences are thinnest). A lot of the "just maximize, symmetric noise cancels" replies look to me like answers to sign cluelessness that get used to dismiss scale cluelessness, and vice versa. But maybe those don't actually come apart in practice.

2. It's a formal disagreement disguised as an empirical one. On my side of the internet people almost always model cluelessness as precise-but-high-variance credences, and then the Bayesian machinery keeps delivering an answer, so paralysis looks like a fallacy. The paralysis people often talk as if the uncertainty is genuine imprecision — a set of distributions, or incomparability — and then there's no expectation to maximize and the paralysis is real. If that's the crux, then the debate is about which formalism represents this kind of uncertainty, not about how predictable the world is, and all the arguing about history and chaos and flow-through effects is downstream of that.

There's a third thing in the background that I think might be doing more work than either: a half-empirical question about the shape of causal influence over time, whether the effects of most interventions tend to wash out (ripples averaging away, differences getting scrubbed by everything else that happens) or lock in (switching the world onto a different track, where small early differences compound). My sense is that each side has a background picture here that they never argue for and that actually drives their conclusion.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, is it two questions under one heading, is there a hidden empirical or formal question carrying the weight, or is it just bedrock intuition about how much confidence a human is allowed to have? I'd rather have a diagnosis with reasons than a survey of the positions, and I want an honest answer about what would count as progress — a theorem about decision rules for imprecise credences, an actual research program on wash-out versus lock-in, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. And if the whole "is consequentialism paralyzed?" framing is ill-posed, say that too, and tell me what the well-posed version is.
