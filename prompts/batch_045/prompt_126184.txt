I teach a graduate seminar on American pragmatism, and for next week I built the following argument as a way of pressing a Deweyan account of democracy to the point where it embarrasses itself. I now think it proves far too much, but I cannot locate the exact point of failure, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a bibliography.

The view I am working with, stated as neutrally as I can. Inquiry, on this account, is not the application of antecedently fixed ends to given means; it is the directed transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate one, and the ends-in-view that guide it are themselves formed, tested, and revised within the inquiry rather than supplied to it from outside. Democracy is then not a mechanism for tallying preferences that exist prior to political life; it is the conduct of associated living under the condition that the ends of that life are themselves open to inquiry. This is what separates the position from the technocratic alternative Dewey argued against in his disagreement with Lippmann, on which the public is incompetent by constitution and must be administered by experts who can discern its real interests.

The argument:

(1) On this account, whatever authority a collective decision has — epistemic and moral — derives from its being the outcome of inquiry rather than the registration of preferences that were fixed in advance of it.

(2) So a procedure that confers that authority must leave open, and submit to revision, the ends of those who take part in it; a procedure that treats its inputs as settled is executive technique, not inquiry.

(3) Openness to the revision of the participants' ends is therefore the mark by which we distinguish procedures that confer democratic authority from procedures that do not.

(4) A procedure is open in the relevant sense if it reliably changes the ends of those who undergo it.

(5) Commercial advertising, propagandistic framing, and the rearrangement of choice architecture all reliably change the ends of those who undergo them; that is precisely what they are designed and selected to do, and their success is measured by it.

(6) So advertising, propagandistic framing, and nudging are, by (3), procedures of the kind that confer democratic authority — that is, they count as instances of democratic inquiry.

(7) But (6) is absurd. These are the paradigms of what corrupts democratic judgment, not instances of the activity whose exercise confers authority.

(8) So the identification of democracy with inquiry cannot mark the difference between deliberation and manipulation: either one of (1) through (3) is false, or (7) is.

My own sense is that the trouble is in the move from (3) to (4), since openness to revision seems to be a matter of what the procedure does with the considerations it turns up, while reliable change of ends seems to be a matter of causal efficacy, and a procedure can have the second without the first. But when I try to say what the missing clause is — revision in light of reasons the participants can themselves take up — I seem to reintroduce an agent who stands outside the process with standards antecedently in hand, which is the spectator picture the whole account was meant to displace. And I am not confident that (3) is innocent either: it may be that no single procedure can bear the mark at all, and that the mark belongs to an extended sequence in which consequences feed back into revised ends.

What I want, then, is this. Name the step that fails and say why it looked sound. Then price the alternatives honestly: for each premise one might give up, say what the resulting position can and cannot still claim about democracy, expertise, and the formation of ends, and say which of my own commitments I would have to surrender to hold it. Of (1), (2), and (7) I am unwilling to let go; if the cost falls on (3) or (4), I want to know exactly how much of the Deweyan program survives.

As a check on whatever proposal emerges, apply it to two cases I did not build into the argument. First: a citizens' assembly convened once, with members chosen by lot, briefed by opposing experts, and asked to recommend a policy to a legislature that is free to ignore them. Second: a standing online forum in which an automated system restates each participant's contribution in a shared vocabulary before others see it, on the stated ground that most disagreements there are verbal and dissolve once translated.
