So I do this thing at work — every few months someone from the desk gets fifteen minutes at the Friday brown-bag to explain one quantitative idea to everyone else, and next week its my turn. The room will be about a dozen people: two politics reporters who are fine with numbers but go glassy at any notation, our data guy (who has definitely seen log utility somewhere and I think that's going to be a problem), an arts writer who says she's bad at math and who will ask the actually useful questions, and an editor who is going to want one sentence he can turn into a headline. Ive decided to do Kelly betting / geometric rationality: the point that if you're going to face the same multiplicative gamble again and again, maximizing the growth of your bankroll is a different thing from maximizing expected value, and that log utility kind of falls out of the setup instead of being a taste you announce up front. Please don't just explain it to me and please don't give me the derivation — I want a plan for explaining it.

What I'm least sure about is the order. My instinct is that "what happens typically" has to land before anyone hears the word log, and I've been playing with: a coin that comes up heads 60% of the time, pays 2:1, you can bet any fraction of your bankroll, a hundred flips. Stake everything each time and your expected wealth multiplies by 1.8 per flip while you go bust with probability one; stake 40% and I get expected growth of about 32% a flip but a typical (median) flip is only about 16%, which is already the whole idea in miniature. But I don't know if that example is carrying too much or the wrong things, so tell me what the one case I build on has to have and what it has to lack — I think it has to be genuinely multiplicative rather than additive, and it probably shouldn't have a tidy round number of repetitions, but I'm guessing. I also want two wrinkles handled rather than waved away, because they're the part I actually find interesting: in real life you don't know your edge, you have a noisy estimate of it, and full Kelly on a bad estimate is brutal; and in real life there's often a floor — drop below some level and you're out of the game for good — plus a finite-ish number of chances rather than infinite ones. I have a half-formed suspicion that both of those quietly undercut the "log is special" argument, or at least change what its telling you, and that once there's an absorbing floor you need a real utility function or a real risk preference and the log stops being free. If that's wrong say so, and tell me where in the sequence I can raise it without loosing the room.

Then the misconceptions, and exactly where to head each off: "Kelly maximizes your expected money" (it doesn't, and I think the arts writer will feel that something is off before the data guy does); "Kelly means you can't lose / it's a system that beats the house"; confusing "bet more when you're more confident" with "bet more when the payoff is bigger"; and "half Kelly is just being timid," when the growth-rate-versus-swing tradeoff there is one of the only practically useful takeaways. Tell me which ones I should preempt up front and which I should let someone say out loud first and then correct, and flag the big one I'm missing — I suspect there's something about "so Kelly is just expected utility with one arbitrary utility function, what's the fuss," and I don't want to either concede it so fully that the talk collapses or overclaim that this is "the" rational rule in some context-free way. Finally: one question I can ask at the end that shows me whether the idea actually landed rather than whether they can parrot a phrase back, the things I should openly refuse to get dragged into (I'm thinking the ergodicity reframing, the derivation, and anything about the stock market), and a one-sentence takeaway that is not "a formula that makes you money."
