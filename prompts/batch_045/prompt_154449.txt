I've been following a guy's blog where he keeps asking the same question in Hofstadter's careenium-and-simmballs framing (brain goo, with symbols as little balls rolling around inside it): do the symbols actually shove the substrate around, or does the goo just do what physics says and the symbol story is bookkeeping? His verdicts so far:

- a thermostat kicking the furnace on: no
- a hand-coded chess engine picking a move because its king is in check: no
- an LLM filling in the next word: no
- a warehouse robot re-routing because its learned map says the corridor is blocked: no
- a person taking an umbrella because she believes it's raining: yes
- a person who orders sparkling water at dinner because she knows red wine gives her a headache: yes

He's ducked exactly one case so far, said he'd get to it later: any system with an explicit self-model that isn't conscious.

So here's the case I want to bring him. A non-conscious routing system (stipulate that nobody is home and that whatever intentionality its symbols have is borrowed from the engineers who built it) which keeps a running, slightly lossy simulation of itself, and which picks route B over route A specifically because the sim says: if I commit to A, my own planner is going to thrash and I'll miss the deadline. Its substrate really does move differently because of what that self-model says.

Do you think he says yes or no? I can see two rules that fit his six verdicts — roughly, "only a tangled hierarchy gets to push" and "only consciousness does" — and they come apart here, but I suspect there's a third rule that fits the track record just as well and that I haven't thought of. Give me the candidate rules, what each one predicts about this case, your best guess with the reason for preferring it, and the single case I should bring him next that would most cleanly tell the rules apart.
