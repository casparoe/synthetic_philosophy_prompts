Hey — I need a hand drafting something for Thursday.

I'm the engineer on our company's Vendor Risk Review Board (8 people: finance, ops, legal, me, and one SRE). We sign off on anything over a spend threshold, and I'm supposed to circulate a written opinion before the meeting with an actual recommendation. I keep going in circles on this one, so I'd like you to write the thing.

Setup — these numbers are mine and I've already sanity-checked them, so please take them as given rather than re-running the arithmetic:

We're re-procuring the service that sits in front of our payment pipeline. 40M requests a month.

- Corvus (incumbent): flat $41k/month, no variable component. Four years of logs, so we basically know what we're getting.
- Bittern (new): $5k base plus a surcharge per request that gets routed through the legacy interchange. The share of our traffic that goes that way after the migration is the thing nobody knows — call it p. Our SREs' guesses run from 0.2 to 0.8 and I don't think anyone can defend a tighter range. Surcharge is $0.0015/request. On a middle estimate of p, Bittern is clearly cheapest; at the top of the range it's clearly worse than Corvus.
- Egret (also new): $5k base plus the same $0.0015 surcharge, but per request routed through the *modern* interchange — i.e. indexed to (1 - p). p is a fact about our traffic and the networks, not about either vendor, so the two formulas move in opposite directions.

That gives a third option: sign both, split traffic 50/50. Then the variable part is $0.0015 × 20M × p + $0.0015 × 20M × (1-p) = a flat $30k whatever p turns out to be, plus $10k of base — about $40k, i.e. within noise of Corvus, and the thing we don't know stops mattering at all. It costs a seperate second integration (call it $60k one-time) and doubles the number of vendors who can screw up.

Here's the bit that makes this feel like a philosophy problem rather than a spreadsheet problem. In the straw poll, the same people who ranked Bittern below Corvus because p might sit at the top of the range *also* ranked Egret below Corvus — even though Egret is cheap in exactly the worlds where Bittern is expensive. Six of eight did both. A friend of mine who did some decision theory in grad school said this is basically the Ellsberg urn thing, that it means the board isn't ranking options by expected value under any single probability for p, and that this may or may not be irrational but it definitely means we should stop writing "expected value" in our minutes. I half understand it and I can't write it up in a way finance will accept.

One more wrinkle: Bittern's paper lets them set the surcharge rate anywhere in a band (0.0011–0.0019) thirty days after signature, by which point they'll have seen a month of our traffic. Egret's rate is fixed in the contract.

What I want is a memo, addressed to the board, that:

1. Commits to one recommendation — Corvus, Bittern alone, the 50/50 split, or send it back with a specific ask — with a concrete next step and a time frame (the contract window closes end of month).
2. Explains, in language that won't make finance and ops roll their eyes (assume theyve never heard "ambiguity" used as a technical term and will bail on anything that smells like a seminar), why refusing Bittern *and* refusing Egret is a different thing from simply being cautious, and what the board should do about the fact that it does both. I want the actual reasoning: what would have to be true for that pattern to be a mistake, and what would have to be true for it to be perfectly fine.
3. Gives the losing side its best shot. My own sense is that the strongest argument against whatever we pick runs something like: the split removes the unknown about p but not the unknowns about the vendors themselves, and if the two share an upstream dependency or just both turn out mediocre, we've doubled our exposure instead of halving it — and if Bittern can price after seeing our traffic, the real problem is a counterparty with better information and discretion, which is a contract-drafting problem, not a portfolio problem. Use that or a better version, but don't strawman it.
4. Ends with the two or three things we'd have to find out to flip the recommendation — a measurement, a contract term, a clause.
