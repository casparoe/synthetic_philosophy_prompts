I spent my career as a field biologist, and in retirement I have been reading about collective judgement. I keep meeting a pattern for which I have no word, and I would like to know whether one exists.

Two instances. A conservation trust on whose panel I sit funds species-recovery projects. Formerly the verdicts emerged from discussion among people of quite different training: a hydrologist, a soil chemist, two field botanists, a gamekeeper. The panel then adopted a common scoring protocol and a calibration workshop, so that everyone assessed applications in the same way. Individual scoring afterwards tracked outcomes better than before; the panel's majority decisions did worse, in that a smaller share of the projects it backed were still running five years on. Second: a national moth-recording scheme gave its observers a single identification aid. Individual recorders became more accurate, but the scheme's consensus determination of the genuinely difficult taxa — the pairs that separate two similar species — now goes wrong more often than it did when the identifiers disagreed freely.

What I take to be happening is that improving the individuals makes their mistakes coincide. Each member is better than before, yet they are now wrong about the same cases, and those cases are the hard ones, where everyone is below even odds, so that if my arithmetic is right adding further members drives the shared verdict further below chance rather than towards the truth. Had the improvement left the errors scattered, the group would have gained from it.

Does this configuration have an established name and literature? I know it lies near the jury theorem associated with Condorcet, but that result, as I understand it, assumes independent members each more likely than not to be correct, whereas here the independence is destroyed by the very improvement; and the popular material on crowd wisdom seems to assume what my cases lack. If there is a name, I want the concept defined precisely and separated from its neighbours — correlated or herding effects that arise from members copying one another, and from disagreement being suppressed by social pressure, neither of which is what is going on here. If there is no name, I should welcome a coinage with a definition, the conditions under which the effect appears, and the boundary between a homogenisation that helps and one that harms. A bare label will not serve: I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and how one could tell in advance whether a given reform will help or hurt. I should be grateful for a reply in British English.
