I run a small commercial printing shop, and last spring I kept a wide-format press running for seven months after it was already obvious the machine would never pay for itself; every week I told myself we had put too much into it to walk away. A friend who plays a lot of poker and reads about game theory for fun has spent several Sunday evenings trying to convince me that the lesson I eventually drew from that episode is either empty or self-refuting, and I cannot find the hole in what he says. His argument, as carefully as I can write it out: (1) Rational choice looks only forward, since the past cannot now be changed, so nothing that has already happened can be a reason to do one thing rather than another; only the future consequences of the options still open to me can count. (2) In the dollar auction — a $20 note sold to the highest bidder, except that the runner-up also pays his final bid and receives nothing, and each bidder ends up paying only his own last bid — every dollar I have already bid is gone whatever I do next, so it is sunk. (3) So, from (1) and (2), my past bids give me no reason either to continue or to stop; the only question is whether the next bid is worth making, and it is, because stopping now loses my standing bid for certain, while one more dollar risks only a single additional dollar against the chance of turning that loss into a win of the $20. (4) So at every point in the auction rationality requires me to bid again. (5) The other bidder faces exactly the same reasoning, so the two of us climb past $20 and both end up poorer than if neither of us had ever bid; and a rule that predictably ruins everyone who follows it cannot be the right rule. (6) Yet (1) is precisely what we appeal to when we condemn escalation of commitment in ordinary life, my press included. (7) And it proves too much: if nothing in the past can be a reason, then at the moment of performance it is irrational to keep a promise, to repay a debt one could get away with not repaying, to carry out a threat, or to finish anything one has begun, since the promise, the debt, the threat and the beginning are all in the past. (8) Being able to bind oneself is enormously valuable, and people who cannot be trusted do worse, so (7) is unacceptable and (1) must be false. (9) But if the past can be a reason after all, then the man who keeps funding the doomed project is not making any error, and the whole idea of the sunk-cost fallacy collapses.

I have tried the two escapes he offers and cannot make either of them work. He says the error in the auction is entering it, or failing to bind oneself in advance, rather than the continuing; so I invented a variant with no entry: you leave written instructions with an assistant to bid on your behalf while you are unreachable, and you land to find yourself in at $18, or, in the real-world version, you are handed responsibility for a failing project somebody else started and told to finish it or write it off. Forward-looking reasoning still says one more bid, and there was no moment at which you could have refused to play or tied your own hands. He also says rationality governs strategies or policies fixed ahead of time rather than single acts; but my variant has no ahead-of-time either, and even where it does, whatever policy I settle on, the moment I am actually in the auction it seems more profitable to abandon it than to follow it, so I do not see what the policy-level answer buys. If I have to choose, I would rather keep the claim that throwing good money after bad is a genuine mistake than keep (1) in full generality, but I would also rather not say that keeping a promise is irrational. Which step in the chain do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
