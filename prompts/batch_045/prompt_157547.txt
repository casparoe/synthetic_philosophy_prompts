I run a fortnightly reading group at my company — eight engineers, one cognitive scientist, nobody with philosophy training past a first-year elective — and for the last three sessions we have gone in circles about whether the systems we build are ever really *about* anything, or whether that is just a way of talking. The argument we keep replaying is the one between Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality (the man in the Chinese room manipulates symbols whose meaning is entirely borrowed from the programmers and the Chinese speakers outside, while our own thoughts mean what they do on their own account) and Dennett's reply built around the "two-bitser": a box with two binary input lines and two output lines, one for the sum bit and one for the carry bit, which takes two one-bit numbers in and puts out their two-bit sum. I have seen it presented in four variants: designed by an engineer who intended it to add; cobbled together by a tinkerer out of spare parts with no idea what the box does; produced by a random process, lightning striking a junkyard; and found already in service, its designer dead and its specification lost. The follow-up I take Dennett to care about: if one of these boxes develops a stuck bit and outputs the wrong sum, is there a fact of the matter about what it should have output?

Treat that summary as stipulated. I am not asking you to correct my Dennett or my Searle, and I don't want a bibliography or further reading — I can't check any of it, and a literature survey would be useless to me. What I want is a classification instrument I can turn into a small quiz on a static site for the group, so that we stop disagreeing about labels and start disagreeing about cases.

What I need: a decision tree that starts from a case, asks at most six questions, and ends at a named position. I will implement it as nested if/else, so every node must have at most three answers and every path must terminate in a leaf within six questions. The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of isms: take the questions first, note how many answer combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and tell me which combinations nobody occupies and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed).

Requirements:

1. Nodes are cases, not labels. Each node is a concrete scenario — a lookup-table two-bitser versus one built from transistors; a two-bitser with a stuck bit; a net trained by gradient descent to add, where nobody wrote the rule but somebody chose the loss; an interpreter running someone else's program; a weather vane and tree rings; a human following a rule by rote; a model emitting an answer to a question it was never asked. No node may name a position or a philosopher, and no node's question may use the words "intentionality", "intrinsic", "derived", "semantics", or "aboutness". An engineer who has never opened a philosophy book must be able to answer it.
2. Six leaves must be occupied by named positions: Searlean intrinsic intentionality resting on biological causal powers; the artifact view, on which a device's content is real but borrowed from designers or users; teleosemantics, on which proper function comes from a selection history and malfunction is thereby a real notion; informational or nomic-covariance semantics; the intentional-stance or real-pattern view, on which "really or merely?" has no further answer beyond the usefulness of the pattern; and the deflationary or eliminativist view, on which nothing literally has content. At least three further leaves should be combinations nobody defends.
3. Each leaf: a name (or "no one defends this"), one sentence of positive claim, one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
4. Flag every pair of leaves your fixed six questions cannot separate, rather than merging them, and give the extra question that would separate them.
5. Alongside the tree, give me a case bank of twelve cases as a table with these columns: case id; the scenario in one sentence; the axis it probes (history versus current disposition, the possibility of genuine error, implementation independence, parity with a human doing the same task, where the regress of borrowed content stops, whether consciousness or biology is required); then one column per named position from requirement 2, each holding one of four codes — "real", "derived", "as-if", "none"; and a final column saying whether the case discriminates and between which two positions. At least two cases should come out non-discriminating, because every position codes them the same way, and I want them marked so I don't put them in the quiz.

Deliverable, in this order: the table described in (5), then a single JSON object. I want no prose beyond what fits in the fields. The JSON should have these top-level keys:

- "tree": an object with "start" (a node id), "nodes" (array of objects with fields "id", "case_id", "question", "options" — an array of {"label", "text", "next"} — and "splits": one sentence on which positions this question separates and why), and "leaves" (array of objects with "id", "position", "claim", "awkward_commitment", "occupied" as a boolean).
- "space": an object with "questions", "possible_paths", "occupied_paths", and "empty_paths" — an array of {"answer_pattern", "diagnosis" (one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "unexplored"), "why"}.
- "unseparable": an array of {"pair", "reason", "question_that_would_separate_them"}.
- "known_failure_modes": an array of short strings, the ways this quiz will mis-sort someone.
