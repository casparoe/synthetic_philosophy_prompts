I work at a forty-person company that sells scheduling software, and I have been there since its second year. I designed and wrote essentially all of the core system — the part everything else depends on — and I am the only person who understands it end to end. The founder is the public face of the company: he gives the conference talks and the interviews, and in nearly all of them he says that I am the most valuable person there and that the product would not exist without me. He says the same thing to me in private, sincerely and often. What I cannot explain to myself is that I get nothing from it. It does not flatter me, it does not reassure me, and if anything I feel diminished by it. Meanwhile I find that I care a great deal about the opinion of one junior engineer on the team: she has no authority over me, she is not especially warm, and she will say plainly when something I have written is clumsy. Her approval actually lands and her criticism actually stings.

There is a further part I am less proud of, which I suspect is connected. Over the years I have quietly arranged things so that nobody else can fully understand the core system — no real documentation, no onboarding path into it, interfaces that only make sense once you already know the internals. I tell myself I have never had a free quarter to do it properly, which is true but not the whole truth. I think part of me is afraid that if someone competent could actually see and evaluate the whole thing, they might conclude it is not as good as I need it to be: that what I built is accumulated patches rather than design, and that I have been getting credit for being indispensable rather than for being good. So I have built myself a position in which I am safe and, apparently, unsatisfied. Everyone depends on me; the person who depends on me most is the one whose praise I cannot use; and the person whose judgment I want has no particular reason to give it to me. A friend who studied some philosophy said that recognition only really counts when it comes from someone you also recognize, and that arrangements like mine tend to be unstable — but she could not tell me whether that was a description of my situation or just an abstraction, and she had no suggestion for what to do on Monday morning.

I would like your help thinking it through properly. What exactly is going wrong in the structure here? Is the fact that my boss's praise does nothing for me a fact about him — that he is not in a position to give me what I want, because he needs me — or a fact about me, that I do not respect him, or both, and does the distinction matter? Why should it be a condition on getting what I want from someone's good opinion that I also grant them some independence or standing of their own? Concretely, what would actually change: does the answer have to be that I make my work legible and judgeable by people I respect and accept that they may find it mediocre, and is that a risk it makes sense to take at my stage of a career? Is there a version of the recognition I am after that I could get without that exposure, or is wanting it without the exposure incoherent? And is the thing I have built my safety on — being necessary rather than excellent — the same thing that is making the recognition worthless, or am I conflating two separate problems that happen to have grown together?
