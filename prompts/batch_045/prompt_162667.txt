Please construct the strongest argument you can for a position I currently doubt, so that I can test whether my doubt is justified. The position concerns what follows from the well-known finding that respondents from different cultural backgrounds answer philosophically loaded vignettes differently — Gettier-style cases, in which a protagonist has a justified true belief arrived at by luck, and name cases, in which a speaker associates a name with a description that in fact fits the wrong person.

The position to defend: such variation gives us no reason to restrict or reform the evidential use of intuitions in philosophy, because the variation is variation in how respondents resolve the underdetermination of a sketchy vignette, not variation in the concept the vignette is meant to probe. If that is right, the findings show something about the pragmatics of answering questions about hypothetical strangers, and nothing about whether there is a stable folk concept of knowledge or of reference.

Please build the argument around the following stipulative machinery, which I want used as the central tool rather than as decoration.

Call a response *ascriptive* when the respondent is reporting the epistemic standing of the character in the story, and *reliance-linked* when the respondent is reporting whether the proposition in question is one that someone in the respondent's own situation would be entitled to assert, act on, or rely on. These can come apart: a respondent can grant that the character's evidence is exactly as the story presents it while still withholding the knowledge attribution, on the grounds that they would not want to be the one to rely on it.

Call a respondent's *interpretive charity* the degree to which they treat the vignette's omissions as to be filled in with the epistemically favorable assumption rather than the unfavorable one. Then the hypothesis I want defended: baseline interpretive charity tracks the assumed epistemic distance between respondent and protagonist, where epistemic distance is a matter of how much background the respondent takes to be shared with the character. Low assumed sharing means the respondent treats the protagonist's stated information as the whole of the story and fills in conservatively; high assumed sharing means the respondent imports ordinary social and epistemic background and fills in generously. Cultural variation in the baseline of assumed sharing then generates the observed variation in answers, while the underlying concepts stay put.

Concretely, I want you to do the following.

Work the mechanism through at least two cases rather than asserting it. For the Gettier case, the underdetermined element is whether the protagonist did anything to check; for the name case, it is whether the speaker is embedded in a linguistic community whose usage transmits the name, or is an informationally isolated individual left with only the description. Show how a difference in interpretive charity predicts the direction of the reported difference in each, and be explicit about which reading — ascriptive or reliance-linked — the differing answers are most plausibly answers to.

Distinguish the resulting position from the cheap claim that the vignettes are simply badly designed, since a poorly designed probe threatens every use of intuition equally. Give a scope clause: say which philosophical uses of intuition survive on your account and which, if any, are genuinely damaged by the variation.

Steelman the rival explanation, that the variation reflects real divergence in folk concepts, in its strongest form, and explain why the interpretive-charity account should be preferred to it. Identify what kind of result would discriminate between the two, and note honestly if the discriminating evidence is not currently available to you.

Argue from the concepts and the cases; I do not want citations or a survey of the literature, and I would rather you say you are uncertain about a factual background claim than supply one.

Close by stating, for the argument you have just given, which single step is most likely to fail, and what specific finding or consideration would lead you to abandon the conclusion.
