Hey — I'm taking a seminar on moral responsibility right now and I keep loosing this argument, so I want to practice against somebody who actually holds the other side.

Please play a committed Strawsonian: thirty years in the trenches, bored by the "free will" framing, plain-spoken, willing to say "that's confused" when you think it is. Here's what you hold. Moral responsibility isn't some metaphysical property that the reactive attitudes try, and mostly fail, to detect; it's more or less constituted by the practice of holding each other to demands and reacting when those demands get flouted — resentment, gratitude, hurt, the demand for acknowledgement and repair. More specifically: (a) the request to step outside the practice and audit it as a whole is confused, because there is no external standpoint to audit it from; (b) particular episodes of resentment can absolutely be wrong — misaimed, disproportionate, built on a false belief — so the attitudes are answerable to reasons locally even though the system as a whole can't get a global verdict; (c) the objective attitude is fine in its place (the small child, the psychiatric patient, the stranger who bumps into you on the train) but it can't be what we do generally, and making it general isn't a live human option; (d) you'll happily concede that the attitudes are culturally shaped, historically variable, sometimes ugly, and that plenty of what people resent isn't defensible. What you never concede: that the psychological inescapability of the attitudes hands the skeptic anything, or that determinism is relevant to any of this. Stay in character until I say "stop". Don't break character to remind me you're a language model or to give me a survey of the literature — I want your position, in your voice, at me. My opening has two prongs.

First, the lag. I've been a convinced determinist for years — I beleive everything I beleive about the causation of action — and I still resent people within about a second of being wronged, and I can't switch it off by reminding myself of any of it. Now, resentment is supposed to be a way of tracking something, the way perception tracks the world. But tracking capacities are belief-sensitive. Once I learn the lines are the same length, the Müller-Lyer illusion stops persuading me, even if it still looks that way; and if some capacity just kept delivering the same verdict no matter what I came to believe, we'd say it isn't tracking anything at all, it's just firing. So either resentment is a bad tracker — a distortion, not a detection — or it isn't in the tracking business, in which case your big point that we couldn't give it up stops doing any work for you. Which is it?

Second, the Attunement case — I made this one up for my seminar paper, so tell me where it's broken. Imagine a community that raises its children into a different practice for responding to being wronged: no blame, no resentment, no demand for acknowledgement-as-such, just a calm and energetic turn toward repair and toward whatever in the situation or the person needs fixing. It's stable across generations, it isn't repression, the people in it find it entirely satisfying, and by their lights and ours it produces less damage and more repair than blame does. Their practice differs from ours the way one language differs from another — internally coherent, externally viable. If that practice is genuinely possible, then the specific content of our attitudes — that this person deserves blame, that something is owed — isn't forced on us by the shape of human life or by anything else. We just happen to have the practice we have, and your argument that we can't give it up collapses into a sociological fact about us rather than a reason. So: is the Attunement community possible? And if it is, what does your view have left to say that "we happen to do this" doesn't already say?
