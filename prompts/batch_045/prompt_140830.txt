I clerk for the Working Time Commission, the statutory body that decides what counts as working time under our Hours of Work Act. The Act caps working time at thirty-five hours averaged over twelve weeks, per employment relationship; it defines working time as time during which the worker is at the employer's disposal; and it permits hours above the cap only under an individual agreement that the Commission has certified as genuinely voluntary and not incompatible with the protection of the worker's health. The health limb was added after a run of well-publicised overwork deaths in the years before the Act. An application is now before us on which I need both a recommendation and, separately, a prediction.

Our precedents, as shortly as I can state them:

1. A night porter required to sleep on the premises, woken about three times a night: the whole overnight period is working time. But a maintenance engineer who must stay reachable and able to reach the plant within twenty minutes, and who is in fact called out about once a month: only the call-out time counts. We said the difference was that the engineer could genuinely use his evenings as his own.
2. Time a courier spends travelling between deliveries: working time. A sales representative's drive to the office: not working time, even though she uses it to rehearse pitches. A ride on a company shuttle during which a mandatory safety briefing plays: working time.
3. A paid Saturday session on stress management and wellbeing, with attendance recorded and noted in appraisals: working time, notwithstanding that it was for the workers' own benefit.
4. An unpaid weekend build week that the employer described as optional: working time, because we found that declining to take part was in practice a serious career disadvantage.
5. Time a platform courier spends logged in and waiting for offers: not working time, even though the platform's allocation algorithm penalises low acceptance rates. We said that economic pressure of that kind does not by itself place a worker at another's disposal.
6. An unpaid minister working sixty hours a week: outside the Act altogether, there being no employment relationship. A worker holding two jobs of thirty-four hours each: no breach, since the cap attaches to relationships and not to persons.
7. Two years ago, on facts much like the present ones, we dismissed an employer's application without reaching the merits, because it kept no records adequate to establish anyone's hours. The substantive question has therefore never been decided, and the present applicant has been careful to keep records.

The application now before us concerns a twelve-person software studio. No assigned hours, no timekeeping, no monitoring of when anyone is online. Each employee nominates a weekly window of core availability in a shared scheduling tool; colleagues may book meetings inside that window and the tool will not allow meetings outside it. Nominating a window shorter than twenty hours is permitted and has never been discouraged in writing; no one has ever nominated fewer than thirty. There is one mandatory half-hour meeting a week. A screen in the studio displays a contribution dashboard: each person's hours in the studio's chat and repositories, recorded automatically, ranked, and routinely quoted in promotion write-ups. Nobody is told to work long hours; pay is not tied to hours; the founder says publicly that she would do the work for nothing. Median hours are forty-eight a week. In the past year two employees have taken medical leave for stress-related conditions, and both said in exit interviews that no one had pressured them and that they loved the work.

The employer asks us to determine that no employee's working time exceeds thirty-five hours; in the alternative, to certify individual agreements of up to fifty hours, each signed by the employee, revocable on two weeks' notice, with an annual health check paid for by the firm. The employees' union objects. One employee has intervened in support of the employer, arguing that a refusal would infringe her right to work and that the Commission has never treated self-directed devotion to a calling as a harm to be prevented.

What I need from you:

First, a recommendation on both the determination and the fallback certification, identifying the closest of our precedents and how far it actually carries.

Second, the two or three principles, stated in general terms, that fit all seven precedents; what each predicts on these facts; and precisely where they come apart. I am especially interested in three places: whether structural or career pressure can put a worker at an employer's disposal in the absence of any instruction to work; whether the health limb can defeat an agreement the worker sincerely wants, given that we declined to police self-imposed labour in the minister's case; and whether an employer disposes of a worker's time by designing a system — the scheduling tool, the dashboard — that it does not itself operate.

Third, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly, and set out what principle we would be adopting under each of the available decisions, including what we would be committing ourselves to about overwork as partly self-inflicted harm and about whether the right to rest is waivable.

Fourth, the smallest variation of these facts that would separate the surviving principles — the dashboard removed, say, or the median at thirty-eight hours with no illness, or the employees holding profit shares — and what each variation would show.

Fifth, separately from the recommendation, a prediction of what the Commission will in fact do, with the reasons for expecting that outcome rather than the one you recommend.

Please argue from the precedents as stated and do not cite authority or supply a bibliography; I can find our own decisions.
