I'm retired now — forty years in a groundwater chemistry lab, mostly fitting transport models to contaminant plumes — so my instinct is always to ask what the units are and where the threshhold sits, and moral philosophy's habit of running on adjectives drives me up the wall. A reading group at the public library dragged me through a couple of sessions on suffering-focused ethics, and I came out convinced of one thing and thoroughly unsure about the rest. What I'm convinced of: preventing one hour of cluster-headache agony matters more than preventing a hundred person-hours of moderate pain (kidney stones, say), and it isn't close. The rule I came up with, which I'm calling the max-first rule until someone tells me it's silly: look at the worst single episode each option contains, and whichever option contains the worse worst-episode loses, full stop; only when the worst episodes are of comparable intensity do you tot up durations and headcounts. My worry is that this is just emergency-room triage dressed up as ethics, and that it breaks down somewhere I haven't noticed.

The rest of my verdicts, gut only, so you can see the shape of the mess. Ten hours of moderate pain in one person versus one hour of moderate pain in each of five hundred people: the five hundred, obviously. One hour of torture versus ten hours of severe-but-not-torture pain: the torture. One hour of torture versus a million person-hours of barely noticeable discomfort, rooms a degree too warm: here I wobble — max-first says the torture, but something in me says that at some point the sheer pile has to win, and I can't say at what point or why. A climber's legs screaming on the last pitch of a route she later calls the best day of her life: counts in full, and I don't want to hear about how that suffering was worth it — do not subtract the glory. A trial volunteer who consents in advance to an unpleasant month and says afterwards they'd do it again: somehow this one feels less bad to me, maybe two thirds as bad, and I can't tell you what the difference from the climber is supposed to be, since both of them consented. Pain under full anaesthesia, stress hormones and all, and a hypothetical drug that leaves every pain pathway firing but takes away the unpleasantness entirely: nothing to count in either case. Creating a person whose life would be mildly happy: no improvement, so no reason to create them. Creating a person whose life would be ecstatic except for one genuinely bad week: I'm inclined to say still no improvement, but I'm not confident. And the one I keep going back and forth on: a woman with severe chronic pain who says she is glad to be alive and does not want to die — my rule seems to imply something about her that I definately don't want to imply.

So: is there a principle that gets these, or close, and if not, which verdicts have to go? I'd much rather drop one of my own verdicts than bolt on a clause that exists only to save it — that's the habit of a man who spent his career throwing out parameters you can't identify from the data. Concretely, I'd like to know whether max-first can be repaired into something with bands of comparable intensity, or whether the honest move is to replace it with a weighting scheme, suffering weighted by intensity to some power — in which case tell me roughly what exponent my verdicts pin down and where that exponent puts the million-warm-rooms case. And please say what the resulting view predicts on two cases I haven't given a verdict for: insects and other small wild animals, and a small reduction in the probability of something catastrophic and suffering-dense in the far future versus a certain modest relief today. One more thing that's been nagging me: suppose it turned out — I half-remember someone arguing this from the way opioid systems regulate themselves, though I may well be mangling it — that every intense pleasure is followed by a homeostatic downshift that leaves the subject slightly worse off than before the pleasure started. I don't want the whole view to hinge on an empirical guess like that, so tell me whether the principle you land on is robust to it either way or whether it quietly assumes an answer. If whatever I'm groping toward already has a standard label, just give me the label; don't hand me a reading list, I've got enough stacked by the armchair as it is.
