Hi — I need an audit of an analogy, and I want it done properly rather than politely.

Context: I'm in the first year of a PhD, rotating through a microbial evolution lab, and I'm trying to turn a toy model into a short theory note. The system, stylised a bit: cells live in patches of about ten, founded by sampling from a global pool. Inside a patch they secrete an enzyme that digests the substrate, and the digested product diffuses, so non-secretors can feed without paying the metabolic cost. Secretors always lose to non-secretors within a patch, but patches with more secretors release more propagules. Then propagules are pooled and the next round of patches is founded from the pool. In the version I care about, the number of founders a patch contributes scales with its propagule output; if I instead hand every patch the same number of founders no matter what it produced, the whole between-patch effect disappears. (I think this is what people call hard versus soft selection, but I might have the labels backwards.)

My collaborator — senior, and very much in the camp that says do inclusive fitness properly and drop the group talk — keeps reaching for this picture: think of an organism as a company. The genes are employees, they're all paid in the same currency, which is getting into the next generation, and the company's rules — fair meiosis, Mendelian segregation, repair, checkpoints — stop anyone from cheating, so the employees all want the company to do well, and the company is what competes in the market. He uses it to conclude that the individual is the unit, that individual fitness is a proper quantity you can add up, and that whatever I'm seeing at the patch level is a by-product of individual-level selection plus the fact that patchmates are relatives. He's also said the same picture is why he doesn't lose sleep over within-genome conflict stories.

What I want:

1. The mapping written out explicitly — what plays the employee, the pay, the currency, HR, compliance, the market, bankruptcy, a whistleblower, a hostile takeover. A table is fine. Mark which rows are load-bearing and which are decoration.

2. Where it breaks, seperated into the breaks that matter for the use he's making of it and the breaks that are just noise (yes, genes don't literally have intentions, I know).

3. What the picture smuggles in that a genome doesn't have: a decider, a single objective function, an enforcer standing outside the parties, a boundary fixed by something like contract rather than by whatever the transmission system happens to be. Also — and this is the bit I keep going in circles on — the picture has the firm as the thing that competes, which sounds like group selection at the level of the organism. So is that a problem for him or for me?

4. Whether his conclusion survives. My suspicion is that the load-bearing premise isn't anything about genomes at all but the assumption about how patches contribute founders, plus maybe an assumption about whether patch-level traits are just the average of member traits or genuine properties of the patch (total yield rather than mean secretor frequency). Is the conclusion coming from the analogy, or is the analogy decoration on an argument that stands or falls somewhere else?

5. Two complications from my own system: there's a cytoplasmic element that only transmits through one of the two mating types, and a nuclear locus that biases transmission in its own favour. Where do those sit in the mapping — embezzler, strike, faction, something else? Do they break the single-currency premise, or can the company absorb them?

6. Is there a better analogy, or a patched version of this one that I should just accept and stop arguing with?

Please say how confident you are in each of the calls you make, and flag the one you're least sure of. I'd rather know which claim to go check than get a smooth answer.
