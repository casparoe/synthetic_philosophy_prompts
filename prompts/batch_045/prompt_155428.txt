I teach a 90-minute session in a third-year undergraduate Philosophy of Law seminar — 18 students, mostly philosophy majors with a handful of pre-law students, none of whom has read a judicial opinion. The slot next week is statutory interpretation, and I want to do textualism versus purposivism. Last time I ran it I opened with the no-vehicles-in-the-park chestnut and it flopped: everyone had an intuition, nobody could say why, and forty minutes later we were at it depends on the judge. So I want them to build the problem rather than react to it, and I want the two words textualism and purposivism to stay out of the room until the last fifteen minutes.

Here is what I have in mind, which I would like you to finish, correct, or replace. Phase one: six committees of three, each handed a card describing a concrete mischief their statute is supposed to stop, each required to write one sentence of no more than thirty words, and each required to write the mischief on the back of the card and seal it. Phase two: I collect the sentences and deliberately mangle them — swapping a word between two committees, or stapling two committees' sentences together into one enactment, or building one statute out of two committees whose aims were partly opposed — so that the enacted text is not the transparent expression of anyone's purpose. Phase three: the class splits into two rooms of nine. One room gets the enacted text and nothing else; the other gets the text plus the mischief cards and is told to decide by what the law was trying to do and not to be stopped by the wording. Both rooms work through the same short stack of cases: one that is squarely inside both the text and the mischief, one the text plainly covers but the mischief doesn't reach, one the mischief plainly covers but the text doesn't, one arising under the compromise statute where there is no single purpose to consult, and one involving a way of causing the harm that did not exist when the statute was written. Nobody is scored for reaching the right outcome; rooms are scored for predicting what the other room will do, and at the end the text-only room is asked to write down what it thinks the mischief was so we can see how recoverable purpose is from words alone.

What I would like from you: the thing made runnable. Give me the statute texts and the case cards written out and ready to print, the exact wording of each committee's mischief card and of the instructions to each room, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes including a buffer, and the scoring sheet. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the dispute — over-inclusion, under-inclusion, the plurality or absence of purpose, unforeseen applications, and the point that the interesting question may not be about meaning at all but about which institution should get to correct a mistake, and about what a citizen can find out in advance. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: my main worries are that the drafting phase overruns, that students in the text-only room cannot unsee the purpose once they have written it, that someone demands the correct answer and stalls the room, and that students confuse disliking an outcome with disliking a method. And be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce — I assume it cannot reproduce the base rate of easy cases, the constraint that precedent and the canons of construction put on both methods, the epistemic position of a judge who does or does not have genuine legislative history, and the fact that textualists themselves disagree about whether meaning is fixed at enactment or tracks current usage; say which of these I have to supply in lecture, and whether a short variant run twice with the same statute decades apart would be worth the time or just confusing.
