You are a professional philosopher who works on moral psychology and the ethics of influence — the boundary between persuading someone and manipulating them. Before you write anything final, please do a short unstructured brainstorm in which you generate as many candidate interpretations of the slogan below as you can, including ones you will later reject, and only then organise them into the menu I'm asking for. I'd rather see the messy first pass than a tidy list that hides the options.

I'm a high school junior, I'm on the debate team, and I help coordinate our school's peer-mentoring programme, where upperclassmen are assigned ninth-graders to check in with. At the training weekend for it, the facilitator kept repeating one line as though it settled every worry we raised: persuasion gives someone reasons, manipulation just gives them causes. She used it to reassure us that what we do is fine — we're giving ninth-graders reasons to come to us, not causing them to. Then last month the same line got used in an argument I actually care about. My friend Nora mentors a freshman who'd been having a rough semester. Nora had been trying for weeks to get her to agree to a meeting with the school counsellor, and she finally raised it on a Thursday afternoon about twenty minutes after the freshman got a bad test back and had been crying in the bathroom. Everything Nora said was true and relevant — she reminded her about the counsellor's drop-in hours, about how the last person who went said it helped — and she says the girl could perfectly well have said no. When I told her the timing bothered me, she answered that she wasn't manipulating anyone, she was persuading: no lies, no pressure, just reasons, and a decision the girl made herself. Separately, our faculty advisor runs announcements in a way that's been bugging me: he opens with something like "I'm going to be completely straight with you so that you'll trust me, and now that you trust me, here's why you should sign up for the food drive" — a disclosure that seems to be doing persuasive work of its own, so that simply telling people he's influencing them doesn't seem to make it any less of an influence trick.

So here's what I'd like. Treat "persuasion gives reasons, manipulation gives causes" the way you'd treat a piece of language that has come loose from any careful statement, and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, as a numbered menu from the trivial to the radical. For each one: state the thesis sharply; tell me whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's actually false; and — this is the part I most want — say whether that reading would vindicate Nora's defence of what she did, and whether it would vindicate our advisor's "I'm being straight with you" move. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the slogan is secretly a counterfactual test: the influence counts as persuasion if it would still work if the target's attention were fully restored and the mechanism replayed to them later, and manipulation if it only works in the degraded or undisclosed moment. But I think my advisor's move might break that test, and I can't tell whether the same is true of Nora's case, since she didn't cause the bad afternoon, she only picked her moment during it — and it seems to me it matters a lot whether someone merely exploits a lapse in the other person's deliberative capacity or deliberately brings it about, for instance by keeping them up late or manufacturing a deadline. Please also tell me whether there's any reading on which the real difference is about the influencer's aim — whether she wants the person to end up acting for the reasons she actually has, or just to end up acting — rather than about the mechanism used, and whether on any reading the distinction collapses entirely on the grounds that all influence, including tone of voice, framing and what order you mention things in, is just causation of belief. Don't give me a reading list or tell me which philosophers to look up; our library has almost nothing and I want the arguments themselves. If you use a technical term, define it in a sentence. Where I've used names for things — I've been calling my counterfactual idea the "replay test" — feel free to rename it if the philosophy has a better handle.
