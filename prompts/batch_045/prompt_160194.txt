Something happened last week that I can't stop turning over, and I want help taking it apart properly. I'm the senior postdoc in a small biology lab and I run our shared Slack. A rotation student put a figure into a draft; I hadn't seen her raw files, and in the lab channel I wrote something close to: "I'd want to look at the raw files before any of that goes in the paper." She's filed a complaint saying that I called her a fabricator — that I said she made her data up. In the meeting I said, come on, don't put words in my mouth, I never said that. The person running the process replied with something like "that's not a defense, we go by how a reasonable reader in the lab would have taken it, not by what you had in your head," and said it as if that settled it.

So I want a menu of the precise things "don't put words in my mouth" could actually be claiming, from the trivial to the strong, and for each one whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false — and, most importantly for me, which of them would actually do the work I was trying to make it do. Part of what I'm unsure about is whether her "reasonable reader" line is even a rival theory of meaning or just a policy about who eats the cost of a misunderstanding dressed up as one. And I keep circling the Grice-style distinction between what a sentence says and what a speaker conveys without saying: is the distinction doing any real work here, or does it collapse as soon as you ask what "says" means for a one-line Slack message?

Three wrinkles I'd like folded in, because they're where my case actually lives:

1. Mixed audience. There was an external collaborator in that channel who had none of the backstory. If the complaint is about what the "reasonable reader" took it to mean — which reader? Does a meaning get to be audience-relative in a way that matters for blame?
2. My own made-up category. I've been sorting what people convey into: what the sentence says on any way of disambiguating it; what a competent reader infers and I'd cheerfully confirm; and what a competent reader infers but which I set up so I could walk it back. I've been calling the third sort "deniable implicature," and I suspect the standard picture — where conveying something involves intending your hearer to recognise that you intend them to think it — doesn't even count those as cases of conveying, since the whole point is that I can deny the intention. Is the category real, and does it matter for how much blame I deserve?
3. Endorsement by omission. Earlier in the same thread someone else flagged a weird outlier in her data, and I replied without saying "no, that's fine." How much of what I conveyed came from what I conspicuously didn't write?

Give me the readings as a table with these columns: reading (one line) | the precise claim it makes | verdict (platitude / substantive and defensible / contested / false) | whether it would actually get me off in my case | confidence (high, medium, or low, plus one line on why) | the single thing that would most undermine this row. Then a short second table for the three wrinkles with columns: wrinkle | best reading of the slogan applied to it | does it help me | confidence. For everything, say how confident you are, and at the end single out the one thing you're least sure of. Don't bother with citations or reading lists — I want the disambiguation, not the bibliography.
