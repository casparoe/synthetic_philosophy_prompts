Here's roughly what the guy says, as best I can reconstruct it from memory:

"In a no-collapse theory every term in the superposition really happens, so there's no randomness anywhere in the physics — and that's exactly the problem, because the Born rule tells you the probability of an outcome is the squared amplitude, and you can't get probabilities out of a theory where nothing is chancy. There are two things to fix. First the qualitative worry: it looks like there's nothing left for probability to be about. The answer is self-locating uncertainty — before the measurement you know the multiverse will contain future copies of you who saw spin-up and future copies who saw spin-down, you just don't know which one you're going to be, so it's ordinary ignorance about where you are in the multiverse, basically the same as not knowing which copy is you after a duplication experiment. Second the quantitative worry: why the squared amplitude rather than some other function of it? Deutsch and Wallace showed that a rational agent who knows she'll have successors in all the branches, and who's about to be offered a bet on the outcome, is forced to set her credences equal to the squared amplitudes — if she doesn't, her betting policy is exploitable, she'll accept a series of bets that's guaranteed to lose her money. The trick is that after you coarse-grain, branches with a bigger amplitude are literally more numerous, so counting branches and weighting by amplitude give the same answer, and the agent's indifference between branches does the rest."
