I'm on the grants panel of a small charity that funds practical work to reduce suffering in wild animals — I'm the token natural scientist, thirty-odd years as a freshwater ecologist measuring nutrient loading before I retired, and now I mostly stare at deer and read philosophy, which my son-in-law finds hilarious. We decide everything case by case and rank applications by expected pain-days averted per pound, and I've started to worry that our track record doesn't add up to anything I could actually state as a rule — we've never written the priciple down. Over the last three years we have: funded high-welfare quick-kill traps for rats around two barns; funded immunocontraception for a fallow deer herd on an estate with no predator left, where most fawns die in their first winter; funded supplementary winter feeding for roe deer, but only with conditions about disease at the feeding stations; funded a rapid-response euthanasia team for wildlife injured in heathland fires, which we approved almost without discussion because the animals were already badly burned and the only real question was how fast they died; refused to fund a habitat-enrichment scheme for an already-thriving population — salt licks, better browse, the lot — on the grounds that we fund the reduction of suffering, not the provision of nice experiences; and refused, after a very long meeting, to fund a scoping study into suppressing a very widespread small mammal species whose lives are probably mostly nasty, with the minutes recording only that it was too speculative and too large-scale for us to touch.

This week we recieved a proposal from a big upland estate in partnership with the national park: a five-year programme to reduce a red deer population of about nine thousand by professional shooting to a high welfare standard — trained and tested marksmen, thermal imaging, mandatory follow-up of any wounded animal, independent monitoring of wounding rates — followed by fertility control on the survivors. Their modelling, which the vet on our panel says is optimistic, says the average deer year in that landscape contains more days of severe pain (winter starvation, injury, difficult calving) than of anything you would call enjoyment, although the summers are good and the animals are not currently in distress at all. Two wrinkles: the estate says culling will happen whatever we decide, but only to the standard we pay for; and our own ecologist thinks thinning the herd will let more calves survive, so most of the benefit is the suffering averted in the first five years rather than any lasting fall in numbers. We can fund the whole thing, fund only the monitoring and fertility-control components, or decline the lot.

I've been reading around in retirement and have picked up labels like negative utilitarianism, lexical priority and person-affecting views, and I half-suspect we have been running something close to the first while telling ourselves we are just being pragmatic. What I would like from you: set out the two or three principles that fit all six of our past decisions, say what each implies about this deer proposal and which of our precedents is the closest analogue, then give me your best guess at what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and why that guess beats the others. Be honest about what we would be committing ourselves to with each option — I would rather know that we are adopting a rule than stumble into one. And tell me what kind of application we would have to see to actually find out which rule we have been using all along. Please answer in British English, and keep it concrete: I can read a textbook, what I cannot do is see what our own decisions amount to.
