I write about climate and risk for a living, so I end up in a lot of press breifings where numbers get thrown around, and there's one line I keep hearing that I think its doing more work than it deserves. It came up on a climate attribution call last month: a reporter asked roughly how likely it was that this particular flood was caused by climate change, and the modeller shut her down with "there's no such thing as the probability of a single event" — all you can say is how often floods like it happen in a modelled world with our emissions versus a world without. Fair enough, maybe. But then a few weeks later a disease modeller used the same sentence to refuse to put any number on whether a specific outbreak started with a lab accident, and a philosopher friend used it to argue that the scary percentages people quote for AI risk are really just statements about how worried the speaker is.

I can't tell whether those three people mean the same thing, and I suspect the sentence is smuggling in several different claims I'd want to pull appart before I let anyone use it in a quote. So: lay out the distinct precise things it could be saying. Some of what I think might be hiding in there:

- a claim about meaning — statements of the form "the probability of X" only have content when X is a kind of event that can repeat;
- a claim about what there is — the world contains frequencies (or maybe tendencies or propensities of setups), but no single-case chances;
- a claim about what we could ever check — even if there were a single-case chance, no evidence could pin down what it is, so keep it out of a press release;
- a claim about whose state is being described — any number you hand me is some person's or some model's uncertainty, so there is no "the" probability;
- a claim about sorting — "this flood" can be filed under endless classes of event, and it has a different frequency in each, so there's no unique answer.

Say which of those are near-trivial, which are live and defensible, which are just false, and — the bit I actually care about — which of them would really license the modeller's refusal at that press conference. Because as far as I can tell he publishes ratios of frequencies for a living, and the reporter could have re-asked her question in exactly those terms, so I don't see that the slogan got him out of anything. Also: does any reading of it touch the lab-leak case, which is a probability about a one-off thing that already happened rather than the chance of something future? And someone told me physicists happily talk about the decay of a single atom as if it had a probability, which sounds like a counterexample — where does that fit, or is it a different sense of the word?

Last thing: give me your confidence on each verdict and single out the one you're least sure of, so I know what to push back on if I call you about it.
