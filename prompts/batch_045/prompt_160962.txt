I've been reading around on my own for a while (no philosophy or economics background, just whatever I can find free online, and a lot of that writing is pretty terible) and I've drifted into holding a position I want stress-tested: that Malthus was right but early.

Let me pin down what I mean so there's no wiggle room later. The claim is that there's a real mechanism where population tends to grow by compounding — doubling on itself — while the means of feeding people can only be expanded by something closer to fixed increments, so that living standards get pushed back down toward subsistence by some check, whether that's starvation and disease or people choosing to have fewer kids. And that this mechanism still describes the actual structure of the world; we just got a couple of enormous one-off reprieves that pushed the reckoning out by a couple of centuries — cheap fossil energy, the package of agricultural improvements in the twentieth century, and then the genuinely unexpected bit where getting richer made people want fewer children. On my version the reckoning doesn't have to be mass famine: the binding constraint might turn out to be not calories but something like phosphorus, topsoil, fresh water, or the capacity of the planet to soak up our waste. But the shape of the story is his.

So: what would count against this? I don't want a list of what critics say, I want what someone who actually holds the view should concede would count against it, and I want the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward.

The thing that worries me most is that "right but early" can absorb any amount of waiting. If I say it's coming, just not yet, and someone in 2300 says the same thing, am I making a claim or just expressing a mood? I've been toying with a rule for myself that I call 'charging rent': every century that passes without a check should cost the view some credibility, and there ought to be some number of centuries after which "early" collapses into plain "wrong". Is that a fair rule, or is it rigged? How much should two hundred years of being wrong about the timing have cost so far, and is 'early' even the right unit — should the deadline be measured in calendar years or in how many doublings of population the world has to get through before the squeeze shows up, since his original claim was about rates, not dates?

Some concrete things I'd like graded as evidence:

1. Population peaks somewhere around now and then declines nearly everywhere, material living standards keep rising, nobody is especially hungry, and that just stays true. Malthus's mechanism seems to need pressure from growth — does a shrinking, comfortable population count as a check, or as the theory simply no longer applying?
2. Energy becomes effectively abundant and materials get close to fully recycled, so the "fixed increment" side of the equation stops being fixed at all.
3. The fertility collapse. Is that a preventive check he actually predicted, in which case he wasn't early, he was vindicated and boring — or is it a completely different mechanism wearing his costume? This one feels like a fork to me and I can't tell which way it cuts.
4. The rival story — more people means more minds means more capacity — has obviously been partly true for two centuries. Is that evidence against Malthus, or just a temporary elasticity inside his mechanism?

Also: does this view predict anything its rivals don't, or does it only re-describe whatever happens afterwards? And if it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, is that a problem for this kind of claim specifically, or is it fine because the claim is about a mechanism and not a forecast?

What would move me most is an observation I or my kids could actually see that would settle it, since I can't wait three hundred years. I should say I have no library access, so please argue from the structure of the argument itself, not from who said what in the literature.
