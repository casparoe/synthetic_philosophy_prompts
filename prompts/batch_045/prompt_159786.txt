So my department — and basically every academic hiring committee everywhere — does this thing. We ask referees for a letter, and everyone agrees the letter is where the real information is, even though the letter almost never contains a single negative sentence. Someone writes that a candidate is unfailingly polite and that they have no doubt she'll do well wherever she lands, and the whole committee hears "not for us." Meanwhile the same committee also makes each referee fill out a grid with explicit numbers: top 5%, top 10%, top 25% for research, teaching, and so on. And here's the part I can't stop thinking about: when the numbers and the tone of the letter come apart, committees reliably go with the tone. The grid gets treated as noise, or as something the referee filled in carelessly, even though it's the only part of the file that's literally, checkably about the candidate.

I've heard there's a name for this distinction — something like what a sentence says versus what a speaker means by saying it, and a story about how hearers get from one to the other using shared expectations about how much to say, whether to be relevant, and so on. I'd like to know what this practice presupposes:

- What view of meaning does it only make sense under? It seems to need (a) a real difference between literal sentence content and speaker meaning, and (b) some account of how a reader recovers the second from the first that's reliable enough that a hiring decision can hang on it. Spell out what exactly has to be true for the practice to work.
- The deniability thing. Letter writers almost always keep literal deniability, and the institution lets them keep it — nobody accuses a faint-praise writer of having said anything false. What does that presuppose about where the norm of honesty bottoms out? That implicating something bad isn't asserting it, so it can't be a lie? Is that coherent, or is it a convenient fiction everyone maintains?
- Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Would a few rival accounts of how implicature works license the same practice, or does the fact that committees prefer the letter over the explicit numbers rule some of them out? I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
- The inconsistency I keep tripping over: we happily let meaning ride on implicature in letters, but everywhere else in the same process we insist on explicit, comparable, checkable statements — percentile grids, standardized teaching evals, written rubrics, a rule that every claim about a candidate must be backed by a documented example. Is that actually inconsistent, or is there something about the two channels that makes the split fine?
- And concretely: if we replaced the letter with something fully explicit — drop the prose, keep only the grid, or require that every evaluative claim be a flat checkable assertion — what would we lose? Don't say "nuance." Tell me specifically what information implicature can carry that assertion can't, if there is any, and what would break in the process.

One wrinkle I want you to take on too: imagine the opposite convention caught on. Letters are read strictly literally; referees are expected to state the bad stuff plainly; anything merely hinted at is treated as not part of the letter's content at all, so a committee is barred from acting on it (no reading between the lines, no phone calls to fill in the gaps). Is that a coherent practice? Where would it collapse — would referees just stop writing, would the real evaluation migrate somewhere else, or would the information simply stop being produced? Does that tell us anything about why the implicature-based version survives?

Please say how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
