I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about lying vs. misleading and I cant find a word for it.

The shape: nobody lies, and theres no single sentence you can point at and call misleading either. Every claim the person makes is true and, on its own, painfully honest. What does the work is *where* they spend their honesty.

Case one: someone is famous for being blunt about small stuff — they volunteer that they were late, that they got a number wrong, that they hate the plan — so everyone trusts them completely. Then in the one conversation that matters they do the opposite: answer a direct question with a true but useless detail, let someone elses wrong assumption stand, pick a technically accurate framing that points the wrong way. And it lands far better than it would coming from anyone else, because of a reputation built entirely out of true statements.

Case two: a researcher who discloses every limitation and conflict of interest in the ten pages nobody reads, and then writes the one paragraph the press will quote in a way that is true but that everyone reads backwards. Same thing: no lie, no clearly misleading sentence, credibility honestly acquired.

Its not that these people dont care about the truth — theyre punctilious about it, and that is exactly what makes it work.

Is there an established concept for this, or a literature on it? And if not, what would you call it, and how would you mark its edges? Does it matter whether the small-scale honesty is itself calculated or just who they are? What if they have no idea they do it, its only a habit? What if theyd be equally blunt in the big moment and the misreading is really the audiences fault? And the bit I actually care about: if this is real and common, does the usual claim that lying is worse than misleading still do any moral work, or does the difference collapse?
