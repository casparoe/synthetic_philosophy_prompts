I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy. I work in backend and infrastructure engineering, and I got into this sideways: I read a couple of popular books on the Buddhist no-self teaching, found the idea that identity is nothing over and above the continuities among changing parts both plausible and oddly hard to hold onto, and then found myself unable to stop restating the questions in systems terms. A running process is the only vocabulary I have for this, so please work with my cases rather than quietly substituting the standard ones from the literature. I have listed my gut verdicts; what I want from you is the principle behind them, if there is one.

A. A long-running service upgraded one component at a time over ten years, never restarted, state carried forward the whole way. Verdict: trivially the same process. The question doesn't even arise, and I notice that I would not accept an answer that said it had quietly died somewhere along the way.

B. Overnight the process is checkpointed to disk, the host is decommissioned and physically destroyed, and in the morning the checkpoint is restored on new hardware elsewhere. Verdict: still the same process. I would keep its name, its dashboards, its on-call rotation, and I would treat an incident from its past as its own history.

C. The process forks into two children with identical state; both keep running and immediately begin to diverge. Verdict: neither child is the original, and I don't think this is because we lack information — I think "which one is it?" has no answer at all. But I also don't want to say the original died, which seems like a different claim, and I find that I am genuinely uneasy at the prospect of undergoing C myself in a way I am not uneasy about A, even though I suspect the unease is irrational by my own lights.

D. As in C, except one child is killed a millisecond after the fork, before executing a single instruction. Verdict: this is just B again; the survivor is the original, no residue.

E. Three deterministic replicas, started separately in three regions from the same image and fed identical inputs, so that they are bit-identical at every moment. Verdict: three processes, not one process in three places.

F. The original hardware is scrapped and the process is moved, live and continuously, onto an emulator running a different instruction set. Verdict: the same process, and more clearly so than in B, even though nothing about the substrate survives.

G. A rollback: the process is restored from yesterday's checkpoint and a day of accumulated state is discarded. Verdict: still the same process, and I am much less troubled by that loss than I am by C — the discarded states simply never happened, as far as what is running now is concerned, and that seems acceptable to me in a way that C does not.

H. Two branches of the same service that diverged a year ago are manually merged into a single running process, with conflicts resolved by hand. Verdict: I don't want to call the result either branch; it feels like a new thing, and I feel something close to the unease of C.

I. A ten-year migration in which every module is replaced one at a time, data moved across as it goes, until nothing of the original code, data layout or host remains — and no moment you can point to where it changed over. Verdict: I still want to say the same system, and if you told me it stopped being the same I could not tell you when.

Here is my tentative stab at what I believe, stated as carefully as I can: a later thing is the same as an earlier one just when (i) there is an unbroken chain of the right kind of causal and functional dependence carrying enough of the earlier structure forward, (ii) this does not require unbroken spatiotemporal continuity, and (iii) no competitor existing at the later time stands in an equally good relation to the earlier thing.

What I would like from you:

Test that principle against the nine cases and tell me where it gives my verdicts, where it forces one I rejected, and how it would need to be repaired. Be precise about clause (iii): I can see it is doing the heavy lifting in C and D, and I suspect it can be stated non-circularly, but I can't do it myself. Say what counts as "enough" in clause (i), and whether my contrast between A and I, where the change is large but gradual, and H, where it is large and abrupt, is something the principle can generate or something I have to stipulate separately.

Then give the principle's verdicts on four cases I have not listed: a fork whose branches initially differ by a single bit and then diverge further; a fork followed an hour later by a merge of the two branches; a process whose state is progressively sharded across a cluster until no single machine holds any of it; and a fork in which one branch runs on while the other is immediately written to cold storage and never restored — that last one feels to me like it could go either D's way or C's way and I cannot settle it.

Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the best available principle cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn if you had to drop exactly one. And answer the question I actually started with: does the principle you end up with entail the no-self conclusion in anything like its Buddhist form — that there is no further fact about who persists beyond the continuities, and that identity is therefore not what matters — or does it leave room for a further fact of the kind that conclusion is supposed to deny? If it does entail it, say what follows about whether my unease in C is irrational, and whether "not what matters" is supposed to dissolve it or just to overrule it.
