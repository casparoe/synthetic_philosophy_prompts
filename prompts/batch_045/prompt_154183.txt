I'm one of six people on the safety release board where I work. We make the control firmware for autonomous forklifts that drive around warehouses, and nothing ships until all six of us sign off. For the last couple of years we've had a model checker in CI that proves about 1,100 safety properties per release from a formal spec — stuff like "the mast never lowers while a person is in the aisle." Roughly 3-4% of them, typically 35 to 45 per release, the solver can't settle: it times out or blows up on memory. Today we handle those by hand — strengthen an invariant, add a runtime guard — and it costs us around two engineer-weeks per release.

A vendor has offered us an add-on to the checker, which I'll call the Gap Estimator. For any property the solver can't settle, it outputs a number between 0 and 1. They say it's calibrated: among properties it scored near 0.95 on their benchmark corpus, about 95% turned out to be provable once somebody put in the effort. It's trained on features of the property and of the solver's partial progress, not on any actual proof.

The proposal in front of us is to let those numbers gate the release: anything scoring above 0.98 gets waived with a runtime monitor, everything else blocked as today. They also suggest using the numbers to triage which of the 40-ish open properties we spend our two engineer-weeks on. We have to decide by Thursday and the board is split pretty much down the middle.

Half of us say: this is just uncertainty about our own ignorance, we already attach probabilities to component failure rates and nobody thinks that's spooky, and calibration on held-out data is the only test that matters. The other half say: these aren't events. Either the property follows from the spec or it doesn't, there's no chance involved, and the 95% is a frequency over which properties the vendor's customers happened to work on — it tells you about the estimator's inputs, not about the math. Their real worry is that once the numbers exist, people will start multiplying them and doing expected-value math on them, and then consistency across logically related claims starts to matter.

Three things came up that I think are the actual crux, and I'd rather you reason about these than about anything general:

First, I ran my own test before the last meeting. I took one property and encoded it two provably equivalent ways (one direct, one going through a lemma I'd already machine-checked). It returned 0.91 and 0.97. That gap is annoying but it's not nothing — 0.91 is blocked and 0.97 is waived, and both encodings are the same claim. I also fed it P, Q, and literally the conjunction P-and-Q as three separate inputs, where Q was provably implied by P and another property we'd already proven; it gave 0.96, 0.94 and 0.31. The vendor's answer was that these are independent point estimates, they never promised the joint outputs would be coherent, and near the 0.98 threshold calibration is what matters.

Second, someone in QA proposed that we stop arguing about what the numbers mean and just score them with bets: log every number, and when a property is eventually settled one way or the other, settle it as if the estimator had bought or sold a ticket at its own quoted price. She built a small demo showing that with the P / not-P style incoherences you can assemble a set of tickets that loses money no matter how the math actually comes out, without knowing anything at all about forklifts. So maybe the acceptance criterion shouldn't be calibration at all but "no cheap strategy can reliably extract money from it." I don't know how to weigh that against the calibration numbers, which look good.

Third, our most theory-minded engineer says this whole thing is a known problem, "logical induction" or something like that, and that you can't have it both ways: anything computable that stays perfectly consistent with respect to entailment at every single moment will fail to converge to the truth on some true patterns, so demanding consistency-at-all-times is demanding the impossible, and the realistic ask is that it converges eventually and isn't exploitable in the meantime. I have no idea whether that's a real result or something he half-remembers from a blog post, and I'm not going to go read a paper to find out. Also, we're never going to wait for "eventually" — our release is Thursday. Separately: their demo happily scores statements about itself, like "the score for this property after the refactor will be above threshold," and it output 0.99 for one that was basically "this number is wrong." One board member wants us to just hard-ban self-referential inputs.

Could you draft the memo I circulate before Thursday? It should:

- give a bottom line the board can vote on — adopt, adopt with conditions, pilot, or reject — with a time frame and what we'd actually measure;
- be readable for the two board members from hardware and ops, who will check out the moment anything sounds like philosophy (I have no philosophy background either, so please don't assume any jargon);
- say plainly what we'd be buying: are these numbers probabilities that a claim is true, or expected frequencies of "we'd manage to prove this if we tried," or something else entirely — and does that choice change what we're allowed to do with them, like multiplying them or doing EV math on where to spend the two weeks;
- treat the triage use and the release-gate use separately, because my gut says those are held to different standards and I can't articulate why;
- tell me how much weight to put on the "you can't have both consistency and convergence" claim, and whether it changes anything for a board that only cares about the next four releases;
- include one paragraph the losing camp would grudgingly accept.

Keep it short enough to actually send, maybe 800 words, and recieve-able by people who don't care about this as much as I do.
