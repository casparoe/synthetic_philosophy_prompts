I'm writing a paper for a moral philosophy seminar on whether facts about brains should change how much a being counts, and I keep getting stuck before I even begin. My supervisor's line is that brain size is never what matters directly, only a stand-in for something else; but when I ask what the stand-in is supposed to be, I get a different answer every time (total neuron number, cortical neuron number, synaptic density, integrative complexity, richness of the conscious repertoire, capacity for suffering, higher-order or narrative capacities, or something relational). So before I commit myself to a view I want the specification. Assume the theory is meant to be usable as an input to actual decisions: how to weight a pig against a chicken against a shrimp against a human in an institutional cost-benefit exercise, and how to rank welfare research and spending when the evidence is thin. So it has to output something like a weight or at least an ordering, and it has to do so from measurements we could in principle gather. Given that purpose, write the desiderata such a theory has to meet.

For the motivating cases, please work with the ones below rather than substituting your own favourites, since these are the ones my seminar actually fought about. First, the dissociation cases: as brains get larger, neuron density falls and tissue is allocated differently, so absolute size, total neuron count, and forebrain neuron count come apart, and any theory must say which currency it tracks and why, and what it says when the currencies disagree. Second, a stipulated duplicate-module species: its nervous system contains two complete, functionally redundant copies of the same processing machinery wired in parallel, so it has twice the neurons of its single-copy conspecific while remaining behaviourally indistinguishable and, stipulate, having one unified stream of experience rather than two; does its moral weight double? Third, a stipulated insulated-upgrade variant, where extra tissue is real, metabolically expensive, and computationally sequestered from anything valence-relevant. Fourth, a slow-giant species: enormous neuron numbers with signalling and integration an order of magnitude slower, so that it may live at a different subjective tempo — say what the theory owes us about whether what matters is richness per moment or number of moments per unit clock time. Fifth, variation within a single life: the same individual as a juvenile and as an adult, after a large-volume resection, and the sort of clinical case where adults are reported to function near-normally with drastically reduced cortical mass (take the case as stipulated if you doubt the reports). Finally, the very small: animals with brains of a fraction of a gram that nonetheless learn flexibly and reversibly adjust their behaviour.

Alongside the cases, I want you to police some distinctions I think people collapse: whether neural quantity is evidence of status or constitutive of it; whether what varies is status at all rather than how much welfare is at stake; whether the quantity tracks a being's welfare or the badness of its death; and whether the theory is supposed to scale within a species as well as across them. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Then tell me which subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most work in knocking out otherwise popular theories. Please keep the weight on the criteria rather than on scoring the views, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or further reading — I have library access and will do that part myself. Structure your answer in these sections and lengths: Currencies (what the theory's verdicts track, about 120 words), Requirements (numbered, each with its motivating case and a non-negotiable/negotiable tag, about 600 words), Where the List Breaks (about 200 words), The Most Exclusionary Requirement (about 150 words), and Scorecard (brief, about 200 words, on sentience-based views, aggregate-neuron or capacity-aggregating views, higher-order or personhood views, information-integration views, and relational or social views).
