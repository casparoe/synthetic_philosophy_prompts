I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of cognitive science, and we have spent nearly a month on the extended mind thesis. What strikes me is not that people disagree but that the disagreement looks structurally frozen. On one side there is the parity argument — if a process outside the skull plays the same functional role in guiding behavior as one inside it, then there is no principled basis for counting one as cognitive and the other as mere input — together with the suggestion that the boundary question should be settled by which framing yields the best explanation of the task rather than by anatomy. On the other side there is the objection that causal coupling is being quietly converted into constitution, the demand for a real mark of the cognitive that notebooks and smartphones simply do not have, and the further claim that the extended hypothesis and its more modest embedded rival predict nothing differently, so no evidence could decide between them. Overlaid on all of this is the later literature that reframes the issue in terms of integration, trained practices, and the designed structure of the environment rather than in terms of vehicles and boundaries.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not about the location of the mind at all, but about what the predicate cognitive is supposed to do in a scientific theory: whether it names an explanatory kind that has to earn its keep by supporting its own generalizations, breakdown patterns, and methods, or whether it is a role-term that anything with the right functional organization can fill. If that is right, the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, and the notebook example that anchors the literature is functioning as an intuition pump rather than as data.

I want to test that hypothesis against a construction of my own, because I am not confident it survives. Call a component constitutively involved in a system's cognition just in case, for every manipulation that changes the timing, reliability, or availability of that component's outputs, there is some manipulation of an intracranial component that produces the same change in task performance, and conversely. This substitutional symmetry condition can be satisfied in principle by external resources: imagine an implantable memory device whose retrieval latency, cue dependence, and degradation under interference match those of hippocampal retrieval, and a notebook whose breakdown profile under stress and distraction mirrors a documented amnesic profile. My worry is that both sides can absorb this result without moving. The critic says symmetry of manipulation profiles is just a dressed-up version of parity and begs the question; the proponent says the demand for a mark of the cognitive is a stipulation with no independent motivation, and that explanatory fruitfulness is all we ever had.

So: what kind of standoff is this? A verbal dispute about cognitive? A substantive disagreement about which cases count as data? A difference about the standards a theorized kind has to meet, which neither side can argue for? Or a hidden empirical question — whether real coupled systems actually exhibit the relevant profile — that happens to be discussed as if it were conceptual? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a formal criterion like the one I sketched, an experiment, a reframing of the question, or nothing at all? I would rather have a diagnosis with reasons than a survey of the positions.
