I'm on the committee that writes my department's PhD comprehensive exam in political philosophy and social choice theory, and this year the assignment I drew was the question on Sen's liberal paradox, the impossibility result about a Paretian liberal. The candidates we see are well drilled. Nearly all of them can reproduce the original example with the prude and the lewd and the single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, state the three conditions in roughly Sen's own form (unrestricted domain, a Pareto condition, and a minimal liberalism condition giving each person decisiveness over some pair of alternatives), derive the resulting cycle, and then recite Sen's own reply that the conflict depends on meddlesome preferences and disappears once Pareto is applied only to the non-meddlesome parts of people's rankings. I want a question on which that repertoire earns a B at best, and on which an A requires actually doing some work.

What I have in mind is to force them out of the three-alternative textbook example into a slightly more structured setting that they cannot have memorized: say three individuals, with the set of social alternatives given as a product of personal spheres, where each person has a protected coordinate with two states (whether they read a certain book, wear a certain garment, post a certain thing) and the alternative space is the full product of those coordinates. Rights are then formalized as own-sphere decisiveness: each person is decisive over every pair of alternatives that differ only in their own coordinate. Alongside that, a Pareto condition of your choosing, stated precisely. In that setting I want them to do three things. First, reconstruct the impossibility, producing an explicit profile that forces a cycle, and identify which structural feature of the profile is doing the work. Second, settle a claim I have been turning over and that I suspect is correct, but which I want proved or refuted rather than asserted: suppose a condition I will call Sphere-Deference holds, meaning that for every individual i, every sphere j, and every pair of alternatives differing only in coordinate j, i ranks that pair exactly as j ranks it. Then, assuming nothing beyond the transitivity of individual preferences, the rights constraints and the Pareto constraints cannot conflict, because every rights-generated social preference is already unanimous and hence already Pareto-generated, so the union of the constraints is contained in the Pareto relation, which is acyclic as the intersection of finitely many strict partial orders. If that argument goes through, they should also say what it buys and what it costs: it apparently yields a complete and transitive social ordering satisfying both conditions, and it pays for that by giving up unrestricted domain, since Sphere-Deference is a restriction on which profiles may arise.

Third, and this is the part I care most about, I want them to say whether the meddlesome-preferences reply really dissolves the theorem. The reply is usually implemented by laundering preferences: replacing each person's ranking of other people's spheres by the owners' own rankings, then applying Pareto to the laundered profile. As far as I can tell, laundering is not well defined for pairs of alternatives that differ in more than one coordinate, and the natural ways of carrying it out either leave cycles in an individual's laundered relation, so that there is no ordering for Pareto to be applied to, or else quietly collapse into Sphere-Deference, that is, into a domain restriction that Sen's theorem never claimed to rule out. I would like them to settle this one way or the other: either give an explicit profile in which laundering produces a cyclic laundered relation for some individual, or show that a canonical implementation always yields an ordering, and then explain what the example or the proof shows about whether the meddlesome-preferences reply is a dissolution of the paradox, a domain restriction, or a restatement of it in different vocabulary. Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 to 900 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with marks assigned to each, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. The question should be self-contained: define the alternative space, the rights condition, and the Pareto condition in the question itself, so that a candidate who has forgotten the details of Sen's original presentation can still attempt it and so that answering requires no sources, citations, or further reading. Aim it at a strong candidate working under exam conditions for about ninety minutes.
