I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar on collective rationality and the law, and this semester's take-home examination included the following question on judgment aggregation. Below it is one student's answer, reproduced as submitted. I need it graded: a mark out of 25, the letter grade it corresponds to, and then specific comments I can hand back to the student — organized part by part, stating what is correct, what is wrong or only partly right, and exactly why, with genuine errors clearly distinguished from loose or imprecise phrasing. Where an error is easy to miss on a quick reading, say so explicitly. Please do not rewrite the answer; the assessment is what I need. Everything required is contained in the question itself, so no sources are needed.

**Question 4 (25 points).**

A state intermediate appellate court decides breach-of-contract actions in panels of three. In the case before it, the governing doctrine can be represented as follows. Let

- **P** = the parties formed a valid contract
- **Q** = the defendant's non-performance was material
- **R** = no legally recognized excuse applies
- **F** = the contract was valid and the non-performance was material (the condition for shifting attorney's fees under a separate fee statute, which states its own test in terms of validity and materiality)
- **L** = the defendant is liable in damages

The panel is required to return a verdict on each of P, Q, R, F, and L, and every judge accepts two entailments: F holds if and only if P and Q both hold, and L holds if and only if P, Q, and R all hold.

The judges vote as follows:

| Judge | P | Q | R | F | L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aldrich | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Berrio | yes | no | yes | no | no |
| Cho | no | yes | yes | no | no |

**(a)** *(6 points)* Determine the outcome under the premise-based procedure (majority votes on P, Q, and R, with everything else derived) and under the conclusion-based procedure (majority vote on L). Then explain why the panel's difficulty counts as a discursive dilemma rather than as an ordinary disagreement among the judges about the facts, or as an instance of Condorcet's paradox.

**(b)** *(7 points)* Does sorting the propositions into premises (P, Q, R) and conclusion (L) dissolve the difficulty? Address in your answer the fee proposition F, and whether the answer depends on how the doctrine is formulated.

**(c)** *(6 points)* State the familiar impossibility theorem for judgment aggregation (the result often called the List–Pettit theorem), specifying the conditions it invokes, and explain what it does and does not rule out. Is the conclusion-based procedure a counterexample to it?

**(d)** *(6 points)* The chief judge proposes the following practice: the panel announces the majority verdict on liability and then states as its reasons only those premise findings that are consistent with that verdict, omitting or qualifying any finding that would entail the opposite outcome. Assess the proposal: does it avoid the impossibility theorem, and if so by violating which of the theorem's conditions? What does it cost the institution?

**Student's answer**

**(a)** Under the premise-based procedure the panel takes majority votes on the three conditions: P carries two to one, Q carries two to one, and R carries three to nothing. All three conditions are therefore satisfied, and F and L are derived from them, so the plaintiff recovers damages and fees. Under the conclusion-based procedure the panel takes the majority vote on L alone, which is two to one against liability, so judgment is entered for the defendant and there are no fees. The two procedures disagree in the strongest possible way: the one yields liability with fees, the other yields neither.

This is a discursive dilemma because each judge's individual set of verdicts is fully consistent and closed under the two entailments, while the proposition-by-proposition majority set — P, Q, R, not-F, not-L — is not. That set asserts P and Q and denies their conjunction, and it asserts everything the doctrine makes sufficient for L while denying L. It is not an ordinary factual disagreement, because the difficulty is not localized in any single proposition: R is unanimous, and both P and Q carry clear majorities, so there is no one finding whose revision would make the problem go away. What fails is the combination. Nor is this a Condorcet-style cycle: no set of pairwise majorities cycles here, and the defect in the majority set is not the intransitivity of a social preference ordering but a failure of logical consistency and of deductive closure.

**(b)** Sorting the propositions into premises and conclusion does dissolve the difficulty. The three conditions P, Q, and R are logically independent of one another, so taking majority votes on them cannot generate an inconsistency: any assignment of truth values to three independent propositions is consistent, and whatever follows from a consistent set is consistent with it. The premise-based procedure therefore guarantees a collectively rational judgment, and the discursive dilemma turns out to be an artifact of letting the court vote separately on the conclusion.

The fee proposition F does not change this. F is not an independent premise but the conjunction of P and Q, and the fee statute merely attaches a consequence to what the court has already found. Once the panel has found P and Q by majority, F follows, and no separate vote on F is needed; taking one is simply the paradox reappearing one level up, and a court that has committed itself to the premise-based procedure should decline to take it. Nor is the outcome hostage to how the doctrine happens to be spelled out. The legislature fixed the premises when it wrote the conditions for liability: P, Q, and R are the conditions the doctrine lays down, L is the legal consequence drawn from them, and that distinction is a feature of the doctrine rather than a drafting choice available to the court.

**(c)** The theorem states that no judgment-aggregation rule satisfies all of the following: universal domain (the rule accepts every profile of individually complete and consistent judgment sets and returns a collective judgment set), collective rationality (the collective set is complete, consistent, and deductively closed), anonymity (no judge carries special weight), and systematicity (a single proposition-wise voting rule applied independently and in the same way to every proposition on the agenda). On an agenda rich enough to contain two propositions together with their conjunction — as this one is, since it contains P, Q, and F — no rule meets all four conditions. If anonymity is dropped, the rules satisfying the remainder are dictatorial or oligarchic. Majority rule satisfies the other conditions but not collective rationality, which is why it produces the dilemma in part (a).

The theorem therefore shows that collective rationality is unattainable for a panel in this position: whichever procedure it adopts, its collective judgment on this profile will be inconsistent, since the impossibility holds for every procedure and every profile. The conclusion-based procedure is the nearest thing to an escape. It is anonymous, it applies majority voting, and its output is a single proposition together with its negation, and a single proposition cannot be inconsistent with itself. So it satisfies the conditions of the theorem apart from the completeness requirement, which shows that the theorem is really a constraint about completeness rather than about collective rationality.

**(d)** The chief judge's proposal does avoid the impossibility, but only by giving up systematicity and completeness. It is anonymous, since both the verdict and the findings are determined by majority votes, and its published output is always consistent, because any finding that would contradict the announced verdict is withheld. Given that it is anonymous and always returns a consistent collective judgment, it meets every condition of the theorem except systematicity, which suggests that systematicity is doing all the work in the impossibility result.

The proposal is also not, as stated, a genuine aggregation rule. If the announced verdict is not-L, the majority findings on P, Q, and R all point the other way, and something must give: the opinion must either stay silent about R, or stay silent about F, or report a finding on some condition that a majority did not in fact make. Nothing in the proposal says which, and the choice matters, since suppressing R and suppressing F yield different opinions and different precedents. The rule is therefore indeterminate until a tie-break is supplied, and whichever tie-break is supplied can be controlled by whoever drafts the opinion. The cost to the institution is that published reasons cease to be a report of what the panel actually decided and become whatever reason can be offered for an outcome reached by other means.
