I have recently retired from a career in experimental fluid mechanics, where I spent most of my working life on problems in which everyone accepted the governing equations and the arguments were about which terms could legitimately be neglected. For the last three years I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group on the evolution of cooperation, and one exchange in it has not advanced by a millimetre in that time, which I find at once fascinating and irritating. Take the ordinary two-player game with the usual payoff ordering (temptation better than mutual cooperation better than mutual defection better than the sucker's payoff, with mutual cooperation also beating an even chance of the two extremes). One camp says: in a genuine one-shot encounter with a single opponent, no iteration, no communication, no reputation, no correlating device, defection strictly dominates and the unique equilibrium is mutual defection, so defection is not merely permissible but the only rational act; the whole apparatus of reciprocity only engages once some mechanism — a long enough shadow of the future, network structure, reputation transmission, assortment — makes the encounter effectively not one-shot. On that view every argument for cooperation in the one-shot case has either quietly changed the game or quietly changed the subject from what an agent should do to what a population had better be like. The other camp says: if the two players are relevantly identical — same information, same capacities, in fact the same decision procedure run twice — then the mixed outcomes are not available, and of the two outcomes that are available the cooperative one is better for each; and moreover in evolutionary settings the things that are selected are not agent-tokens but rules and dispositions, so the right question is which procedure to be rather than which act to perform. Each side has a stock reply it deploys and then stops: the first says the second has smuggled in a correlation, which is simply one of the known mechanisms and so concedes the point, or else has redefined 'rational' so that it no longer means payoff-promoting; the second says the first has begged the question by individuating acts causally and treating the two deliberations as causally and informationally independent, which is itself a contingent claim about how the world is wired.

My own hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the fight is not really about the word 'rational' or even about decision theory, but about a parameter nobody writes down: the degree of what I have started calling procedural overlap, meaning the extent to which the physical processes that produce the two choices share causes, so that the pair of outcomes is drawn from something narrower than the full product of two independent choices. Index the question by that quantity and the dispute seems to dissolve — at full independence the classical answer looks right, at full overlap the symmetry answer looks right — and the substantive question becomes an empirical one about what the quantity actually is in real populations of animals or people, which looks to me like a close cousin of the assortment and relatedness parameters that do the work on the population-biology side of what we read. But an evolutionary theorist in the group objected that this cannot be the diagnosis at all, because the one-shot case is defined by stipulating that correlation away, so there is no parameter left to index over in the case under dispute, and my quantity only has room to vary in exactly the evolutionary settings where nobody denies that assortment matters. I genuinely cannot tell whether that objection refutes my hypothesis or is itself evidence for a different one: that a large part of this debate is verbal, a fight about what the phrases 'one-shot' and 'single opponent' are being stipulated to exclude, with each side's stipulation quietly loading the answer.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I am not asking for a survey or for anything to read. Is this a verbal dispute, or a substantive one; and if substantive, is it a normative disagreement about what 'rational' and 'should' mean, a formal disagreement about how an agent's options and their counterfactual structure should be represented, a disguised empirical question about how much procedural overlap real agents have, or two or three questions running under one heading — the rationality of an act-token, the fitness of a rule-type, and the advice owed to someone choosing which procedures to build? Where does the burden of argument actually sit: must the first camp defend the independence assumption, or must the second defend a change of formalism? Tell me whether my overlap parameter is worth keeping, whether it needs to be split into two or collapses into something already familiar, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, a computational or behavioural experiment, a shift to a different question — or give me an argument that no progress should be expected here and say why. One last thing, from my own disciplinary bias: when a model's equilibrium set becomes very large, as it does in the iterated game once the continuation probability is high, I would expect the explanatory burden to move off equilibrium properties and onto dynamics, initial frequencies and drift. Say whether something analogous is true of the iterated case here, or whether that is a false analogy imported from a science where the equations were never in dispute.
