I teach a 75-minute upper-level seminar on the epistemology of aesthetics: 14 students, no screens in the room, materials limited to index cards, sealed envelopes, and poker chips. Assume they already know the general epistemology of testimony — chains of informants, reductionism vs. anti-reductionism, how reliability decays down a relay — but that nobody has read anything on the aesthetic case specifically, and that no one says the word aesthetic or testimony until the debrief.

Design a game for that room: rules, materials, a payoff scheme in chips, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief. The goal is that students run into aesthetic-testimony pessimism in their own betting behaviour and feel its pull before it is named. Two constraints do the real work and I would rather you satisfy them than make the game clever.

First, isolate the variable. The naive version is a whisper-down-the-lane relay in which verdicts degrade; that only demonstrates generic reliability decay, and anyone can absorb it into ordinary testimonial caution. So every claim a student receives should arrive attached to a stated track record for that informant on claims of that exact kind, with a short calibration round up front so the students have first-hand evidence the hit rates are real. For a non-aesthetic claim with that hit rate — the length of a paragraph, which of two unlabeled cups is hotter — updating is obviously correct and betting on it is free money. The only thing that changes in the target round is the subject matter: verdicts about two works nobody in the room has encountered, reproduced on card stock so they can be revealed at the end but not before. I want students to decline a bet that is free money by their own lights, and to be unable to articulate why.

Second, separate the things pessimism could be claiming: coming to know that the work is good; coming to understand why it is good; and being able to recognise its goodness when finally shown it. Include a fourth contrast I think is the real crux — verdictive testimony (it is good) versus predictive testimony about one's own response (you will like this), which students will happily act on. The activity should let them discover which of these they were actually missing.

The debrief should map specific moments onto each of these, steelman the optimist as strongly as you can, tell me what will predictably go wrong in the first run and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the dispute the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in words. End with a last line of the form \answer{...} giving, in at most twelve words, the single design choice the activity cannot survive without.
