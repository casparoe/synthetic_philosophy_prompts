I write novels for a living, and I have come to a philosophical argument by way of a piece of journalism that I suspect does it some violence. Before I repeat any of it in an author's note, I would like to know how much of it to trust.

The essay in question is a long and generous piece in the books pages of a weekend paper, by a writer who plainly means the philosophy no harm. It summarizes the paradox of fiction roughly as follows:

"Emotions such as pity and fear are responses to real things: you pity someone only if you believe that they are suffering. Readers nonetheless weep for characters they know perfectly well never existed, and philosophers call this the paradox of fiction. The solution most of them accept today is that we are not really undergoing the emotion at all. We imagine it, or work ourselves into a simulation of it, and what we end up in is a kind of shadow state — a quasi-emotion — that resembles the real thing but carries no belief and no impulse to act. This is why fiction is safe: you can enjoy the terrors of a thriller without ever being in danger, and it is why we do not think worse of a person for sobbing over a novel than for sobbing at the death of an actual stranger, since in the first case nothing is really believed and nothing is really felt. The lesson for writers is that emotional effect is pure technique: what matters is not truth but the reliable triggering of these pretend states."

The phrase "nothing is really felt" rings false to me, but I cannot say why, and my editor thinks the passage is essentially right. My doubts come from my own workbench. I weep at a death in the fourth chapter of the book I am finishing, although I invented the character, although I know the scene is invented, and although I could delete the death tomorrow; my conviction that none of it is real is stronger than any reader's, and the response seems if anything stronger for it. That death, I should say, is revealed in the last chapter to have been staged, with the narrator covering it up; readers who have been warned of the twist in advance still weep in chapter four, and readers who meet the twist at the end tell me that on rereading the earlier grief feels cancelled, or betrayed, and yet is still, apparently, grief. In an earlier novel a central character was closely based on a friend of mine who had died; readers who never met her wept for the character, I wept for both, and I have never been able to tell whether that was one state or two.

What I want is an audit of the quoted passage. Take it more or less in order and tell me which sentences someone who actually works on these questions would accept, which are simplifications of a kind that a specialist would tolerate in a newspaper but would correct in a seminar, and which are straightforward misreadings.Three things in particular: whether "we are not really undergoing the emotion at all" is a fair statement of any of the main accounts or only a caricature of one of them; whether the passage has slid from not really believing to not really feeling; and whether it is using the word pretend in its everyday sense where the theory in question uses it technically. Then tell me whether the essay's closing lesson — that emotional effect is pure technique — actually follows from the defensible parts of the summary, or only from the parts you think are wrong. Finally, does the corrected version of the view have resources for my cases, or are they genuinely awkward for it?

Two practical requests. First, I have no access to a university library at present, so please argue rather than point me at the literature; I need the reasoning, not a reading list. Second, I would like you to register, as you go, how confident you are in each verdict you give, and to single out the one claim in your answer that you are least sure of, together with what would settle it. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — in the manner of a considered letter rather than a set of notes.
