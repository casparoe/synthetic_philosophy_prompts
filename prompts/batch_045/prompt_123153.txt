I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and our office book club has been slowly working through popular cosmology books — I've been the one slowing it down, because there's a move physicists keep making that I can't reconstruct as an argument. The setup, as I understand it: in some otherwise respectable models of the universe (an eternally inflating one, or one that sits in a warm de Sitter state for long enough), the overwhelming majority of conscious observers are not creatures like us at all but "Boltzmann brains" — momentary thermal fluctuations that assemble something brain-shaped, have a few seconds of experience complete with apparent memories of a life, and dissolve. What puzzles me is not the physics but the practice: cosmologists treat the fact that a model predicts Boltzmann-brain domination as a serious strike against that model, sometimes as a decisive one, and there is real work devoted to adjusting the way one counts observers so that the models come out with ordinary observers being the common case. Nothing about the model's agreement with galaxy surveys, the microwave background, or anything else we actually measure is supposed to change; what changes is only the census of who is doing the observing.

Two things about that practice look inconsistent to me, and I'd like them separated. First, in every other corner of science I know about, you judge a theory by whether its predictions match your data, and a theory isn't discredited by a story about how many observers of various kinds it contains. Second, this same style of reasoning — we should expect to find ourselves in the situation that most observers are in — seems to get treated as respectable when it's used to explain why the constants of nature have the values they do, and as lethal when it delivers brains. I've tried to sharpen it myself and got stuck on a case I haven't seen discussed. Suppose a model in which fluctuations do occur constantly, but essentially all of them produce a second of incoherent noise, so that among the observers whose entire stream of experience is exactly like mine, ordinary evolved beings vastly outnumber the fluctuations — even though, counted over all observers whatsoever, the fluctuations vastly outnumber us. Under the practice as it's actually exercised, is that model condemned or not? And if it isn't, then what was the objection in the original case: something about total numbers, or something about my specific evidence being untrustworthy? I also can't tell whether the practice needs a threshold — brains at 51 percent of observers, or 99 percent, or 99.99 percent — or whether it's meant to be all-or-nothing.

So here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this practice: what does a person have to believe about evidence, about self-trust, or about what a theory owes us, in order for "this model makes observers like us atypical" to count as a reason to reject it? Be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes, as opposed to what it merely sits well with — I suspect several quite different theories would license it, and I'd like to know which, and whether they'd disagree about the case I invented above. Tell me too whether the practice is really one rule or a bundle that happens to agree on the famous cases, and whether the version I sketched (counting only observers with evidence just like mine) is a genuine alternative or just the same rule restated. Finally, what would cosmology have to look like if the rule were dropped or replaced — if, say, one treated this purely as a question of how probable our evidence makes the theory, the way one weighs any other hypothesis in science? I tried mapping it onto problems I do know, like a monitoring system whose telemetry might itself be corrupted, or a compiler whose output you can only trust if the compiler that built it was already correct, and both analogies seem to light up part of the problem and go dark on the rest; if one of them is closer than the other, say which and why.
