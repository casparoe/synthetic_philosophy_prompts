I teach a fourth-year undergraduate metaphysics seminar: sixteen students, one 90-minute session, movable tables, one whiteboard, and a box of ordinary stationery. It is the first meeting on structured propositions, and I want the activity — not a lecture — to carry the session. Assume the students have read nothing on the topic, and assume that none of them should hear the words regress, unity, unsaturated, or multiple relation theory until the debrief. You may name the positions in your answer to me; they should not be named in the room.

What I want is a build task. Groups of four have to physically construct a proposition out of card and string, and the pressure to keep adding a further entity — something to bind each thing to the relation, and then something to bind that — has to come out of the rules rather than out of my mouth. The mechanism I have been toying with, which you should refine or replace if you have better: a rule that no two pieces may merely touch, on the grounds that bare adjacency is not unity, so that every linkage must be made by a labeled linker card, which is itself a piece and therefore must itself be linked to what it links by a further linker. Set against that, a legibility test: an assembly scores only if a student from a different group, who has not watched it being built and may not ask the builders anything, can read a determinate claim off it. A heap of pieces is not a claim, and the question the session should press is what has to be added to a heap for it to become one. Groups should be handed target sentences on slips — I want at least one that turns on order, and at least one that does not, since that difference should matter later. Tell me whether some groups should also be handed a false target claim, and what that would buy.

Then I want the escape routes supplied as materials, and only handed out when a group has genuinely stalled: cards with slots cut into them that physically hold their arguments; hook-and-loop backing that lets two cards grip each other with no third piece; and a fan-shaped card with many slots that takes an assertor plus each constituent directly, so that there is no single assembled thing at all. Each of these should be genuinely attractive, and each should carry a cost the students can find for themselves rather than being told about. Give me rules, materials, a scoring scheme that needs no money or prizes, a timeline with minute marks including a rotation of readers, and the text of the two or three sentences I will actually say at the start.

The debrief should map what happened onto what the students were doing philosophically: the move of positing a further entity and why it does not stop; what the slotted card solves and what it cannot handle; what the gripping backing purchases and the way it starts to dissolve distinctions until everything a thing is includes what it is related to; and what the fan-shaped card achieves by dispersing the proposition, together with the fact that it lets you assemble judgments no sentence could express. I want them to come away seeing three things: that the regress is not a parlor trick but falls out of a principle they were happy with ten minutes earlier, that every way out has a specific price, and that the live disagreement is about the price and not about whether there is a puzzle.

Finally: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect someone to build a picture instead of a claim, at least one group to quietly tape things together and declare victory, and the readers to be far too charitable — and how I should handle each. And be frank about which feature of the real debate the activity cannot reproduce. My own suspicion is that the activity makes the regress feel fatal only because I have imposed a completion condition on being able to assert anything at all, and that this condition is itself one of the contested points; say whether you agree, and what else is missing.
