I work on multi-objective fine-tuning for a small product team, and I keep running into the same argument in our checkpoint reviews. We take one base model, fine-tune it with a weighted sum of three per-population quality metrics (say, quality for speakers of a low-resource language, instruction-following quality, and a safety metric), sweep the weights, and end up with a cloud of candidate checkpoints. Serving constraints rule out per-population routing, ensembling, or weight interpolation, so we ship exactly one checkpoint: our feasible set is a finite, scattered, non-convex set of points rather than a smooth frontier. A friend with philosophy training told me that our argument is just a bargaining problem, and that the two canonical answers disagree: the Nash rule picks the checkpoint maximizing the product of per-metric gains over the no-fine-tuning baseline, while the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule looks at each metric's own best achievable value (the ceiling we get by fine-tuning for that metric alone), equalizes the fraction of the baseline-to-ceiling gap each metric closes, and takes the checkpoint that pushes that common fraction as high as it will go. She added that the two are usually presented as differing over which axiom to keep: Nash keeping independence of irrelevant alternatives and violating a monotonicity condition, Kalai–Smorodinsky the reverse. I want that restated in the vocabulary I actually use, and I want you to correct my gloss above wherever it is wrong or missing a precondition, including preconditions I don't have, like convexity or strictly positive gains. Please do not name papers, authors, or theorems I would have to look up — I have no library access and nothing useful will come of a citation I can't read; describe any result by what it says.

Concretely, I'd like three things. First, both rules stated as selection rules over a finite, non-convex table of checkpoints, saying plainly what each needs that my setting may not supply. Second, an explicit small worked example: a made-up table of six to eight checkpoints with two metrics where the two rules pick different rows, and then a second version of the table in which the cloud has been expanded only in the first metric's favour — more achievable for metric one at every fixed level of metric two, and nothing lost elsewhere — showing what each rule does to metric one's score, and telling me directly if my picture of which rule violates which condition is backwards. Third, and most importantly, the residue report, and I have guesses about where the residue will be that I want you to test rather than humoured: (a) both rules are built to be invariant under rescaling each party's utility separately, but our metrics have no such privileged scale, and switching from raw accuracy to log-odds to some monotone squash changes the pick, so I suspect the fairness content is leaking in through an arbitrary choice of units; (b) our populations are not agents, they make no threats, concessions, or walk-aways, and the baseline is a default rather than a strategic fallback, so I don't know what the disagreement point is doing any work for, or whether the choice between the rules changes its meaning when the chooser is a designer imposing a compromise on non-agents rather than an arbitrator predicting what parties would settle on; (c) the two axioms in dispute are claims about what my selection rule would pick on feasible sets I never enumerated, which in my vocabulary is close to a claim about nothing at all, since the cloud is just whatever the sweep and the compute budget happened to produce.
