I teach an upper-level seminar in political theory and comparative political ethics (advanced undergraduates and first-year master's students), and I am writing a closed-book final. I need one essay question on the definition and scope of corruption — specifically on clientelism, gift-giving, and the suspicion that our standard anti-corruption concepts are parochial — that separates students who have grasped the structure of the argument from students who have memorized the labels. A paper that recites the public-office definition, or that gestures at 'relativism versus universalism' and concludes 'it depends on the culture,' should land in the B range at best. I want the question to force a decision about which feature is doing the work in a case, and why.

The cases should be invented types rather than real countries or real scandals: students have no materials, so nothing may turn on outside knowledge, and I also want to block the move where a student substitutes a familiar headline for analysis. Here are the three I want used (tighten the wording as you see fit, and keep them short):

1. The Rally. A municipal councilor allocates a genuinely scarce public benefit — say a water connection — to households that attend her rallies and publicly support her. The benefit is really delivered. There is no functioning bureaucratic queue; the realistic alternatives are a lottery among equally needy households or allocation to whoever shows up at the office first. Households that do not support her are not worse off than they would have been under any of those alternatives.
2. The Wedding Gift. A businessman with a permit application pending gives the daughter of the deciding official a wedding gift of locally conspicuous value. In local custom, refusing such a gift is a grave insult that severs the relationship. Evidence suggests the official would have approved the permit anyway.
3. The Ledger. A jurisdiction in which gifts, patronage payments, and allocations to supporters are fully disclosed in a public register, and in which voters systematically reward officials who channel resources to their own networks. Transparency without impartiality.

I also want one invented doctrinal device in the question, because the crux I care about is hard to reach without it. Call it the Empty-Detour Test: an act is corrupt only if the personal relation it runs through is a detour around an impersonal mechanism that actually exists and could have handled the case at acceptable cost; where no such mechanism exists, calling the practice corruption is either false or is really a claim about what institutions ought to exist. Rename it if you have something better. The question should force students to say whether this test is the universalist's friend — one standard, applied everywhere, with only the factual inputs varying — or whether it quietly presupposes a parochial picture of the impersonal bureaucratic state as the default and the end point. Students should also be made to disentangle three claims that the literature routinely runs together: that outsiders are poor judges of local meaning; that the concept itself has different content in different places; and that, whatever the verdict on the practice, external actors lack standing to condemn or to enforce. And they should be pressed on what an external actor — a donor, an NGO, a multinational bound by its home jurisdiction's anti-bribery law — is permitted to conclude, including the second-order risk that imposing a standard without the institutional substrate that supports it produces something worse, such as selectively enforced anti-corruption rules used to remove opponents.

What I want from you:

- The exam question itself, self-contained, answerable in ninety minutes of closed-book writing (roughly a thousand words). It should name the three competing accounts students are choosing between — roughly, an office-based account that ties corruption to violation of norms attached to a public role for private gain; an institution-based account on which a practice can be corrupt by subverting an institution's proper purposes even when no rule is broken and no one enriches herself; and a legitimation-based account on which the extension of the term is fixed by what a political community has actually authorized, locally but not arbitrarily. It should not be satisfiable by listing them.
- A model answer of about nine hundred to a thousand words, somewhat fuller and tidier than what a strong student would actually produce under time pressure. No citations, no author names, no bibliographic gestures: the answer has to stand on argument alone.
- A marking scheme out of twenty-five marks: five must-hit points at four marks each, plus five marks for overall argumentative quality. For each must-hit point, state what earns the marks and add a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The five points I have in mind, which you should use or improve on, are: (i) saying what work a concept of corruption is meant to do — guiding officials, designing institutions, assigning blame, licensing external intervention — before defining it, and noting that these jobs can pull apart; (ii) distinguishing outcome-based, relation-based, and institution-based accounts of what the wrong in corruption is, and using that distinction to adjudicate rather than to assert a verdict on the no-loser defense suggested by the Rally case; (iii) bringing at least two accounts to bear on the Ledger case and arguing whether disclosure cures the wrong, merely relocates it, or is irrelevant to it; (iv) using the Empty-Detour Test to settle whether universalism survives the functional-substitution point, and stating plainly which way the test cuts; (v) separating the epistemic, conceptual, and jurisdictional readings of the claim that Western standards are not universal, and drawing the consequence for external action, including the weaponization risk.
- A short note on the A/B boundary: name at least one move that should earn full marks even when the student reaches a verdict you would reject, so that the scheme rewards structure and not conclusions, and name the specific failure that should cap an otherwise fluent paper at a B.

Please write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme in American English.
