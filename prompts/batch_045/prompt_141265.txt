Hi — I work at a smallish foundation (about £14m a year, four programme staff) and last year our trustees got excited about the idea of moral trade: the thing where two groups who disagree about what's actually good each do what the other wants, and both come out ahead by their own lights, more or less the way two countries trade when each is better at something. They've now made it a formal funding line — we put up money when two organisations with genuinely conflicting moral views each commit to doing something the other values that they wouldn't have done on their own — and I'm the one who has to turn that into something we can actually run.

The problem is we now get three or four of these a month, I have roughly two days per proposal, and sign-off is a single meeting with a committee of four: me, a trustee who used to run a logistics company, and two programme officers with no philosophy background at all. Our trustees also require that any yes be explainable in one sentence to a journalist who is going to write it up as "foundation pays charity to do thing it thinks is worthless." And about half our money comes from a restricted gift whose donor died in 2019 and left a letter of wishes, which matters for one of the cases below.

So I need a procedure, not an essay: ordered questions I can walk the committee through, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the points where the answer is a hard no and we stop, and the points where the honest move is to stop and say this is a values call, and here is who makes it. Please be blunt about which steps are really judgment wearing a lab coat.

Things it has to handle, because they've already bitten us:

- The would-have-happened-anyway problem. Half the proposals are just two groups discovering they wanted the same thing, or one group announcing it will now do the thing we were about to fund anyway. I've started calling these coincidence deals and I need a test that kills them in the first ten minutes — including when we can't establish the counterfactual, because everyone lies about what they would have done.
- The overbidding problem. Since I can only be paid in the other side's values, the other side has every reason to tell me they care about my thing far more than they do, so that I give them more. We have no way to audit what anyone actually values. What's the least-bad way to handle that — staging, matching, symmetric disclosure, third-party holds, something else — and when does the right answer become "we can't price this, so we cap the deal"?
- Hostage deals. Twice now a group has come to us offering not to do something harmful it was going to do anyway — a campaign, a legal challenge, a regulatory complaint — if we fund its programme. Sometimes that reads to me as extortion with better manners, and sometimes the threatened harm is real, the people making the threat are sincere, and the deal would genuinely leave everyone better off by their own lights. I need the procedure to tell those apart, or to tell me plainly that it can't.
- Dirty hands on their side. A couple of proposals require one party to do something that's fine by our lights but that they say they think is wrong, and they've agreed only because we're paying. My instinct is that this isn't a partner, it's a corrupted partner, and it will fall apart. I'd like that turned into a check rather than a vibe.
- Who is actually trading. One case below involves an endowment whose mission was written by someone who's dead. Do we count the mission as a party to the trade? I keep going back and forth on whether a commitment that outlives the person who made it can be traded away by whoever is holding the pen now, and what it would even take for that to be legitimate rather than just convenient.

Then please run it on three:

1. A global-health charity (think animal suffering counts for little, wants to buy bed nets) and an animal-welfare charity (thinks bed nets are a rounding error next to farmed fish). Proposal: we fund each of them to run one year of the other's programme, matched pound for pound. On its face this is the cleanest thing we've seen.

2. A well-funded advocacy organisation — call them the Alliance — that exists mostly to oppose a hospital expansion our health grantees want. They offer to withdraw their objection and not file suit if we fund a year of their community health worker programme. They say, and I half beleive them, that they would file and would probably win.

3. The one I know I'm biased on, so be especially annoying about it: a religious hospital network in a country where we work will take on a full maternal-health service that we think is the best thing on offer in the region, but only if we fund a chaplaincy and end-of-life counselling programme that we think does nothing — some of us think it does less than nothing, that it talks people out of decisions that are theirs to make. As far as I can tell the theology behind the network's side of the deal is straightforwardly false, and I find parts of it grim. My instinct is to find a reason to say no. If the procedure says yes, I want to be told that clearly, along with what I'd have to put in the committee note and what the strongest version of my own objection actually is.

Finally, tell me what the whole frame can't do: the cases where "go find a trade" is the wrong move and we should just be doing ordinary grantmaking, or politics, or nothing at all.
