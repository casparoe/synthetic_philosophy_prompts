Half the cohort will have read the relevant chapter of the big popular history-of-philosophy book my department sets as background reading (marble bust on the cover, "father of modern philosophy", you know the type), and they'll have taken it as gospel. I've only skimmed it myself, and I want to be fair to it — it's better than most — but I also dont want to spend the hour quietly undoing it. The gist of what it says is this:

"Descartes shut himself in a stove-heated room and resolved to withhold assent from anything he could doubt even slightly — not because he was really a sceptic, but as a method for finding something solid. He runs through three waves of doubt: the senses sometimes deceive us; for all we know we are always dreaming; and finally an evil demon of unlimited power could be feeding us the whole show, so that even the simplest arithmetic might be false. Nothing survives. But then he notices that even if the demon is deceiving him, he has to exist in order to be deceived, and being deceived is a kind of thinking. So: whatever thinks, exists; I think; therefore I am. That's the cogito, and it's the one piece of reasoning the doubt cannot touch. What it hands him is his own mind: he now knows that he is essentially a thinking thing — a soul — and that he could go on existing without a body. On that one self-certain premise he then proves that God exists, gets the external world back, and founds modern science. It doesn't really work, though. To trust the argument he has to trust clear and distinct ideas, and to trust those he needs God, whom he proves by using them — the famous Cartesian circle, which sinks the whole project. And as Nietzsche pointed out, 'I think' already smuggles in a thinker: all Descartes was strictly entitled to was that thinking is going on, which is more or less what Buddhists had been saying for centuries."
