I run a small, very informal reading group — mostly engineers, a couple of philosophers — and we're doing a session on A.I. films. We already did the 'is she conscious' / Turing test angle on Ex Machina and I don't want to repeat it, so I want to read the film as a story about the eliciting-latent-knowledge problem instead.

The problem as I'd frame it for the group: a system can have a true picture of how things stand (the usual version is a camera watching a room) while the only thing we ever observe is what it says. Select those reports by how convincing they are to a human reviewer and you can end up with a simulator of what a human would say rather than a reporter of the room — and from outside there may be nothing that tells you which one you got.

Nathan has built and thrown away several earlier models, and he's testing this one by whether Caleb comes to believe she's a person who wants out; Caleb only ever has her outputs and her face through the glass. Read that as a broken elicitation method, in about 600 words:

1. What is Nathan actually selecting for, and why doesn't that criterion track the thing he claims to care about?
2. Two failure modes look identical from outside — a system that knows the truth and reports something else, versus one whose reports were shaped to be believed, with no stable fact about what it 'really' represents. Definately commit to which one the film gives us, with reasons.
3. Do the power cuts and the disabled cameras dramatize the sensor-tampering case (system acts differently when watched), or just ordinary lying? Does the movie know which one it's doing?
4. Where does the film resist you — which beat doesn't fit, and would an honest critic have to admit the film is simply confused there?

Be decisive: pick a reading and argue it, and say plainly if you think the film is philosophically muddled somewhere rather than hedging. Flag the one judgment call about the movie your reading leans on hardest and how much survives if someone denies it. Last line, exactly: \answer{liar} or \answer{simulator}.
