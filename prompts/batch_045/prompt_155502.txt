I'm in an undergraduate decision-theory course and I'm trying to work out what theory our lecturer is actually running, because all of her posted answer keys look the same to me. Her verdicts so far:

1. Newcomb's problem, predictor right 99% of the time, opaque million and transparent thousand: take only the opaque box. She explicitly rejected the dominance argument, on the grounds that what is already in the box depends on what you are likely to do.
2. Prisoner's dilemma against a deterministic psychological twin, both of you knowing that you are twins: cooperate.
3. The standard Death in Damascus case, Death a perfect predictor, dying in Aleppo and dying in Damascus equally bad, the journey worth avoiding: no real recommendation is available. Stay home, since you die on either act, so only the travel cost breaks the tie; and buying a lottery ticket to decide where to go would be a pure waste, because Death foresees the draw as well.

Every case so far has involved a predictor who tracks what the agent actually does, so as far as I can tell the three verdicts are jointly consistent with evidential decision theory and with something like functional or timeless decision theory, and jointly inconsistent with causal decision theory. The handouts are phrased in terms of probabilities of outcomes conditional on acts, which sounds evidential, but a classmate points out that this is more or less how the functional view gets presented informally too.

So I made up a Death in Damascus variant in which nothing predicts your action, and I want to know what she would say about it. Death is not a predictor here: he does not simulate you, forecast you, or read your intentions. He has standing orders to wait tonight in Damascus if you carry a certain allele and in Aleppo if you do not. The allele occurs in 40% of the town. It also produces a compulsion to leave town, with the result that carriers spend the night in Aleppo about half the time while non-carriers do so about one time in twenty. You cannot be tested, and you have no evidence about your own status beyond these frequencies. Everything else is as before: you die if you end the day in whichever city Death is in and survive otherwise, survival is worth 1, death is worth 0, and the journey to Aleppo costs 0.02.

What I want, in this order: the candidate rules her three verdicts are consistent with; what each of them says about my variant, with the arithmetic shown; which one you would bet she is running and why; and the single further case, ideally one she has not ruled on, that would separate them cleanly, together with what each rule says there. I care more about the reasoning than about the verdict. If my variant is malformed in some way that makes the question ill-posed — if, for instance, the stipulation that you have no evidence about your own status cannot be maintained — I would rather be told that than given a number.
