Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas writer at a magazine, mostly covering AI, and I keep losing arguments with our style desk about when we're allowed to write that a system thinks, decides, wants, or refuses. Last month we ran a piece saying a chatbot "refused to answer" and a reader wrote in to say we'd smuggled in a mind; the month before we said a chess program "sacrificed its queen" and nobody blinked. I was arguing about this with a friend who edits at another outlet and her rule is just "never use mental verbs about machines," which seems definately too crude to me — we use them about firms, markets, and ant colonies all the time. So I want something better than a vibe: a short procedure my team can actually run. Constraints: reporters with no philosophy training, filing in a few hours, an assigning editor has to sign off, and it has to fit on one page of the style guide and work for headlines and photo captions, not just body copy.

The theory I want it built out of is Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — plus the companion idea that a pattern counts as real rather than merely projected when there is a description of the data that compresses it, so that predicting via the pattern is cheaper than tracking the underlying detail and any observer doing the bookkeeping would extract the same pattern. Please turn that into ordered steps, and please fix my draft list rather than rubber-stamping it (say which of my steps are circular or in the wrong order). Roughly what I have in mind: (1) a compression audit — the reporter must be able to write down, in one sentence in their notes, the cheaper description that the mental verb is buying; if they can't produce it, stop and no intentional language; (2) a design-availability check — a lot of the systems I write about are trained rather than built, so nobody can say what the parts are for, and I suspect this case is where the usual three-stance picture quietly breaks down and the intentional language starts doing work the design story should have done; (3) whether the intentional description buys predictions the design or physical story doesn't already give, over a prediction horizon the reporter has to name at filing time (new prompts? new board positions? new temperatures?) rather than just over the cases already in the story; (4) whose design it is — if the design stance is unavailable, does the attribution belong to the trainers and objective-setters instead of the system; and (5) some handoff point where the remaining question is about readers rather than metaphysics, i.e. how likely this sentence is to be read as a causal explanation rather than a summary, and how much it matters if it is. I also want a retraction clause: when an intentional prediction later fails on a new case, what exactly are we obliged to print, and why "the system changed its mind" is or isn't an available move. And I'd like the procedure to sort sentences into grades — unhedged attribution, hedged "as if", scare quotes, "in the vocabulary of the field" — and say which grade it licenses and with what typographic marking, since half our fights are about whether to use the quotes at all.

Then run it on three sentences. One: "the model refused to answer" for a chatbot that was tuned to decline certain requests. Two: "the chess system sacrificed its queen" and "the system prefers positions it can win from." Three: something like "the ant colony chose the shorter route" or "the thermostat wants the room at twenty degrees" — a case where I suspect the procedure licenses unhedged mental language and I will hate the result. Please include at least one worked case where the procedure gives me an answer I don't like, and be explicit about the cases it can't settle at all, where an editor just has to decide. If you can also flag where the procedure will misfire in practice — the failure modes of the checklist itself, not just of the sentences — that would be genuinely useful.
