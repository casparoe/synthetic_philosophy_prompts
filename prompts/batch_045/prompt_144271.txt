I write a weekly column on the behavioral and social sciences, and I would like to run a satirical piece built around a mock leaked document: a client guidance memo from a fictional crisis-communications firm that has begun marketing a service it calls attribution management, derived from the side-effect effect in experimental philosophy. I need roughly 800 words of memo, set out with numbered sections and containing at least one worked case study, preceded by a short news peg of no more than sixty words in my own voice.

The memo should be in deadpan consultant register from the first line to the last: no winking, no tell, no moment where the author steps outside the fiction.

On content, I have the following requirements.

The finding being monetized has to be stated correctly, and without quotation: the case of the chairman who approves a program he knows will affect the environment, says he does not care about the environment either way, and is then asked whether he brought the change about intentionally — together with the fact that most respondents say yes when the change is a harm and no when the very same change, produced by the very same indifference, is a benefit. The humor should come from the firm's discovery that its only lever is the valence of the description, since on the actual view there is nothing about the client's aims for it to work with.

The firm's technique should therefore manage descriptions rather than conduct. I want one case study that covers both directions: the harm trap, in which foresight by itself convicts the client no matter how the client's goals are described, and the help deficit, in which a client who genuinely aimed at a good outcome still cannot get credit for it.

Stage the live theoretical disagreement inside the memo, in the person of a rival firm whose doctrine is that the asymmetry is a fact about what people are doing when they answer a question about a norm-violating agent rather than a fact about the concept of intentionality at all — so that the rival's product works on the wording of the accusation instead of on the valence of the outcome. The memo must rebut the rival while conceding the one thing that is actually true of that position: that minute changes in how the question is framed move the numbers.

Build the reductio into the firm's flagship product. Because credit for a good side effect appears to require visible evidence of aiming, the firm now sells costly public rituals whose sole function is to make an aim legible, and the memo should note, in the same flat tone, that the ritual now routinely costs more than the harm it was commissioned to offset.

Add two self-indicting details: a fee schedule whose tiers are named after experimental conditions, and a closing compliance note in which the memo's own prose cannot bring itself to say that the firm did anything intentionally.

Two constraints. Nothing in the memo should be a claim that a specialist could not recognize as an overreaching but recognizable reading of the real literature — I want a philosopher of action to feel the piece is unfair in the way that good satire is fair. And a reader who has never heard of experimental philosophy should still be able to tell precisely what is being ironized.
