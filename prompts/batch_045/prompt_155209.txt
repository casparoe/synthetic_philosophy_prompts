So I've ended up as the philosopher on our hospital's clinical genomics advisory board — we meet a couple of times a term and sign off on what the genetics clinic offers and how the consent conversation goes, and it's the same argument every time: is it actually good for this patient to be offered this result? I'd like to build the protocol around Good's theorem (the value of information can't be negative, since you can always just ignore what you learn), which I teach as the clean case, but the theorem stops doing any work the moment its assumptions break, and what I want is the assumptions turned into steps.

Write me a procedure a genetic counsellor can run in about twenty mintues at intake, before any optional result gets offered: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the question back to the patient because it's a values question dressed as a factual one. It has to survive these, which are the ones that actually come up:

- results that discriminate between no acts the patient would take anyway — variants of uncertain significance, or a risk score for something with no prevention — so the decision value is zero while the hedonic cost isn't;
- patients who ask for a deliberately coarsened signal, just tell me if there's anything I can do about it, which looks like throwing away information for nothing;
- patients who decline free results outright, where I want the procedure to tell a mistake apart from a preference about the weeks of waiting;
- results that are durable and semi-public — in the chart, in the family — so the patient isn't the only one who ends up informed;
- patients who say they'll act on a result and won't.

At each step say plainly which bit is decision theory and which bit is a value judgment I've laundered as arithmetic, and flag the cases the procedure can't settle. Then run it on three: the secondary-findings panel we currently offer by default to everyone who gets sequenced; a polygenic score for a late-onset condition with no intervention; and a patient who wants the panel disclosed only if it's actionable. If the procedure tells me we shouldn't be offering something we offer by default, I'd rather hear that now than at the next meeting.
