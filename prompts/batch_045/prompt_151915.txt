I'm a retired analytical chemist (most of my career was trace metals in river water), and since I stopped work I've been reading a bit of philosophy os science. There's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a word for.

The setup is always the same. Something people care about gets measured by counting how many things fall on the far side of a line: samples above a detection limit, sites below a standard, patients waiting past four hours. Then the count gets used as a target, the count improves, and the thing anyone actually cared about hasn't moved. But nobody has lied and nobody has gamed anything in the obvious sense. The count is still an accurate count of exactly what it says it counts. What has moved is either the line, or what gets fed to the line.

Two cases I know first hand. In our lab the number of samples with a detectable result dropped sharply between two years, and everyone read that as the river getting cleaner; what had really happened is that a new instrument had a slightly different detection limit, so samples that used to sit just above the cutoff were now reported as not detected. Same water, same method on paper, and no one had any reason to prefer either answer. Second one, from a catchment committee I sit on: the number of river bodies classed as failing went down, partly because sites that sat right on the boundary between two classes had their reference conditions quietly redrawn, and partly because the worst sites stopped being monitored and simply left the list.

People brush this off with a management one-liner I've heard a lot — once a number is used as a target it stops being a good measure of anything. But that doesnt seem to fit. Here the number never stopped measuring what it measures; what I care about was always a messy aggregate sitting behind a definition, and what changed is the definition or the population, not the fidelity of the measurement.

So: is there an established concept for this, and is it really a separate thing from the target-breaks-the-measure case? I also want it distinguished from a couple of neighbours I can only half describe — the trick where shifting a borderline case across a line raises the average in both groups at once (that one is about averages, and mine happens with a single count), and the general business in optimisation where a system exploits the gap between what you specified and what you wanted. If there is no name, give me one, define it so I can tell it apart from those, and say where the edges are. In particular: is the version where nobody intends anything and there is no incentive at all the same phenomenon, or a different one? A bare label won't do me any good.
