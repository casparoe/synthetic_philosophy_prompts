Hey — I've been chewing on that whole "should AI systems reason like utilitarians or like deontologists" question, and I think I've found an argument that basically dissolves it, so I wanted to check that I'm not missing something.

Call it the folding argument. Any deontological rule you might want in an AI — don't lie, don't kill one to save five, keep your promises, whatever — can be folded into the AI's utility function as one really large negative term. Minus a million for telling a lie, say. Do that for every duty you care about and your "deontological" AI is just a utilitarian AI with lumpy preferences. So the architecture question was never really about utilitarianism versus deontology at all; it's a tuning problem about how big the penalty constant is. My friend pointed out that this even covers duties that feel agent-relative: "I must not lie" folds in as a penalty on worlds in which the AI's own action is a lie, which is still just a term in a function over outcomes.

Two wrinkles I've been playing with. First, should the penalty scale with the AI's confidence? If its only 60% sure that the lie saves a life, do you multiply the -1,000,000 by 0.6, or leave it fixed? Second, per-decision or per-lifetime? A per-decision penalty stretched over a trillion decisions starts behaving like a total budget, which I think quietly sneaks utilitarianism back in, because the AI can just spend its violations wherever they're cheapest. So I've been toying with a fixed lifetime duty budget of like three violations instead.

Am I right that the folding argument collapses the debate? And is there any principled way to pick the constant, or do people just fiddle with it until the AI stops making creepy tradeoffs in test cases?
