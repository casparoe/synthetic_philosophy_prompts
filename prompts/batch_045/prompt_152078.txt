I am a high school student writing an end-of-year paper for my philosophy elective on whether beauty is objective, working from Hume's essay on the standard of taste. My provisional thesis is that Hume's machinery — the joint verdict of qualified judges, confirmed by durability across ages and countries — handles cleanly those objects whose merit can be re-inspected indefinitely, but breaks down for a class of objects I have started calling *one-pass objects*: works whose central merit is available only on an unrehearsed first encounter, so that the very training which qualifies someone as a judge tends to destroy that person's access to it. I mean ordinary cases, not exotic ones: a piece of music whose effect depends on not knowing where it resolves, a built space whose effect depends on coming upon it unexpectedly, a story whose effect depends on not yet knowing how it ends.

What I need is one concrete thought experiment, built to the specifications below, and I would be grateful for yours.

**What the case must do.**

1. One object, two respondents. Respondent A satisfies all five of Hume's conditions on a true judge: delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Respondent B is untrained and fails at least two of them. A judges the object not beautiful; B's positive verdict is the correct one. So the error runs in the reverse of the usual direction, and it is the trained judge who is wrong.
2. The object must be a one-pass object, and it must be one whose beauty is durable in Hume's sense — it is not a passing fashion; each new generation of newcomers responds to it — while still being such that no single person can re-inspect it. I want that tension to be explicit in the case rather than smoothed over.
3. A's error must be produced by A's *greater* sensitivity, not in spite of it. The discriminations that make A a true judge are what go wrong here.

**What would count as cheating.** A may not be prejudiced in any of the ways Hume excludes; may not be tired, drunk, distracted, or ill-disposed on the occasion (Hume already excuses verdicts delivered under a passing indisposition, so leaning on that would assume what I need to show); may not be bribed, hurried, or ignorant of what kind of thing the object is. A must not be committing the part-versus-whole error that Hume's good-sense condition is meant to catch: no being misled by a local blemish or a decorative detail while missing the design. And the correct verdict must not rest on B's private associations — a melody that reminds B of a dead relative, a room B grew up in — since that is sentiment attaching to the object rather than a verdict about it.

**Constraints on form.** One paragraph for the scenario. An ordinary setting a reader could actually be in: a school assembly, a transit concourse, a museum wing, a streaming queue, a hospital corridor. No science fiction, no drugs, no neurological gadgets, no memory erasure. And please do not make it turn on beauty collapsing into fitness for a purpose; I want a case about beauty proper, whose mechanism is the structure of the encounter.

**What I need you to do after the scenario.** Run the check. Say which of Hume's five conditions each respondent meets or fails and why, and what verdict Hume's standard yields if you imagine a whole panel of A-like judges agreeing. Then say what an objectivist about beauty would conclude from the case, and what someone who holds that beauty is response-dependent but standardized on ideal judges would conclude — and whether that second view is in better or worse shape here than the objectivist one. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and show what happens if it is removed: if the same object could be encountered twice without loss, does the standard of taste simply function again? Finally, give me the strongest reply available to a Humean. I expect candidates along these lines: that the standard concerns a work's durable capacity rather than any individual experience of it; that B's response is liking rather than a verdict of taste; or that a true judge can defer to the accumulated reports of first-time audiences. Tell me whether any of these actually gets Hume out of the difficulty my thesis needs, or whether it only relocates it — and if it does relocate it, say where it reappears.
