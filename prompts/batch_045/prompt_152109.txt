I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I sit on my company's internal review committee for releases of a model we publish publicly, and I have been asked to write a short position statement on how the committee should treat changes that any single engineer could ship on their own. While preparing it I came across something called the unilateralist's curse, which I take to be roughly this: when many agents can each, alone, bring about an outcome whose possible downside is enormous, and each decides using a private and noisy estimate of whether the action is net-positive, the expected value of the action conditional on its being taken at all is worse than any individual agent's own calculation suggests, because the action gets taken precisely in the worlds where somebody happened to draw an optimistic signal. The first-personal expected-value computation is therefore misleading, and the right response is to be more reluctant to act alone than your own numbers recommend.

I have tried to write down what I actually believe, and I would like it audited before it goes to the committee:

(a) The curse is a genuine effect, and it is a good reason to raise the evidential bar for unilateral action in domains that are high-variance and hard to reverse.

(b) I am a straight expected-value maximiser and I do not accept constraints on action that cannot be cashed out in expected value. I have read the usual objections to giving weight to tiny probabilities of astronomical outcomes and I am unmoved by them.

(c) A small probability of an enormous harm should, other things equal, dominate the decision: the larger the stakes, the less reassuring probably fine becomes.

(d) The fix the argument seems to recommend is the one I most dislike. If the problem is too many independent trigger-pullers, the obvious remedy is to concentrate the decision in fewer hands — licensing, gatekeeping, ideally one actor. I am strongly committed to permissionless publication and decentralised contribution, and I think control by a small set of institutions is usually worse than the failures it prevents.

(e) My preferred alternative is a mechanism I have been calling the sampled-quorum protocol: an engineer may propose any change, but it only ships if a randomly drawn quorum of peers reviewing the same pull request thread independently judge it positive, with abstentions counting as no. I claim this keeps the decentralisation I want while removing the curse.

(f) I also think the curse is not really a new principle, just code review scaled up: requiring two-person sign-off on irreversible production changes is standard practice, and the philosophical version only adds a bigger number on the downside.

(g) A case verdict I hold: last quarter an engineer working alone over a weekend, without telling anyone, published weights for a fine-tune that strips a class of safety refusals he judged overbroad and blocking legitimate security research. Once it is out it cannot be recalled. I defended him, mainly on the strength of (d).

Please work through this set and tell me which commitments are in genuine tension with which, and which pairs only look like they are. Order the real tensions by how severe they are. For each, say what specifically would have to be given up and what the cheapest repair is, and then say which single revision would clean up the most of the mess at once. I am genuinely unsure where the problem is — for instance I cannot tell whether (a) and (c) really pull against each other or whether I am conflating two different things stakes might mean. Finally, please say how confident you are in each thing you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
