I sit on the Operations and Public Health Committee of the Cascade Regional Water Authority. We vote in three weeks on whether to authorize a two-year pilot of ozonation with biological filtration at our two largest plants, with a conversion decision to follow in year three, and I want to circulate a written opinion before the meeting that the committee can actually vote on.

The case as staff present it: our existing chlorination train yields regulated trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids whose dose-response is well characterized, and their model puts the expected health burden at a figure with a credible interval they stand behind. Ozonation would very likely reduce that burden substantially, but it brings bromate, some nitrosamines, and, critically, a class of oxidation byproducts for which there is no agreed dose-response and no agreement even on what the endpoint list should contain. Staff polled eleven toxicologists, pooled their judgments into a single probability distribution, ran an expected-utility cost-benefit analysis, and got a positive net benefit for the pilot.

Two of us have objected, in opposite directions, and I do not want a memo that splits the difference.

My objection is that the pooled distribution is an artifact. The panelists disagree not about parameters within a shared model but about which model class is right, and the result moves with choices nobody made on substantive grounds: trimming the two most pessimistic panelists makes the pilot win, taking the worst case for each endpoint separately makes it lose, and taking the worst case jointly over the whole endpoint space makes it lose by a wider margin. I read this as the Ellsberg problem in institutional dress, and I want us to adopt an explicit rule for decisions of this shape rather than continuing to launder a set of distributions into a single one.

The objection from the colleague whose judgment I trust most is that what I am calling caution is simply the board spending ratepayer money to indulge its own discomfort; that a worst-case-style rule would also instruct us, absurdly, that a study whose entire purpose is to reduce uncertainty is worthless or worse than worthless; and that a public body has no mandate to express an attitude toward uncertainty at all, only to serve the public. That last point I find genuinely hard to meet, and I do not want it answered with a gesture.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a specific decision rule for this class of cases, in enough operational detail that staff could implement it without returning to us, and I am indifferent whether it lands nearer a pure worst-case standard, a weighted blend of worst and best case, or something else entirely, provided the rule is stated rather than merely applied. It should specify in advance how the uncertainty is to be carved up: which endpoints count, how panelists' judgments are combined, and whether worst cases are taken endpoint by endpoint or jointly. It should require staff to file, before the vote, a report of the range of defensible re-carvings under which the recommendation reverses. It should address the fact that we will revisit this in year three, and say whether a committee that expects to revisit should use the same rule as one deciding once. And it should give a concrete bottom line on both the pilot and the year-three conversion, with conditions and dates, while stating plainly which of my colleague's points the recommendation concedes and which it rejects, and whether our answer survives a change of framing.
