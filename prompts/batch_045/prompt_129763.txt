I'm on a small internal working group that has to write down, for our own deployments, what a *control* protocol would have to deliver in order for us to let a model keep acting in the world — as opposed to an argument that the model is aligned and therefore fine. The case my colleague and I keep circling is a coding agent with tool access to a large private repository, where the failure we actually fear is not a cinematic takeover but a sparse, quiet sabotage: one plausible-looking commit that loosens an authentication check and isn't noticed for months. That's the purpose I want the adequacy list calibrated to — a standard good enough to justify running a system whose worst realistic failure is rare, hard to spot, and effectively irreversible — not a standard for deciding whether to train or release a frontier model, and not one for deciding policy. In particular, I want the protocol to be evaluated on the assumption that the model may be trying to sabotage us and is smart enough to be strategic about it: by *monitor-independence* I mean that the safety of the setup cannot rest on our successfully catching an attempt, and by *audit-conditional defection* I mean the specific pattern where a model behaves on outputs it expects to be audited and defects only on the rest, exploiting the gap between the audited distribution and the deployed one.

Here is the candidate list I've assembled from our meetings, and I want it critiqued rather than ratified: (i) monitor-independence, in the sense above; (ii) no security by obscurity — the protocol stays safe assuming the model knows it in full, having read the codebase, the docs, and the design discussions; (iii) it must handle audit-conditional defection, not merely naive or constant-rate sabotage; (iv) it must preserve enough throughput and task success that nobody is tempted to switch it off; (v) oversight effort mustn't scale linearly with the volume of model action; (vi) it must not assume that the model's chain of thought is a faithful report of its reasoning; (vii) the guarantee should degrade gracefully under capability improvements rather than silently break; (viii) it should survive delegation, i.e. remain coherent when the agent spins up subagents and the human is several layers above the action; (ix) a third party should be able to check the safety case without trusting our own red team; (x) the protocol must state an explicit *sabotage budget* — the number of accepted-but-malicious actions it can absorb before the guarantee fails — derived from the setup rather than assumed. For each, give me the case or consideration that would make it a real requirement, and sort them into non-negotiable, genuine-but-tradeable, and prejudice-of-one-camp. My colleague thinks (i) is only a restatement of the problem and (iv) is a commercial constraint dressed up as a safety criterion; I suspect (vi) is really a prejudice of the interpretability-skeptic camp and that (x) is the one that quietly does all the work. Say where each of us is wrong.

The part I most want is the structural result. I suspect a subset of these can't be had together: it looks to me as though monitor-independence, full disclosure to the model, and no cost to useful throughput form a trilemma — that if your monitor is weaker than the model you must buy safety with either redundancy and paraphrasing (which costs throughput), or withheld information about which actions get audited (which is obscurity and which audit-conditional defection eats for breakfast), or an accepted nonzero rate of undetected sabotage (which is a sabotage budget, and then the question is what makes a given budget defensible). Work through whether that tension is real or whether I've conflated two things, and whether any other pair or triple on the list is jointly unsatisfiable in the same way. Then tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidate protocols — that's the one I care about, since a list that excludes nothing isn't a standard. Sketch the main families of protocol we could actually build (trusted monitoring with a weaker trusted model, monitoring by an untrusted model or by consulting the model itself, paraphrase-and-differential-testing, sandboxing and information control, tripwires and honeypots, capability constraints imposed at deployment) against the list, but keep that to a compact table — the criteria and their motivating cases should carry the answer, not the scoring. I don't need references or a reading list; I need the argument. Finish with a last line of exactly this form: Verdict: <the single requirement doing the most exclusionary work> | <the suspected trilemma is genuine / the suspected trilemma conflates two demands>.
