I teach a first-year seminar on moral disagreement and I keep noticing the same move in section, and then noticing myself do it, and I'd like a word for it — or to be told it's three different things and I'm conflating them. Two cases from this term: a student argues against eating animals on the basis of how much suffering industrial farming causes, and another student says, essentially, that's not really what's bothering you, you just think meat is unclean, and the first student says no, I mean the suffering, look at the numbers, and neither of them moves at all. Then, the other direction, someone objects to commercial surrogacy in a way that is obviously pitched as a degradation point — something about selling womb-space, bodies being treated as equipment — and the students who don't have that register hear it as a harm claim about the resulting child, ask what the evidence of harm is, find the evidence thin, and conclude that there was never a reason there at all. What I think I'm seeing is: each side hears the other's moral reason in terms of the moral concerns it can itself register (I'm thinking of the Haidt-style five-foundations thing — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity), and the translation always makes the reason look weaker than it did in the original, because the translated version is a bad instance of a kind of reason you do have rather than a good instance of a kind you don't. And then it feeds itself: once you've got the thin version you conclude the other person is being dishonest or is irrational, which makes you more confident, and they're doing exactly the same to you, so both sides come away from the argument more certain and the disagreement looks rationally hopeless. I had a version of this with my brother-in-law over thanksgiving about a flag thing, and it ended with both of us thinking the other was faking.

The reason I don't think I've got a handle on it yet is three complications. First, there's a version of this that people do on purpose and charitably: you take your own environmental pitch and rephrase it in stewardship-and-authority terms because you know your audience doesn't respond to harm-and-fairness framings, and that seems like the same translation but done well, and I can't tell whether it's the same mechanism with the valence reversed or a genuinely different thing — the deliberate version seems to require representing the other person's foundation, while what I'm describing seems to involve not being able to. Second, I catch myself doing it on my own reasons: I'm opposed to advertising in schools, or to nuclear power, and when I try to say why I find myself producing a harm argument that I don't actually think carries my objection, and I want to know whether that intrapersonal case is the same phenomenon or just ordinary self-ignorance. Third, I have a hazy memory of things that are nearby but not right — something about moral dumbfounding, which as I remember is about people being unable to justify their own verdicts rather than mishearing someone else's, and something about motive attribution asymmetry, which I think is about attributing the other side's view to self-interest, whereas what I'm describing doesn't accuse anyone of self-interest, it just makes their reason thinner than they think it is.

So: does the automatic, uncharitable, self-confirming translation of someone's moral reason into a foundation you yourself have — the one that leaves you certain they had no reason — have a name and a literature, or am I describing something that falls between the established concepts? I don't want a label; I want the carving. What exactly is the thing, how does it differ from each nearest candidate (including the deliberate reframing case and the intrapersonal case), where are its edges, and is it one phenomenon or several that I've bundled because they feel the same from the inside? Please mark your confidence explicitly: how sure you are that there's an established literature here versus that you're coining, and if you coin something, say what would count as evidence that it's a real psychological pattern rather than a tidy philosopher's distinction. And push back if needed — if my description rests on a cartoon version of the foundations literature, or bundles unrelated things, or if the whole worry is really just "people are bad at understanding reasons they don't share," say that plainly instead of handing me a neat term. One last thing, which may be a separate question I'm confusing in: if the phenomenon is real and it's symmetric, does that tell us anything about whether the psychology of left and right has any bearing on who is actually right, or does it just tell us why the argument never goes anywhere?
