I sit on the fellowship committee of my university's Humanities Center. Each year we award one senior fellowship — a year of salary replacement plus a small research budget — to a single internal applicant. The Center's charter tells us only to award it on the ground of "scholarly distinction" and says nothing about how to weigh cases in which the ground runs out. Over time our minutes have settled into a two-way classification that we now apply before voting: we record a pair of finalists as ON A PAR when both candidacies can be assessed under one covering value such as scholarly distinction, but neither is better than the other and they are not equally good either; we record them as INCOMMENSURABLE when, in our judgment, there is no covering value under which the two can be ranked against each other at all. I need to know what our own record commits us to in the case now before us.

Our precedents, in order:

2016. Two historians of the same period. M stronger on archival originality, T stronger on synthetic reach. Classified on a par. We awarded the fellowship to T by tossing a coin, and the minutes record no reason beyond the toss itself.

2017. A philosopher proposing a monograph on a narrowly technical problem, and a composer-scholar proposing a lecture-recital with an accompanying critical essay. Classified incommensurable: the committee said a recital is not the sort of thing that can be ranked against a monograph under scholarly distinction. Rather than choose, we recommended to the dean that the year's money be divided into two half-year awards, and both were funded.

2019. Two literary scholars, on a par. We awarded the fellowship to the candidate whose project fit the Center's announced annual theme — a theme adopted some years earlier by an earlier committee largely for scheduling reasons — and we wrote to the runner-up that the theme had been decisive.

2021. Two art historians, on a par. R was already the stronger of the two on methodological innovation and then submitted a short revision adding a small but genuine further improvement in that same respect. We awarded the fellowship to R, the minutes saying that the revision made the choice clear.

2022. Two finalists, on a par. A third file was added late: a candidate in a field the Center had never funded, plainly weaker than the other two on every dimension we had identified. We refused to treat novelty of field as a tiebreaker and awarded the fellowship to one of the original two by lot.

2024. Two finalists, on a par. Over the objection of two members we deferred the award for a year and returned the money to the endowment. The chair's note reads: an award we cannot justify to the runner-up is not an award we should make.

The case now before us. Two finalists for the 2026 fellowship. A proposes a monograph on instrument-making in a single city between 1600 and 1750: exceptionally deep archival work, narrow reach. B proposes a large computational study of nineteenth-century periodicals together with a public interface: wide reach, shallower on primary-source depth. On a provisional vote we classified them on a par under scholarly distinction, five to two; the dissenters hold that a monograph and a dataset-with-interface are not the same kind of scholarly object and that the pair is therefore incommensurable, as in 2017. After that vote B submitted a one-page addendum adding a modest but real archival component — a small improvement in precisely the respect in which B was the weaker of the two, leaving B still the weaker on that respect and still the stronger on reach. Three further constraints: this year's payout would in fact stretch to two half-year awards, as in 2017, though neither project could then be finished within the year and we have never split the award in a parity case; a transparency rule adopted in 2025 now requires that every decision be accompanied by a written statement of reasons sent to both finalists; and the two finalists know about the addendum, so any letter that simply ignores it will be read as evasive.

What I need from you, in the form of a recommendation I can put in front of the other six members: what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves; which of our own precedents is the closest analog and which are merely apparent analogs; the distinct rules that fit the whole track record, with what each predicts here; if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, the principle we would be adopting under each live option; what the letter to the runner-up should say under your recommended option; and the strongest consideration available to whichever option you reject. Pay particular attention to two things that our precedents do not settle: whether it matters that B's small improvement is in the respect in which B was weaker, where in 2021 the revision improved an already stronger respect; and whether our 2024 refusal to make an unjustifiable award can be squared with the coin tosses of 2016 and 2022, or whether we changed our minds without noticing. Finally, name the single further case that would best separate the rules you think are still in play, and say why it separates them. Please keep the whole thing under nine hundred words.
