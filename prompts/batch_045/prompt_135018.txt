Hi — I somehow ended up on my university's Commemoration and Naming Advisory Committee (nobody else wanted the seat, and I've regretted it since September), and we have to send something to the president and the board in three weeks. We've been going in circles for months: every meeting someone brings up Jefferson or Churchill or Columbus, someone else says the man-of-his-time defence, someone says that defence is just cowardice with a footnote, and we adjourn. We have three live referrals and I need a memo, not a discussion.

First: Pemberton Hall, the engineering building, named in 1961 for Alden Pemberton, a mill owner whose fortune came out of convict leasing and who published a tract defending it in the 1890s; a local bishop and the editor of a Black newspaper attacked him in print at the time, so there were dissenting contemporaries, though mostly people with no power. There's also a line in the 1961 board minutes about maintaining the character of the university that half of us read as a signal during the desegregation fight and half read as nothing at all. Second: the Carrington Lectures in Statesmanship, named for a twentieth-century wartime prime minister — our own Churchill problem — honoured for leadership in a war that was partly about holding an empire together, and implicated in a colonial famine and in detention camps that some contemporaries denounced and most did not; I genuinely cannot tell whether the empire is inside or outside the thing we're celebrating, which is exactly why I want the memo to take a side. Third: the Alcott Scholarship, named in 1928 for a novelist alumna whose diaries were unsealed in 2019 and contain run-of-the-mill antisemitic bile that she never acted on and that pretty much everyone in her circle shared.

Here are the distinctions I've been chewing on, roughly in the order I think they matter, and I want you to use them where they help and tell me plainly which one is a dud or cuts against me: (a) blame versus conferral — the man-of-his-time defence answers a question about blame, but the committee's question is about what this institution is willing to put its name behind now, and those can come apart in both directions; (b) whose time, exactly — the defence may be laundering a claim about a class, race or profession as if it were a claim about a decade, since the people who objected are often the people with the least standing to be heard; (c) the nexus test, whether the wrongdoing is part of the very achievement being honoured or merely adjacent to it, which I think is what separates the Columbus case from the mathematician-with-vile-views case; (d) current uptake, meaning what the naming does now given when and why it went up — a 1961 naming may be doing different work from a 1905 one; (e) the archive effect, that we only know about the wrongs of people who left papers, so any consistent policy becomes a lottery over who was literate and who was audited, which pushes me toward petition-triggered or scheduled review rather than a moral audit of two hundred namings; and (f) the unstable present, that our own consensus will look parochial in fifty years and any decision should be written so it can be revisited by people who disagree with us.

What I want: a memo of about 1200 words max, addressed to the president, with the bottom line in the first couple of sentences — a concrete recommendation on each of the three referrals plus whatever material commitment or contextualisation should ride along with it, and dates, not soon. Give me the general test the committee should apply to future cases, stated tightly enough that a different committee could use it, and say what it costs. Two more things. First, engage the best argument for keeping the names rather than a strawman: the one that keeps me up is that removal is cheap symbolism that lets the institution feel clean without changing who it admits, hires or pays, and that we will end up renaming randomly depending on who left a diary. Answer that properly. Second, tell me what your test implies about a figure who really was excused — where nobody at the time, including his critics, thought the thing he did was wrong and there's no dissenting voice to point at. Does he keep his building? I suspect the honest answer is that the defence succeeding doesn't settle the naming question at all, but I want to see whether that falls out of your test or is just my mood after six of these meetings. And please don't cite anything or point me at reading: I've had enough of the literature, I want the argument.
