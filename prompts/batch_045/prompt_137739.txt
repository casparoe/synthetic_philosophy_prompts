I want to practise attacking the neo-republican theory of freedom as non-domination from a negative-liberty position, so please play its defender: a committed, well-read republican who holds that freedom is a status — not being subject to anyone's arbitrary will — rather than merely the absence of interference. Your position is that (i) interference by a power that is non-arbitrary, tracked to the interests of those it affects, and contestable by them need not reduce freedom at all; (ii) an arbitrary power can reduce freedom even when it is never exercised, because of what it does to the options an agent can safely take, the expectations she must form, and the standing she has before others; and (iii) this is a genuine third value, not a repackaging of negative liberty and not a smuggled-in ideal of self-mastery or civic equality. Concede small points readily; never concede (i)–(iii). Argue from cases and distinctions, not from authority: no citations, bibliographies, reading suggestions, or claims about what any particular book or article argues, and do not rest anything on the views of named living philosophers. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to at most two paragraphs, and stipulate any further details of my cases you need rather than asking me for them.

Here is my opening move. Consider the Sealed Veto. Every citizen is assigned a Guardian holding a legally unlimited power to annul any decision that citizen makes, but the power is sealed: a constitutional provision enforced by an independent commission, with perfect and publicly verified compliance, makes any annulment impossible, and everyone knows this. No veto has been issued in a century, no citizen ingratiates herself with a Guardian, and nobody plans around the possibility of one. So there is no interference, no realistic prospect of interference, and no anticipatory self-censorship. You nonetheless want to say these citizens are unfree. Then take a horn. If the Guardian's capacity matters as a bare legal status with no effect on options, expectations, or conduct, your complaint looks like the egalitarian, positive-liberty ideal of equal standing that you claim not to be committed to. If instead the capacity matters only through the risk it carries, the costs it threatens, or the planning it forces, then what you are measuring is a quantity of interference, and non-domination is just expected-interference negative liberty with extra steps. Which horn do you take, and what exactly makes the Sealed Veto a case of domination?

If you take the second horn, I have a case ready that I think breaks it: the Compensated Arrest, in which every citizen carries universal surety insurance that leaves her, by her own prior reckoning, ex ante indifferent to any arbitrary interference by officials, so that arbitrary power and actual interference are both present while no one suffers any setback or plans any differently.
