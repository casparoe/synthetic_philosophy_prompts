The position I want stress-tested is the following two-part claim about the frame problem.

First, the relevance-gap claim: the frame problem, in the form worth caring about, is neither the engineering problem of updating a large store of beliefs efficiently nor the practical problem of getting a system to ignore most of what it knows. It is the problem of how a bounded agent with many standing commitments determines, on a new occasion, which of them bear on what it should infer or do next — a problem about the rationality of relevance-sensitive inference — and it is unsolved in principle, not merely unsolved in practice. Second, the frame-luck claim: contemporary systems that behave flexibly are frame-lucky rather than frame-solving. Their success rides on regularities of the distribution that happen to make the right features salient, and they have no account of why the features they in fact ignored were irrelevant.

I want a map of the position's exposure, and I want the following questions treated explicitly rather than folded into a survey.

Which findings would force me to give the view up, which would only force a qualification, and which would I be entitled to dismiss as restating the disagreement? Please distinguish developments that have arguably already arrived from hypothetical ones.

I am most worried about three internal failures, and I would like your assessment of how serious each is. (i) Collapse: does the relevance-gap claim reduce, without residue, to the problem of abduction, or to the problem of induction, or to the qualification problem understood as the problem of specifying an action's non-effects? If it collapses into a neighbor, is that fatal or a friendly reduction? (ii) Splitting: I may have stapled two problems together — relevance for action and relevance for justification. If the first is behaviorally dissolved but the second is not, my view shrinks into something only a normative theorist could care about. Does that count as a defeat? (iii) Insulation: the frame-lucky/frame-solving distinction may be untestable. Propose the sharpest test I would have to accept in advance — my own candidate is that a system told mid-task that a specified consideration is relevant, and why, integrates it without retraining-scale evidence — and tell me honestly whether that test is already passed, or endlessly movable, and whether the resulting insulation is a legitimate feature of a claim about subpersonal mechanism or a vice of the view.

I would also like the discriminating cases treated separately from aggregate performance. Suppose a system reliably tracks relevance that is deep, in the sense that it is reachable only by a chain of five defeasible links from its standing commitments, but fails on relevance that is novel, in the sense that no structurally similar case occurred in its training distribution. Which failure would damage me more, and why?

Finally: say what defenders of this position should themselves concede as counting against it — including that the classical situation-calculus framing may be tied to a discarded model of agency, that a modularist or encapsulationist reply would, if true, dissolve the global problem, and that an anti-representationalist critique would show the problem to be an artifact of assuming that cognition is rule-governed manipulation of representations. If you conclude that the view is well insulated, say so, say whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind, and say what I should do about it: abandon it, downgrade it to a research heuristic, or restate it as a claim about what any system would need in order to justify its actions rather than merely produce them.
