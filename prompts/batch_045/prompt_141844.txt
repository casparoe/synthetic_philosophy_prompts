I am writing up a position paper on self-deception and would like an audit of the package of views I have ended up with before I commit to it in print. I do not want a survey of the literature, and I do not want citations, references, or a reading list: what I want is the structure of my own set of commitments, the places where they pull against each other, and what each possible repair would cost me. Here is where I have landed.

First, I hold that self-deception is a genuine and common psychological phenomenon, and I am opposed to two ways of deflating it: I do not think it is just ordinary error or ordinary bias described in a dramatic register, and I do not accept the psychodynamic picture on which the person contains something like an inner agent that does the deceiving. My paradigm cases are the husband who gathers and then refuses to weigh the evidence of his wife's affair, and the patient who plans a future she has been told she will not live to see.

Second, I hold that it must not be modeled on interpersonal deception: no homunculi, no sub-agents with intentions of their own, no inner committee. The self-deceiver is one agent, and I want the phenomenon describable without splitting the person into parts.

Third, I hold that the acquisition of the belief need not be intentional. Motivated cognition is enough: biased search, selective attention, asymmetric scrutiny of evidence, avoidance of occasions on which the truth would come up. This holds even where the agent never decided to bring the comforting belief about and would deny having done anything of the kind. I also extend this to cases where the belief arrived at is not one the agent wanted to be true: a man who, out of a standing fear of public humiliation, comes to believe that his colleagues despise him. I want to count those as self-deception as well.

Fourth, I hold that in genuine self-deception the truth remains, in some robust sense, in view. It is not mere ignorance, and not merely a false belief held on bad evidence; I resist dissolving the phenomenon into something like akrasia about belief, or into the thin claim that most of the evidence points one way while the agent believes the other with full conviction and no residue. Something like a sustained availability of the disconfirming considerations has to be present.

Fifth, I hold that the self-deceiver is criticizable for the belief and that self-deception is not an excuse: the plea that he could not help believing it does not get him off the hook. This is part of why I think the category is worth keeping rather than replacing with the blander notion of motivated error.

Sixth, methodologically, I want the concept empirically anchored. It should track something measurable in cognition, along the lines of selective exposure or asymmetric scrutiny, rather than being a purely interpretive stance we adopt towards other people. If it turned out that the ordinary notion cross-cuts several unrelated mechanisms, I would treat that as a problem for my account rather than for the psychology.

Seventh, a case verdict I am attached to. Call it the archivist: a man begins to suspect his business partner of stealing from the firm, quietly assembles a meticulous dossier of the evidence, seals it in a drawer, arranges that it never be opened, and goes on trusting his partner, eventually resting easy in that trust. I want to say that this is self-deception, and a central instance of it, not merely a neighbouring phenomenon such as willful ignorance.

Eighth, I hold that self-deception is continuous with the ordinary motivated reasoning documented in psychology rather than being a special pathological mechanism, while also holding that only some motivated reasoning is self-deception and the rest is just bias.

My worry is that I have quietly removed the marks that were doing the work of separating self-deception from plain motivated error, and that my attachment to the archivist case and to the blame claim is being supported by something I have not stated. Go through these commitments pair by pair and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal. For each, say what would have to be given up or added, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most, and say explicitly which of the eight you think I am least entitled to keep. I am attached to all of them, so do not assume I have a preferred one to sacrifice.

One request about form: write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague who has asked you to look over a draft. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered sections, no bold text, no tables, and no block quotes: paragraphs of running prose from the first sentence to the last.
