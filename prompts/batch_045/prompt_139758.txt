So I'm a third-year PhD student in religious studies, mostly working on Indo-Tibetan debate manuals, and this semester I'm sitting in on a philosophy-department seminar on truth and paradox, and the two keep colliding in my head. A friend in that seminar is a dialetheist and keeps telling me everything I read about looks like a non-classical logic wearing ritual costume, which I mostly resist but can't fully answer. Here's the concrete thing I want to pick apart. In the Geluk debate courtyards I've spent time at (mostly Sera, a bit at Drepung), the challenger is the active one: he poses a subject and a predicate and forces consequences — roughly, "it follows that the subject has property P because it has reason R" — while the seated defender has only a small fixed menu of replies: accept, "the reason is not established," "there is no pervasion," and so on. The challenger, as far as I can tell, never states a position of his own; every move is a consequence drawn from what the defender has already granted. But the surrounding institution works the opposite way: years of the curriculum are spent on tenets doxography, grading the systems from the Vaibhāṣika up through the Madhyamaka subdivisions, and in the exams you're expected to hold and defend the Prāsaṅgika presentation as the correct one, and your rank depends on doing it well. And the canonical textual move is the four-cornered one: the opening verse of Nāgārjuna's Root Verses on the Middle Way denies that things arise from themselves, from something else, from both, or from no cause at all.

What I want from you is the implicit theory behind this package of practices — what it strictly presupposes, what it merely sits comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined so that several quite different theories would license the same behavior. Three threads I care about most. First, the no-own-thesis norm: does it actually presuppose that you can refute without asserting, and what is the logical form of the "it follows that" move? I've been toying with a distinction between what I call the conditional-only reading (I'm merely showing you that what you've granted commits you to something you won't accept) and the loaded reading (the consequence is false, and therefore your premise is), and it seems to me only the loaded one needs any real metaphysical backing, while the conditional-only one is nearly free — but then I dont see how mere conditional consequences could ever get a defender to change his view, which is transparently the point of the exercise. Second, pervasion: challengers get hammered for "no pervasion," so the link between reason and target property has to hold without exception. What kind of necessity is that on a view where nothing has intrinsic nature? Does the debate form quietly presuppose that logical consequence has some kind of own-being, or rather that it escapes the emptiness argument that applies to tables and persons, and if so is that a commitment or an accident of the format? Third, the tetralemma itself: refuting all four corners only disciplines anyone if the corners jointly exhaust the space of options, but if the point of the exercise is to reject the idea that every question has exactly one of two determinate answers, then a four-cornered refutation seems to presuppose precisely the tidy bivalent logical space it's supposed to be exploding. I've started calling these the dilemmatic and explosive readings and I have no idea whether that maps onto anything real in the literature.

And the bit that most looks like a flat inconsistency, if you could address it directly: no-thesis norm in the courtyard, but a required, examinable, ranking-dominating thesis in the classroom and the doxography. Does that dissolve once you distinguish conventional from ultimate, or once you distinguish "having a view" from "asserting a view about the ultimate," or is it a genuine tension between what the institution says and what it rewards? Finally, the counterfactual half: concretely, what would have to change in the debate practice if the prasaṅga-only norm were replaced by the kind of autonomous inference Bhāviveka wanted — proper thesis, proper reason, stated in your own voice — and separately, what would change if my friend were right and some of those four corners really were both true and false? I'd rather have the reconstruction and the dependency structure than a survey of who said what, and please flag anywhere you think I've misdescribed the practice itself, since I'm reconstructing a lot of this from field notes.
