I'm putting together a one-page "position profile" on AI takeoff speeds for a policy fellowship application — they want a statement of where I stand on how fast things could move and what follows for governance — and before I send it I'd like someone to check whether the pieces actually hang together. I've read a decent amount of the forecasting and governance material already, so please don't spend the answer summarizing it or pointing me at further reading: I'm writing this offline with no web access and I don't want a bibliography. Just work with the views as I state them. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Takeoff will be continuous in the sense that matters for mechanism — no single training run or single algorithmic insight produces an unbounded jump; what we get is a fairly smooth response of capabilities to cumulative inputs being poured in. But I also think the wall-clock window between "clearly transformative" and "radically transformative" will be short: months to a couple of years, not generations. I hold both halves firmly and don't think the second undercuts the first, though people keep telling me it does.

(2) I reject the decisive-strategic-advantage picture. Capability gradients will be shallow enough, and diffusion fast enough (weight exfiltration, talent mobility, espionage, straightforward independent rediscovery once a recipe is known), that no actor converts a modest lead into durable unilateral control. So: a multipolar outcome, no singleton.

(3) I nevertheless expect real critical-juncture effects: whoever is in the driver's seat during the transition writes rules and builds institutions that persist long after whatever capability lead they had has evaporated.

(4) Methodologically, I want to replace the recursive-self-improvement framing with something I've started calling the "cumulative-input elasticity" framing: what matters is how steeply capabilities respond to cumulative effective investment — compute, data, researcher-years, whatever the binding complements turn out to be — rather than whether AI systems are the ones doing the improving. I think this reframing is what supports (1) and (2), and that it makes the old "how fast can an AI improve an AI" question nearly irrelevant. Someone in my reading group says it's completely neutral with respect to (1), and I'm not sure whether that's right or whether it just sounds like it should be.

(5) On policy: I think a frontier training-compute threshold — a cap above some level, enforced at the hardware or chip level — is the single highest-leverage instrument we have in the near term, and I want to defend it as such rather than as one tool among many.

(6) On method again: I think governance should be empirical and iterative, reacting to capabilities we actually observe in deployed systems rather than to speculative models of what is coming, and revising the rules generation by generation as the evidence arrives.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real tension what exactly would have to give and what that revision would cost me? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me six equally weighted worries. My own hunts: (2) and (3) look inconsistent to me but I think they're fine, because the lock-in could run through institutions and coordination rather than through capabilities; (5) looks shaky given (2) and (4), since if recipes diffuse and inputs are substitutable then a cap on one input may not bind on the thing I care about; and (6) may be in trouble given (1), though I can't quite articulate why. But don't just validate my guesses — tell me if I've mislocated the problem, or if one of the commitments I think is safe is actually where the load-bearing crack is.

On confidence: say how confident you are in each diagnosis, mark explicitly where you're relying on an assumption I haven't stated, and if you think the framing itself is confused — if, say, "continuous but fast" is closer to an empty conjunction than to a view, or the whole profile is missing one distinction that would dissolve most of the apparent conflict — say that plainly instead of politely auditing a muddle.
