I am a novelist, and I am well into a book set in the southern Maya lowlands during the Terminal Classic: a multigenerational novel about a family of scribes attached to a court that is slowly, and without ever deciding to, giving up the habit of raising carved and dated stone monuments. I find that I cannot write a page of it until I know what kind of event I am depicting, so what I need is a case analysis rather than background reading.

The criterion I have been working with — call it the testamentary criterion — is this: a society has collapsed, as opposed to merely changed, when it loses the practices by which it hands a description of itself to those who come after it. Collapse on this view is not the loss of population, territory, or complexity, but the breaking of a chain of address: the cessation of records made for the sake of a future reader who will not otherwise know what has been lost, and who could therefore either reclaim it or knowingly refuse it. What is destroyed in a genuine collapse is not well-being or power but the capacity of successors to know what it is they are missing.

Please take the Terminal Classic transformation of the Classic Maya and analyze it strictly through this criterion, and then tell me where the criterion breaks. Take the following as given: the number of dated monuments erected per year across the Maya area rises to roughly forty around 750, falls back to about ten by 800, and reaches zero by about 900, with no new major construction in the central Maya area after roughly 830; the Copán valley population, which peaked near twenty-eight thousand in the fifth century, declines to about fifteen thousand by 900 and to under a thousand by 1200; the northern Yucatán subsequently prospers, with Chichén Itzá, Mayapán and Uxmal flourishing in very different artistic and architectural styles and with far less monumental hieroglyphic writing; Maya communities and languages persist, and an independent Maya polity survives until the Spanish conquest of Nojpetén in 1697.

I would like four things. First, a disciplined mapping: what the unit is (a city-state, a polity, a scribal institution, the Long Count itself), what on this criterion counts as the record addressed to the future, what it says was happening to people who went on farming, trading and building while the carving stopped, and whether the verdict is collapse, transformation, or something the criterion has no word for.

Second, the places where the criterion misdescribes the case. It appears to make the fate of a whole population track the fate of an elite technology of self-commemoration; I want to know whether that is a fatal class bias or a defensible consequence, and how the criterion should handle a society most of whose members never had a testament at all.

Third, two stress cases from outside the Maya material. For Mycenaean Greece: Linear B was a script used almost entirely for palace administration, it disappears with the destruction of the palaces around the end of the Late Bronze Age, and the following centuries offer no conclusive evidence of writing in Greece until the adoption of the alphabet several centuries later — so does the criterion deliver the result that the Mycenaean world collapsed, or the result that it never addressed the future in the first place and so was never the kind of thing that could collapse? And for the Maya themselves: if the destruction of the surviving Maya books by Spanish missionaries in the sixteenth century, and the long period in which Maya writing was present but unreadable before its decipherment in the twentieth century, both bear on the chain of address, then the criterion seems to say either that the Maya collapse was inflicted from outside in the 1500s or that it was revoked by scholars in the 1900s. Tell me what the criterion should say about collapse imposed by a conqueror and about collapse repaired retroactively. If you bring in Rapa Nui, do not rest anything on the disputed demographic and ecological story there.

Fourth, what the case does to the criterion in return — whether it confirms it, or is an anomaly, or is a case the criterion never anticipated — and one further matter that is personal to the project: my own novel is an artifact of exactly the kind whose disappearance is supposed to constitute a collapse. Does the criterion in fact imply that a people can be un-collapsed by being written about, that collapse is revocable by art, and does it give any guidance to an agent inside the process, such as a lord deciding in a bad year whether the stela still gets carved? Where the criterion is silent, say so rather than making it speak. Do not give me citations or reading lists; work from the facts I have supplied and from what you already know.
