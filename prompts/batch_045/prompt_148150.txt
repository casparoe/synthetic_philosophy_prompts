I've been reading the rationalist blogs for about fifteen years, and I keep seeing Hofstadter's frame/outer/inner-message distinction from the "Location of Meaning" chapter of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* wheeled out in arguments about whether language models "really" understand anything. I read that chapter a long time ago and don't trust my memory, so before I let the circulating version harden in my head I want an audit of it. Here is the gloss from a widely-linked post on interpretability that I'm pretty sure is doing some of the work in those threads:

"Hofstadter's point is that no message contains its own meaning. Every message comes in three layers. The frame message is the physical carrier — the grooves on a record, the bottle floating in the water — and all it says is: this object was produced by an intelligent agent, so try to decode it. The outer message is the code: the conventions and the encryption that turn a physical pattern into symbols, which is the part information theory already handles perfectly well. The inner message is the decrypted content, the symbols the sender actually put there, and it is the same for every receiver who has the code. Since all of the content is recoverable from the carrier plus the code, and the carrier is just inert matter, meaning is not in the object at all: it is created only when a mind reads symbols off the end of the pipeline. That is why a machine can be sensitive to the frame and outer messages and still understand nothing — it can process a code without any meaning ever happening." The post's payoff is that this settles the current argument: the model manipulates codes, the human supplies the meaning, and there is no sense in which the model itself means anything.

What I want, claim by claim, is: which of this a careful reader of the chapter would accept as fair, which are legitimate simplifications a popularizer could defend, and which quietly collapse a distinction Hofstadter keeps apart or run a technical term in its everyday sense. I have two suspicions but no confidence in either: I half-remember the inner message as depending on what the receiver brings, so that the same physical object can carry different inner messages to different receivers (or none), which sits badly with "the same for every receiver who has the code"; and I remember the three levels as being about meaning rather than about codes and information theory, so "the outer message is the encryption" smells wrong to me without my being able to say why.

I also don't want the corrected account merely stated, I want it put to work. Take the Voyager Golden Record — a gold-plated disc launched in 1977, carrying greetings in many languages, natural sounds and music, with a cover etched with a stylus, a playback-speed diagram, the hydrogen transition as a unit, and a pulsar map. On the best reading of Hofstadter, what are its frame, outer, and inner messages, and for whom? Where do the levels come apart in that case? And is it a clean illustration of the scheme, or a stress test that exposes something the scheme handles badly — the fact that we designed the artifact precisely so that a receiver sharing no language with us could climb from one level to the next seems to me to be doing something interesting, but I can't say what.

Finally, be disciplined about confidence: separate clearly what the text plainly says from what is genuinely contested or ambiguous, both in Hofstadter's own presentation and in whether this three-level account is a good theory of meaning at all, and don't hand me a contested reading of a decades-old popular book as if it were the consensus. And tell me whether the post's conclusion about language models follows from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or whether it rests on the parts that don't.
