I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm finishing a piece on how the word "truth" is used in arguments about misinformation and institutional trust. A recent popular book on the history of American philosophy contains a passage that would be very useful to me as a cautionary tale about where pragmatism leads. I would like to quote it or paraphrase it, but I don't want to print a stranger's capsule version of two historical philosophers and then have to defend it when a specialist emails me. Here is the passage, as closely as I can reproduce it:

Both classical pragmatists gave the same surprising answer to the apparently arid question of what truth is: a belief is true just in case acting on it works. William James, the Harvard psychologist-philosopher, gave the answer its most famous form — the true, on his account, is nothing over and above what it is expedient for us to believe, what has cash value in experience — and he was content to be read as saying that a belief's truth consists in its usefulness to whoever happens to hold it. C. S. Peirce, the logician who coined the word pragmatism and later, in irritation at what James had made of it, relabelled his own version pragmaticism, said something more cautious: truth is the opinion on which inquiry would eventually settle, which is to say that truth is whatever the scientific community ends up agreeing on. The two men differed mostly in venue and temperament — Peirce wanted a maxim for laboratory inquiry, James wanted one for life — and that difference has not worn well. If truth is usefulness, then a belief stops being true when it stops being useful, which is why the pragmatists were committed to saying that continental drift was false when Wegener proposed it in the 1910s and became true around the late 1960s, once the seafloor-spreading evidence had accumulated. Nor can the view accommodate the obvious case of an extremely useful false belief — the cancer patient's confidence that she will recover — without simply declaring it true, which is a reductio of the whole approach. And Peirce's version has a hole of its own: if the last inquirer dies, there is no final opinion for true beliefs to be, so on his account nothing would be true. Small wonder the program had been abandoned within academic philosophy by the 1940s, and that what survives of it in common parlance is a licence for saying that everyone has their own truth.

Two things in it make me suspicious, though I can't say exactly why: my instinct is that Peirce is treated less charitably than he deserves, and that the continental-drift example is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear. I also don't want to end up defending pragmatism; I just want to know whether I can use the paragraph.

What I need is an audit I can act on, sorted into three groups: what a historian of American philosophy would grant as it stands; what is a compression I could still print provided I add one or two qualifications (tell me what they would be); and what I must not repeat in any form. Within that, please be explicit about which of the two men each of the book's criticisms actually lands on, since my piece turns on the book's claim that they were saying the same thing. In particular: does James's account really make truth relative to the individual believer and to her practical projects, or is the book mistaking a claim about how truth is assessed or verified for a claim about what truth consists in? Is Peirce's final opinion an actual future consensus or an ideal limit, and does it make any difference to him that the community is real scientists rather than an idealized one? Is the difference between them really one of venue and temperament, or is there a substantive disagreement the book has flattened? And how much of the book's punchline survives if its formulations are corrected — does either version license the everyone-has-their-own-truth reading, and is the historiography of the abandoned-by-the-1940s sentence fair? If the corrected version still supports a cautionary tale, I would rather tell that one than the book's.
