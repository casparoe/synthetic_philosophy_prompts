I am helping a foundation spend a single large grant, and I want your reasoning rather than a survey of a field.

The grant will support one new research group: roughly fifteen researchers for five years, trained as theorists and philosophers, with a small budget for modest human-subject studies and no access to frontier-scale compute. Their mandate is the conceptual and theoretical foundations of inferring what people want from how they behave — the family of problems that includes inverse reinforcement learning, preference learning and reward modelling. The group is not meant to build better systems. It is meant to establish what question such formalisms are actually answering, where they are confused, and which of those confusions are load-bearing for the people who do build the systems. Please write in British English, and do not organise your answer around papers, authors or a reading list: I want the arguments and the structure of the problem.

Three stipulations, so that we are arguing about the same thing.

First, most behaviour is *laundered*. Call a behavioural record laundered when it was produced by someone acting inside a choice environment that another optimiser arranged for its own purposes: a recommender's ranking, a platform's defaults, an employer's incentive scheme, a market's price signal, the ordering of options on a form. Any realistic dataset of human behaviour is largely of this kind, and the laundering is not noise added to a clean signal. It is a second agent's objective written into the data-generating process, and it is invisible in the data itself.

Second, the reward–rationality degeneracy. If we model a person as choosing with probability proportional to an exponential of the value of each option, then multiplying all rewards by a factor and dividing the temperature by that same factor leaves the choice probabilities unchanged: behaviour can reveal the ratio of preference strength to noise, never either quantity alone. Observed near-deterministic choice is equally compatible with someone who cares a great deal and trembles a lot and with someone whose preferences are mild but who is an unusually consistent chooser. Relatedly, many distinct reward functions make the very same policy optimal: add a constant, rescale by a positive factor, or add to the reward a term of the form *potential of the next state minus potential of the current state*, which telescopes away across a trajectory, and nothing observable changes. And a reward is only ever identified relative to a chosen representation of the situation, so the features the reward is permitted to care about are themselves a substantive assumption smuggled in before inference begins.

Third, call the *recovery picture* the view that there is a fact of the matter about a person's reward function and that inference aims to estimate it; call the *construction picture* the view that what inference delivers is a useful summary which partly constitutes, rather than discovers, the preference it reports. I do not know which is correct, and I doubt the group will settle it.

Given all that: what should these fifteen people work on, and in what order? Give me five or six questions, ranked, and defend the ranking. For each, I want to know what it gates — which other questions, and which downstream engineering or policy defaults, cannot be responsibly settled until it is answered; whether it is tractable in five years given this training and this budget, or whether it secretly needs scale empirics the group cannot run; whether it is genuinely neglected or already crowded by people who will get there anyway; what a finished answer would look like, so the group can tell whether it has won; and what result or evidence during the first two years would move it up or down the list. Please also indicate which questions depend on the recovery-versus-construction dispute, and either show that your ordering is robust across both pictures or tell me explicitly which picture you are betting on and what in the ordering changes if that bet is wrong.

Finally, name two or three lines of work that look prominent or attractive but that you would deliberately not fund here, and say why: because they are crowded, because answering them would change little downstream, or because they presuppose an answer to something else on your list.

Aim for about 1,500 words, and flag the points where your ranking rests on empirical guesses you are least confident in.
