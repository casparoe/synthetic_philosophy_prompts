I have been reading a popular treatment of Global Workspace Theory — the family of views that runs from Baars' theatre metaphor to Dehaene and Changeux's global neuronal workspace picture — and I want to know how much of it I can rely on before I build a seminar presentation around it. I am reconstructing the passage from memory rather than quoting it, and I am not asking you to identify the piece, supply references, or check any empirical literature; I want the accuracy audit, done on conceptual grounds.

The passage runs roughly like this:

"On the global workspace account, consciousness is what happens when a piece of information wins a competition among many unconscious processors and gets broadcast, from a set of workspace neurons in the prefrontal cortex, to the brain at large. That broadcast is just what being conscious consists in: before it, processing is unconscious and modular; after it, every system has the information and can act on it, which is why consciousness is unified and why it is the thing that lets us speak, reason and decide. Critics complain that this only explains access — the availability of information for report and control — and not why any of it feels like anything, but that complaint is fatal to the theory, because the feeling is the whole phenomenon: a theory of access is no more a theory of consciousness than a theory of circulation is a theory of hunger. The empirical situation is worse still. Researchers in practice just ask subjects to press a button, so what they measure is report, and a theory that defines consciousness as whatever gets reported is unfalsifiable — anything a subject cannot report is unconscious by stipulation. And the theory's own methods have turned against it: in one study a stimulus that participants swore they had not seen produced the full neural 'ignition' signature and still influenced a decision made seconds later, which shows the workspace can light up with nobody home."

Here is where I stand: the line about access not being the same as experience matches what I have heard about Block's distinction, so I am inclined to accept that part, but the "unfalsifiable" charge and the closing example feel as though they are doing more work than the argument can support, and I cannot tell whether the article has refuted the theory or only refuted a careless version of it.

Please do the following:

1. Go through the passage and sort it into what a careful defender of global workspace theory would accept as stated, what is a compression or simplification that a defender would accept with a qualification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — in particular where a technical term is being used in its everyday sense, where a thesis about access is being presented as a thesis about phenomenality (or vice versa), and where the word "modular" is doing something other than it does in the usual modularity-of-mind thesis.

Say which of the article's claims a defender would say is a standard objection presented as a refutation, and what the defender's actual reply is — especially against the charge that the theory is empirically empty and against the claim that report-based methods make it unfalsifiable, given that no-report paradigms exist and are directly aimed at the report confound.

3. Assess the closing example on its own terms. Assume it is as described: full ignition, no report, and a downstream effect on later decision. Does that outcome refute the theory, refute a particular reading of it, or is it roughly what one should expect given that the researchers identified the case by a neural signature rather than by a report? A commenter on the piece drew a distinction the article does not: report-access, availability to whatever generates a verbal or button-press judgment, versus control-access, availability to systems that shape action, learning and long-term planning. Say which of these — if either — is what the access/phenomenal distinction is about, whether the hypothetical case above is best described as control-access without report-access, and whether a global workspace theorist could reasonably identify consciousness with control-access rather than report-access. Do not score the competing theories of consciousness against each other; I want to know what more would have to be true, not who is winning.
