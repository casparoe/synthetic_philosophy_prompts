I'm rewriting my third-year Kant sessions for next term (we're on Groundwork II, the universalizability stretch), and I've relised I don't actually have a spec for what I want out of a reading of the test before I go pick one to teach. So help me build the spec.

Purpose first, so adequacy has something to be relative to: I want a version of the test that a student can apply to a maxim they wrote down themselves, in a few minutes, without already knowing the answer — and that returns Kant's own verdicts on the four stock examples (suicide from self-love, the lying promise, the unused talents, the man who walks past the sufferer). Anything that only works if a philosophically trained person reverse-engineers the description is out.

Here's the part I most want. Below is a battery of maxims I made up because my seminar kept breaking on them. Any adequate reading has to return a verdict on each, and the verdicts have to come from the reading and not from me nudging the description:

1. As a referee, I'll take money to favour one side. (Is the maxim indexed to a role or office? Universalise over everyone, or over office-holders?)
2. I'll lie whenver I'm in a city I'm only passing through. (Class indexed by place, not person.)
3. I'll break a promise when I feel like it. (Class indexed to the agent's occurrent desire.)
4. When the queue is longer than six people, I'll cut in. (The universalised world falls apart only above a certain occupancy level — so the verdict seems to move with population size, or with whether there's a ticketing system, or with whether everyone else is queueing.)
5. I'll get ahead by taking credit for colleagues' work. (Universalised, the end — credit — becomes unachievable, but not because the maxim is incoherent.)
6. As the only person who knows where the archive key is, I'll deny anyone else access. (Universalisation over a class of one: vacuous permission, or no verdict at all?)
7. I'll let people sort out their own problems. (An omission, not an act; and nature plainly subsists under it.)
8. I'll lie to myself about why I did it. (Self-directed; universalisation ranges over one agent's own future states.)
9. I'll fiddle my taxes, but only if most others already do. (Compliance conditional on others' non-compliance.)
10. I'll help people when it suits me and not otherwise. (A permissive maxim: not "do," not "don't," but "either.")

For each requirement you state, give me the case from that battery (or one of your own) that motivates it, and tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one family of readings wants. Things I think belong somewhere on the list, but you order them and you can throw any of them out: that the reading say what a maxim is and how finely it gets individuated; that it distinguish failing in conception from failing in the will from merely being unwise or unstable; that it say whether the verdict attaches to the maxim, to the action, or to the world the maxim would make; that it handle omissions and maxims of pure indifference; that it explain where the extra premise comes from if the law-of-nature clause is doing the work on case 7; that it tell us whether verdicts are allowed to shift with background empirical facts (case 4); that it be usable first-personally, in the moment, by someone who doesn't know the outcome; and that it not let you gerrymander the verdict by re-describing.

Two things I especially want. First: is there a pair on your list that can't be jointly satisfied? My candidate is that the verdict has to be insensitive to how finely you describe the maxim, and yet you mustn't be able to get any verdict you like by choosing the description — coarse enough to avoid false negatives, fine enough to avoid false positives. If that pair really is unsatisfiable, say so and tell me which half to keep and what to put in place of the other. Second: which single requirement excludes the most readings?

Then, briefly — a few lines, not an essay — score the four families I keep circling: the logical/semantic-contradiction reading; the practical-contradiction reading on which the maxim's own end can't be achieved in the universalised world; the law-of-nature reading that leans on a premise about what ends a rational will (or a natural organism) must have; and the reading on which the test is about whether you could will your maxim as a law for a community of rational agents. No names needed, no bibliography, and please don't just walk me through the four stock examples again — I've taught those to death. It's the spec I'm after.
