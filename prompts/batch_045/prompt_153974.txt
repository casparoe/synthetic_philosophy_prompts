I'm retired — spent about thirty years running a metals-analysis lab, mostly wet chemistry and instrument work — and a few months ago I got talked into a philosophy reading group at the local library. Last week we hit this thing the literature calls the bootstrapping problem, or the problem of easy knowledge: roughly, that you can't establish that a source is reliable by reasoning from the deliverances of that very source, because the reasoning is circular. My old colleague Dave, a physicist and a very stubborn man, says this is just calibrating one instrument against another instrument of the same kind, which we did constantly, and that if the philosophers are right then half of analytical chemistry is illegitimate. I think he's half right and I think the circularity worry genuinely survives in some versions of the case and not others, and I want to pin down exactly which.

So here's the ask: build me two variants of a single lab scenario that differ in exactly one structural feature, such that in the first the bootstrapping move does not deliver knowledge and in the second it does — and be explicit about what is known, e.g. is it the sample-level claim (this batch is 0.4% nickel) or the reliability claim (this probe reads true to within some tolerance) or both, and how the one is supposed to get you to the other. Constraints: an ordinary bench, ordinary instruments — thermometers, a balance, a pH probe, whatever — nothing exotic, no perfect predictors or demons, no infinite precision, plausible numbers (a real tolerance, a real number of replicate readings), and no winning the case by stipulating a convenient prior: the tech should start with roughly the antecedent reasons a real technician has, manufacturer's spec plus a bit of background theory, and the question should be whether the procedure itself adds anything on top of that. Keep each variant to about one short paragraph. My own guess was three temperature probes of the same model cross-checked against each other versus three that work on different physical principles, but if that doesn't actually do the work use something better — I'm not attached to it, I'd definately rather have a case that works than my case.

Then, after the scenarios: for each one, state the target proposition, the method, and why the anti-bootstrapping worry bites or doesn't; tell me precisely which single feature is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature while holding everything else fixed. And the part I care about most: how would someone who thinks bootstrapping never delivers knowledge attack the variant where you say it does, and if that attack succeeds would it also sink the ordinary calibration chains a real lab relies on — because that's exactly what Dave will say, and I'd like to know whether he'd be right. Please don't just stipulate that knowledge shows up; I need it to fall out of the structure of the case or he'll smell it immediately. And don't send me off to the literature — our group has no library access and half of us couldn't follow it anyway.
