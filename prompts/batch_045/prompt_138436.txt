Not sure this is quite the right corner of philosophy to ask in, but here goes. I'm finishing a PhD in systems neuroscience (mouse visual cortex, mostly chronic recordings, a bit of modelling on the side), and I keep running into a kind of case where I have no idea what to say about function — and I'd like to know whether the thing I keep noticing already has a name, or whether I'm carving up something nobody's bothered to separate out.

Three instances, then what I think they share. (1) You immunize a mouse with some synthetic molecule that has never existed anywhere in the history of the planet; a few weeks later the animal is full of cells churning out antibodies that bind it, because the cells whose receptors happened to bind it got told to divide and mutate some more and the ones that didn't got nothing. Everyone in the lab says, without any hesitation at all, that the function of those antibodies is to bind that molecule, and if a mutation wrecked the binding we'd say they weren't doing their job, that they were malfunctioning — but there's no selection history behind this at the level of the species or even of the animal's ancestry, the whole thing is weeks old and happened inside one individual. (2) During the critical period in visual cortex, neurons compete in a use-dependent way; the ones that get driven by the statistics of whatever the animal actually grew up looking at keep and strengthen their connections, the underdriven ones lose out. My lab says these neurons are "for" the contour statistics of that particular upbringing. Again: variation, differential retention keyed to what the variants actually do, timescale of weeks, inside one animal. (3) A case I think might be different but I can't say why: a bacterium starts making an enzyme only once the relevant sugar shows up. There's no variation-and-retention story at all, just a switch, and yet "the function of the enzyme is to break down the sugar" seems fine and also seems like it's doing something totally different from (1) and (2). What I think (1) and (2) share is a selection-ish process running on a timescale shorter than a generation, and I've been writing "somatic selected-function" in my own notes, which is either already a term of art I don't know or just a bad label.

So: does this have an established name, and if several established concepts are near it, which fits best and how exactly does it differ from the others? I've read a bit of the debate between the people who say a function is what the thing was selected for and the people who say it's what the thing currently contributes to some capacity of the system it's in (etiological and causal-role, I think they're called?) — but I specifically don't want a bare label, I want the carving. In particular: is this just the causal-role account plus a causal story about how the role got there, or does the within-lifetime history have to be doing real work, e.g. for malfunction attributions? Is it a different thing from co-option/recycling of an old trait for a new use (feathers, the panda's wrist bone), from byproducts, from vestigial leftovers, and from traits that are still around because selection used to like them but doesn't anymore? And one case I want a direct verdict on: if within-lifetime variation-and-retention is enough to ground functions, then a tumour is also a within-lifetime selection process with heritable variation in a cell lineage, so do the traits of a tumour cell get "functions" — or is that a reductio that shows my whole category is a mistake? Please actually commit rather than laying out the options: tell me what you think the right answer is and why, say whether (1) and (2) are even the same phenomenon, and then tell me which part of your answer you're least confident about and what would change your mind, since I'm presenting this at journal club on Thursday and I'd much rather have a definite claim I can argue with than a balanced overview.
