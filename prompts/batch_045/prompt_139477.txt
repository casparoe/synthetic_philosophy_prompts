I convene a monthly seminar in political epistemology, and over the last two sessions we have worked through the standoff between generalists, who hold that conspiracy theories as a class carry an epistemic taint that makes believing them prima facie irrational or at least epistemically imprudent, and particularists, who hold that the class is picked out only by its content — an event explained by the covert action of a small coordinating group — so that nothing follows about epistemic status until you look at the evidence for the individual case. Both times we finished exactly where we started. Nobody conceded a sentence, and I don't think the problem is that anyone was uncharitable.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on the merits, and I would like it to engage three things I have tried — without success — to get the seminar to use.

First, a disambiguation of targets. It seems to me that three different questions are being run together: whether the account is true; whether the belief is rational for this believer given her evidence; and whether the practice of amateur inquiry that produces such beliefs, in an evidential situation where the primary sources are sealed and the inquirer cannot inspect them, is epistemically defective regardless of whether any given output is true. I suspect generalists are often answering the third and particularists the second, and that both can be right at once.

Second, a symmetry constraint. Every structural marker offered as a mark of a bad conspiracy theory — dependence on secrecy, the number of people who would have to keep quiet, reliance on defectors and leaked documents, resistance to refutation via auxiliaries, the way the account expands to absorb each attempted rebuttal — is also a feature of many true official accounts, including several twentieth-century covert state programs now documented but dismissed as paranoid when first alleged. So the generalist's real claim has to be about some *difference* in how those features appear, not their bare presence. But when I press for the difference, what comes back is a claim about how much discretion a credentialed institution is owed, which is political philosophy, not epistemology.

Third, a structural point I have not seen handled. The scale objection — that a conspiracy of that size would have leaked — only bites against conspiracies whose participants all know the whole plan. Compartmentalization answers it, but at a cost in the complexity of what can be executed, so for any operation of a given complexity there is presumably a minimum number of co-knowers and a corresponding leakage risk. That makes the generalist's case look like an empirical claim about a feasibility frontier rather than a conceptual point about a genre of belief. Worse, compartmentalization has a distinctive evidential signature: partial, mutually inconsistent leaks that no one participant could assemble into the whole. Conspiracy theorists treat precisely that signature as their strongest evidence; one side counts it as confirmation, the other as the predictable output of confabulation.

So my hypothesis is that this is a displaced disagreement — about institutional authority and about which epistemic position the evaluation is conducted from, the armchair inquirer's or an idealized inquirer's with institutional access — wearing the clothes of a disagreement about a class of propositions, with a verbal component about how the class is delimited layered on top. Tell me whether that is right, or whether it is really a substantive empirical dispute in disguise, or bedrock intuitions on both sides, or several of these at once. Argue for your diagnosis against the alternatives rather than surveying the literature, and say what each side would have to give up if your diagnosis is correct.

Then tell me what would actually count as progress. In particular, test one candidate: suppose we somehow had a trustworthy adjudicated base rate for the true proportion of conspiracy allegations against governments that turn out to be correct. Say what range of values would vindicate the generalist and what range the particularist, and whether either side could in good faith accept such a figure as settling the matter — and if not, why not.

Please mark your confidence in each substantial claim you make, single out the one you are least sure of, and flag anywhere you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting what its defenders say.
