I'm a senior at my high school, and I edit the philosophy page of our school magazine. Each issue closes with a short piece of humor, and for the next issue I need a single joke on the topic of dispositions — specifically, the trouble with analyzing a disposition such as fragility or irritability as a simple conditional of the form if it were struck, it would break, together with the two standard families of counterexample: finks, where the triggering condition itself destroys or alters the disposition (the bomb wired to a device that disarms it the moment a detector draws near), and masks, where the disposition is fully intact but something prevents it from being manifested (the fragile glass packed in foam).

The joke has to work on two audiences at once. It will be printed in the magazine, where most readers will only know the ordinary meanings of fragile and stubborn, so it should make some amount of sense to them. But it will also be read aloud at a club meeting where our faculty advisor, a philosopher, will be in the room, and for that audience the punchline should turn on the actual structure of the distinction. What I want to avoid is a joke that is funny only because the characters happen to be nerds, or one that treats fink and mask as interchangeable synonyms for a thing that does not happen.

Two further requests. First, set the joke in a high school and build it around a case that is genuinely difficult to classify rather than a textbook one: my own attempt was a student who never speaks in class, where you cannot tell whether the moment she is called on the desire to speak evaporates, or whether she still wants to speak and something about the situation blocks the speaking — but please replace this with a better scenario if you have one. Relatedly, I have been toying with a third kind of case that I have been calling a double case, in which the trigger both removes the disposition and independently blocks its manifestation, so that there may be no fact of the matter about which is doing the work; I would like the joke to still be funny, or funny in a different way, when read as a double case, ideally making people want to argue afterward about whether the difference matters.

Second, it must be printable in a school publication with a student's name attached to it: no profanity, nothing grim or crude, nothing that would embarrass the magazine.

Please write the joke in American English, since the magazine follows American spelling and usage, and keep your entire reply under roughly 200 words. Give the setup and punchline first, and if you add any explanation of why the case is a fink, a mask, a double case, or none of these, put it after the punchline so the joke itself could be printed on its own.
