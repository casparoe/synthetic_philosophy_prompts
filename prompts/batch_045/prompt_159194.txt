I work on enforcement infrastructure at a mid-size social platform — my actual day-to-day is severity ladders, action rates and precision/recall tradeoffs, appeals queues, repeat-offender escalation, reach reduction vs. hard removal, and endless arguments about how much of a decision to expose to the person it was made about. I had one undergrad philosophy course and I basically can't read the literature on cancellation, deplatforming, and social sanction as punishment without wanting to throw it across the room, mostly because it seems to run on distinctions my job has no words for. A friend of mine who organizes workers keeps insisting that half the disagreement is just people talking past each other about who a sanction is really *for*, and I can't tell whether that's deep or obvious.

So here's the exercise. Take the main positions in that debate and restate them, one at a time, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. I want at least these four: (1) social sanction really is punishment, and so should be judged by the standards we apply to punishment — proportionality, who has standing to impose it, whether there is anything like a sentence that ends; (2) it isn't punishment at all, just lots of people exercising their own rights of association and expression, so there's no case anyone is bringing and no verdict anyone is issuing; (3) the expressivist-ish view that what's going on is a community publicly re-marking a norm as still in force, with the target somewhat incidental to that; (4) the forward-looking version where it's about protecting audiences and deterring future speech, so the interesting worries are chilling effects and error rates rather than fairness to the person sanctioned.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want: for each position, what translated cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit my vocabulary, and what has no expression in it at all. Some of my own guesses, which I want you to check or push back on: my framework has no slot for the *address* of a sanction — whether it's aimed at the person being sanctioned or at everyone watching — even though that distinction seems to be carrying a lot of weight in (3); it represents coordinated crowd action only as a pathology to be rate-limited (brigading, pile-ons) and never as a legitimate enforcement mechanism; and it quietly assumes there is always an actor who can reverse an action, whereas the sanctions people actually get upset about — a lost job, a thing that stays attached to your name forever — seem to have no expiry field and no owner. Also, proportionality for me means matching a severity tier to a category of violating content, and I suspect the philosophers mean matching a burden to a person's desert, which doesn't feel like the same shape of claim at all.

For each bit of residue, tell me what diagnosis you'd give: is my framework blind here, or was the original claim less determinate than it sounded? I especially want your view on that last one. When someone says a particular pile-on was disproportionate, is there anything in that judgment that could in principle be checked, or is it just a way of registering that you think the outcome sucked?
