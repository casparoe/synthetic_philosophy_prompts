I convene a graduate workshop on the foundations of cooperative bargaining, and I am putting together the session in which we ask which rule a real-world arbitrator — a contract arbitrator, a judge approving a class settlement, a mediator with power to impose terms — should use to divide a surplus: the Nash rule or the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule. Every example I can find fails for my purposes. Either the feasible set is symmetric and the two rules pick the same point, so nothing is at stake, or the example is a bare list of payoff pairs with no story attached, and then there is no way to tell whether the shape of the set is doing the work or whether the author simply stipulated the verdicts. So I want a case built to order, and let me be explicit about what would count as cheating: the two rules have to be applied to a situation I can picture, and their divergence has to fall out of that situation rather than being written into the utility numbers by hand. My audience can follow arithmetic and does not need the literature, so please do not pause to attribute or summarize anything.

Define the two rules as follows, so we are working from the same ones. There is a feasible set of achievable payoff pairs (convex, since the parties may make an agreement contingent on some public randomising device a real contract could use), a disagreement point d giving each party what they get if talks collapse, and an ideal point u whose coordinates are the most each party could get while the other still receives at least their disagreement payoff. Nash picks the feasible point maximising the product of gains, (x₁ − d₁)(x₂ − d₂). Kalai–Smorodinsky picks the furthest feasible point on the straight segment running from d to u — equivalently, the point at which both parties receive the same share of their own ideal gain. I will compare the two rules by asking how large a share of its own ideal gain each rule gives each party, since that comparison survives any rescaling of either party's utilities.

The case I want:

- Two parties in a concrete institutional setting of your choosing — a licensing or supply agreement, a municipal labour dispute, a university and its spinout over intellectual property, a hospital department's budget, anything an arbitrator could actually face — with a menu of four or five feasible agreements and a disagreement point that is itself a real outcome rather than a modelling convenience. The payoff numbers must be derived from facts in the story: each party's exposure to risk or liquidity constraints, a statutory or contractual cap, an indivisible asset, a reputational or autonomy cost, whatever you use. State each party's attitude to risk over the relevant range, say why the story supports it, and show how the coordinates follow. One paragraph of prose plus a small table of options; no science fiction, no demons, no perfect predictors.
- No unrestricted cash side payments. At least one thing at stake must not be reducible to a money transfer at a common exchange rate, so that the frontier stays kinked or curved rather than collapsing into a straight line. Say which feature of your story blocks fungibility.
- The two rules must disagree, and in a specified direction. One party must have on the menu an extreme option — a structure that would be a bonanza for them and near-worthless for the other side, which sets that party's ideal point and which no one expects to be chosen. That party must be the one who does better, as a share of their own ideal gain, under Kalai–Smorodinsky than under Nash. The two shares that Nash awards must differ by at least fifteen percentage points, and Kalai–Smorodinsky's common share must sit at least ten percentage points below the larger of them.
- Menu-shrinking test. The extreme option from the previous bullet must be one that neither rule selects, and deleting it from the menu must leave the Nash outcome completely untouched while moving the Kalai–Smorodinsky outcome far enough to reverse the comparison: whichever party came out relatively better under Nash should, after the deletion, come out relatively better under Kalai–Smorodinsky instead.
- Menu-expanding test. There must be a further agreement the parties could put on the table which leaves both coordinates of the ideal point unchanged, and whose availability makes one party strictly worse off under Nash while leaving both parties weakly better off under Kalai–Smorodinsky. That is the asymmetry I most want exhibited inside a story: with the ideal point held fixed, Kalai–Smorodinsky is monotone in the feasible set and Nash is not.

What I want back from you:

- The scenario, the table, and the derivation of the numbers, followed by the working for both rules: each party's ideal gain, the point each rule selects, and the share of ideal gain each party receives under each rule.
- Both tests, with the arithmetic shown, including an explicit check that the Nash point is still feasible after the deletion (so that independence of irrelevant alternatives genuinely applies) and that the added option really does leave the ideal point where it was.
- A note on which structural feature of the case is doing the work. My own hypothesis is that two things are load-bearing: first, the slope at which the frontier crosses the diagonal in coordinates where each axis measures a party's gain as a fraction of their ideal gain — steeper than a forty-five degree line at that crossing and the Nash product-maximiser slides toward the party on the horizontal axis, shallower and it slides the other way; second, the fact that Kalai–Smorodinsky's ideal point is hostage to the most fanciful option on the menu, which is what the deletion test exploits. Tell me whether that is right, and if it is not, what is actually doing the work. I also take it that Kalai–Smorodinsky's common share must fall between the two shares Nash awards, since both points lie on the same downward-sloping frontier; flag it if your numbers say otherwise.
- What the case degenerates into if the non-fungibility constraint is lifted, and why that matters for how much weight my workshop should put on the whole disagreement.
- The strongest reply available to whichever rule comes out of this looking worse. I suspect neither rule emerges clean, and I want the steelman for each, including the procedural point that an arbitrator's menu is not handed down by nature: either party can quietly keep an option off it. Given your case, what does each rule predict about strategic withholding of options, and does that count for or against adopting the rule?

If two of these requirements turn out not to be jointly satisfiable in a case anyone would recognise, tell me which pair conflicts and why, and give me the nearest miss you can construct, rather than quietly dropping one.
