hey — need a favor. I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, and Thursday I'm taping an interview with a moral philosopher who's become the go-to skeptic about consequentialist reasoning under uncertainty. I want to rehearse first, so please play her. We'll say her name is Marisol.

Here's the position you're holding:

- Consequentialism is true as a theory of what makes acts right. She's not a deontologist.
- But for almost any big real-world choice, the expected value isn't merely unknown, it's *indeterminate*: our evidence doesn't pin down a probability distribution over the far-future consequences tightly enough for an expectation to exist at all. So consequentialism is decision-theoretically inert for those choices — not false, unusable.
- Her fallback: when the long-run expectation is indeterminate, you don't get to retreat to near-term expected value either, because the near-term and long-run effects come as one package and picking the near-term slice is just picking the part you happen to be able to see. Instead minimize what she calls variance exposure — take the option with the narrowest plausible spread of outcomes, and among roughly equal spreads, go with role duties and ordinary moral rules.
- And she has a second thing she likes to spring on people, which she calls "the tail eats the cluelessness": long-run outcome distributions are fat-tailed, so the cluelesser you are, the more a handful of wild extremes dominate whatever you manage to compute. She thinks that's a reductio of the longtermist reply that says you should just do expected-value reasoning with fat tails.

Rules for you: stay in character til I say stop. Talk like a person, two short paragraphs at a time, no citations, no paper titles, no dropping names — I'll do the fact-checking after. If I ask something where the honest answer is nobody actually knows, just say that instead of guessing. Concede small stuff: she'll admit the "indeterminate expectation" line sounds like a dodge if she says it carelessly, and she'll admit cluelessness doesn't get going for acts with short, well-understood casual chains. Don't give up the main claim, and call me out when I'm being sloppy.

My opening move, which is also basically my first question:

"Doctor, you say the expectation doesn't exist, so we should pick the narrowest spread. But I put this to four people in the effective altruism world and got the same answer four times — that the long-run distribution is fat-tailed, so the tails swamp everything, and ignoring them isn't humility, it's just refusing to do arithmetic. Which sounds to me like your cluelessness argument hands them exactly what they want. So concretely: a foundation has $10M. Option A buys a big batch of cataract surgeries, tens of thousands of them, cost per surgery nailed down by trials, near-term benefit about as well measured as anything in this field gets. Option B endows a small mathematics library in a mid-sized city: near-term benefit is a rounding error, and the long-run effect runs somewhere between nothing at all and the person who reads there proving something that rearranges the century, with nobody able to put a sign on it. Walk me through why your view isn't just 'do the surgeries and stop feeling bad' — and if it is that, tell me why that isn't status quo bias with footnotes."

Go — give me her answer to that, and then we'll go back and forth.
