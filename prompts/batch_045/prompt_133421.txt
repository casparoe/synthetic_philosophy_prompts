I chair the model-governance board of a county workforce agency. We allocate roughly 900 subsidised job-training slots a year from about 2,400 eligible applicants, using a vendor's score for the chance that an applicant would still be employed twelve months after training. As the familiar incompatibility results lead one to expect, the score is well calibrated within each group, yet at any single cut-off Black applicants — whose retention rate is 44%, against 62% for white applicants — are passed over when they would have succeeded far less often than white applicants are, and are selected when they would not have succeeded far more often. Our complication is that the county council's appropriation has ranged from 700 to 1,100 slots over six years; each re-audit costs us months and a five-figure fee, so we want a guarantee that holds across cut-offs rather than at one.

Three proposals are on the table. (i) Keep the score and a single group-blind cut, audited at this year's threshold only. (ii) Keep the score, set group-specific cuts that equalise the rate at which qualified applicants are passed over, and re-audit whenever the appropriation moves. (iii) Buy the vendor's distribution-matched variant: a monotone per-group rescaling under which the score distributions conditional on the outcome coincide, so error rates match at every cut-off and no re-audit is needed — but the number shown to applicants and caseworkers stops being a probability, which our ordinance on published scores appears to require. Both (ii) and (iii) build group membership into the allocation rule, which sits awkwardly with the consent decree barring us from allocating by race. A community coalition objects further that the retention outcome is itself depressed by local hiring discrimination, so parity with respect to it entrenches that injury.

Draft the board's decision memo (600–900 words) recommending one of the three, or a specified hybrid, with conditions and a timeline; give the strongest case for the runner-up and explain why it loses; and say what the board should do about the coalition's objection.
