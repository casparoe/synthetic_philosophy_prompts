I play double bass in a community orchestra and I somehow ended up writing the program notes, which nobody else wants to do. For the next concert I'd like to close the note with a poem instead of the usual paragraph. I've been reading Schopenhauer on music on my own — an old paperback translation from a library sale, almost no footnotes — and its gotten under my skin.

The bit I keep chewing on: he apparently says every other art copies the Ideas, the patterns things fall into out in the world, but music skips that step and copies the will directly — the blind striving underneath everything, no reason, no final rest. So music isn't depicting sadness; it's sadness in general, or really the shape of the wanting that sadness is a case of. It doesn't mean anything at all, and that's supposed to be why it lands on you harder than anything that does mean something. And he maps the low voices to dumb matter and the tune up top to us, which I take personally, though he admits that part is only an analogy and not a proof.

I want a sestina: six six-line stanzas where the same six end words cycle round in that rotating order, then the three-line envoi that gathers all six back up. My suggested end words are will, want, bass, copy, resolve, word — swap one or two if you have better ones and tell me why in a line. The reason I want a sestina specifically is that the same six words keep coming back and you can never quite guess which one is going to arrive where, and that feels like the right shape for what he's claiming: one single wanting showing up everywhere, in rocks and in us, never as a picture of anything.

Two things I don't want. First, no "music is a universal language, it expresses what words cannot." That's the greeting card version and its not his claim — his claim is about what there is, not about communication, and if the poem only says the greeting card thing I can't use it. Second, don't let the ending be satisfied. On his picture every satisfaction is instantly replaced by a new want, so nothing ever finally resolves; whatever the last stanza does, I don't want it to land like a resolved cadence. End on the wanting rather than the rest. If you can make "resolve" do double duty — literally a musical resolution and also not one — that would be ideal.

And there's one thing I'd like you to actually have a go at, because nothing I've read deals with it. If music is supposed to skip the world of appearance — it isn't a picture of anything in space, it doesn't represent separate individuals or causal goings on — then why is it so obviously made of structure? Keys, voice leading, sonata form, a bass line that has to go exactly where it has to go. Music looks like the most rule bound, most architectural thing humans make, which makes it look a lot like the world of appearance it was meant to bypass. I don't want the poem to smooth that over or explain it away. Let the fact that a sestina is a sestina be the point: the form is a straitjacket, and the straitjacket is exactly where whatever moves you comes from.

Length of a normal sestina is fine. It's going in a program note so it has to work as a poem for people who've never heard of him, but I'm also going to show it to someone who has actually read him, so it shouldn't get anything flat out wrong. After the poem, give me a sentence or two on what you were doing in the final line, since the conductor will ask me what it means.
