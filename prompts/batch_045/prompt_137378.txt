I'm writing a long-form piece for a general readership about when philosophical disagreements turn out to be disagreements about language. The background article I've been working from states the metaontological version of that argument roughly as follows:

"Take the old dispute about whether the statue and the lump of clay are one thing or two. Both sides agree about everything you could check: what weighs five pounds, what survives being squashed, what a museum would pay for it. Each can restate the other's position without losing any information. So they aren't disagreeing about the world; they are disagreeing about how to use the quantifier — about whether 'there is' counts the statue and the clay separately. And since nothing we could observe would settle that, and each side can translate the other perfectly, neither side is mistaken: each speaks truly in its own language, and there is no further fact of the matter about how many things there really are. That is what ontological disputes always turn out to be — disagreements about which of several equally good languages to speak, never about how things are. Once you see this, the questions do not vanish, but they stop being philosophical: they become questions about which way of talking is handier, and a discipline organized around them has nothing distinctive left to do. The argument also generalizes. Disputes about whether there are numbers, or moral facts, or reasons for action, are verbal in exactly the same sense, which is why there are no irreducible normative facts either."

There is also a case I want to use as my central example, and I'd like to know whether the corrected version of the view can handle it. Two historians disagree about whether the wave of uprisings that crossed Europe in 1848 was one revolution or a dozen unrelated ones. They agree on every dated event, every proclamation, every troop movement; each can restate the other's claim without loss. But the disagreement cannot simply be dissolved, because a school curriculum — or a heritage register, or a memorial inscription — has to record it as one item or as several. A single institutional practice has to settle on a single counting rule, and there is no way to encode both. My inclination is to say that this shows a dispute can be verbal about meaning while still having a genuine answer of a different kind: not which framework is true, but which framework the practice should adopt, given that it can adopt only one.
