I hold a position about consciousness that I will call introspective error theory — the view usually labelled illusionism. Its three commitments are these. First: there are no phenomenal properties. No state of any system has qualitative character of the kind we take our own experiences to have, and there is nothing it is like to undergo anything. Second: we nevertheless have introspective states that represent our own mental states as having such a character, and those representations are systematically mistaken — not merely incomplete or schematic, but false. Third: the mistake is not a bare absence. Everything that seems to need explaining — why we sincerely and confidently report that experience has qualitative character, why those reports carry a peculiar first-person authority, why no amount of functional or neuroscientific detail seems to close the explanatory gap, why we take consciousness to be what makes a creature's fate matter — is to be explained by appeal to the misrepresenting introspective mechanisms together with the functional facts, with no residue.

Two refinements matter to me. The first is a coverage requirement: I do not count the view as adequate unless it can explain, in non-phenomenal terms, both why introspective states present their objects as luminous — such that being in the state puts one in a position to know one is in it — and why the sense of an explanatory gap survives complete functional description rather than dissolving on reflection. If the view can only insist that the gap intuition is false, it has relocated the mystery rather than dissolved it.

The second is a prediction I take the view to be committed to, and its rivals not to be: there is nothing the misrepresentation was tracking. A subject who came to fully accept introspective error theory about her own states — not as an intellectual exercise, but in whatever way counts as fully taking it in — would lose no information about herself, would retain every reason for self-directed concern she had before, and would have no epistemic or practical loss to register. The illusion, on this view, was never concealing anything from her.

Now: what would count against this?

I want a map of the position's exposure, not a catalogue of objections. Tell me what would have to be true for introspective error theory to be false, ranked from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward. Distinguish throughout between defeaters a committed illusionist would herself concede as counting against the view and defeaters she would dismiss as presupposing the very datum in dispute. Say which of these are live rather than merely logically possible, and do not stop at the well-rehearsed material. In particular, I would like you to work through the following.

One: the misrepresentation worry. For a representation to misrepresent, must there be a way things could have been on which it represents correctly? If introspective content has no possible veridical instance, is the illusionist helping herself to a notion of content she cannot make out? Is there an innocent reading available — content fixed by a cluster of roles that is satisfiable in principle though not in fact — and what does that reading cost?

Two: the conceptual worry. A natural realist story is that our introspective concepts are recognitional concepts whose content is partly fixed by the states they pick out. If that is right, can the illusionist even formulate her thesis — state what the misrepresentation represents — without reintroducing phenomenality? If instead introspective concepts are theory-laden and mostly theoretical, the view looks much more available. So the fate of the view may turn on the semantics of introspective concepts. Is that a genuine exposure point, what would settle it, and is the semantic question one the view is entitled to an opinion about?

Three: the collapse worry. If the positive account of the introspective mechanism must posit states that play the phenomenal role — states whose identity conditions are given by how they appear to the subject, or which are self-representing in the relevant way — does introspective error theory collapse into a representationalist or self-representational realism, i.e. into a rival, rather than refuting it? Is that outcome decisive or merely verbal?

Four: two cases, and whether they yield differential predictions. Case A: a creature with no introspective system at all, whose states are functionally like ours but which represents nothing about them. Case B: a pair of creatures functionally identical except that the first's introspective system represents its states as having qualitative character while the second's represents the very same states as purely relational and functional, with no downstream difference in discrimination or control. Does the view predict anything here that a realist does not predict, and would either case, if realised and interrogated, be evidence? Be careful: both views can tell stories about both cases, so I want you to say what the observation would have to be, and from whose standpoint, for it to discriminate.

Five: the evidence we arguably already have. Introspection is known to be unreliable in familiar ways: people confabulate the grounds of their own judgments, miss large changes in their own experience, and shift their reports with framing. Does any of this raise the probability of introspective error theory, or is it equally predicted by realism plus a modest claim about introspective fallibility? Say what already-available evidence does and does not do.

Six: the methodological trap, which I take to be the deepest issue. The view's central claim is that the evidence which seems most decisive — how things seem — is systematically misleading, so the illusionist can always say of any apparent counterevidence that it is what the illusion predicts. Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If it has, is that a defect here, or just the shape of a thesis with this content? What would be a fair evidentiary standard for such a thesis? Analogous views — about systematic cognitive bias, or about the unreliability of memory — discredit their own evidence base and are nonetheless respectable; by what standard, and does introspective error theory meet it?

Finally, what would move me personally, and I would like you to assess whether my demand is coherent: I would be most moved by a case in which a subject's sincere, conceptually competent first-person report is best explained by her undergoing something she could not misrepresent — a report not mediated by the introspective system at all. If that demand cannot be met even in principle, say so, and say what I am really asking for and whether some weaker demand does the same work.

Please write this as a sustained essay in prose, reaching a verdict on how exposed the view is and how, rather than ending in equipoise. I have read the standard dialectic, so I do not want a literature review, a bibliography, or citations; argue from the structure of the position, from the cases, and from what the view predicts. If your honest assessment is that introspective error theory is very well insulated, say that plainly, and say whether insulation is a problem for a view of this kind.
