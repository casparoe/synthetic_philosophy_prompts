I'm trying to get my position on commitment races into a form I'd be willing to defend in a thread, and I keep losing track of whether the pieces actually fit together. By a commitment race I mean the case where two or more parties are each better off being the one who locks in first, so they race to make an irreversible, publicly observable lock-in, with an outcome worse for everyone than a world in which nobody could lock in. The instances I actually care about have two further features: the moves are staggered rather than simultaneous, and the lock-in is genuinely costly to whoever makes it — you have to actually burn the boats, not merely announce that you have — so the winner's gain is usually smaller than the loser's loss.

Where I've landed, in six pieces:

(1) Commitment races are races to the bottom: given that the other side will lock in, locking in is rational for me, and the side that locks in first does get its preferred outcome — but ex ante, before anyone has moved, every party including the eventual winner is worse off than in a world where lock-ins weren't available.

(2) So when I'm actually in one, I should lock in early and hard. Staying flexible while my counterparty locks in just donates the surplus to them.

(3) I also think the only real fix is institutional: contract rules, cooling-off periods, norms against public irreversibility, design that makes lock-ins hard to enter and cheap to exit. And I'd want to be able to advocate these out loud, not just quietly benefit from their absence.

(4) Separately, I'm a heavy user of precommitment devices in my own life — pledges, blockers, asking friends not to let me renegotiate — and I think someone who never binds themselves is leaving value on the table.

(5) Methodologically, I want to assess moves at the level of policies rather than one-off acts, and I'm suspicious of any story on which whether a move is rational turns on who happened to move first, or on when someone happened to think about the problem.

(6) My test for which lock-ins are legitimate is whether the lock-in leaves everyone affected better off than the no-lock-in baseline; a lock-in that's a pure transfer and destroys surplus is objectionable.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, with what would have to give in each case and what giving it up costs me. If there's a single revision that cleans up more than one of them, I'd like to know which.
