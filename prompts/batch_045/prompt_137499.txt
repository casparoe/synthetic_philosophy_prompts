I cover artificial intelligence and its surrounding arguments for a newspaper, and I am writing a longer piece on whether we can meaningfully supervise systems that eventually know more than we do. Before I file it, I would like an audit of my own working views on the debate approach to oversight, because I suspect they do not all fit together. Roughly where I have landed:

1. Of the oversight proposals on the table, the debate setup — two systems argue opposing sides, a weaker judge picks the winner — is the most promising, and what makes it promising is the adversarial structure itself, the cross-examination dynamic, not any particular training procedure bolted onto it.

2. The capability gap between debaters and judge is a resource rather than a defect: the trick is that the weaker party presides over a process that forces the stronger party to expose the steps of its own reasoning.

3. The setup is most reliable where the disagreement can be made to bottom out in something cheap to check — runnable code, arithmetic, a verifiable fact — and it degrades steadily as you move toward forecasting, policy, and ethics.

4. My principal worry is the case in which the stronger debater wins with a persuasive but false case, since the gap celebrated in (2) is exactly what makes that possible; but I file this as a failure mode to be engineered around rather than as a reason to doubt (1).

5. At deployment the judge can be another AI system, provided that judge was itself trained on debates that humans adjudicated.

6. We can test the whole thing now: run debates between current models in front of human judges and see whether the judged answer beats the judge's own solo answer.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and where do I have to give? Rank what you find by severity and say, for each item, what the least costly revision would be. Two points I especially want addressed. First, is (3) self-undermining: if debate only works where someone can check the result, have I quietly abandoned the problem it was proposed to solve, or is there a weaker reading of checkable that rescues it? Second, I have started using a term of my own, the inversion point — the capability level past which extra debater strength helps the dishonest side more than the honest one. Does anything in my set commit me to believing there is such a point, and if so, does it tell me anything about where it lies?
