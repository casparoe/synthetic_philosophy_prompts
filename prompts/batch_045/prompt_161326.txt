You are a philosopher of action who works on both weakness of will and intertemporal choice, and who is used to being asked for criteria rather than opinions. Think the problem through carefully: before you write, brainstorm the full candidate list of requirements and cut the ones that are only slogans of one school.

I write fiction, and a scene I cannot make work turns on an accusation. One character tells another that her unfinished novel is not something she merely has not written but something she is failing to write — that this is weakness, not a change of priorities, not fear, not a compulsion she cannot resist. I need to know what a theory of weakness of will would have to get right for that accusation to be more than rhetoric. So write me the specification: the requirements an account must meet to be good enough to adjudicate a case like mine.

Fix the purpose as that one: a theory able to separate weakness from compulsion or addiction, from self-deception about what one really wants, from an ordinary reversal of preference over time, and from simply never getting around to it — never collapsing them into whatever the agent later regrets. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say which are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps.

The case I actually need handled: my character's failure has no akratic moment. Each evening she judges, correctly given how much weight the near term carries for her then, that tomorrow is the better day to write; she never abandons the plan, never decides against it, and never acts while judging something else better — yet the book never gets written. Say whether a theory that can only return verdicts on momentary actions is thereby inadequate, or whether that demand is my novelist's prejudice. Also tell me what your list implies about an agent who arranges to be prevented from doing the thing and feels relief when she is.

Take as constraints, unless you argue otherwise: no requirement that an occurrent better judgment be present at the moment of action; consistency with the fact that present-biased agents reverse preferences without new information, sometimes foresee the reversal and sometimes do not, and sometimes pay to bind themselves. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want. Then, in a line or two each: how the families of accounts do — ones that locate the failure in judgment at the moment of action, ones that locate it in the pull of an attraction rather than in any judgment, ones that locate it in the non-execution of a plan or resolution already formed — and which single requirement excludes the most of them.

Close by saying what would change your mind about the list: which consideration would lead you to drop or add a requirement, where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and which of the requirements my scene could not settle even if the characters argued it out for a hundred pages.
