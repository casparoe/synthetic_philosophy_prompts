I'm writing a short essay for a module on medieval philosophy — we did Avicenna a couple of weeks ago and it's due in a fortnight — and I keep going round in circles on how to get past just describing the flying man thought experiment. The question we were set is basically "what does the flying man argument actually establish?", and I want the essay to actually land somewhere rather than end with "more work is needed". Could you write me an essay of about 1200 words that does that?

The two readings I want played off against each other are: a strong one, on which the case is meant to show something like substance dualism (or at minimum that the self/soul isn't the body and isn't dependent on it), and a deflationary one, on which all it really shows is that self-awareness is primitive, unmediated, or not acquired via the senses. Steelman both. If the strong reading depends on the doctrine of knowledge by presence, or on some principle about whatever is known immediately being known in its essence, say what work that premise is doing and whether it's doing more than the thought experiment itself earns.

There are three objections I've been chewing on that I'd like you to take seriously rather than gloss over (and feel free to tell me bluntly if one of them is just confused):

First, Avicenna's man gets created all at once, already adult and rational, and then floats in the void with no sensation. What if instead he's created as a newborn and grows up there with no sensory contact ever? If self-awareness would just never get going in that version, then the argument looks like it's helping itself to a fully-formed rational soul at the outset, and its not obvious any more that the scenario isolates self-awareness from the body rather than assuming the interesting part. If you think the objection fails, explain why.

Second — this is the one my supervisor kept poking at — imagine two flying men created at the same instant, qualitatively identical in every respect, no bodies to locate themselves by, no distinguishing features at all. Each seems to end up judging that he exists. But what makes it the case that each one is aware of that very self rather than merely of a self, or of both? If the argument needs self-awareness to be irreducibly de re, is a completely featureless void scenario even capable of supplying that, or is it quietly borrowing an anchor from somewhere it hasn't declared?

Third, a smaller worry: could the flying man judge that he exists if he had no concept of existence? If not, maybe what the case really establishes is just that there can be bare occurrent consciousness that doesn't depend on the senses, and the bit about self-affirmation is extra machinery doing work it hasn't paid for.

Don't spend more than a paragraph on comparisons with Descartes — I've read enough of that already — and please don't give me references, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading; our tutor wants us arguing from the text rather than the secondary literature. Finish with a verdict, even a hedged one: something like "the argument gets you X but not Y, and the gap can/can't be closed by adding such-and-such premise."
