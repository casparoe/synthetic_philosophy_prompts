I sit on a municipal commission considering whether to rename two streets and move a statue out of a public square. Every proposal, whatever its specifics, gets met with the same line: "You can't erase history." It is clearly meant as a conversation-stopper, but I cannot tell what it is supposed to establish, and the different things it might mean pull in very different directions. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making — a causal one about whether removal actually changes what people remember or can find out, an epistemic one about monuments as evidence or as prompts to inquiry, a claim about what an act of removal expresses or asserts, a claim about who is entitled to shape a city's symbolic landscape, and any others worth separating — and for each, say whether it is trivially true, substantial and defensible, or false, and whether its truth would actually support leaving the name or the statue in place.

I would like the menu to be tested against two things. First, a distinction our commission has begun using, between a monument's honoring function and its recording function, together with the compromise some of us have floated and called "archival removal": take the name off the street or the statue out of the square, but reinstall it with the full documentary record of how it got there and why it is being moved now, so that nothing about the past becomes harder to learn afterwards than it was before. For each reading, does archival removal answer the objection, or does the objection survive it untouched? Second, a case that seems to me to make the epistemic reading bite unusually hard: a statue whose subject no one still living can identify and whose inscription has weathered away, so that what would be lost is not information about the person but the fact that this community once chose to honor someone. Does "you can't erase history" do genuine work there, and if so, does that tell us anything about the ordinary cases where it is deployed?
