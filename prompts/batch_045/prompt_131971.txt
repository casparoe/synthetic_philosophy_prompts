I am trying to work out how much of a popular account of money pump arguments I should believe. The account comes from a chapter on rational choice in a general-audience book about decision-making that a friend lent me and that I no longer have; what follows is reconstructed from the notes I took, so the wording is mine, though I have tried to keep it faithful in substance:

Rational choice theory rests on a handful of axioms, and there is a beautifully simple argument that violating any one of them makes you irrational: the money pump. Take transitivity. Suppose you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. I can then stand in front of you with the three items and a cash register. I offer to swap your A for C if you pay me a dollar; then your C for B, for another dollar; then your B for A, for a third. You accept every time, because each trade moves you to something you prefer, and afterwards we are back where we started and you are three dollars poorer. So I do it again, and again. The same construction works for the remaining axioms, including independence. Independence is just the requirement that your ranking of two options should not depend on what else happens to be on the menu, and if your ranking does shift around with the menu, I can walk you in a circle and charge you at every step. The argument is therefore completely general: any departure from expected utility maximization leaves you exploitable, and an agent who can be reliably drained of money by someone offering her trades she herself prefers is not fully rational. There is an obvious reply — you can see what I am doing, so you can simply decline the first trade, or bind yourself in advance never to trade. But that reply fails on both horns. Binding yourself is only available if you can find a commitment device, and usually you cannot. And simply declining means turning down a trade which, taken on its own, makes you better off, which is just a local version of the same irrationality. The money pump argument therefore stands, and with it the claim that rationality is expected utility maximization.
