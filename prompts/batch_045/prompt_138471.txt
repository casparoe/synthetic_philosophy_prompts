Writing a novel and I've kind of painted myself into a corner. It's set about thirty years out in a mid-sized city run under something I call the Harm Ordinance: the city may not restrict expression except to prevent harm to others, and the ordinance's own commentary says that offense, shock, outrage and distress are not harm, and that a setback doesn't count as harm if it reaches someone only through the choices of other people who were free to react otherwise. (I reread Mill's On Liberty while drafting, so I know roughly where the clause comes from; my city's version is deliberately stricter about the third-party bit, which is half the point of the book.) There's a five-member Harm Review Board that issues short rulings, and chapter 19 is a long scene where they have to decide a new case and split 3-2. I need to know what the majority opinion actually says, because my POV character is on the board and whether she joins the majority or dissents decides whether she keeps her job, and my writing group keeps telling me my board sounds like it's making it up as it goes along, which... fair.

Here are the rulings I've already written for earlier chapters, so you can see the track record: (a) a gallery piece submerging a crucifix in a tank of the artist's own urine, forty thousand protesters and one broken window — allowed 5-0, "outrage is what art is for and outrage is not harm"; (b) a data collective publishes block-level heat maps of lawful firearm ownership, built from a leaked registry, no names and no addresses, after which several residents report fear and three burglaries happen on mapped blocks — allowed 4-1, "we will not count harms inflicted by those who choose to misuse true information"; (c) anonymous posts giving the real names, photographs and home addresses of three people acquitted of violent offences, captioned "the system let them go", followed by threats and a torched car — blocked 5-0; (d) a podcast naming the water commissioner and saying she "should be made to drink her own tap water until something in her gives", with no address, no call to action and no history of violence by the host — allowed 5-0, "political invective has to be allowed to be ugly"; (e) a fringe theatre company stages a play in which a named, living city councillor takes a bribe onstage, program says fiction, ticketed ninety-seat house, no recordings, and she loses her primary by four hundred votes — allowed 3-2, the dissent saying a foreseeable electoral setback is a harm whatever the program says, the majority saying a paying audience at a play knows the frame and that reputational damage from what everyone knows to be invention isn't the sort of harm the ordinance counts; (f) personalized letters sent to tenants naming their neighbour and falsely calling her a police informant — blocked 5-0; (g) synthetic audio of a real, private person's voice confessing to insurance fraud, emailed to her employer with no label — blocked 4-1.

The new case is called The Sixth of August: a hundred-minute synthetic "reconstruction" of the night a patient died in the care of a named, living nurse, assembled from public trial transcripts and old photographs, showing her injecting the patient. She was acquitted after a mistrial and no court has ever found that she did anything at all. The film opens with a card and every file says "synthetic reconstruction, no assertion of fact," but a nonprofit true-crime outfit releases it free, builds it to be clipped, and the card is usually stripped in shares; inside a month she's fired, her license is suspended pending review, people picket the hospital and she can't get work. She never sought any public attention, and the filmmakers say they're only asking a question the trial never answered. So: what does my board do, and what rule is it really running — something about the means of the speech (falsity, threats, exposing addresses), something about reasonably foreseeable setbacks to an identifiable person even when third parties deliver them, or something else I haven't thought of? Which of my earlier rulings is genuinely the closest analog, (e) or (c) or (f), and why? If they block, are they committed to counting downstream uptake in a way that should also have swallowed (b), (d) or (e), and if they allow, is (e) distinguishable only by the ninety-seat house and the fact that the councillor ran for office — i.e. what happens in my draft if I flip those two details? And finally, if my precedents honestly underdetermine this, tell me which principle my character is adopting with each vote and invent me the one further case that would cleanest separate the rules, since I can always add a chapter 21. Don't need citations or a reading list, I've read plenty — I need the reasoning.
