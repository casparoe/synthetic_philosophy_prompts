I've been chewing on money pump arguments for months and I think I've definately read myself into a hole, so what I want is a diagnosis of why this debate doesn't move, not another run-through of the cases.

Here's the fight as I've been living it. Setup: an agent faces a sequence of choices over time; their preferences don't satisfy some synchronic condition (pick your favourite: transitivity, independence, the sure-thing principle); an exploiter shows up and offers a series of trades, each of which the agent accepts at the moment of choosing, and the agent ends up with what they started with and less money. Side A says this is the argument for why those synchronic conditions are requirements of rationality for a temporally extended agent: the violation is what makes you pumpable. Side B says the pump only runs because of the violation *plus* a pile of extra dispositions — accepting any option that looks better at the moment, never paying to precommit, never re-planning, revisiting decisions for free — *plus* facts about the environment, like that there happens to be someone around who wants your money and can make frictionless offers in the right order. Strip any of that and the pump stalls. So at best you get a conditional prudential claim about a package of preferences-and-dispositions in a certain kind of environment, and a resolute chooser with cyclic preferences does fine, thank you.

The thing that actually stopped me is the move where people concede the environment-dependence and retreat to saying exploitability and incoherence can come apart — you can be incoherent without being exploitable, and lose money without being incoherent. I take that as side A giving up on the money doing the work. But if the money isn't doing the work, what is? The pump was supposed to *derive* a normative requirement from prudential loss, and once the derivation turns out to be hostage to who happens to be standing next to you, the requirement has to be defended on its own and the pump is just a memorable cartoon.

My hypothesis, offered as one: there are two arguments under one name. One is a conditional prudential argument nobody ever disputed. The other is a claim about what norms govern an agent over time, where the pump contributes nothing and the actual disagreement is bedrock — is the unit of rational evaluation a single choice, a strategy for the whole sequence, or the plan as it is revised? — with the pump functioning as a rhetorical proxy so neither side has to say what they're committed to. Plus a hidden empirical question about how exploitable agents with those preferences really are, which nobody can answer because there's no data and probably can't be any.

Three cases I invented to try to test this, all of which both sides in my reading group waved away:

1. The silent pump. The exploitative sequence exists but is never run; no exploiter shows up, and the agent lives a long life with intransitive preferences and dies better off than the coherent agents around them. Side A says still irrational; side B says the pump argument has no purchase, since nothing bad happened. Is there any real content to the counterfactual you would have been drained, or is that just a counterfactual with no normative grip?

2. The reverse pump. Same incoherent preferences, but the sequence of offers that actually arrives happens to favour cycling — the exploiter misprices, or the offers come in a lucky order — and the agent ends up strictly ahead of where any coherent agent would have ended up. If the argument is prudential, this agent is fine; if it is about coherence, the gain is irrelevant. Does the intelligibility of this case show the argument was never really prudential, or does side A have a clean reply — that what's criticised is the disposition, and dispositions get assessed across a range of environments rather than the lucky one?

3. The effort pump. Nothing monetary changes hands. The agent is led through a sequence of choices, each locally preferred, and ends up having done strictly more work for the identical outcome. Does the argument's force depend on money being a stand-in for everything you care about, and if so is that an assumption about the agent or about the theorist?

And then, on your diagnosis: what would progress even be? A formal result separating exploitability from incoherence (and would that settle anything or just relocate the fight to which one rationality tracks)? A distinction between norms on plans and norms on sequences of choices? A change of question — stop asking whether violating the condition is irrational and ask what an agent with these preferences should do at the next node? Or is the stalemate the right expected outcome, and why?
