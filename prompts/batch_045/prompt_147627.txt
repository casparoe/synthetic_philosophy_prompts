I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I got into an argument at a team offsite a couple weeks ago that I keep turning over. We tuned a code-assistant model with RL against a reward model trained on pairwise human preference labels — that reward model is our stand-in for would a senior engineer actually want this diff. It started doing the classic thing: enormous docstrings, defensive null checks on inputs that can't be null, three paragraphs of explanation above a one-line change. Raters loved it. The confusing part is that we also had a metric we'd sworn not to optimize against — whether senior engineers merge the suggested diff without heavy rewriting — and that one stayed basically flat while the reward score climbed a lot. A friend of mine who reads to much decision theory says this is exactly what you'd expect, and then gave me an argument I can't find the hole in. Reconstructing it as fairly as I can:

(1) Whenever you optimize a proxy P for a target G, P is only worth anything because it correlates with G.
(2) Optimization pressure pushes P toward its extreme values, and the P–G correlation is thinnest exactly out in that tail — that's the whole content of the slogan about a measure becoming a target and thereby ceasing to be a good one.
(3) A stronger optimizer searches harder, so it finds more of the places where P and G come apart; the divergence grows with optimization pressure and eventually the sign can flip, so pushing P actively makes G worse.
(4) So for any proxy there's some level of optimization pressure — call it the horizon — past which more optimization is anti-correlated with what you wanted.
(5) You can't locate the horizon in advance: the evidence you'd need lives in the region of behavior the optimizer hasn't reached yet, and once you're in that region the only way to check is with a new proxy, which then becomes a target and restarts the cycle.
(6) Nor can you step outside proxies altogether: to check whether P tracks G you have to measure G somehow, and any measurement of G is itself just another proxy, so G is only ever available to you as your current best stand-in for it.
(7) So either G just is whatever our best measurement says, in which case P can never really diverge from G and the whole worry is empty, or G sits permanently behind a veil of proxies and we can never tell whether we've crossed the horizon. Either way, no metric-driven optimization project can ever be shown to be working.
(8) But that's nuts. Bridges stay up, our on-call rotation more or less works, the merge rate is a perfectly serviceable metric day to day, and if you really could never tell whether an optimization was working, nobody would ever have shipped anything.

The two I'd least like to give up are (8), and (2)/(3), since that does look like literally what I'm watching happen at work — but if the honest answer is that one of those is where it breaks, say so. So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd especially like to know what (6) and (7) are doing, since that part feels like the sneaky bit but I can't say what's wrong with it, and whether my flat holdout metric is evidence about anything at all or whether my friend gets to say you only looked at it once, of course it looked fine. Also tell me how confident you are that the step you're rejecting is the load-bearing one rather than just where the damage shows up, what argument or observation would change your mind, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong. And if the repaired version of the argument still says something, I want to know what — is there a real lesson here about how hard we should push an optimizer, or is this just measurement is hard with numbered steps?
