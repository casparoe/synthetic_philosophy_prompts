I'm running a 90-minute session at our local rationalist-ish meetup next month — about a dozen people, mostly programmers plus one biologist, all of them have read the Sequences and argue like they have something to prove. I got landed with verbal disputes and what the metaontology people do with them. Don't explain the topic to me, I've definately read the standard stuff; I need a plan for how to teach it.

The thing I actually want them to walk out able to do is pull apart three cases they currently collapse into one: (1) a dispute that is merely verbal; (2) a dispute that is "just about words" but where the word choice is load-bearing, i.e. the real normative or social stakes ride on which meaning gets adopted; and (3) a dispute where the verbal diagnosis is locally correct but the disagreement bounces — you dissolve "do Xs exist?" into a choice between two candidate meanings of the quantifier or two redescriptions of the same facts, and then find yourself in the exact same fight one level up, now about which redescription carves things at the joints. I've been calling (3) the bounce, and I want the session built around it, but I also want you to tell me if the bounce is a real phenomenon or just me failing to finish a paraphrase and mistaking my own incomptence for a philosophical discovery.

So: what's the order the pieces have to go in, and what breaks if I reorder? I'm guessing I can't open with a definition of "verbal dispute" and I can't get to (3) before (1) and (2) are solid, but I want the dependency argument, not just the sequence. Give me the one example that should carry the whole session — it needs to be something this crowd cares about (agency, preferences, goals, mesa-optimizers, whatever), not the Ship of Theseus or tables or the hotdog thing, and tell me what property it has to have and what property it has to conspicuously lack to do the job.

Misconceptions I predict: they'll decide this is just "taboo your words" with a philosophy hat on; someone will immediately try to use "that's a verbal dispute" as a debate-stopper the second he's losing; and at least one will take the deflationary payoff to be "the question was meaningless" rather than "the question was a choice with costs". Where in the sequence do I head each of those off, and is there a fourth one I'm not seeing?

Then: how do I check at the end whether they can use the tool rather than repeat the slogan, and what should I openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes — including, maybe, whether (3) is a third-session topic I should cut entirely. Don't give me a reading list, nobody will do it, and please don't build anything on getting names and dates right.
