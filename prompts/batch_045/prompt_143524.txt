I run a fortnightly reading group with a mix of cognitive-science grad students and philosophers, and I also referee for an interdisciplinary journal, so I end up reading maybe a dozen papers a year whose entire argument rests on one invented scenario — a trolley variant, a brain-upload case, a room with a rulebook, a perfect predictor offering a box, sometimes a brand-new one about AI systems. I have no consistent way of deciding whether the scenario in front of me is doing real argumentative work or is just an intuition pump dressed up as one, and my verdicts are all over the place: I'll be harsh on a paper whose case I find manipulative and soft on one whose case is vivid, even when I can't say what the case established. I'd like an actual procedure I can run — tests in a fixed order, each with a stated "this is what counts as passing," a place where it stops and says no, and an explicit hand-off to human judgment where the question is one of values or of theory-choice rather than fact. Constraints: it should be runnable in about twenty minutes by a second-year grad student with no philosophy training, it has to end in something I can turn into a sentence in a referee report, and it needs to be honest about which steps are judgement calls wearing a lab coat.

Here's the material I want it built out of, roughly. I keep a list of failure modes in my notes and I'd like the procedure to catch at least these, in whatever form you think is right: (a) off-stage variables — the reader's verdict is being driven by some feature of the setup the author never mentions and never varies, like spatial distance, whether it's an action or an omission, how many people, whether anyone is named, how much time passes; (b) what I've started calling the mannequin problem — the scenario is too thin, so readers are reacting to a schema and filling the gaps from genre conventions (legal drama, lab report, sci-fi), and in fact different readers are filling them in incompatible ways, which is why the verdict looks stable when it isn't; (c) borrowed intuition — the case's force comes from silently importing a verdict on a nearby, more familiar question that differs from the target in a way that matters (using a teleportation case to get a verdict about ordinary survival, say); (d) calibration failure — the intuition is trained on real-world regularities (that predictors are unreliable, that copying preserves psychology, that promises need memory) which the case stipulates away, so the reader is really doing bad empirical reasoning while feeling like they're doing conceptual work. I'd also want the procedure to handle two distinctions I think must survive any simplification: whether the case is being used evidentially (to discover or support a verdict) or decoratively (to illustrate a conclusion already argued for, or to make a paper feel concrete), and whether it's load-bearing or whether you could delete it and the paper would stand. A friend I argued with after a talk last month thinks that a case producing a confident, stable verdict across audiences is ipso facto doing something right even if nobody can say what; I think stability is cheap and manufactureable. Take a side on that, because I think it decides how the whole checklist is built.

Then I want it run on three cases, please. One: the footbridge-style variant of the trolley setup — the one where you push a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley, versus diverting it — used in a paper arguing that there is no morally relevant difference between the two, so our ordinary harming/allowing distinction is an illusion. Two: the famous Chinese-room-style setup, where someone who speaks no Chinese sits in a room with a rulebook for manipulating symbols and produces fluent Chinese answers, used to argue that a system running a program doesn't understand anything, and hence that current AI systems don't. Three: a case from a paper I'm refereeing right now, which you won't have seen — a language-model assistant that has no memory between conversations and is reset every time tells a user "I'll remind you next Tuesday to take your medication," the user relies on it, and nothing happens because the system can't initiate contact; the paper concludes that no promise was made, since promising requires a persisting agent, and so no existing AI system can promise. I'd like at least one of the three to come out the way I won't like — my guess is my checklist is going to be too harsh on exploratory papers and too soft on vivid ones, and I'd rather find out from you than from an author. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: what it will systematically misfire on, and which of its steps a determined author could game by writing the case differently.
