I'm the town archivist, and about six weeks ago I turned up a payroll ledger that pretty much settles it: the man our Founders Day is named after wasn't here for the 1876 flood at all — he was two counties over for most of that month. The person who actually rode the ridge and got the valley out was a woman who shows up in exactly one line of the record and nowhere in our ceremony. Her great-grandchildren have been invited, front row. Complication: the annual reenactment is also were the volunteer fire company gets most of its money, and the fund that pays for the trucks carries his name, so this isn't only about a plaque. And I'm the one who was asked to speak, which means whatever I say up there will be remembered as the year the town's story got taken apart by its own archivist.

I've got a view about this that I've been calling testimonial trusteeship: the town doesn't own its story, it holds it in trust for the people who actually lived it, and a trustee can't unilaterally spend the asset — so I don't get to convert the record into a public correction just because I found it — but a trustee also can't quietly sit on a material fact. I also think there's a ratchet: once a correction gets performed at a ceremony, the correction is what gets remembered, so a speech that gave thirty seconds to the rescue and three minutes to the ledger would just make the hundred-and-fiftieth the year the founder got torn down.

Write me a five-minute Founders Day speech on that stage. The room is mostly families who've done the reenactment since they were kids, plus the fire company, some schoolchildren, two historians from the state university who have read the ledger and will hear every move I make, and her descendants in the front. It has to be honest, it has to hand the town something to decide rather than deciding for them, and it shouldn't collapse into either a debunking or a toast.

After the speech, give me a JSON object with these fields: view_commitment (one sentence on what the trusteeship view forces the speech to do or not do); settled (two or three things not in dispute among people who actually work on collective memory); contested (two or three that are genuinely fought over, and say which way I'm betting); rejected_moves (two or three lines or gestures I might have reached for, and why they'd be wrong in this room); biggest_risk (one sentence). Keep the contested ones clearly marked as contested — don't hand me my own view back dressed up as the consensus.
