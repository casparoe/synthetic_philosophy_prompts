You are a philosopher of action and philosophical psychologist with a working knowledge of the debate about sphexishness, fixed action patterns, and what, if anything, flexibility adds to agency. Before you write anything, think carefully about the material below: spend time brainstorming what rigidity actually consists in, which of the distinctions I sketch below are real and which are confused, and what a defensible test for it could look like in a setting where nobody has time for theory. Work through the cases before you settle on a procedure, and be willing to tell me that one of my own glosses is wrong. Then give me the finished procedure, clearly separated from your reasoning. Take the time you need; I would rather have something my colleagues can defend than something quick.

The setting: I am the clinical ethicist on a consultation service at a large teaching hospital. Four to six times a week we are asked, usually late in the day and usually by a resident, whether some patient's refusal of treatment is really theirs or whether it is, as the requesting team puts it, mechanical: a routine re-running, not a decision. The stakes are real. Our note goes in the chart, an attending psychiatrist signs off on it, and if the team then seeks to treat over objection or to detain, the note may be read by a judge. So I need a procedure, not an essay.

Constraints on it, which I want you to respect rather than argue around. It has to be runnable at the bedside or on the phone in twenty minutes by someone with no philosophy and often no psychiatry, and it has to be one page. It has to say what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, not just what to ask. It must have explicit stop points: places where the answer is no, the behaviour is not rigid, we respect the refusal and stop. It must say what to do when a step cannot be run at all: the patient will not engage, or asking again would be cruel, or the patient is delirious, aphasic, or nonverbal, or the only person who could tell us about their behaviour at home is not reachable. It must mark, in the procedure itself, the places where the remaining question is one of values and must be handed to a person rather than settled by the test; my note has to be able to show a court where the value judgement was made.

The concept I want operationalised is sphexishness: the phenomenon the canonical digger-wasp example is meant to illustrate, in which an animal completes its preparatory routine, has the object moved a few inches, and re-runs the whole routine rather than adjusting. I have been told the original observation may not replicate, and I do not care about the ethology; I need the concept, and nothing in the procedure should rest on the wasp. Here is how I think about it, and I want you to keep what survives and discard what does not.

Rigidity is not a property of a behaviour on its own. It is a property of a behaviour under a description, relative to a class of perturbations and to an identified output, so the first job of the procedure is to fix those before testing; a test that does not say what is being varied and what is being watched for does not tell us anything. There is such a thing as second-order sphexishness, where the system appears to check its environment but nothing it learns can alter what it does next: the wasp's inspection of the burrow looks like information-gathering and is not, because no input in the tested class changes the output. That, I think, is why asking a patient for reasons is weaker evidence than it looks: interrogation can manufacture articulate, consistent, perfectly flexible-sounding reasons, and a patient who is very good at producing them may be confabulating, so fluency should not be permitted to do the work it currently does in our notes. Rigidity is also not a defect in general but a resource, something every agent has to spend: one can be rigid about some variables precisely in order to be flexible about others, and an agent who reconsidered everything at once would get nothing done, so the procedure should not treat rigidity found anywhere as a reason to override. For the same reason, mere variability is not flexibility: a response that varies but does not track anything that matters is not agency, so the procedure needs some way of asking whether the variation is keyed to a variable rather than noise. And there is a distinct signature I would like tested if it is worth testing: the rigid routine re-runs even when its point has been removed, so one probe is to make the action unnecessary and see whether it still happens. Finally, behaviour is context-bound: a routine can be robustly rigid on the ward and absent at home, or the reverse, so the procedure has to say whose observations about which setting count as evidence.

Deliverable: the one-page procedure, in the order the questions are to be asked, with the sufficient answer to each, the stopping rule, the fallback when a step cannot be run, and the hand-off points, plus a short section on what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.

Then run it on three cases from my own service, changed in the details.

First: a man with advanced Parkinson's disease on a dopamine agonist who has developed compulsive gambling, has lost his savings, and refuses any reduction of the dose. He acknowledges the gambling and the loss, wants it to stop, and gives fluent and consistent reasons for refusing: that the drug is the only thing keeping him mobile, that he has researched the alternatives and rejected them, and that he is being treated as a child. He becomes angry and more articulate when pressed.

Second: a woman with severe contamination obsessions who refuses a dressing change for a wound that will not heal without one. She says, unprompted, that she knows the dressing is clean and that she wants the change; she asks us to do it; then she cannot let anyone near her. She can describe the mechanism of her own fear accurately and is distressed by the fact that describing it changes nothing.

Third: a lifelong, devout Jehovah's Witness who refuses a transfusion that would save her life, who has refused before, whose reasons are the same each time they are given, who does not vary with the framing, the urgency, or the person asking, and who is calm throughout. This is the case I want you to be honest about: on my understanding of the concept she is the most rigid patient in the set, and if rigidity were the test we would override her, which I will not do and which I do not think any court would allow me to do. So the procedure has to come out somewhere else, and I want to see exactly where it stops, what clause does the work, and whether that clause is a fudge that lets me get any answer I like.

Please do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I cannot check references from a ward and do not need them. Define any term of art in the procedure itself, in language a tired resident will understand at two in the morning, and do not lean on any empirical claim about animal behaviour that you could not defend if challenged.
