In my seminar on uncertainty in economics we are reading Keynes's A Treatise on Probability (1921), where probability is a strictly logical relation of partial implication between evidence and hypothesis, often merely comparative rather than numerically measurable, so that two hypotheses can simply be incomparable in probability, and where this is distinguished from the weight of the argument, that is, the absolute amount of relevant evidence, which always grows as new evidence arrives even when the probability itself falls.

To make weight usable I want to propose a reading I have not come across in the literature: call it evidential exposure, on which the weight of a judgment about a hypothesis is the expected size of the difference between one's present probability for it and the probability one would assign after one more unit of ideally relevant evidence, so that weight measures how much one's judgment is still in play rather than how much one already knows.

What are the most important implications of reading weight in this way, for (i) whether probability and weight can remain genuinely independent dimensions of rational belief, as Keynes seems to want, and (ii) how an agent whose probabilities are only partially ordered should choose between two courses of action when she could instead postpone the decision and gather more evidence? Please concentrate on what follows from the reading itself rather than on exegesis of Keynes.
