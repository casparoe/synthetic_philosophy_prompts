hey — I'm a TA for a final-year undergrad seminar on ethics and economics (about 18 students, mostly PPE and politics people plus a couple of philosophers and two maths majors who will see through anything in about four seconds, one 80-minute slot, no real money allowed but I've got poker chips, index cards, a whiteboard and a projector). My session is on interpersonal utility comparisons and I want them to actually run into the problem instead of me writing 'each person's utility function is only defined up to a positive affine transformation' on the board and everyone nodding. Last year I did the obvious thing — everybody privately writes down how well off they are on a scale from 0 to 10 and we compare — and it was a disaster: eleven people wrote 7, one guy wrote 9.5 basically to be funny, and the entire discussion collapsed into whether people were reporting honestly rather than whether the numbers meant the same thing by '7'.

So: design me a runnable game or structured activity where the group has to make a real allocation decision across people whose private conversion rates between stuff and satisfaction are set by cards only they can read, and where what they hit is this — that two different translation schemes between those private scales, both perfectly consistent with everything anyone can observe about anybody's choices, recommend opposite allocations, and that once you notice that there's no further behavioural fact left to appeal to. I've been toying with a few ingredients and you can use, improve or scrap them: each student gets a private payoff table in their own unnamed units over some small set of goods (chips, minutes of quiet, songs played on the projector, whatever); one participant is publicly designated as the 'reference person' whose unit is fixed by fiat so everyone else's scale has to be expressed as a multiple of theirs; a round where students can bid chips to swap entire situations with someone for the rest of the session (which is how I was hoping to sneak in level comparisons rather than just comparisons of gains); and a final round behind a veil where they choose a rule without knowing which card they'll hold, except that the lottery is deliberately not uniform — it's loaded, and they're told how — so they can't just default to averaging. The point should land in what happens, not in what I say afterwards: ideally they construct the competing translation tables themselves and only at the end see that the disagreement was never about facts about anyone.

What I need is: rules and roles, what goes on the cards, a payoff scheme that doesn't need money, a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific bits of the problem (level vs gain comparisons, the unit vs the origin, why the swap round and the veil round can come apart, sum vs prioritising the worst off). Also: tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time — the strategic-misreporting derailment, whoever draws the 'utility monster' card, the reference person getting weird about it, the maths majors solving it in their heads and either spoiling it or checking out — and how to handle each, plus how to make those two useful instead of lethal. And be frank about which feature of the debate the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the last ten minutes: my worry is that it can show the comparison isn't recoverable from behaviour but can't show anything about whether it's determinately true, and I don't want to accidentally teach them that it isn't.
