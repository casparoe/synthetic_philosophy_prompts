After roughly twenty years away, I have recently begun attending church again — mainly because my mother asked me to, and at eighty-two she does not ask for much — and I have somehow ended up co-leading the small adult discussion group that meets after the Sunday service. We are working our way through the moral law, and last week the Euthyphro question arrived in its usual form: is an act right because God commands it, or does God command it because it is right?

One member of the group proposed what he called the two-keys answer: God's character or nature fixes which acts are right, so the content of the moral law is not arbitrary; and God's commanding those acts is separately what makes them binding on us, so the law is not a free-floating standard with no lawgiver attached. He insists this is a genuine third option rather than the first horn in a costume.

My own sense is that it only relocates the dilemma. Once you have settled which acts are right, there still seems to be a further question — why does someone's saying so give me a reason to act? — and that further question looks as though it has the same two-horned shape: either God's commands bind because it is in some way good or fitting to obey them, which is the first horn again, or they bind simply because they are his, which looks like the second horn. I also suspect I am leaning too hard on the familiar thought experiment about God commanding something cruel: another member says that objection is weaker than it looks, and I cannot quite articulate why.

Please explain, at a level suitable for a serious adult discussion group rather than a graduate seminar, the difference between the question about the content of the moral law and the question about the authority of the lawgiver, and whether an answer to the first really does anything to settle the second. Please do not give me a general survey of the Euthyphro dilemma, a catalogue of positions in the literature, or any reading suggestions — I have no library access here, and I want the shape of the argument rather than a map of who has defended what.

I would find it most useful if you used these sections, in this order:

- Content and authority (about 150 words): set out the two questions precisely and say how they are related.
- Why the cruelty objection is weaker than it looks (about 200 words): explain what that thought experiment does and does not establish.
- What the appeal to God's nature buys (about 200 words): explain what is gained, and what is left open, by answering the content question in this way.
- The authority question on its own (about 250 words): explain what would have to be true for a command to generate a reason for action, and whether that can be secured without going back through the first horn.
- Verdict on the two-keys proposal (about 150 words): say whether it is a genuine third option, a relabeling of one horn, or something in between, and what you would actually tell the group.
