Hey — you're a philosopher of action who also knows your way around behavioural economics, the sort of person who has taught both Aristotle on akrasia and the standard quasi-hyperbolic models. Take this seriously and work it out properly: before you write anything polished, brainstorm me a quick list of half a dozen or so candidate accounts of what's going on in the practices below, junk the ones that obviously don't fit, and only then commit. I don't want a balanced survey where every paragraph ends in "on the other hand." I want you to pick the account you think is actually right, defend it, apply it to all four cases, and then — last paragraph — be straight with me about how confident you are and tell me the single consideration that would most quickly move you off it. Don't cite anything; I know the literature, I want your argument.

What's bugging me is a cluster of things institutions actually do, which don't seem to add up to a single picture of the will.

One: in most gambling regimes you can sign a form barring yourself from a casino or a betting site for a year or more. Turn up three weeks later sober, calm, articulate, explain that you've changed your mind and want back in — they refuse. Worse, the fact that you now want in is treated *in favour of* keeping the bar in place, as evidence it's working, not as a change of mind that deserves respect. The will speaking now has no authority at all.

Two: consent to a medical procedure can be withdrawn at pretty much any point, including after you've signed the forms, fasted, been wheeled in, and had the line put in. The will speaking now has absolute authority.

Three: cooling-off. Buy something from a salesman who knocked on your door, or sign a timeshare or some kinds of credit agreement, and you get a window in which you can tear the whole thing up for no stated reason. Sign the same contract in a shop and "I changed my mind" is no defence at all.

Four, and this is the bit I think nobody has really gotten to the bottom of: look at the *shape* of the help institutions will give you against your own future weakness. They will enforce a commitment to an omission (self-exclusion, non-competes, lock-ups) and a commitment to a penalty (you owe the deposit if you skip the gym, your contribution rate ratchets up automatically at your next raise unless you opt out). They will not enforce a commitment to an action. No specific performance of personal services, no enforceable promise to turn up, no handing a future decision over to someone else, no voluntarily contracting into service even for money. Every anti-akrasia device I can think of works by removing an option or repricing it; none works by moving your body. (There's also, in a handful of places, the "Ulysses clause" in psychiatric advance directives: detain me during a manic episode even if I'm refusing at the time. I may have the details slightly wrong, but take the practice as I've described it.)

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. What view of agency over time, of what makes a decision *yours*, of what weakness of will even is, do you need for this whole package to make sense as a package? Give it a name if you like.

Then be careful about strength. What does the package strictly *presuppose*, versus what it merely sits comfortably with? Could someone who thinks the present will is sovereign get all of it by treating pre-commitment as an exercise of autonomy rather than a restriction on it? Could a multiple-selves theorist who gives every temporal slice's welfare equal weight get there, and if so does the package still look like *akrasia* rather than like one person imposing costs on another? Could someone who thinks akrasia is a synchronic failure of attention or self-control, not a change in preference over time, get there?

And is it consistent? Case one and case two pull in opposite diretions about the authority of the present will. What's the line — arousal, addiction, having previously put something in writing, the reversibility of the harm, something about who bears the cost? Is it a line the practices themselves could state, or are we rationalising?

Then the asymmetry in four. Is that principled, or is it just the law of specific performance plus dignity plus enforcement cost wearing a philosophical costume? If it is principled, I want the principle: what is it about compulsion that makes it unavailable as a cure for weakness, even when the agent begged for it earlier? And is the omission/penalty-only pattern really universal, or do auto-enrolment, defaults and deadlines count as devices that push you into action — and if they do, does that break my pattern or actually confirm it?

Finally: what would have to change if we dropped the theory you've reconstructed and adopted instead (a) full sovereignty of the will at the moment of action, or (b) a temporally neutral view on which future selves have exactly the same claim on us as the present one? Go case by case. And tell me what, concretely, we'd lose — something we'd actually notice, not something we'd only miss in a seminar room.
