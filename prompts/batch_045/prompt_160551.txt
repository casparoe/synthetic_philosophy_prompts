I've been asked to circulate, ahead of a reading-group meeting next month, a short statement of where I've landed on the socialist calculation debate, so that the others can press on it; the group is mostly political theorists with two economists, and they want positions rather than summaries. Here is my bundle, and I'd like an audit of it rather than a survey of the literature.

The framing I've been using is to split the anti-planning case into three separable complaints, which I call the index problem (there is no common unit in which to compare heterogeneous capital goods and alternative production plans once the means of production aren't exchanged), the update problem (the knowledge relevant to allocation is dispersed, local, largely inarticulate, and re-made faster than any central procedure can register), and the loss problem (whoever sets a price does not bear the loss from setting it wrong, and there is no reliable mechanism that removes them when they get it wrong). My commitments are: (1) the index problem is answered — Lange's model, in which a central board announces factor prices, instructs managers to minimize average cost and to expand output until price equals marginal cost, and then revises prices in the direction of excess demand, is a genuine solution in principle to the problem as Mises stated it; (2) the update problem is the real objection and the one Lange cannot meet, because a trial-and-error revision that would converge in a stationary world is worthless when the data change faster than the iteration, so the board is always computing last quarter's economy; (3) the index problem and the update problem are not rivals, and holding (1) and (2) together is fine, since solving the first is what lets you see the second; (4) planning as such isn't refuted by Hayek's argument, because large firms are planned hierarchies that allocate capital internally by shadow rates and transfer prices set by committees, at a scale larger than many states, and the firm/market boundary is set by comparative transaction costs, which is a matter of degree rather than of principle; (5) most of what actually went wrong in the command economies had nothing to do with either index or update — quantity quotas, ratchet effects on targets, chronic shortage, and budgets that were never allowed to bind — so the empirical record is evidence about the loss problem, and I don't think it confirms the calculation argument at all; (6) on the Hayekian side I want to say both that the price system is a discovery procedure whose virtue is epistemic and that Hayek was right to reject equilibrium benchmarks (perfect competition, given data, the auctioneer) as the standard by which an economic order is judged, since positing the data is what begs the question against him; (7) finally, the "who adjusts the adjustment rule" worry — the meta-rule problem, as I've been calling it — cuts both ways and can't be used against Lange alone, because the price system has no market in interest-rate rules either, and someone has to set those too.

What I can't see is whether this is one position or three that happen to share a vocabulary. Please go through the set, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what that would cost me. Be definite: I'd rather be told which commitment to drop than handed a menu. In particular: do (4) and (5) together leave me any reason to keep (2) as the centerpiece, or have I quietly turned an epistemic argument into an argument about property and residual claimancy while still describing it as one about knowledge? Is (7) really symmetric, or is the asymmetry precisely the thing I need in order to defend (2) against (4)? Can (6)'s two halves be held together at all, and if not, which is cheaper to lose? And please flag anything that only looks inconsistent and isn't — I'd rather have that marked than have every item treated as a problem. Mark which of your verdicts you'd defend with confidence and which you'd abandon on a good counterexample, and say where you're going out on a limb. Please write in American English.
