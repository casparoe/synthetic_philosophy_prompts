hey — a question about Hofstadter's essay on the shallowness of machine translation (the one where he walks through a pile of sentences a translation system mangles and says it's really doing something like pattern-matching against a huge memory of text rather than understanding anything). No citations or reading list please, I've read it and I can't look anything up from where I am anyway.

As I read him, the complaint is basically this: the system never contains anything that *means* anything. It shoves surface items around on the basis of learned associations between them; nowhere in the pipeline is there an explicit representation of who did what to whom, of what the situation being described is actually like, or of what the sentence is for. Fine.

But then the fix looks obvious to me and I don't get why nobody is doing it. If the problem is that the whole thing is learned associations between surface items, then don't do that. Parse the source into a language-neutral semantic representation — explicit symbols for objects, events, agent/patient roles, time, causation, plus commonsense rules of the kind you need for "the trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because it's too big" — and generate the target language from *that*. Symbolic operations on meanings instead of vector-y associations between strings. It's deep by construction, so "shallowness" can't even get a grip on it anymore. My actual question is about implementation: which half is really the hard part, the parse into the meaning representation or the generation out of it? And is the reason nobody does this anymore just that hand-built semantic grammars turned out brittle and expensive, or is there something principled against it?

While you're at it, a smaller thing I use to decide whether a system "gets" a sentence, and I'd like to know if its fair. Call it construal parity: take a source sentence with two live readings — an idiom that also has a coherent literal reading, say. A system passes if its output keeps both readings alive in the target language, and if the target language can't carry both, it passes by flagging what it dropped instead of silently picking one. To me that looks like a direct test for whether something is working with construals instead of collapsing them, which is exactly what Hofstadter says the systems can't do. Is it a good test?

I care about the confidence level on this as much as the content: tell me where your answer is most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind — e.g. what would actually convince you that a system built the way I describe understands something, or that it doesn't, and what would convince you construal parity is measuring the wrong thing.
