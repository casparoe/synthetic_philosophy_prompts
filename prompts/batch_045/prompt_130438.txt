I run the quarterly model review at a long-horizon research funder. A dozen or so times a year, my two analysts and I have to decide whether a long-run predictive model — usually a system-dynamics or agent-based simulation with a ten-to-forty-year horizon, funded at a few hundred thousand a year — should be renewed, sent back to be re-specified, or dropped. The board signs off on a one-page recommendation and is not methodologically trained; we have about half a day per model. The recurring hard case is the one you would expect: the model's headline prediction has not come true inside its stated window, and its authors say the mechanism is right but the timing was off, or that the outcome was averted by an intervention the forecast itself helped trigger.

I want a written decision procedure for exactly that case, runnable by an analyst with social-science training and no philosophy of science, that separates a theory that is genuinely early or self-denying from one that has degenerated into date-slipping. I have a provisional rule, which I want you to sharpen or replace rather than endorse: one free retiming per model, after which any further rescue must be entered in a public pre-commitment ledger with a new date and a new independently checkable consequence, and rescues invented after a miss count for nothing. I suspect that rule is too crude about predictions that never had a date attached and too generous to predictions that are self-denying. I have read the Lakatos material on progressive and degenerating research programmes; do not restate it, give me rulings.

The procedure should specify, in the order a reviewer runs them: how to classify the object under review before any falsification is attempted (a dated point forecast; a dominance claim that one force will outweigh all countervailing ones by a given date; or a bare mechanism or existence claim); what must be established before an it-was-averted defence is credited rather than merely asserted; how to audit whether the load-bearing auxiliary hypotheses were declared in advance or retrofitted; the points at which it stops with a no; and the questions that are judgment calls, which must be handed to a named person rather than settled by the checklist.

Then run it on three reviews and give the one-page recommendation for each: a neo-Malthusian food-and-population model whose two predicted famine windows have both passed while yields kept rising; a resource-depletion model that predicted a price spike, saw one, and then saw prices collapse because of an extraction technology absent from the model; and our own ten-year-old flagship model of institutional-trust decline in rich democracies, up for renewal next quarter, authored by a senior colleague, whose predicted threshold has not been crossed. I expect the third to come out badly and I want it run straight.

Begin with the one-sentence version of the procedure that a reviewer could keep on an index card, then the steps, then the three applications, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for.
