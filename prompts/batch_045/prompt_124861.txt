I co-teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science — fourteen students, a single hundred-minute session, a seminar room with a projector and movable chairs, no lab equipment beyond index cards, a sealed envelope and a laptop. We have already spent two weeks on confirmation and on the holist point that a hypothesis is never tested alone, and I want this session to make them *feel* the pull of the underdetermination of theory by evidence before anyone says the word. My attempt last year was a black box they could query for input–output pairs; it degenerated inside ten minutes into simulation-skepticism and I never got them back. So I need the rules and the scoring arranged so that the cheap skeptical move is expensive and the construction of a substantive rival is cheap, and I need the word withheld until the debrief.

The shape I have in mind, which you should improve or replace rather than just fill in: four teams of three or four; a dataset of maybe forty noisy observations generated by a secret rule that I can implement in a spreadsheet formula in ten minutes, which you should invent and specify along with the noise model; a first round in which each team commits in writing to a rule and to predictions for five held-back queries, scored against the truth; a second round in which they get more data and may revise, with the scoring arranged so that wagering on your own rule has real downside; and a third round in which each team is *required* to produce, against the best rule in the room, a rival that fits every observation made so far and that no future query from this apparatus could distinguish from it. The facilitator's secret is that the true rule is one that nobody in the room proposes, and the reveal is that even the reveal does not settle things. I want the payoff structure to be points that cost me nothing and the students only a trivial stake — picking next week's reading topic, say — and I want a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes, including what the facilitator does while the teams are working.

Here is the part I care most about, and where I think the activity can be made to do real philosophical work. I want the debrief to prise apart three claims that I think get run together: the near-trivial modal claim that for any finite body of evidence there are indefinitely many incompatible hypotheses equally consistent with it; the historical claim that at most actual junctures there were serious alternatives that the community had not yet conceived, so that the first claim has teeth in practice and not merely in logical space; and the methodological claim that the canons we use to choose between empirically equivalent theories — simplicity, conservatism, scope, whatever else — do not track the truth. My hunch is that a ninety-minute game can make the first vivid, can only *stage* the second by fiat, and cannot touch the third at all, and I want you to tell me if that is right and which of the three your design actually gives anyone warrant for. I also want the teams' rivals in round three to be sorted by an explicit admissibility test you specify in advance — something like being statable in the vocabulary already available to the community, not working by deferring the disagreement to a place or time no query will ever reach, and not being generated solely by negating the rival — because I suspect the interesting disagreement is not about whether empirically equivalent rivals exist but about whether contrivance is a defect, and I would like the activity to surface that rather than have me assert it. If you think my three-way taxonomy is wrong, or that two of the claims collapse into one, say so before designing around it.

Two further requests. First, be honest about what goes wrong when this is actually run: I expect someone to produce a gerrymandered rival and conclude that science is irrational, I expect the scoring to reward overfitting, I expect the reveal to be dismissed as the facilitator cheating, and I expect it all to overrun — tell me what else to expect and how to handle each, including how to keep the session from landing on global skepticism about inquiry. Second, and most important: when you write the debrief, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, do not present a contested view as the consensus view, and mark the places where the design itself rigs the intuition — where the activity makes one side of the debate look stronger than the literature supports, and what I should say out loud to compensate.
