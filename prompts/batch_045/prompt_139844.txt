I work as a clinical data manager at a hospital, and this year I was appointed to the committee that signs off on recruitment materials for our clinical trials. I am the only Black member of the committee and the only one without a clinical qualification. Whenever discussion turns to "community trust" or to recruiting a more diverse group of participants, the chair turns to me and the room treats whatever I say as close to decisive — even when I say plainly that I am not a community liaison and have no more access than anyone else to what people in the city actually think. In the same meetings, on the parts I genuinely know — a consent form that overstates the likely benefit, eligibility criteria that will quietly exclude exactly the people we claim to be reaching — I am thanked, overruled, and asked to put my concerns in writing afterwards.

A colleague I trust says I should be flattered: this is prejudice in my favour, and nobody is deprived of anything by being believed too readily. I am not convinced. My suspicion is that the two things are a single mechanism — that the authority I am handed on questions about my own identity is what confines it to those questions, and that politely handing it back would cost me what little credibility I have on everything else, and would likely leave whoever comes after me on this committee in a worse position, not a better one.

Is my colleague right that only the discounting is a real wrong and the favouritism is harmless? Is my suspicion about the mechanism and about the cost of pushing back coherent, and does the risk to the next person change what I owe? I would like exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: identify the distinct things going wrong here, say whether the favouritism is among them and on what grounds it would or would not be, say whether I am right about the mechanism and about what resistance would cost me, and tell me what I should actually do.
