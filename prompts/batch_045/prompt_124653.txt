I'm on the volunteer acquisitions panel of our city archive — five of us, we meet maybe six times a year and decide what gets offered a home and what gets politely turned away — and I've realised I have no idea what we're actually doing when we decide. So I want to test our own track record against a case we've got coming up in March.

Here's what we've done so far. (1) We accepted the papers of a colonial district commissioner who served overseas from 1919 to 1954 — diaries, official correspondence, and a punishment register with the names of local men he ordered flogged — and we made the whole thing open to anyone with a reader's ticket; the chair's line was that "we are the memory of the city, not its conscience". (2) We turned down the papers of the family who owned a big local department store — household ledgers and society letters, 1890s to 1960s — on the grounds that we already have shelves of that sort of thing. (3) We accepted a shoebox of 1983 cassette tapes recorded by a tenants' association on a housing estate that's since been demolished: no release forms, several interviewees untraceable, and we digitised them and put them online with a takedown request form. (4) We declined the working notebooks of a local crime reporter who'd kept detailed notes on private conversations with bereaved families after a pit disaster, even though his son owns them outright and offered them properly. (5) We accepted, but sealed for twenty-five years, the staff rotas and incident books of a long-closed psychiatric hospital, which name patients, and we were split on that one.

The March case: the surviving records of a children's home run by a religious order until 1981 — admission registers, medical inspection cards, and a misconduct-and-absconsion book covering roughly nine hundred children, most of whom are now in their fifties to seventies. A survivors' association wants the material preserved and open, and has also said it doesn't think we should hold it at all: they want it in their own community archive, and they've offered to take it if we decline. Separately, two survivors have written to us asking that their names never be searchable, and the order's solicitors will let us have scans but not the originals. What I'd like from you: the two or three different rules that fit all five of our past decisions equally well, what each of them predicts for the children's home, your best guess at what we'll actually do, which past decision is the closest analogue and why, an honest statement of what principle we'd be committing ourselves to either way, and — the bit I care most about — one further hypothetical case I could put to the panel that would separate the rules that our precedents can't tell apart. One more thing I've been obsessing over, which may be a red herring or may be the whole point: whenever we take material like this, our cataloguers end up describing it in the institution's own vocabulary, so someone searching in their own words for what happened to them never finds it even once it's technically "open"; I've started calling this a second-order silence, as opposed to a gap where nothing was ever written down in the first place, and I'd like to know whether that distinction does any real work here or whether it just moves the problem around. We can only fund one accession this year, so a genuine "not us, them" is on the table too. Please write in British English, and keep it to a couple of pages — no footnotes, no reading lists, no citations, I want the argument, not the bibliography.
