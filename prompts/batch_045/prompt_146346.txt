I'm the pharmacovigilance person on the data and safety monitoring board for a 14-site trial of ALX-204, an oral agent added to standard of care for a rare autoimmune condition. We're 340 of a planned 600 enrolled. I need to draft the memo we'll vote on Thursday and I'd like you to write a first pass of it.

Here's the case. Two serious adverse events, eight weeks apart, different sites:

- Site 4, 68M, week 6: acute kidney injury, creatinine peaked at 3.1, biopsy read locally as granulomatous interstitial nephritis. Drug stopped, steroids given, renal function back to baseline in 19 days. He'd had a contrast CT four days before the event, and the local PI logged it as possibly contrast-related, possibly drug-related.
- Site 9, 34F, week 19: asymptomatic ALT found on routine draw at 40x upper limit of normal, biopsy read locally as granulomatous hepatitis. Drug stopped, no steroids, liver enzymes normalized in 5 weeks. She was also on a comedication with a known hepatotoxicity signal, and the site logged it as probably comedication-related.

The unifying read: both are one drug-induced sarcoid-like / granulomatous systemic reaction, showing up in whatever organ it shows up in. The disunifying read: a delayed-hypersensitivity tubulointerstitial nephritis with a few giant cells that the pathologist called "granulomatous," and an unrelated comedication hepatitis — two unrelated events that happen to share a histological adjective. There's also a third event from nine months ago I want to flag: a patient at Site 1 with incidentally discovered bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on a staging scan, asymptomatic, logged at the time as probably sarcoidosis, unrelated to study drug, still on drug, still fine. Under the unifying hypothesis that's a third case. Under the disunifying hypothesis it's noise. It also makes the cluster more frequent but milder.

The thing that's tying me in knots is that the pooling decision seems to come before the evidence rather than after it. Our statistician says neither event is individually significant against its own background rate (she puts each well under 1 per 10,000 patient-years, but admits the numbers are soft), and that the co-occurrence of two rare granulomatous events is the whole signal. But the co-occurrence only *is* the signal if they're one mechanism, and whether they're one mechanism is supposed to be what the signal tells us. Also relevant: our safety database codes by MedDRA preferred term, which is organ-based, so these two land in different buckets and would never pool unless someone decides in advance to group them, and our charter has no mechanism for doing that.

I've been pushing a criterion on the board for a couple of years, which I call interventional indistinguishability: two events are the same mechanism iff there is no intervention we could actually run that would separate them. The trouble is that in this case the separating intervention is rechallenge, which nobody will approve, so my own criterion returns "no answer" on the exact question we have to decide Thursday. And central re-read of both biopsies takes about three weeks, which is longer than any pause we could realistically vote for.

So the memo needs to do the following. Recommend one of our four options — continue unchanged, continue with a protocol amendment, pause new enrollment, or pause dosing in everyone — with dates attached (our next scheduled meeting is in six weeks, enrollment would complete in about eleven). It should say what to do about the Site 1 lymphadenopathy, including whether re-adjudicating it now is changing the hypothesis after seeing the data. It should either use my criterion, repair it, or replace it with something the board can actually apply to a pair of events it has to judge in one afternoon, and if it replaces it, say what my criterion was getting right. It should tell the sponsor's medical monitor something he can respond to rather than just telling him he's conflicted — he has the strongest version of the disunifying case, which is that we'd be halting a trial of a drug that may work, for patients with no alternative, on the strength of two events that each have a decent alternative explanation. Give that its due, and then say what would change our mind and by when.

One more constraint: whatever we recommend has to be defensible if it turns out the two events were never related, and also if it turns out they were. I'd rather be slightly wrong in a way I can explain than right by luck.
