I work in philosophy of mind but have drifted into machine-translation adjacent work, and I want a consistency check on where I've landed about whether such systems understand anything. My commitments:

(1) The Hofstadter-style shallowness critique is basically right: what is diagnostic is not the error rate but the error type. Systems fail exactly where success would require representing what was meant — resolving an anaphor or a lexical ambiguity by appeal to the situation rather than to surface statistics — and that pattern shows there is no semantic level of description at which the system is operating.

(2) Translation is the re-expression of a meaning, not string-to-string mapping: understanding a sentence involves representing the speaker's communicative intention and the situation the sentence is about.

(3) None of this is a behavioral criterion. No battery of input–output tests, however large, could establish understanding; benchmark success is never sufficient, and the error typology in (1) is constitutive of the absence of understanding, not merely a symptom of it.

(4) I am a functionalist: there is no fact about understanding over and above causal-functional organization — no extra ingredient, no inner glow.

(5) Understanding is scalar and domain-relative, so the question "does the system understand?" is ill-posed; a system can understand more or less, and here but not there.

(6) Meaning requires grounding, in the sense of causal contact between words and the things they are about, and a system trained only on text cannot have it.

One case verdict I am attached to: a self-taught reader who learned literary Japanese entirely from books and dictionaries, has never spoken to a Japanese person or set foot in Japan, and yet reads novels with genuine comprehension — I want to say she understands Japanese. The move that saves this case is what I'll call derivative grounding: the texts she learned from were produced by grounded speakers, so her word–world links are mediated rather than absent.

Please audit this package rather than lecture me about the topic. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than listed as equals, and what exactly would have to be given up in each case — in particular, whether (6) survives the case verdict once you notice that an LLM's training corpus is also produced by grounded speakers, and whether (3) is something a functionalist is entitled to say. Which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and why? Then name the single revision that would clean up the most, and say what it costs me.
