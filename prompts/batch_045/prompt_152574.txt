I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a thing set in Athens in the 330s, and I've written myself into a corner. One of my characters is a retail trader — exactly the sort Aristotle obviously can't stand — and I want his self-defense to be genuinely Aristotelian rather than a modern economist in a bedsheet. So I've worked out a reading of the reciprocity section in book 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics (the builder / shoemaker / house / shoe bit, where need is said to be what holds a city together and money is described as a conventional stand-in for it), together with the wealth-getting chapters near the start of the Politics, and I want it beaten up before I build a scene on it.

Where I've landed: Aristotle isn't offering a theory of value at all. Not labor, not utility, not subjective preference. On my reading, need isn't a quantity that money measures; it's a relation that money makes room for. Before money there's just my shoes and your house and nothing that makes them comparable; money is a conventional device that sets up a single scale on which a proportionate return can be worked out, and the ratios on that scale are perfectly real, but there is no fact about how much the house is "really worth" sitting behind the scale waiting to be measured. I've been calling this constitutive measurement versus derived measurement. What it buys me dramaturgically: my trader can say his prices aren't distortions of a true measure, because there is no true measure for them to distort — and Aristotle can reply that this is precisely the indictment, that a measure with nothing to measure is a tool you can point anywhere, which is why the same device that makes proportionate reciprocity possible also makes indefinite accumulation look like a sane project. On this reading the "unnaturalness" of retail trade is about the end it's hitched to, not the mechanism, so the complaint in the Politics and the account in the Ethics are consistant.

So: what would count against this? Both as exegesis (what in the text should make me drop it) and as a seperate question, as a position in its own right (what would make it a bad thing to believe even if Aristotle did hold something like it). Rank whatever you find from fatal down to merely awkward, and mark which items a sympathetic defender of the reading would concede as counting against them versus which they'd explain away.

The problems I already know about and want you to get past: the obvious ones, where he says everything exchanged must be measured by some one thing and that this thing is really need, and the line about exchange ceasing if people stopped having wants. Those read like need is doing independent measuring work, prior to money. Is there a harder case than those? In particular: is there anywhere he distinguishes the proportionate return from whatever the two parties actually agree to? If there is, I'd treat that as close to fatal and I'd rather find out now.

The worry I actually lose sleep over is vacuity. If the scale is constituted by the practice, on what grounds can any actual price be called wrong? He needs the account to have teeth — he wants disproportionate returns to be a real failing, and in the Politics he wants some ways of getting wealth to be perversions of the natural kind. If my reading turns him into "whatever it clears at is proportionate," I've handed my trader a defense that makes Aristotle a relativist, which he plainly isn't. Is there any way to get teeth back without reintroducing a mind-independent measure, or is that just the fork?

A case I made up to stress-test it: two traders from different cities with no shared political life and no common deliberation about what living well requires — say an Athenian and a Corinthian. If the needs doing the measuring are constituted by a community's shared life, my account seems to predict there's nothing for the price to be, yet nobody thinks cross-border trade is unintelligible or that its prices are nonsense. Does that kill the constitutive reading, or does it only show that the relevant community is "whoever happens to be trading" — and if it's the latter, has the reading become so elastic that it survives everything, which is its own kind of defeat?

And the meta-question, since I've noticed I can absorb nearly every counter-passage with "he's speaking loosely there": is that a real defect for a view of this kind, or is every reading of a hard philosopher like that, and it only matters if the view sticks its neck out? So tell me what my reading predicts that its rivals don't. Where should the text look different on my reading than on (a) the reading on which he's groping toward something like a labor or value-form analysis and just can't get there, and (b) the reading on which money is for him basically a useful commodity whose worth is its own use? If the honest answer is "nowhere," say so plainly.

Last thing: how much of this rides on money having to be a commodity? If it does, my reading may be fine for the fourth century and useless for anything with fiat money, which matters because my reader is going to make the modern comparison whether I invite it or not. And if you think the strongest version of my view is secretly a retread of the "he was blocked by the structure of his own society" reading, say that too — I'd rather know I've reinvented a wheel than sell it as new.
