I help run a screening-and-discussion thing at my uni (sci-fi, philosophy, whoever turns up) and for next month's session I want to do the Black Mirror Christmas special White Christmas as a case study for the moral status of digital minds. Quick recap in case it helps, though I assume you know it: the setting has copies of a person's mind — the show calls them cookies — stored in a little egg-shaped device and run as personal assistants; Jon Hamm's character used to be the guy who trains them, and he describes one, Greta, who refuses the job and then gives in once he runs six months of her subjective time past in a few seconds of his own; the Rafe Spall character turns out himself to be a copy who has just been tricked into confessing, and his punishment at the end is being left running at a thousand years per minute with a cheesy old Christmas song on repeat; Hamm's character gets a different punishment, permanent social blocking by everyone. I'd like a reading of what the episode is actually claiming about copies: where it treats a copy as a full person with interests of its own, where it treats one as a disposable tool, and whether those two moves can be made consistent with each other.

Three things I especially want you to get into, because they're the bits my group will bog down in. First, time. If a digital mind can be run faster or slower than the world it's emulating, does the moral weight of what happens to it track its own subjective duration or ours? The Greta scene is only a couple of seconds long on screen but six months of solitary refusal inside the egg, and the ending is a punishment that is, in the copy's own time, astronomically longer than any human prison sentence — so is the episode quietly committed to subjective-time accounting, and if it is, does that make ordinary carelessness about spinning up, pausing and deleting copies much worse than we instinctively feel? Second, copying. A friend of mine insists that making a thousand copies of one mind doesn't multiply the harm at all: it gives you a thousand witnesses to the same person's suffering rather than a thousand sufferers, so punishing the Joe-copy for what the real Joe did isn't obviously worse than punishing Joe himself. I think that's wrong but I cant say exactly why. Does the episode come down on one side here, and does its horror depend on having it both ways — treating the copy as Joe when we need the punishment to feel deserved, and as mere code when we need to feel OK about it? Third, engineered consent. The Greta copy is, in effect, broken until it wants the job it was built for. Is a mind that has been shaped to desire its own servitude wronged by being given it, or is objecting to that just paternalism — and what should that tell us about how to build digital minds on purpose in the first place?

What I'd end up with is something I can hand to the group: two or three scenes to point at and argue over, an honest account of where the episode is doing real philosophy and where it's cheating or trading on a confusion (I suspect the revenge structure smuggles in a view about personal identity that a Parfit-style copy theorist couldn't accept — tell me whether that's fair or whether I'm overreading it), and a line on what a story like this adds to the substrate-independence and functionalist arguments that the papers don't get to: something about dependence, about ownership, or about the sheer asymmetry of power between a mind and whoever controls the box it runs on.
