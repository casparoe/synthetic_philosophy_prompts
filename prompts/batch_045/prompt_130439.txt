hey -- I'm TAing a 200-level philosophy of law course at a big state school this fall, 26 students, one 75-minute session, and I get exactly one shot at Hart vs Devlin. My roommate (1L, very smug) said Devlin obviously just loses on the harm principle, and I realized I couldn't explain to him why anyone ever took the guy seriously, so that's kind of what I want to fix. My professor has banned me from lecturing: no opening slides defining the harm principle, no walkthrough of the Wolfenden-committee background, nothing. She wants them to hit the thing themselves before it gets named. I've got no budget, no money for payoffs, and I'm not allowed to grade it. The only stake she'll let me use: whatever side loses the final class vote has to spend three minutes at the start of next session arguing sincerely for the statute they voted against. Tiny, public, mildly humiliating. That's it.

The case I've been toying with (tell me if it's wrong and fix it): a legal, cheap, safe, fully private thing people can do -- I've been calling it the Long Quiet. You give 72 hours' notice and for a weekend you're suspended from legal personhood: no contracts bind you, no duties run (no jury service, no caregiving obligations, no taxes for those days), nobody can sue you, and because notice is given up front nobody is deceived or left stranded. Fully consensual, reversible, no direct victim. But uptake is climbing fast; the effects only show up at population scale (jury pools, emergency cover, who ends up doing the unpaid work); and surveys show that people who merely know others take Long Quiet weekends are themselves likelier to take one and less likely to volunteer for anything -- not because they're upset about it, they just quietly revise what they think everyone else is willing to do. So it isn't offense, it's something else. There's also no stable public consensus: polling is something like 40/35/25 and moves depending on how you ask. And visibility is baked in -- you basically can't prohibit it without advertising it, and any registry of participants creates its own problem.

What I want is a runnable session built on a case like that: roles and private instructions for groups of 4-5, each with a seperate evidence packet, arranged so they run into the problem before it's named. I'd especially like the no-consensus discovery to come out of polling the room itself, so they find out they *are* the fractured community that Devlin's test needs a consensus from, rather than me telling them pluralism is a problem for him. Give me a two-vote structure with a mid-session reveal that makes them catch themselves shifting or refusing to shift, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes (no lecture up front), and a final ballot preceded by a prediction step so that getting the room wrong stings a little.

Then the debrief, mapped onto specific moments rather than onto a list of positions: Devlin's disintegration / social-cohesion thesis, including the bit where he says suppressing vice is as much the law's business as suppressing subversion; Hart's charge that this conflates the morality a society actually shares with the morality it merely enforces, and his positive-vs-critical-morality move; his harm principle and where it runs out; and the space between harm and mere offense -- where exactly does the belief-mediated effect in my case land, and does it collapse Devlin's argument into Hart's or not? Please also be blunt about what will go wrong the first time: the room detouring into drugs / abortion / same-sex-marriage culture-war mode, groups just doing utilitarianism and never touching moralism, and the one student who says if there's no consensus then Devlin's own test says don't criminalize so Hart wins by default -- which I think is the most interesting moment available and I have no idea what to do with it. Finally, tell me which feature of the debate the activity genuinely cannot reproduce and what I therefore have to say out loud myself, and flag anything in Devlin's actual position you're not confident about so I don't assert it from memory in front of 26 people.
