hey — I'm doing a seminar paper on punishment and free speech and I cant work out what the practice on my own campus actually assumes about punishment. The pattern: hundreds of people sign an open letter, the organisers disinvite the speaker or the publisher drops the book, and everyone insists this isn't punishment at all but just other people exercising their own speech and their freedom of association — the consequences-not-censorship line. But the same letter talks about holding the person accountable and sending a message, the sanction costs them income and career, people who merely shared a panel with them sometimes get dropped too, and there's no hearing, no procedure, and nobody who decides when it's over or whether an apology was enough. Meanwhile my uni wouldn't fire a tenured professor over a tweet without a formal process and a burden of proof, and the people organising these things are also the loudest critics of prison.

So what view of punishment does this presuppose, if any? I've been calling the core puzzle sanction without a sentencer: a penalty nobody has the authority to impose, calibrate or lift, made up of hundreds of separate withdrawals that no one can reverse even if most signatories change their minds. Does the practice only make sense if being platformed or published is a privilege rather than something you're owed, so withdrawing it isn't a harm at all? Does it need an expressive or communicative theory where the point is denunciation and the cost is incidental — and can that survive without anyone authorised to declare the debt paid? Is 'answerability rather than punishment' a real distinction here or a slogan? Which parts are just underdetermined, so that several theories license them equally? And if the implicit theory were right, what would organisers have to change: publish every signatory, drop the it's-not-punishment framing, set an end date, stop sanctioning third parties?
