Consider, as a candidate escape from the repugnant conclusion, "saturating" population axiologies of the form V(P) = f(mean welfare) × g(population size), where f and g are increasing, f is continuous with f(0) = 0, and g is bounded above: extra people always help, but with steeply diminishing marginal value, so a population's total value has a finite ceiling.

Boundedness appears to block the repugnant conclusion in its strong form — for any number n of lives at some very high welfare W, and any ε > 0, however tiny, there is an N large enough that N lives at ε are better — since f(ε)·sup g falls below f(W)·g(n) once ε is small enough, so no N rescues the comparison. But I suspect the escape is illusory: requiring that adding a life worth living never makes an outcome worse seems to force g to grow at least like some fixed positive power of N, because the mean-welfare factor drops by a factor of order 1/N whenever you add someone below the current average and g must compensate at every N; and once g is unbounded, the repugnant conclusion returns for every ε > 0.

Give me your credence that some view in this family meets both constraints, decomposed: (i) that the no-worsening constraint really does force unbounded g given only that f is increasing with f(0) = 0 — does the argument survive if f is extremely flat near zero, or if f saturates at high welfare?; (ii) that bounded-but-increasing g is genuinely sufficient to block the strong form as I have stated it; (iii) that the multiplicative-separable form is where the difficulty lives, or whether relaxing separability changes the answer; (iv) how much of the question's force rests on the repugnant conclusion being genuinely unacceptable rather than merely surprising, and on no-worsening holding for lives that are only barely worth living. A number and one line of justification for each, then the combination, then the term you are least confident about and what would move it most.
