Hi — I co-teach a third-year philosophy of mind course with a neuroscientist, and we keep talking past each other about computationalism. His is the modern version of the claim: the brain is a computer, and now that we actually build artificial neural networks the analogy has finally cashed out — neurons are units, synapses are weights, development and learning are just training, so the old worries about rules, symbols and representations were artifacts of the symbolic era. He uses it to license something fairly strong in front of the students: that there is no in-principle obstacle left to a machine having a mind, only engineering ones, and that asking "yes, but is it really computing, or really understanding?" is on a par with asking whether a simulated storm is really wet. I want to push back but I cant put my finger on where the analogy is carrying the weight.

A second move he leans on: since computation is just whatever the physical hardware is doing, the brain trivially computes, so the theory can't even be empirically wrong — which feels like cheating to me, and half of it sounds like its own conclusion, but I don't know how to say why without sounding like a mysterian.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Tell me what plays what role — the programme, the hardware, the input, the algorithm, the programmer, and what plays the role of the thing being computed — then where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he is drawing and which are pedantry. And tell me whether the strong claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether the simulated-storm move is doing any independent work or is just the analogy again, and whether there's a better comparison available that would still do the modest work he really needs it for.

Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Compress the correspondences into a sentence rather than laying them out as a table, if you can.
