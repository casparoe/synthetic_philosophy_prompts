I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend — an economist who dismisses governance by prediction markets as, in his words, a focus group with odds — about whether futarchy deserves a real municipal pilot, and the argument ended somewhere more specific than either of us expected. His two objections were the familiar ones: that it collapses into cost-benefit analysis with extra steps, and that it lets whoever has the deepest pockets set policy. My reply was that both miss the separation at the heart of Hanson's original framing: a citizens' assembly fixes the welfare metric, and then separate conditional markets on each candidate policy aggregate dispersed information, with only the branch that actually gets implemented settling in money — so a trader is paid for being right about consequences, not for having preferences. He conceded the point and then went for the sabotage objection instead: if I hold a large position saying the light-rail line will make the life-satisfaction index fall, I can earn a second and larger profit by making sure the line fails.

The version we ended up arguing about, and the one I want the joke set in, is a bit more baroque than the textbook case. Our hypothetical city charter has the treasury acting as market maker of last resort with a capped annual loss, and — this was my addition, meant to answer the sabotage objection — a paired shadow market on what would have happened under the policy that lost, so that anyone profiting from a bad outcome has to be right about the counterfactual too. My friend's gleeful reply was that the shadow market can never be resolved, because the rejected policy is never implemented and so the counterfactual is never observed; the charter, as drafted, has it settling on a panel of experts' retrospective judgement of what would have happened.

So: I'm opening a short panel on this next month in front of a room of mechanism designers and prediction-market people, and I want one joke — a setup and punchline, or a brief exchange, set in that city, ideally at the first audit of the shadow market. The punchline should turn on something that is actually true of the setup and not of voting or polling generally: the unresolvable counterfactual, the conditional payoff structure, the fact that the government both subsidises the market and is bound by it, the incentive to bring about the outcome you forecast, or the way that choosing a metric quietly settles the values question the mechanism was supposed to leave to the voters. My own attempt — a council that shorts its own light-rail line and then wonders why construction keeps mysteriously stalling — got one of those pitying groans, and on reflection I think it fails because you only need to grasp that sabotage is possible, not anything about how the mechanism works. After the joke, add a sentence or two on which feature you're exploiting and why the joke couldn't be retold about ordinary elections or opinion polls.
