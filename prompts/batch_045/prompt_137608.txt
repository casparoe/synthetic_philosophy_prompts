I want an argumentative essay on anthropic updating, not a survey of the literature. Set up three candidate rules as follows: for a hypothesis H with prior P(H), n(H) observers in total, of whom m(H) have evidence e, the self-sampling rule ranks hypotheses by P(H) times m(H)/n(H), the self-indication rule by P(H) times m(H), and the existence-only rule by P(H) alone whenever m(H) is at least one, discarding H when m(H) is zero.

Notice that the self-indication rule's bias toward observer-rich hypotheses — the presumptuous philosopher's armchair verdict in cosmology, where a theory positing vastly more observers is favoured in proportion to their number — comes entirely from how many observers are counted as sharing your evidence, and so shifts with the grain at which e is described: it vanishes if e is your total evidence and no two observers have exactly the same total evidence, and it is greatest if e is left as coarse as the bare fact that you are an observer.

Argue whether this grain-dependence is a decisive objection to self-indication and to the verdicts derived from it, or only an apparent one, because there is some privileged grain at which evidence must be described or because the counting should be done differently. If you judge it fatal, defend one alternative: the self-sampling rule (and say whether the doomsday argument against it, from our early birth rank to an imminent end, is really as damaging as the presumptuous philosopher is for self-indication, or whether the two embarrassments are symmetric and simply cancel), the existence-only rule, or some intermediate rule that counts observers with an exponent between zero and one. Say also whether it matters to your verdict that, in the cosmological case, the extra observers lie forever beyond our Hubble volume and can never be observed or interacted with. Take a definite stand and defend it rather than closing on a balanced demurral, and write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
