I host a small reading group — six of us, retired mostly, we meet in the back room of a pub — and we've spent this term picking at chance: coin biases, the Sleeping Beauty business, that business about a family with two children where at least one is a boy. I'm the one who closes each session with a short poem, and for the last meeting I want one about the chord puzzle: you draw a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle in it, and ask how often a chord picked "at random" comes out longer than the triangle's side. I used to work as a land surveyor, so the geometry I can follow; what loses me is that the answer depends on what exactly you're randomising. Pick the chord's two endpoints independently on the circumference and you get a third. Pick the chord by choosing a distance from the centre along a single fixed radius and drawing the perpendicular, and you get a half. Pick the midpoint uniformly anywhere inside the disc, and you get a quarter. Someone in the group keeps saying the real target is the "principle of indifference" — that if you have no reason to favour one case over another you should weight them equally — but my difficulty is that all three procedures look equally indifferent to me, each one spreading its own quantity out flat. (I've also half-remembered something from an essay we didn't read: a demand that the answer stay the same if the circle is redrawn bigger, or smaller, or shoved to another part of the page, and that only one of the three survives that demand — the one where the chord's distance from the centre is spread evenly, I think — though apparently people argue about whether that demand isn't just another assumption smuggled in. If I've got that wrong, don't repeat my mistake.)

What I'd like is a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, with six end-words, each stanza recycling the previous stanza's endings in the standard order 6-1-5-2-4-3, then a three-line envoi carrying all six words, two to a line. The end-words I have in mind are: circle, chord, random, third, half, quarter. Three of the stanzas should each stage one of the three procedures — endpoints on the circumference, distance along a radius, midpoint anywhere in the disc — and each of those stanzas must close on the number its procedure actually yields, which means you'll need to choose the order in which the six words first appear so the rotation puts each number at the end of the stanza that earned it. The other three stanzas can do what you like with what's left over: the surveyor's business of actually setting out a line, the thought that "random" was never a property of the chord, whatever. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the puzzle can name which procedure each of those three stanzas is staging without being told, and that a careful reader could reconstruct all three derivations from the poem alone — but it should still be a poem we can read aloud, not a textbook in verse. Please don't reach for the chord/cord pun; we've already sat through it once.

One last thing about the ending. I don't want a poem that crowns one of the three answers, and I don't want one that treats the disagreement as a cheap paradox either. If the invariance thought appears, let it appear as a demand a person might reasonably refuse, not as a proof. What I'd like the last line to land is that "at random" was never describing the chord at all, only the throwing — that the choice of what to spread out evenly is a decision made before the mathematics starts, and that the three numbers are all honest answers to three different questions. No prefatory note and no footnotes: the poem should stand on its own when I read it out.
