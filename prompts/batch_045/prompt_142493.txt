I do volunteer research for a small grantmaking group that funds work on wild animal welfare, and a board member recently asked us for a bottom line on a question I think we have been handling badly: whether habitat destruction is, on net, good or bad for the animals living there. I don't want the general version of that argument, which I've read many times and can recite; I want a number on a specific case. Take a 100-hectare tract of mature temperate broadleaf forest in the eastern United States currently under a conservation easement, and suppose the easement is lifted and the tract is converted to low-density suburban housing with lawns, ornamental plantings, roads, and the usual edge habitat. Fix the counterfactual as: the tract remains intact forest for the next hundred years. Compare the two histories with respect to the total suffering experienced by sentient animals on that tract and within a few kilometres of its boundary over those hundred years, including the construction period and the subsequent steady state. Ignore human welfare, climate feedbacks, and effects on ecosystems elsewhere; hold the axiology fixed as asymmetric and suffering-focused unless told otherwise. My own credence that the conversion reduces net suffering is about 0.6, and a colleague whose judgement I trust puts it near 0.3 — but when we tried to locate the disagreement we found we were weighting completely different considerations, so the gap between our numbers is not evidence that either of us has thought about this well.

So: give me your number, but the number is worth little without the decomposition, and I want the terms cut roughly as follows (re-cut them if you think this framing is wrong, and say why). First, the substitution problem: does conversion actually reduce the head count of morally relevant animals, or does it mostly swap one assemblage for another? Suburbia is not a dead zone — it is good habitat for generalists and commensals, rodents and pigeons and starlings and ants and raccoons — and if the total count barely moves, the entire question collapses into a comparison of per-capita welfare between two species assemblages rather than a comparison between many animals and few. Second, the shift in life-history composition: r-selected species produce enormous numbers of juveniles most of whom die young, so even at roughly constant adult head count the number of lives begun and ended per year may rise sharply under conversion, and whether that increases suffering depends on how much suffering a life of a few days contains, which I am genuinely unsure about. Third, the term I think is most underrated, call it death-mode substitution: in intact forest much juvenile mortality presumably comes from starvation, exposure, and disease, which are slow, whereas an edge-dominated landscape with high predator and scavenger density may shift mortality toward predation and acute injury, which are fast. If the suffering per death from a slow death exceeds that from a fast one by enough, a landscape that kills fewer animals more slowly can be worse than one that kills more animals quickly, and I want a separate sub-probability for the direction of that shift, not just for the head-count change. Fourth, spillover onto the surviving fragment: fragmentation raises nest predation and brood parasitism in what remains, and my tract is small enough that edge effects may dominate the interior. Fifth, timing: the counterfactual is not preservation forever, so I also want the number for the claim that earlier conversion is worse than later conversion, conditional on the tract being developed at some point in the next two centuries. Keep invertebrate sentience and moral weight as one explicitly separated term with its own probability, so I can substitute my own values there.

Please combine the terms into an overall credence, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and name the one field measurement — pitfall traps, camera traps, nest monitoring, whatever, something a field team could actually run — that would move your number the most if it came back the other way. Report the final figure twice: once under the asymmetric suffering-focused axiology I stipulated, and once under symmetric totalism, so I can see how much of the gap between me and my colleague is really ecological rather than axiological. And if you think the proposition as I have stated it is not well-posed, say what the minimal re-framing is that would make it well-posed and give me the number for that instead, alongside the original. I don't need any citations, references, or reading list — I just want the reasoning laid open with its weak points marked.
