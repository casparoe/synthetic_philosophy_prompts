I'm a journalist — I mostly cover AI and tech policy, and every couple of months I end up writing some version of "who's to blame when the machine does it." I've got a panel thing next week with an ethicist and a roboticist, and I want one concrete scenario I can put to them and then reuse in the piece. Not a literature review, not a reading list: one case, built to order.

Here's the spec. Build me a case in which putting a human in the loop makes the responsibility gap *worse* — i.e. the opposite of the usual "meaningful human control closes the gap" story. Constraints:

- Plausible and near-future. A hospital ward, a warehouse floor, an air-traffic desk — pick one and stay inside it. One paragraph.
- No rogue AI, no sci-fi, no cartoonishly greedy vendor. The system can be a machine-learning one whose particular failure isn't traceable to a bad line of code, but it can't be magic.
- The human supervisor has to be doing her job properly: trained, awake, attentive, following protocol, no shortcut, no laziness, no ignoring of alarms. If the gap only shows up because I made her negligent, that's boring and it wont survive a hostile expert.
- The gap has to open *because* she's there. Her presence is what lets the manufacturer say a qualified person was supervising, while her actual control over the outcome is too thin for the outcome to be hers.

Then do the work. Tell me where responsibility is supposed to land on each of three candidate homes — the operator (control / quality-of-will style), the manufacturer or deployer (foreseeability / product-liability style), and the institution that chose to run the system (risk-created-by-your-own-choice style) — and why each one fails to take it here. Name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, then remove it (take the human out entirely, or give her a real veto she actually has time to use) and tell me whether the gap closes, gets worse, or just collapses into ordinary product liability.

Finally: give me the strongest version of the "there is no gap, someone is always responsible" objection, and be straight with me about whether it's an answer or a change of subject. I definitly have a suspicion that pointing at the hospital just relabels a liability-allocation question as a responsibility question — but if that's the honest answer, say so, and say what it costs. Same for the other cheap moves (she chose the job, the hospital should have known): flag which ones are dodges. And tell me plainly if your case only holds together because you stipulated that everyone followed the rules — I'd rather know that before I say it out loud on a stage.
