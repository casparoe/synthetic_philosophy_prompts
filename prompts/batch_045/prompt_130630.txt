I've been poking at a variant of Nozick's experience machine and I want a number out of you, not a tour of the literature.

Setup: you've already lived an ordinary real life to age 70 — job, family, failures, the usual. You're now offered ten years hooked up to a machine. Two twists: inside, you know the whole time that it's a machine and the simulation never hides that from you; and the ten years are as pleasant, as varied and as absorbing as you like, with no boredom and no fade. When it ends you die without learning anything more about the outside world.

The proposition: for that person, ten years in the machine is exactly as good for them as ten equally pleasant, equally varied real years would have been — same welfare, no residue. My credence that this is true is around 0.55. My partner's is about 0.9, and she thinks I've loaded the case until the real objection falls out of it.

Give me your own figure, but I mostly want the decomposition. Assign rough probabilities to: well-being being exhausted by mental states; contact with reality having value that isn't merely instrumental to mental states; knowing the good stuff is simulated necessarily thinning or spoiling it from the inside; achievements and relationships needing real causal footing to contribute what they seem to contribute; and the already-had-a-real-life bit doing any independent work at all. Say how you're combining them, since they aren't independent, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to — the one where moving it 0.2 moves the bottom line most. Then: which of the two of us is more likely wrong about, and what would actually shift your number? Also flag any sub-question I left out that belongs in there.
