You are a philosopher of law and political theory who specializes in precommitment, self-binding, and the problem of past majorities binding future ones. Please reason carefully before you write: first sketch, for your own use, the strongest case on each side of the question, and only then draft the document I describe. I would rather receive a memo that has genuinely engaged the best objection than one that merely reads well.

I am a lay member of my city's charter review commission. I work in insurance, I have no academic background, and I have been reading on my own about Ulysses contracts since this came up. Next month we vote on a proposed charter amendment with two parts: (1) any increase in the property-tax levy above the rate of inflation would require a two-thirds council vote plus a confirming referendum, and (2) the amendment itself could not be repealed or weakened for fifteen years, and thereafter only by a two-thirds vote. Our city attorney has confirmed that the entrenchment is permissible under state law, so the question before us is not legal but normative.

Proponents say the point is credibility: the council cannot today credibly promise restraint, because every future council will face concentrated demands to spend and diffuse pressure to save, and lenders price that in. Opponents, including a citizens' group that has petitioned us, reply that a fifteen-year entrenchment binds a largely different electorate: by the time it expires many current voters will have moved away or died and new residents will have arrived. So this is not a person binding their own future self, which is what a Ulysses contract is supposed to be, but one set of voters binding their successors, which looks like the very thing the Ulysses case was meant to be a narrow exception to.

I have floated a compromise that nobody will give me a straight answer about and that I cannot work out myself: a depreciation clause. The supermajority requirement stays in force but lapses automatically after five years unless the then-sitting council re-enacts it by an ordinary majority, with each re-enactment starting a new five-year clock, and the fifteen-year entrenchment is cut to ten. Does a precommitment that must be periodically reaffirmed still do the work precommitment is supposed to do, or does it collapse into a default that a determined majority simply walks away from? And if it does collapse, does that tell us something about whether the original amendment was legitimate in the first place?

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the commission, roughly 800 to 1,000 words. It should open with a concrete recommendation, adopt, reject, or adopt in some modified form such as mine, then give the reasoning, including a fair statement of the strongest argument for whichever course you do not recommend. Along the way it should say plainly what you take to be the morally relevant difference, if any, between binding your own future self and binding someone else, and how that bears on the compromise above.
