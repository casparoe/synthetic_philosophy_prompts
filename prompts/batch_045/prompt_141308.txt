hey — practical problem that turned into a conceptual one. I'm building a dataset where every party that cleared 2% in about 45 countries, 1945 to now, has to get coded onto a left–right scale (or flagged as uncodable), and before I freeze the codebook I want the spec instead of another round of people arguing about who is really on which side. So: what does an account of the left–right distinction have to deliver to be good enough for that job?

Ground rules: no reading lists, no citations, no "so-and-so argues" — I can't look anything up and I don't need the bibliography. I want the requirements, each one with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and I'll hand you the candidate accounts at the bottom so the scoring is easy.

The cases I keep getting stuck on (names changed, they're invented composites, don't try to place them):

- A party that wants to break up the big tech firms and massively expand public healthcare, is tightly tied to the unions, and also wants a hard line on immigration, a national curriculum with mandatory religious instruction, and much less power for the central government.
- A protest movement of small farmers and anti-corruption types that insists it is "neither left nor right, just forward," and that every single commentator immediately places on one side anyway.
- A post-Soviet case where the party everyone locally calls left is socially conservative, nationalist and fiscally austere, while the party called right is culturally liberal and wants to keep the pension system exactly as it is.
- A party whose manifesto is basically unchanged for thirty years but which slides from far left to center-left because the whole country moved around it.

Things I want the list to settle. Does the theory have to supply a fixed substantive content, or is it allowed to be relational — roughly, left = the side pressing against the local status quo, or the side of the people who are outside — and if it goes relational, what do I lose (cross-time and cross-country comparability?) and what does it buy me on the cases above. Which distinctions it is not allowed to collapse: the label versus the bundle of policies that happens to travel with it; left/right versus authoritarian/libertarian, if that really is a separate axis; which side a party is on versus how far out it is; self-description versus coded position; the axis as it shows up in ordinary people placing themselves on it versus in what parties actually do in parliament. Whether the theory has to explain the thing people call the horseshoe — the extremes resembling each other — or is permitted to say that resemblance is an artifact of something else. Whether it has to predict anything, and what: coalition formation? which policies get enacted? who ends up hating whom?

Also: what would have to be true for us to conclude the distinction has broken down and I should just drop the scale from the codebook, and what kind of evidence would settle that question. Whether "two undergrads who know nothing about the country can apply it and agree" is a genuine requirement or a methodological contamination of the concept. Whether even-handedness is a real desideratum or a prejudice of people who want to sound respectable — I mean, does a decent theory have to avoid making one side look irrational or straightforwardly more wrong about the facts, or can it say the axis tracks something like willingness to override inherited arrangements, which might leave one side better calibrated on some questions. Mark each requirement non-negotiable-for-my-purpose or camp preference. Tell me which pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most accounts people actually hold.

The candidates floating around my head, score them in a few lines each at the end if you feel like it: (1) it's basically about equality — the left wants fewer or more-justified inequalities, the right accepts or defends some; (2) it's about inherited institutions and tradition versus emancipation from them; (3) it's about how wide the circle of "us" is; (4) purely positional — left is just whichever faction is pushing in the direction local reformers have been pushing; (5) it's two dimensions, economic and cultural, that correlate often enough that everyone mistakes them for one. The list is what I need though, not the scorecard.
