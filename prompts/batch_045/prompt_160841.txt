I'm in this epistemology of markets seminar (joint econ/phil, proof-heavy, im slightly drowning) and two people in my study group got into it after Tuesday's class and dragged me in as referee. I kept flip-flopping and now I genuinely can't tell who's winning. The setup: we'd just gone through the no-trade theorem — roughly, if traders share a common prior and it's common knowledge that both sides are willing to trade, then the trade doesn't happen, so any trade that actually occurs has to be explained by something other than disagreement about the value of the thing (liquidity needs, rebalancing, hedging some outside exposure). Then it escalated.

(1) Mira: this isn't a technicality, it's the whole ballgame for the epistemic story. If order flow is all liquidity and hedging, then trades carry no news, and the Hayek-ish idea that prices embody dispersed knowledge needs exactly the thing the theorem rules out — disagreement about fundamentals that survives being common knowledge. So markets are allocation devices, not truth-tracking devices, and "the market thinks X" is at best shorthand for "a weighted average of other people's hedging needs happens to come out at X."

(2) Joel: you've got it backwards. If literally nobody ever traded on private information, there'd be no channel by which prices could come to reflect dispersed information in the first place. You're using the theorem to explain aggregation while denying the mechanism. And the assumptions are load-bearing and mostly false — real people don't share a common prior, they don't run identical models; with genuinely different priors, information-driven trade is everywhere. It's a knife-edge result and you're reading it as an empirical claim about volume.

(3) Mira: "different priors" isn't a free lunch. Let priors come apart and you can explain any trade pattern you like, so your story has no content — and worse, "aggregation" loses its benchmark, because there's no longer a fact of the matter about whose information is being pooled into the price; you just get a weighted opinion pool with the weights picked by whoever writes the model. Also the assumption isn't doing the work empirically: the huge volumes are between institutions running effectively the same model off the same data feed, which is as close to a common prior as the real world gets.

(4) Joel: fine, but you're treating the conditions as all-or-nothing. The theorem also needs common knowledge of the willingness to trade, and that's an infinite hierarchy — fragile to any sliver of doubt. And more importantly: even granting that a lot of trade is liquidity-driven, that doesn't make prices uninformative. Noise trade is exactly what lets informed traders hide their orders in the flow instead of being killed by adverse selection. Non-informational motives are the camouflage that makes informational trade survivable. The theorem says nothing about how much trade occurs, or in what mix, once its conditions fail slightly. You're converting a nonexistence result into a claim about what all observed volume is.

(5) Mira: but then informed traders have to be a minority hiding in the noise, so the price is at best partially revealing — and what it expresses is a mash of first-order information plus higher-order guesswork about who else is informed and how much they're trading. You owe me a reason to trust that number for any epistemic purpose. And information is costly to produce while nobody captures the full value of what their own trade gives away, so everyone free-rides — that's the paradox where fully revealing prices destroy the incentive to gather the information (the Grossman–Stigglitz thing, AER 1980). The price can't be very informative if no one is paying to make it so.

(6) Joel: the standard answer to the paradox is that noise keeps some rent for information producers, so the equilibrium is partially, not fully, revealing. Partial revelation is still revelation. You need prices to carry zero information; I only need them to carry some non-zero amount, and I don't have to say in advance how much.

(6b) Mira: the "how much" is the entire question. If an outside observer can't tell what share of a price move is information and what share is hedging pressure, the price isn't usable as evidence — and usable-as-evidence is the only thing this course cares about. "Partially informative but uncalibratable from outside" isn't halfway to your story, it's epistemically inert.

Two more things. In the last five minutes of lecture our prof floated an idea neither of them used: that you can tell the two kinds of trade apart by their counterfactual sensitivity — an order whose size and direction would have been the same had the trader's own signal come out differently is liquidity, one that would have flipped is information — so the decomposition Joel needs is in principle measurable. And she hand-waved something I only half caught: that if traders have set-valued beliefs (Knightian uncertainty, worst-case evaluation) then two of them can share the same belief set, have the same attitude to risk, and still trade, because each one's worst case gets picked relative to their own background exposure, so their effective priors can differ without anybody "disagreeing" in Joel's sense. She said this breaks the no-trade conclusion without needing different priors at all. I have no idea if that's right.
