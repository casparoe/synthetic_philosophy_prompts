You are a moral philosopher who works on aggregative ethics, population axiology, and the limits of interpersonal comparison. You are known for caring less about whether a view feels right than about what it costs you elsewhere in your theory. Before you commit to any numbers, spend a few paragraphs mapping the logical space: state which premises jointly generate the puzzle, and note for each of the items below exactly which premise it denies. Only then give your ratings.

The case, as it is standardly posed: you must choose between (i) one person being severely tortured for fifty years and (ii) an astronomically large number of people — take it to be 3↑↑↑3, or simply any number large enough that no finite sequence of tenfold increases reaches it — each suffering a dust speck in the eye, a flicker of irritation lasting about a second. Nearly everyone's instinct favours (ii). But there is also a powerful pull toward the thought that, for any number of speck-sufferers, some larger number of them would be worse than the torture. And one can walk from torture to a vast crowd of trivial irritations by a long staircase of small steps, each of which trades a tenfold reduction in severity for a tenfold increase in the number of people affected. If each step is a genuine improvement and better than is transitive, the crowd of specks comes out worse than the torture. Something in that package has to go.

Below are nine exits — ways of blocking the argument. Rate each one.

1. The Null-Harm exit: a one-second speck is not a harm at all, or its disvalue is exactly zero, so there is nothing to sum.
2. The Sub-Threshold exit: discomforts that never reach conscious attention do not aggregate into anything anyone undergoes, no matter how many there are.
3. The Lexical Threshold exit: there is a level of severity such that no number of lesser harms, however many, outweighs a single harm above it.
4. The Anti-Fractionation exit: reject Fractionation Neutrality, the principle that a fixed quantity of suffering keeps its moral badness when it is divided into smaller parcels and spread over more lives. Instead, badness is a convex function of how concentrated the suffering is.
5. The Life-Shape exit: what matters is not the total quantity of suffering but what becomes of a life; the gulf between a ruined life and an untouched one is not something you can build by stacking trivial interruptions.
6. The Individualist Restriction exit: interpersonal aggregation is legitimate only among complaints of comparable kind and urgency; a person's complaint against being tortured cannot be outvoted by an enormous number of trivial complaints.
7. The Bounded Aggregate exit: each additional trivial harm counts for less than the one before, with weights falling fast enough that the total converges to a finite ceiling below the badness of the torture.
8. The Non-Transitivity exit: worse than is not transitive across this series; no individual step is mistaken, but there is no consistent ordering and no best option.
9. The Bullet-Biting exit: the specks really are worse, and the contrary instinct is scope neglect or a failure of imagination rather than a moral datum.

Two stress tests you must apply before scoring. First, the itch variant: run the whole argument again with the dust speck replaced by a mildly annoying itch lasting ten seconds — something the subject definitely notices and mildly dislikes. Which exits were secretly relying on the tiny harm being imperceptible? Second, the arbitrariness constraint: any exit that says the staircase of comparisons breaks at some step must say which step, and the location must be principled rather than merely convenient, given that the argument for a break does not itself seem to select one.

Sort the nine exits into four tiers — those you would defend in print, those that are serious but carry a cost you would have to absorb elsewhere, those that are coherent but would need much more work before you relied on them, and those you think fail — and give each exit a credibility score from 0 to 100. Add one or two sentences of justification per exit, in prose rather than a bullet dump, though the tiers and the numbers must be explicit and easy to lift out of your answer.

Close with two short sections. In the first, identify the hinge: the single disagreement that, once settled, does most of the work in determining the rest of your ranking, and say why it rather than another. In the second, say what would change your mind: name the one argument, variant case, or empirical finding that would most move your top tier, and locate the point where your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong. Be explicit about how confident you are, and do not hedge the numbers into uselessness.
