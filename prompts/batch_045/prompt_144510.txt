I'm a novelist. In a scene I'm drafting, a letterpress printer defends a position on the old question of the one over the many: he says a universal is like the sort in his type case — one piece of type, many strikes. The "e" is not used up by printing, not divided among the copies, and wholly present in each of them, and yet there is nothing to it beyond the lead you can pick up, drop, or melt down. He is using it to steer between two views he calls mad: that redness is a further object existing apart from red things, and that "red" is only a sound we make at many different things. His interlocutor, a bookbinder, says the analogy is a trick — the sort is just one more particular, so the printer has conceded nominalism and then pointed at a lump of lead.

I want the scene to be fair to both of them, so: audit the analogy. Give me what maps onto what, where the mapping fails, and especially whether it helps itself to the very thing it was meant to explain — the repeatability of the letter's shape looks like exactly the kind of entity in dispute. Does the printer's middle way survive once that is on the table, or does it collapse toward one of the two extremes he was avoiding? If a different analogy would carry his point better, say which. Finally, note where your audit is least secure: which feature of the printing case, if he could establish it, would let the analogy do the work he wants?
