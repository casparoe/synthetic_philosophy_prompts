I chair the compensation and recognition committee at a 450-person health system. Four times a year we make roughly forty separate allocation decisions: individual bonuses drawn from a pool whose total the board fixes in advance, promotions onto two higher grade levels with a capped number of slots, and a public quarterly award for living the organisation's values, announced to everyone. The committee is five people — me, two clinical directors, an HR partner, and a rotating staff representative — none with any training in philosophy, and every decision has to be defensible in a one-paragraph written statement addressed to the person who did not get it, because our union agreement gives them a right to appeal. I have about twenty minutes per case and two hours of committee time per cycle, so whatever I adopt has to be runnable by non-specialists, on a form, without supervision.

What I need is a written decision procedure, not a discussion of the theory. For any given candidate and any given allocation, it should determine how far that allocation should track what the person actually deserves, as distinct from three things I keep watching get conflated with it: what the person is entitled to under our existing rules (the bonus plan says the top-rated person takes the top band, whether or not they deserve it); what would produce good work next quarter; and what it would be fitting or gracious for the organisation to say about them in public. Where one of those three legitimately overrides desert, the procedure should say so explicitly and record that it did.

The procedure has to handle, at minimum: the way credit for a good outcome drains away once you notice how much of it traces to circumstance rather than choice — and the fact that pushing that inquiry far enough dissolves effort itself, so I want a stated stopping rule rather than an infinite regress, recorded as a stipulation and not as a discovery; the fact that a claim of the form "she deserves it" seems incomplete until you write in whom she is being compared to and against which baseline, since the same person can deserve the slot relative to the requirements of the role and not relative to peers who started from less; the possibility that our inputs — performance ratings, credentials, who got the good project, who was mentored, who was allowed to fail safely — are themselves the residue of earlier allocations this same organisation made, so that we would be laundering our own past decisions into present desert; the fact that desert may support only a rough tiering and not a total ordering, so I want a rule against manufacturing a ranking where the material supports a tie; the requirement that the justification be stateable to the loser without asserting anything about their character or about the deep causes of their effort; the expressive cost of publicly attributing someone's success to advantage or luck even when the allocation is unchanged; whether a bad quarter generates a claim to less than the standard increment or merely the absence of a claim to more, and why the difference matters; and whether a desert claim can be discharged or stale once it has already been recognised, so that honouring the same act twice is not double-counting. It must also be able to say that a case is not a desert question at all and hand it upward as a structural one — about pay bands, slot caps, or how work gets assigned. Every handoff should name who signs off: me, the HR partner for anything with legal exposure, or the board where the fix is structural.

Please work it through on three of our cases. First: two engineers in the same grade. Priya was handed the payments platform in January by her director and by September it is the fastest-growing line in the department, and her numbers are the best in it. Marcus spent the year keeping the legacy scheduling system alive — no visible wins, but three migrations that would have gone badly if he had not caught what he caught. Both are rated the same on paper; there is one top bonus in the group. Second: two analysts of comparable talent; one put in roughly seventy-hour weeks for two quarters and produced the model that fixed our staffing forecast, the other, with two small children and the caring duties that go with them, worked standard hours and produced good but unspectacular work. Rewarding the first is what the desert reasoning seems to point to, but the hours were available to her as a matter of circumstance, rewarding them sets an incentive the organisation does not want, and we cannot say any of this to her in public. Third: a nurse manager of twenty-two years who held a failing unit together through a genuinely bad year, now performing adequately but not exceptionally while managing a chronic illness; the committee wants to give her the public values award. I expect to dislike whatever the procedure says about the third case. Give it anyway, and tell me which of the three answers the committee will find unwelcome and why it is still the right one.

Assume nothing about what I have read. Define any term you use in ordinary language, and please give no citations, references or suggested reading — I have no way to follow them up.

Structure the response in these sections, in this order:

- Summary — about 100 words: the procedure compressed into one paragraph, plus one sentence on what it deliberately refuses to decide.
- The distinctions that have to survive — about 200 words: the minimum set of contrasts a committee member must be able to hold, each defined in plain words of one sentence.
- The procedure — about 700 words: the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates with a no, where it hands the decision to a named person, and the stipulations that must be written down so that next cycle is consistent with this one. Close this section with a one-page version laid out as lines on a form that the HR partner could drop into our workflow.
- Three worked applications — about 700 words: the cases above, run in order, with the form filled in for each.
- Where it misfires — about 300 words: the failure modes, the points where judgment is dressed up as procedure, and the questions the procedure cannot settle at case level at all.
