I teach a small final-year seminar on mind and moral status, and I'm putting together the take-home exam (48 hours, 1,200 words max, marked out of 20). I want one question on the scale argument for insect suffering that can't be answered by writing 'huge numbers × tiny probability of sentience = large expected disvalue' and stopping there — they can all definately do that in their sleep, and last year's answers were basically the same paragraph with different numbers in it. A friend who works on insect protein farming got me thinking about it: her side of the industry keeps getting asked whether the animals they kill each year matter at all, the honest answer seems to be that nobody really knows, and 'nobody knows' isn't a policy. So I'd like the question to force them to say what actually follows from not knowing, instead of letting uncertainty quietly do the work of the status quo.

What I have in mind is giving them a short fictional policy memo and asking them to audit it. Here's my rough draft (the numbers are deliberately round and illustrative — I don't want anyone arguing about empirical estimates, and I'll say so in the question):

MEMO — Interspecies Welfare Directorate, Advisory Standard 7(b)
(i) For regulatory cost–benefit analysis, a taxon's expected welfare cost = (estimated number of individuals affected) × (probability that members of that taxon are sentient) × (average intensity of a pain-like state, scaled to the human case) × (duration).
(ii) Where the probability in (i) is assessed at less than 0.1, the taxon is assigned a welfare weight of zero and excluded from analysis, on the grounds that acting on probabilities that low would be speculative and would let remoter and remoter possibilities crowd out everything else on the agenda.
(iii) Assessments under (i) are made taxon by taxon; the probabilities assigned to distinct taxa are treated as independent.

Then something like: audit Standard 7(b). Assume, for the sake of the question, that the probability an individual insect is sentient is around 0.05, that the number affected by the policy under review is very large (say 10^13 a year), and that we have no direct evidence either way about the intensity or duration of insect affective states. Does the Standard give the right verdicts, and if not, where exactly does it go wrong?

The cruxes I'd want a top answer to get at, without me handing them over in the question itself: the step from a tiny per-individual probability to a large total isn't the interesting part — the interesting part is the gap between an expectation and a distribution. Clause (iii) treats sentience as independent across individuals when it's really more like an all-or-nothing property of a whole lineage (if one cricket is sentient, a trillion are), and once you see that, the expected total is the same number but it's bimodal: either nothing, or something that dwarfs everything else on the agenda, which is a different decision problem and is what clause (ii) is silently reacting to. Also worth testing: whether clause (ii)'s threshold is arbitrary in a way that matters (0.09 becomes nothing, 0.11 becomes full weight), whether the 'speculative' rationale is symmetric (it should also block the conclusion that current practice is fine), whether the unit in clause (i) is even right given that we don't know if an insect's life is on balance good or bad for the insect, and whether there's an argument for spending a little now on finding out, rather than either acting or not acting. Could you write me the finished question (polish the memo and framing however you like), a model answer of about 900–1,000 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong? British English throughout please — I'm in the UK and they'll copy whatever spelling I use — and no citations or reading needed, everything required should be in the question or be stuff they've already had in the seminar.
