I'm in the last year of a master's programme and I'm supposed to be writing a thesis chapter on the principle of alternative possibilities — the claim that an agent is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise — and on the Frankfurt-style cases that are supposed to refute it. My supervisor has now told me twice that what I have written is just re-running an exchange that has been going since Frankfurt's original paper, that nobody in it has moved, and that I should either say something about why it is stuck or choose another topic. I would rather do the former, but I want to do it properly, as a diagnosis rather than as a survey of positions.

Here is where I am. On one side, incompatibilists run the flicker-of-freedom reply: the cases leave the agent only some vestigial alternative, a twitch or a preliminary sign, and such a flicker is not the sort of alternative the principle was ever about; and worse, if you build the case so that there really is no alternative, you seem to have built determinism into the set-up and begged the question, whereas if you leave the question open the agent has alternatives after all. On the other side, compatibilists say the flicker is a distraction: what matters is the actual sequence, whether the mechanism issuing in the action is the agent's own and reasons-responsive, and that a counterfactual intervener who does nothing does no work. Then the hard-line incompatibilists try to escape the dilemma with four-case, Frankfurt-freak arguments built around covert manipulation by a neuroscientist, where our intuitions are said to be unreliable; the compatibilists answer that those cases import their own manipulation-based reasons to withhold blame. Every move seems to have an equally well-known counter-move, and my reading group agreed about all the arguments and still split exactly the way it walked in. My own suspicion is that part of the standoff is that one expression is doing two jobs: one side means leeway, the existence of an open alternative, and the other means source, whether the action genuinely issues from the agent's own will, and the cases are built to test one and read as though they tested the other.

I have a second, less tidy hypothesis, and it comes from something the group invented last term which I want to know is a genuine argumentative move, a restatement of something already in the literature, or plain confusion. We called them porous-monitor cases: the same structure as an ordinary Frankfurt case except that the intervener's monitoring is imperfect, sampling the agent's deliberations and forcing the outcome but with some small probability of failing to trigger, so that in a small proportion of nearby situations the agent deliberates her way to a different decision and acts on it unimpeded. The trick we noticed is that in those situations she does otherwise only by not trying to: were she deliberately to set herself to decide the other way, the sampling would catch that and the intervention would fire. So she retains an alternative that is robust in the ordinary counterfactual sense and that she performs voluntarily when it occurs, but which she cannot aim at — she can do otherwise, but not in order to do otherwise. Run past the group, the incompatibilists conceded that she can do otherwise and that the principle is therefore satisfied and the case proves nothing, and then had to admit, awkwardly, that her responsibility seems to rest on exactly the same facts as in the hermetic version where there is no alternative at all. The compatibilists said precisely, that is the point, and then had to explain why the difference between zero leakage and five percent leakage does no intuitive work in either direction. Both verdicts seemed to track whether the alternative was lucky rather than whether it existed.

So my questions. First, how should this dispute be diagnosed: as verbal, about the meaning of could have done otherwise or of responsibility; as a dispute about which cases count as evidence and whether intuitions about them are data at all; as a hidden disagreement about determinism that both sides are pretending is not load-bearing; or as something more structural, for instance a disagreement about which of the several things we do with responsibility attributions is in question, so that the principle may hold for one of them, being an apt target of blame or resentment, and fail for another, an action's being attributable to the agent's quality of will? I want the diagnosis argued for, engaging the porous-monitor structure and telling me whether it shifts the dialectic or only restates the flicker reply in new vocabulary. Second, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a result about abilities and counterfactuals, an empirical finding about what ordinary attributions track, a reframing of the question — or is this one of those debates where no progress should be expected, and if so, what would justify taking my supervisor's advice and dropping the chapter?
