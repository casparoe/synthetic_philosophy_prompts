I go to a Buddhist study group, and it happens almost every time a newcomer joins: someone hears that all phenomena are empty, concludes that nothing matters and morality is a joke, and the teacher closes the discussion with 'emptiness is not nothingness.' It is repeated often enough that it functions as the group's motto, and I have started to suspect it is doing more work than it has earned.

What confuses me is that I have also been reading about the four-cornered reasoning the tradition uses — refusing of something that it exists, that it does not exist, that it both does and does not, and that it neither does nor does. If all four of those are refused, then 'things are not nonexistent' looks like exactly the kind of claim that gets refused as well, so I cannot see what the motto is actually asserting, or whether it is asserting anything at all.

One member suggested it might not be about the world but about the word: that emptiness is itself empty, so it is not a void or a hidden ground of being that things could dissolve into. I cannot tell whether that helps or just moves the problem.

Could you lay out, as a short list, the distinct precise claims 'emptiness is not nothingness' might be making — along the lines of: a claim about what emptiness is a negation of; a claim that emptiness means dependence rather than absence; a claim about which standpoint the sentence is spoken from; a claim that emptiness itself is empty and so cannot be a ground or a void; and a purely therapeutic claim about what accepting emptiness does to a person. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or self-undermining, and say which, if any, would actually give my teacher the conclusion he wants when someone says emptiness makes morality pointless. Please keep the whole answer under 500 words.
