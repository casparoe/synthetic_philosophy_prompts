I've been going back and forth with a guy in my local rationalist-adjacent group chat for about a week and I cant find the exit. He's got this argument that moral worth is impossible, and every step looks fine to me individually.

(1) An action has moral worth only if the agent does it because it is right. The right-making features have to be what moves her, not merely be features of whatever she happens to end up doing.

(2) "Because" there is causal-explanatory. Her taking the action to be right has to be part of the causal story of why she did it. Otherwise "acting for a reason" is just the same bodily movements with some narration stapled on.

(3) Beliefs don't move you on their own. On the standard Humean picture a belief can only cause action in tandem with some desire or pro-attitude.

(4) So the morally worthy agent has a desire to do what is right.

(5) A desire is a state whose satisfaction the agent is motivated to bring about. What she is motivated to bring about here is a state of affairs in which she has done the right thing. That is a state of her.

(6) So she is acting in order to produce a pleasing state of herself: clean conscience, the warm glow of being a good person, no guilt. That is self-interest with better branding.

(7) Nothing done from self-interest has moral worth.

(8) So no action has moral worth. The only motive that could satisfy (1) is a motive that trips (7).

And then two corollaries he leans on:

(9) The person who helps from spontaneous sympathy and never once thinks "this is right" has zero moral worth. Also, by the same reasoning as (5)-(6), she is acting from self-interest anyway, since she is doing it to shut off her own discomfort at the sight of suffering. So she's sunk twice.

(10) If you could delete the desire in (4) while leaving the belief intact, she wouldn't act at all. So praiseworthiness requires a motivational structure nobody actually has, and the more reliably good someone is, the less likely they are to be acting for the right reason.

Things that make me suspicious: this has the same shape as "everyone always does what they most want to do, therefore altruism is impossible," which I thought we'd retired around the time of the Sequences. And the whole argument goes through unchanged if you swap in epistemic praiseworthiness — you'd need a desire to believe truly, which is a self-directed desire, so no belief is ever epistemically praiseworthy either. My friend bites that bullet happily, which makes me more suspicious rather than less.

But when I try to point at the exact step where it goes wrong I lose the thread. I'd rather keep (1) than anything else — I really don't want to end up as the guy who says the lifeguard who jumps by reflex deserves as much credit as the guy who thought about it and jumped. But if keeping (1) means I have to buy non-Humean motivation, I want to be told that's the price. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look innocent, and what does each of the other options cost me?
