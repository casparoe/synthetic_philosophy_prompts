hey — I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this semester (Popper, Lakatos, a little Kuhn, and one whole session on whether Malthus was just early rather than wrong), and I also help run the campus forecasting club. We keep a public spreadsheet of about 200 long-range predictions that members have logged since 2016, horizons anywhere from three to thirty years. Twice a semester we have a triage night where ten or so of us go through the unresolved entries and decide, for each one: leave it on the board, extend it with a new deadline, or retire it as busted. Right now it is pure vibes and whoever made the prediction argues the loudest. I want an actual procedure we can run, and I want you to build it around a distinction my TA threw out that I can't stop thinking about (more on that below).

What I need from you:

A triage procedure with the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each one, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a vote because it's really a question about how patient we want to be rather than a question of fact. It has to be runnable in about ten minutes per prediction by undergrads with one shared spreadsheet and no statistics beyond what a spreadsheet does natively. Decisions are public and they stick for a year, so there's some mild reputational sting, and people show up defensive about their own entries — tell me how to structure the run so that the person applying the procedure is not the person who logged the prediction, and what to do when we simply can't get a number for the key variable because there's no public data series.

The part I actually care about is what makes an extension legitimate (right but early) versus just moving the goalposts. Here is the idea I want built in: a rescue can have its own expiry date. If the only reason a prediction hasn't come true is an offsetting mechanism that is itself temporary or exhaustible — industrial nitrogen fixation and cheap fossil energy masking a Malthusian land constraint, say, or a one-shot demographic dividend propping up a growth forecast — then the extension should not be open-ended; it should be bounded by the remaining life of the rescue, and the club should have to say what would tell us the rescue is running out. That's very different from a delay that the theory's own mechanism explains, where the mechanism is real but just running slower than anyone guessed, and extending is cheap. Give me the tests that separate those two, plus a rule for setting the new deadline in the first kind of case, and a rule for the intake end too: when someone logs a new prediction, the deadline should be derived from the timescale of the mechanism they're invoking, not picked because it sounds far enough away.

Then run it on three of our real entries.

1. Malthus 2.0, logged 2017: global per-capita calorie production peaks before 2040, and at least one famine with over a million deaths before 2035, on the mechanism that arable land is fixed, yield gains are roughly linear, and demand compounds. Peak Attention, logged 2015: average daily minutes on social platforms for US adults would peak by 2021 and fall at least 20% by 2025, because attention is a fixed budget and platforms hit a satiation ceiling. I think this should be retired and I want to see whether the procedure agrees or tells me I'm being impatient.

3. The one we'll all hate: our all-time most upvoted entry, from 2021, something like algorithmic feeds will destroy shared reality in the US by 2030. We've got two triage nights left this semester and I'd rather bring something imperfect than another vibes session.
