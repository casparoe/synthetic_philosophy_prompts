I am a novelist, and this autumn I have agreed to run a single ninety-minute session at a writers' retreat: fourteen participants, almost all of them fiction writers with no formal philosophy, around a long table in a room with unreliable wifi. The session is billed as being about building non-human minds on the page, but the novel I am failing to finish contains three of them, and it is really those that I want the group to wrestle with. There is an upload whose computation is spread across forty thousand idle handsets, so that no single device carries anything that looks like a thought; there is a replay, in which one mind's computation is instantiated twice and kept in step, and my narrator cannot settle whether two copies means twice the moral claim or one claim counted twice; and there is what I have been calling a mimic, which produces exactly a person's behaviour by routes that contain nothing resembling the person's reasons — an enormous lookup table, consulted very quickly. I would like the group to feel why these are hard cases rather than be told that they are hard, and to feel it before anyone in the room uses a word like functionalism. Index cards, dried beans, a bell, string and a whiteboard are available; laptops are not, and I have no wish to stand at the front and lecture.

My own draft is the obvious one, and I suspect it is too obvious: make the room be the system. Give each participant a node — a card bearing an update rule and a threshold — have them pass tokens along string to carry out the transitions by hand, feed a pattern in at one edge and read a pattern off at the other, and then repeat the whole thing in variants: the same behaviour produced from a giant table that one participant reads aloud; the same computation replicated twice in parallel halves of the room; the network maimed so that part of the causal work is done by coin flips; the identical transition table run at a tenth of the speed, or implemented by a single participant shuffling cards instead of by fourteen people passing tokens. After each variant I would take a private vote — beans in a cup — on how much moral consideration the thing in the room is owed, and then make the group argue with its own inconsistency. What I cannot construct is the bridge from the room having done something to the genuinely destabilising point, which I take to be this: that the very same physical proceedings can be described as implementing many different computations, at different grains and at different levels, so that the computation alleged to matter is not something the situation hands you but something a describer selects, and that this act of selection is quietly doing all of the moral work. That is precisely what my upload and my replay turn on, and I do not want it arriving as a pronouncement from the front of the room.

So I am asking you to design the session for me: runnable rules, a materials list, any roles I need to assign, and a minute-by-minute plan for ninety minutes with fourteen people, including how the thing starts given that nobody may be told in advance what it is about. Please build in real if small stakes — I will put a modest prize on a vote, or arrange for the group's early verdicts to bind them later in ways they do not foresee — and make the forced choices explicit. Then give me the debrief, and be exact about how each moment of the activity is meant to map onto each part of the problem: which variant is supposed to unsettle which assumption, and what I should do when someone produces the right objection at the wrong moment. I also want an honest account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run — I assume at least that someone will say none of it is conscious because it is just people passing beans, that the votes will be treated as a poll rather than as judgements, and that the room will collapse into we cannot know by minute forty — and, equally honestly, which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I must therefore say in my own voice at the end, rather than pretending the game taught it. One constraint, since the retreat has almost no connectivity and there is no library within reach: please do not point me toward papers, authors, further reading, or anything I would have to look up. I need the version that works with what is in the room; if a named theory or a quotation would improve the debrief, leave it out or mark it clearly as optional garnish I could safely skip.
