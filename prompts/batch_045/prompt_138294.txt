You are a professional philosopher with a working knowledge of contemporary metaethics who also writes comedy in the house style of a very good satirical magazine: patient, deadpan, and willing to keep a straight face for a thousand words. Before drafting, brainstorm and settle four things (do not include the brainstorming in your answer): which real commitment of the expressivist program you are going to exaggerate; which three of its standard manoeuvres for handling embedded moral sentences you will put on display; where in the document the reader first notices that something has gone wrong; and what the final screen does. Write the piece only after those are fixed.

The deliverable: a satirical internal document circulating at a large freight and logistics company, issued by its Compliance and Ethics office and ghostwritten by an outside consultancy that has taken expressivism completely literally and implemented it as an engineering specification. Form: the transcript of a mandatory e-learning module — subject line, numbered sections, on-screen prompts, one multiple-choice item, a footer note recording that Legal has signed off, and a final certification screen the employee must click. Length: 700–900 words, with the apparatus counting toward it, so keep the headings and footers tight.

Premise: the module's governing doctrine, stated without the faintest hint of irony, is that the meaning of a moral sentence is entirely a matter of what attitude its utterance expresses. The consultancy has therefore built the training so that no employee is ever actually required to disapprove of anything. Every moral sentence in the material occurs in an unasserted position: as the antecedent or consequent of a conditional, inside a question, under a negation, within a disjunction, or in a report of what someone else thinks. The employees, reading closely, start to notice. The satire is their attempt to work out whether they have been trained at all.

Requirements:

1. The jokes must come from getting the metaethics right. A reader who works on the Frege–Geach problem should be able to reconstruct the problem from the jokes alone: how a moral sentence keeps its full content while appearing in a context where the speaker is not endorsing it, why inference over moral premises — modus ponens, the transitivity-looking patterns, the reasoning that goes through an unasserted step — seems to require that shared content, and why a theory that identifies content with expressed attitude appears to run out of resources exactly there. Nothing may be explained. The module's own internal logic has to expose it. The target is the position and its strategies for handling embedding, not any named thinker; invent the consultancy and its personnel, and never name a living philosopher.

2. At least three distinct quasi-realist manoeuvres should appear in caricature and be made to fail in the way a critic would say they fail, with no one in the document ever conceding anything. Suggestions, in whatever order serves the piece: the higher-order-attitude treatment of conditionals, where what the module asks for is not disapproval of accepting gifts but a single compound state, an approval of a certain package of attitudes, and where the module then has to say what makes that state a state about that package rather than about something else; the three-way treatment of negation, where not being for a thing and being against it are recorded as separate HR-attested conditions, and the module cannot explain what the third condition — the withholding — is a withholding of; and the minimalist turn, where the word true is dispensed as pure grammatical garnish, so that the sentence about it being true that falsifying a delivery record is wrong can be certified without anyone having to mind falsifying delivery records. A fourth, if you want it: the idealization move, where a wrong act is defined as one that a fully informed and fully impartial version of the employee would disapprove of, followed by the observation that no such employee is on the payroll and the clause therefore binds nobody.

3. The corporate form should carry the argument, not decorate it. The certification click, the attestation, the audit trail, the policy on retaliation for expressed disapproval, the vendor-management clause that governs relations with subcontractors who may or may not have attitudes — these should be what makes the philosophical difficulty bite: the institution needs the employees to be in some state that plays the content role across inference and reporting, and it will quietly redefine its own vocabulary in order to get one.

4. End on the final screen. The employee is asked to certify that they have understood the module and that they hold the attitudes it requires, and the employee who has read carefully cannot see how to click it: assenting would require either an insincerity the module has no vocabulary for or a concession the theory does not want to make. Stop there. Do not resolve it, do not add a coda, and do not let any character in the document use the words expressivism, quasi-realism, content, force, or Frege–Geach.

Constraints: deadpan corporate voice throughout; no puns, no exclamation marks outside the module's own interface copy, no footnotes explaining a joke. Internal consistency is part of the brief — this must read as a document the consultancy could have produced in good faith, with every absurdity following from taking the doctrine seriously. Fairness matters more than savagery: exaggerate a commitment the view actually has and that its defenders actually take themselves to have answered, so that a specialist finishes feeling got rather than misrepresented. At the same time, someone with no metaethics at all should be able to follow and enjoy it on the strength of the corporate details alone.
