Bit of context first so the prep has something to grip. About three years ago a handful of us set up a mutual-credit network for small trades in my city — two plumbers, a bakery, a bike repair shop, some graphic designers — where you log a job done for another member and get a credit in the shared ledger instead of cash. Last spring it nearly came apart over one case: a member wanted to draw credit against work she hadn't done yet, rent was due, and the group split more or less down the middle. What I keep coming back to is that nobody in that argument mentioned quantity. Same number of credits either way. The fight was about whether the credit answered to anything real — whether there was a job, a thing actually done, on the other side of it.

That sent me to Berkeley's book of short numbered questions about the Irish economy (written while he was bishop in Cloyne; he floats some kind of national bank in it too, I think), and I've come out with a reading I'm going to defend out loud next month. Roughly: Berkeley gets filed either as a metallist-ish crank or as a mercantilist with a weird side interest in money. I think he's better read as a proto-chartalist with what I'm calling a tracking constraint. Money is a social kind, and its existence conditions include an audit relation: issuance has to be answerable — publicly, to somebody — to real productive activity. That gives us two seperate failure modes instead of one: over-issuing, which shows up in prices, and mis-tracking, which need not. I'm claiming Berkeley's constraint is the second one, and that it is not a claim about backing and not a claim about quantity. If I'm right, it hands the MMT people the thing I think their account lacks — a story about how a currency can stop being money that isn't just a story about inflation — and it hands the sound-money people a version of their worry that doesn't need any metal in it.

My argument in five steps, so you have something to aim at: (1) money is whatever occupies the social role, unit of account plus general acceptability in settlement — role first, substance second; (2) roles like that have existence conditions going beyond "people happen to accept it," because plenty of tokens get accepted for a while and then stop, and we want to say retrospectively that they never were money; (3) what separates durable from fizzling acceptance is the audit relation; (4) Berkeley is doing something like this — his queries keep relocating wealth from coin to industry and labour, and the bank he sketches is built so notes go out against productive activity rather than against metal; (5) test case: our dispute was mis-tracking without over-issuing, and how members actually reacted fits the tracking prediction better than the inflation prediction.
