I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the so-called moral equality of combatants — the view that soldiers on both sides of a war, just side and unjust side alike, stand in the same moral position: same permission to kill legitimate targets, same liability to be killed, same innocence when they follow the rules about civilians. I keep running into the slogan "war is hell" doing heavy lifting in this argument, most recently at a Q&A after a panel where a retired colonel used it to shut down the whole revisionist challenge: an audience member asked whether a soldier who fights for a cause he knows is unjust does something wrong when he kills an enemy soldier who is himself doing nothing wrong, and the colonel said, basically, war is hell, and that was that — as if the question answered itself.

Here's the thing: I can tell "war is hell" is doing at least three or four different jobs at once, and I want them separated before I write. So please lay the readings out as a menu, from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each — pure platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false. Some candidates I want you to sort through: that war involves terrible suffering (fine, but then how does that bear on anyone's blame?); that being in a war is so frightening and disorienting that soldiers deserve an excuse of the duress-or-terror kind; that war is a zone where ordinary morality is suspended and nothing that happens in it is morally assessable; that there's a tragic moral remainder — the soldier acts permissibly and still does something bad, and there's nothing to be said but "war is hell"; and anything else that's plausibly in there. For each reading, tell me whether it would actually support the colonel's move, i.e. whether it gets him the conclusion that the unjust-side soldier isn't morally criticisable, and separately whether it gets him the stronger claim that both soldiers are in the *same* moral position rather than just both being pitied.

Two wrinkles I want built into the answer, because they're what actually made me suspicious of the slogan. First: does the excuse-style reading — the one that leans on fear, exhaustion, being thrust into an unbearable situation — reach a drone operator launching a strike from a base in Nevada who clocks out and drives home to dinner? If not, the slogan looks like it's smuggling in a picture of war that's now obsolete, and I want to say that in print without overstating it. Second: a friend of mine (a philosopher, though not a specialist) suggested the slogan conflates two things that are worth keeping apart — the claim that a combatant *acts permissibly* and the claim that he *isn't to blame* — and also that "moral equality" itself might come apart into several theses (about permissions, about liability to be killed, about blame, about what we may say to soldiers afterwards at a peace ceremony) that don't stand or fall together. I don't know if that's right and I don't want to repeat it as received wisdom. Tell me which of those distinctions are real and load-bearing, and where each leaves the colonel. Please write in American English, and keep it plain enough that I could quote chunks of it in a magazine piece.
