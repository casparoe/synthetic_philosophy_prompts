i'm in an african philosophy seminar this term and i've ended up defending a view in my final essay that i'm now worried i can't actually lose, which feels like a bad sign. Roughly the position: being a human organism isn't enough for being a person — personhood is a status you come to have by standing in the right kind of relation to others, it's a matter of degree, and it can in principle fail to develop or be lost. It's the radical reading we read alongside Menkiti's line about how "personhood is the sort of thing which has to be attained", as against the moderate one where you're already a person and community just develops you (Gyekye's version, basically). My own addition is that the relations have to be ones of mutual recognition: being monitored by a state, or depended on by an employer, or even being cared for, doesn't do it on its own — the recognition has to be recieved and given back, at least dispositionally.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which ones a defender of the view would themselves concede rather than just what the critics say. Here's where i've got to, and i'd like you to push on it.

1. The wrongly-ostracised person. Someone is expelled from every community they have — a whistleblower, someone accused of witchcraft, a prison informant held in permanent solitary. If personhood tracks actual recognition, the community's injustice literally unmakes the victim as a person, which seems to blame the victim at the metaphysical level. Is that fatal, or does it just force the view to switch from actual recognition to fitting recognition? And if it switches, hasn't it become a capacities view with extra steps — i.e. collapsed into the rival it was meant to replace? I think that dilemma (either extensionally the same as the moderate view, or committed to monstrous consequences about the excluded) is the strongest thing against me, but tell me if i'm overrating it.

2. My own patch, which I'm suspicious of. I want to say personhood has hysteresis: once attained it persists when the relations are stripped away, like a skill you don't lose. Apply that to someone with advanced dementia who no longer recognises anyone, and to someone who spent thirty years in solitary keeping relationships alive only in memory. Is the persistence move a principled consequence or an ad hoc save? Same question for the case I keep coming back to: a mother caring for an adult child who can't reciprocate at all. She's clearly a full person; the view has to explain that without saying she's coasting on leftover status.

3. The swapped twins. Two genetically identical people, one raised inside a thick community, one raised in near-total social deprivation. If my view says they differ in degree of personhood, is that a cost or the whole point? And what empirical finding would actually move the number here — say it turned out that children adopted after years of severe deprivation reach the same moral-psychological profile as their peers within months. Would that count against the view, or is it the wrong kind of evidence because personhood is a normative status?

4. Does the view do any work? My prof keeps saying its bite is political — reconciliation, restorative justice, why exile is the worst punishment. But if personhood is losable, doesn't it also underwrite expulsion, i.e. the thing it's supposed to heal? And take a companion chatbot deeply embedded in something that looks like reciprocal recognition: does the view predict partial personhood for it? Is that a reductio or a feature I should bite?

Be straight with me about whether "it's a matter of degree" plus "it persists once attained" plus "recognition has to be fitting" has insulated the whole thing into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a claim of this kind, or whether expecting a testable prediction is just the wrong standard for a view about what a person is.
