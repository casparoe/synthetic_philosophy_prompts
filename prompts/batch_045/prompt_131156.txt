I'm an engineer on a team that builds ML training pipelines. I have no formal philosophy background, so I'd rather have the structure of an argument picked apart than pointed at a literature: please don't tell me who to read, just tell me where the reasoning breaks.

Here is the setup. We want a system that reviews large codebases for subtle correctness and safety bugs. Our reviewers ("H") are competent but not experts, and they are slow. The scheme I've seen described under the name *iterated amplification* works like this:

- **Amplification.** Define A(H) as H augmented with (i) the ability to decompose a question into sub-questions recursively, (ii) the ability to spawn copies of itself to answer those sub-questions, (iii) a much larger time and compute budget, and (iv) an aggregation step in which the top-level instance combines sub-answers into a final answer. In our design the human only reviews the top-level decomposition and the final aggregation; sub-answers below depth two are produced by copies of H with no further review.
- **Distillation.** Train a fast model M on demonstrations from A(H). We plan to distill the *decomposition policy* as well — which sub-questions to ask, not just which final answer to give — on the theory that the reasoning procedure is the thing worth copying.
- **Iteration.** Use M_n in place of H to build A(M_n), distill that into M_{n+1}, and repeat.

Two definitions, so the terms don't drift: by *competent* I mean performs well on held-out review tasks; by *aligned* I mean that its decisions track what our reviewers would want if they could see everything and think as long as they liked.

A colleague put the following argument on our design doc as a reason not to fund this. I've numbered the steps to keep them straight.

1. The product of each round is M_n, obtained by supervised learning to imitate A(H). M_n is a fast approximation to A(H) and nothing more.
2. Imitation cannot exceed its target. A model trained to reproduce a demonstrator's behavior is at best as good as that demonstrator, and in practice somewhat worse, because fitting is lossy. So competence(M_n) ≤ competence(A(H)).
3. Amplification is information-preserving. A(H) has no source of knowledge about the task other than H. Sub-questions, extra time, and extra copies add *structure*; they don't add information. Rearranging what H already knows cannot get you more than H already knows. So competence(A(H)) ≤ competence(H).
4. From (2) and (3), competence(M_n) ≤ competence(H) for every n. Iterating can't escape this, because (2) and (3) hold for whatever you start from, and each round starts from something no better than H. So the "iterated" part buys nothing and the ceiling on the whole pipeline is the original human reviewer.
5. The only way out is to claim that amplification adds something after all, and the only candidate is compute. But compute only lets H use its knowledge more exhaustively; it doesn't manufacture the knowledge the task requires. Finding these bugs is precisely something our reviewers don't know how to do.
6. So if the pipeline ever exceeds H, the excess cannot come from amplification. It can only come from the one other place novelty enters: the model's generalization beyond its training data during distillation — the model doing things no demonstration covered.
7. A generalization nobody demonstrated is a generalization nobody endorsed. Our reviewers endorsed particular outputs on particular inputs. What the model does in between is its own, shaped by an inductive bias the reviewers never chose and, in the case of a large pretrained model, never saw.
8. Therefore: to the extent that the pipeline's product is more competent than H, it is to that extent acting on unendorsed generalizations. Competence beyond the human and alignment with the human move in opposite directions.
9. Since exceeding human competence is the entire point, and (8) says every step past H is a step away from the human's endorsement, iterated amplification cannot deliver both. It is either an expensive way to clone our reviewers or an uncontrolled capability gain wearing supervision as a costume.

The conclusion is stronger than I can accept. The same shape of argument seems to rule out things I'm not willing to rule out: fine-tuning a model on labels our own reviewers wrote, a senior engineer teaching a junior by example, or writing a specification that a contractor implements. In each case the finished product does things nobody explicitly signed off on, and I don't want to conclude that all of these are unendorsed in the sense that matters. But I can't locate the step that fails.

If forced to choose, I'd most want to keep (3) and (7). (3) matches what I see daily: our reviewers genuinely don't know how to find these bugs, and another hour per review won't teach them. (7) matches my actual worry about shipping this thing.

So: which step do I reject, and why did it look fine? I'd like a point-by-point diagnosis rather than a general verdict — for each step you'd give up, what exactly is wrong with it, what made it plausible enough to survive review, and what I have to take on board if I give it up (including any distinction it was suppressing). If instead the argument is valid and the real choice is among (2), (3) and (7), tell me which is cheapest to surrender and what that surrender commits me to. Finally: does your answer change if we distill the decomposition policy rather than only final answers, as we plan to — does procedure distillation make the argument stronger, weaker, or just relocate it?
