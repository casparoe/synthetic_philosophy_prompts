Here's my crude version. Forget the usual indices — I don't want to argue about scores. I want a structural test with three pieces:

(a) the insurance test: does the change raise the incumbent's probability of keeping office conditional on becoming unpopular, i.e. does it degrade the machinery that converts unpopularity into removal? That's what's supposed to separate backsliding from ordinary unpopular governing.

(b) a two-step taxonomy: rule-changing moves (electoral law, districting, thresholds, appointment terms — all legal under the existing constitution, often passed by a legitimately elected majority) versus referee-capturing moves (courts, electoral commissions, audit offices, media regulators, prosecution services). My claim is that the second kind is what makes the first kind irreversible, and that any account that only looks at rule changes will misclassify things in both directions.

(c) what I call the restoration trap: because reversal has to go through referees that were captured in step (b), a new majority that tries to undo the changes either uses the captured tools (and thus keeps them) or breaks them (and thus commits the same kind of move it's prosecuting). If that's right, backsliding isn't just hard to reverse, it's structurally set up so that reversal looks like backsliding to somebody.
