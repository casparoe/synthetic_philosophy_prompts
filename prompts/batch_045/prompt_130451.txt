I help run a volunteer community workshop (tools, evening classes, a shared space), and at our last annual general meeting I put forward a motion to delete one of our oldest standing rules: that any purchase above a fairly small amount has to be countersigned in a physical ledger before it can be reimbursed. We now do everything through an app that logs who approved what, and as far as anyone can tell the rule dates from a period when there was no treasurer and money went missing. The motion was blocked, more or less single-handedly, by one member who said only: Chesterton's fence. Since then I keep meeting the same move everywhere — a friend at a small software company wanted to delete a legacy check in their deployment pipeline that nobody has understood for years, and was told the same thing — and I've become convinced that the slogan is doing several quite different jobs at once, most of them unstated. I've been turning over distinctions on my own and would like them laid out properly: is it a claim about who bears the burden of proof when proposing a change; a claim of epistemic deference, that a practice which has survived for a long time carries more information than any one person can reconstruct, so that my inability to see its point is evidence about me rather than about the rule; a claim about invisible functions, where the fence is holding up something nobody designed and no individual member could articulate even under questioning, so that reading the minutes and asking around wouldn't discover it; a claim about function drift, where knowing the original reason isn't enough because the rule may have quietly started doing a different job after the original reason expired; a claim about coordination, where each person's reason for complying is that everyone else complies, so the function isn't visible in any survey of reasons and wouldn't be reproduced by knowing it; a claim about irreversibility and option value rather than about knowledge at all, so that a reversible trial removal would satisfy it while a permanent deletion wouldn't; or something close to plain status quo bias dressed up in a cost-asymmetry argument, on the grounds that the costs of removing a needed fence exceed the costs of keeping a useless one. I'd also like to know whether the principle is symmetric — whether it says anything about adding a new rule, or about a gap that was never filled — and if it isn't, where the asymmetry is supposed to come from; whether it still applies when you do know the original reason and the reason was a bad one; whether "know why it was put up" means being able to describe its function or being able to secure that function by other means; and whether, on some of these readings, the requirement can ever be discharged at all, or has quietly become an infinitely demanding licence to block any reform by gesturing at a function nobody has thought of yet.

What I want back is a menu rather than a verdict: the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered roughly from trivial to ambitious, each with a note on whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false; which reading the people who repeat it are most likely to mean in practice, and whether that differs between a self-described Burkean conservative, a rationalist blogger, and an engineer; and then, most usefully for me, which readings would genuinely support the two uses I described — blocking my motion and blocking my friend's cleanup — and which would not. If it helps, my own suspicion is that the fence talk at our meeting was really about irreversibility and about nobody wanting to be blamed if something went wrong after the change, and that the grander readings about inherited wisdom were nowhere in play; but I'd like that suspicion stress-tested rather than confirmed. Finally, tell me what would actually satisfy the principle on the readings you think are strongest: what would I have to show, in a five-minute reply at the next meeting, to have earned permission to delete the rule rather than merely to have annoyed the person who objected.
