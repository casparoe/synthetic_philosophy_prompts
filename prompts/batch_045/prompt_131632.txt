I teach a third-year seminar on philosophy of perception and I'm putting the take-home final together right now — students get 48 hours, this one question should eat about 90 minutes of that and it's worth 30 marks. I want a question on the cognitive penetrability of perception that can't be answered by reciting the setup (encapsulation, the modularity-flavoured arguments, the usual greatest-hits examples like cross-cultural differences in illusion susceptibility or hills looking steeper when you're carrying a heavy pack) or just by asserting a definition and moving on. The failure mode my students keep falling into is running three or four different things together under the one word "penetration": attention doing the work, a shift in response criterion rather than in what is actually experienced, and changes that are diachronic — the system has been retuned by training, so that at the moment of seeing there is no occurrent cognitive state doing any penetrating at all. So I want the stem to force them to seperate those cases rather than let them gesture at the distinction, and I want it built around cases instead of around authors: no "discuss so-and-so's argument" framing, nothing that requires citing a paper, self-contained enough that someone who did the reading badly can't just spill a summary onto the page.

Here's the material I'd like the question to run on, though please sharpen it however you like — I'm not attached to my version. Case A: after two years reading scans alongside an assistive system that draws boxes around suspicious regions, an experienced radiologist finds that tumours now jump out at her even on days when the system is down; she has no occurrent belief about any particular image, and her unassisted accuracy has genuinely improved. Case B: in a lab analogue, participants are told before each block what the ambiguous target is most likely to be; they report the target popping out vividly, and the effect is real, but it shows up entirely as a shift in response criterion with sensitivity unchanged — and then a second version of the study where sensitivity does move. I want them asked what would have to be true of the data for either version to count as penetration rather than a decision-level effect, and what a change in sensitivity would and wouldn't settle. Then: does it matter, for the epistemic argument people associate with Fodor (roughly, that if perception is penetrated then it can't serve as an independent tribunal for the very states doing the penetrating), whether the penetrating state is a true and rationally held belief, a mere desire that isn't truth-assessing at all, or a belief that has been absorbed into the perceptual system by training and is no longer occurrent? I also want them to say what would make a penetrable perceptual system epistemically benign rather than circular, and I have a suspicion the honest answer is that the benign/malignant cut doesn't line up with the penetrable/encapsulated cut — but I'd rather see whether your question can make students get there on their own than have me hand it to them inside the stem.

Deliverables: (1) the exam question itself, written as it would appear on the paper, with whatever subparts and mark allocations you think work; (2) a model answer of about 600 words — the sort of thing that would get a high first, not a platonic ideal answer nobody would actually produce in 90 minutes; and (3) a marking scheme as a markdown table. The table's columns should be "Required move" | "Marks (out of 30)" | "What a first-class answer does" | "The specific mistake that costs the mark", where the last column names a concrete failure mode rather than something vague — e.g. treating a criterion shift as a change in what's experienced, or assuming the diachronic case counts as penetration merely because a belief was causally involved at some earlier point. Then, after the table, a JSON object with these fields: "question_stem" (one sentence), "discriminates" (one sentence on what separates an A answer from a B answer), "time_budget_minutes", "total_marks", and "bleedover_risk" (the neighbouring topic — attention, the theory-ladenness of observation, disjunctivism about experience, whatever — that students are most likely to answer instead of this one).
