**Question 3.** *Inverse reinforcement learning is often advertised as a way of teaching machines what we want by showing them what we do, rather than by getting us to say what we want.*

*Suppose a robot is learning kitchen work from video of a human chef. In the filmed kitchen the walk-in fridge is normally locked; the chef can only get into it by finding a manager, so she very rarely uses anything stored there, and when she does it's after a long delay. Call this* the locked-pantry case.

*(a) Set out how IRL would model this: what is taken as given, what is inferred, and what assumption the inference needs in order to get off the ground at all. (6 marks)*

*(b) Someone objects: "this is nothing new — reading an agent's wants off its behaviour under constraints is just revealed preference theory, and it has the same limits and the same remedies." Is that right? (8 marks)*

*(c) Suppose the fitting procedure returns a set of reward functions that all explain the demonstrations equally well, and they disagree about what the robot should do in a different kitchen where the fridge is unlocked. Does that show that IRL has told us nothing about what the chef wants? (6 marks)*

**Student's answer**

(a) IRL runs the normal RL problem backwards. In RL the reward is given and you solve for a policy; here we are given the chef's trajectories and we solve for the reward under which those trajectories come out looking good. To do that you need everything in the MDP except the reward — the states, the actions, the transition probabilities, the discount factor — all treated as known, with the reward as the only unknown. The extra assumption that makes the inference possible is that the chef is approximately optimal with respect to her own reward: either strictly optimal, or Boltzmann-rational, with a temperature saying how steeply she prefers better trajectories. Some assumption like this is essential, because otherwise the problem is empty: for any behaviour whatsoever there is a reward that makes it optimal, and a constant reward makes every policy optimal, so with no rationalisation assumption you can explain anything and therefore learn nothing. Note that this is different from just copying the chef. Behaviour cloning fits the policy directly and comes apart as soon as the robot is in a situation the chef was never filmed in; IRL is supposed to generalise to new situations because it recovers the goal and not the movements.

(b) The objection is half right. Both projects read something unobserved off behaviour under constraints, and both are hostage to those constraints. In consumer choice you are given the budget set — prices and income — so a choice of x when y was also affordable tells you x is preferred to y, and you don't get to read it as indifference. The locked-pantry case has exactly that shape: the chef doesn't walk into the fridge, but that is a fact about what she could do, not about what she wanted, and IRL will read the locked door as a taste unless the constraint is built into the model explicitly. Revealed preference theory makes the same mistake if you forget the budget set. So it is the same mistake. But IRL also does something new here, and that is where the analogy breaks. In the expected-utility setting, beliefs and desires are only ever identified jointly: for any pattern of choice you can hold the desires fixed and move the beliefs, or vice versa, which is why the holism argument is supposed to show that preference never gets pinned down. IRL doesn't face this, because the transition model and the action set are inputs to the inference rather than things being fitted. Everything on the belief-and-competence side is pinned down by the modeller and the only free parameter is the reward, so what comes out is the chef's desire rather than a mixture of the two. And where the chef is plainly not optimal — she's tired at the end of a long shift, she reaches for the wrong jar — the rationality parameter takes the strain: you fit a temperature, and the temperature tells you how much of the behaviour was noise. Desires come out one side, noise comes out the other.

(c) No, it doesn't show that. The underdetermination is real but it's the boring kind, the same kind you get in ordinary decision theory. Utility functions are only ever unique up to positive affine transformation — you can rescale and shift them without changing any of the agent's preferences — so the fact that a whole family of functions fits the demonstrations just reflects that familiar freedom, and functions that differ only in that way will never disagree about what to do, in the new kitchen or anywhere else. So if the fitted rewards really are telling the robot different things about the unlocked fridge, then the fit has gone wrong rather than the method being bankrupt: the demonstrations must have been too few, or the reward class too rich, or the temperature badly set. The remedy is more data from the chef, not more philosophy.
