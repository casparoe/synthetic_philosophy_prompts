I've got no academic background at all — I do data migration for a living, mostly moving old insurance systems onto newer platforms, and I got into this stuff by arguing with a freind about whether the big features of history 'had to' happen the way they did. Two years of that and I've ended up holding something like what I call the funnel view:

At the level of what need gets met, history mostly converges — delete some famous person or some famous accident and the same functional destination still gets reached, usually by a different route and on a somewhat different schedule. At the level of which exact form fills that slot, history is genuinely stuck: once a form is in place, coordination, habit, training and sunk investment keep it there long after the original reason for picking it has expired. And that stuck part is shallow — it lasts only as long as the surrounding conditions stay roughly the same, and when conditions shift enough the board gets reset and convergence takes over again.

I've also invented a test for it, which I lean on harder than I probably should. Call it the second-arrival test: for anything someone claims was path-dependent, ask whether some other society, starting far away and with no contact, later arrived at something functionally equivalent. If they did, the outcome was overdetermined and the lock-in story is only about which local variant got there first. If nobody ever did, across plenty of independent tries, that's real path dependence.

So: what would count against this? I want the arguments and the kinds of evidence that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to seperate what someone defending my view would actually concede from what only a critic would say. In particular:

- Is the second-arrival test any good? I'm worried 'functionally equivalent' is doing all the work, and that independent arrival might only show the destination was reachable, not that it was reached for comparable reasons or anywhere near the same time. If timing is what does the real work in history, my test may be blind to exactly the thing that matters.
- My day job is probably a biased sample — migrations fail for boring organizational reasons and I might be overgeneralizing from that. But is there anything in it worth keeping?
- The 'as long as conditions stay put' clause worries me. Have I quietly built in an escape hatch that makes the whole thing untestable, and if so is that fatal or just normal for a claim at this level of generality?
- What does my view predict that the strong path-dependence story doesn't, apart from the second-arrival pattern itself? If the answer is nothing, say so.
- Does it collapse into just saying big things were inevitable and small things weren't? If that's all it amounts to, I'd want to know.

The thing that would move me most personally is a case where a clearly worse option stayed in place long after conditions changed a lot — that's the one I don't have an answer for, and I'd rather you pressed on it than padded the rest out.

Don't give me reading lists or names to look up; I can't get to a university library, and I'd rather have the arguments straight than know who made them.
