I run a ninety-minute seminar for fourteen final-year undergraduates — mixed philosophy and law — on precommitment: Ulysses contracts, entrenchment, the ways in which an earlier self or an earlier generation reaches forward and ties down a later one. One session of it I would like to be an activity rather than a discussion, and I had a long argument about exactly what it should do. The friend I was arguing with is a psychiatrist who spends part of her week helping patients draw up advance directives, and her view was that the interesting problem is purely enforcement: of course the calm self speaks for the person, and the only question is how you build an institution stubborn enough to hold when the storm arrives. I disagreed. It seemed to me that the calm self is also the more ignorant self, and that from the inside the two situations — I am being tempted away from what I really want, versus I have learned something and now want something else — feel exactly alike, to the agent and to anyone asked to judge her. And I think the same is true one level up: a constitution's amendment rule is not really a device for protecting principle against appetite, it is a wager on how often later majorities will turn out to be corrupted rather than better informed, placed at a moment when nobody knows the base rate. She said that is what sunset clauses are for; I said a sunset clause just relocates the wager rather than declining it, and we went round on that for an hour.

So: design me an activity for that seminar that makes the students run into the indistinguishability themselves, before anyone names it. No lecture up front, and I would rather they not hear terms like temptation, conversion, learning or dead hand until the debrief. My rough thought is a game in three or four rounds along these lines — an early cool hour in which each student writes a binding rule over her own later choices and also sets, in advance, how costly it will be to release herself from it; then some private mechanism, envelopes or similar, that changes what some of them have reason to want, where the change is genuine and lasting for some, a passing pull for others, and for a few simply a correction of a mistake the earlier self made; then a period in which the rule binds unless they go through a release procedure, which requires persuading a classmate who holds the key and paying the cost they themselves fixed before they knew which kind of case they would be in; then a short final round in which the whole group has to write an amendment rule for a shared pool of points that a later cohort will inherit. But that is only my sketch, and I do not trust it — in particular I cannot see how to make the keyholder unable to tell the cases apart, or how to keep the release cost from turning into a pure bet on a base rate that the students will just guess at, or how to stop the whole thing from collapsing into a signalling exercise in which everyone performs being tempted. Please fix those, or replace the machinery with something that works better.

What I need on paper: the rules, the materials (we have a whiteboard, paper, envelopes and one helper, no laptops and no money — counters or points only, and nothing that puts anyone on the spot about their own mental health, since in a group this size there is usually someone with direct experience of the psychiatric side of this), a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the payoff scheme with the numbers worked out, and the exact wording of any instructions or cards handed to students. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in what happened onto the distinction I care about, including how you would handle the likely case that the students mostly guess the base rate wrong and either over- or under-entrench. Tell me plainly what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will simply have to supply in the debrief — my suspicion is that the really hard metaphysical question, whether the later self is the same agent at all, and the fact that in real precommitment what is at stake can be a life, are both things a game can only gesture at. Please write it in British English. And two things about how you answer: first, if you think the task as I have posed it cannot be done well — if no game of this shape can make the two cases genuinely indistinguishable from the inside, or if the whole idea of staging this is ethically or pedagogically questionable in a way I have not noticed — say that clearly instead of handing me a design that only looks as though it works. Second, mark how confident you are that each moving part will actually produce the effect I want, and flag the pieces you would want to pilot before trusting them.
