I've ended up in a bit of a rabbithole and I want an essay that actually takes a side. A colleague on our interpretability team sent me a blog post built on an old thought experiment of Hofstadter's — early-80s paper I think, later recycled in one of his books — about something called the careenium: a big billiard-table-like box full of tiny magnetic balls (symms) careening around, clumping into larger patterns he calls simmballs, whilst the walls of the box change their bounciness depending on what's happening outside. The question the whole setup exists to ask is who, or what, is really doing the shoving: is it all just symms bumping into symms, or do the simmballs — the higher-level patterns, the symbols — get to do some of the pushing? The post maps it straight onto LLMs: symms are the weights, the activations and the float ops, simmballs are the features and concepts the interpretability crowd keeps claiming to find in there. I do infra rather than research, so the version of this I actually care about is practical: is concept-level talk about a model latching onto something real, or is its just a tidy story we lay over a pile of matmuls.

There are three things I keep going back and forth on and I want the essay to chew on them rather than glide past. (1) At inference time the weights are frozen, so in the most literal sense nothing is pushing the substrate around at all during a forward pass — only activations move. Does that make symbol-talk epiphenomenal at inference and genuinely causal only during training, or is pushing simply the wrong verb, and the real question is whether symbol-level facts are difference-makers for what the activations go on to do? (2) My own pet framing, from my day job: in a language with type erasure, the types genuinely determine what machine code gets emitted and then vanish from the running program — nobody says the type shoved a register around at runtime, but nobody says types are merely comments either. Is a simmball more like a type or more like a comment, and where exactly does that comparison break down? (3) In Hofstadter's version the walls are the only place the outside world gets in. For an LLM the outside world is a prompt, which is already made of symbols — tokens literally perturb the substrate on the way in. So do symbols push the substrate around looks trivially true at the boundary and interesting only for symbols the system forms internally, and I suspect that trivial case is quietly doing a lot of the work in people's intuitions. Call that out either way.

So: write me the essay. Weigh both sides properly — the worry that only one level can be doing the pushing and that one simmball can be realised by countless different symm configurations, against the thought that some interventions are only describable at the higher level and that this is what causal relevance amounts to — but don't fudge the ending. Give me a real verdict on whether simmballs push symms around in a modern LLM, in training as well as at inference, and say what the verdict buys us: should we expect interpretability to find genuine features in there, or not. Tell me how confident you are, which step of the argument is load-bearing and most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind — including what experiment on a real model (steering along a discovered concept direction, patching activations between runs, whatever you'd actually run) would move you, and what result would count against you. Don't bother with reading lists or citations, I can't follow them up from here anyway.
