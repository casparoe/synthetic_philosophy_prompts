I work in reinforcement learning — policy gradients, bandit formulations, the occasional POMDP when the state is badly observed — and I've been arguing with a friend in a philosophy department about Buridan's ass, the case of an agent facing two options it has no reason to prefer and which, on some tellings, starves to death exactly halfway between them. She thinks the case shows something about agency that no decision procedure can capture; I think it shows something about the gap between specifying an objective and specifying an implementation. I'd like to settle it by translation rather than by more argument, because I suspect we keep talking past each other.

So: restate the case and the main positions about choice without preference in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Here is my own first pass, and I want to know where I have smuggled something in. Two actions, a and b, in a one-shot decision problem; the value function is exactly flat across them, Q(s,a) = Q(s,b). In a single-agent MDP a deterministic optimal policy always exists, so nothing in my optimality criterion distinguishes picking a from picking b, and in practice the tie is settled by whichever index the argmax implementation happens to return. Against that background, translate each of the following: the answer that both options are permissible and the agent should simply pick one; the view that the options are not equally good but incomparable, or 'on a par', so that no scalar reward function represents the agent's attitudes at all, which in my terms looks like the difference between a scalarised reward and a Pareto front I have declined to scalarise; the view that an agent can will an option without a comparative reason, so that the deadlock is a failure of the will rather than a feature of the value function; and the randomisation answer, on which the optimal policy is stochastic over {a,b}. Say what each of them comes to in terms of policies, value functions, Pareto fronts, exploration bonuses, information value and regret, and flag any place where you had to import an assumption my framework doesn't actually make in order to get the translation through.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Three observations of my own, on which I'd like your verdict. First, perturbing the value estimates with noise does not seem to help: by symmetry the perturbed estimates for a and b are identically distributed, so the expected returns are still tied, and the trick only works if I change the decision rule from 'maximise expected return' to 'act on a sampled estimate' — which is a change of criterion dressed up as a solution. Second, the regress worry — if the agent cannot choose between a and b, how does it choose to toss a coin? — seems to dissolve in my framework, because the mixing is baked into the policy by me at training time and the coin is a line of code rather than something the agent selects; but I can't tell whether that is a genuine dissolution or just a relocation of the regress to the designer. Third, the deadlock itself: the only way my vocabulary can express the ass starving is as non-termination, a policy that never emits an action, which is a bug with a timeout bolted on, not a state of agency the theory has anything to say about. So: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly, which only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? And where the residue is real, is it a defect of my framework — say, its having no notion of deliberation as opposed to policy execution, and no notion of a reason as opposed to a tie-break — or is it a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looks? If you think my three observations are wrong or question-begging, say so directly; I would rather keep my framework and give up the puzzle than keep the puzzle and be told my framework is impoverished, but I want to know what that choice commits me to.
