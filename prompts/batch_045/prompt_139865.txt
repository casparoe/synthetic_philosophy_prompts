In a graduate seminar on the ethics of belief, another student ended an exchange by reciting the Cliffordian line that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. The work it was being made to do: I had argued that in what James called a genuine option whose evidence is partly produced by the believing itself, one may believe ahead of the evidence, and that the confirmation that later arrives is not tainted by having been gathered that way. My example was a joint research project in which the only way to find out whether my collaborator was trustworthy was to commit to trusting her and see; the evidence came in, and I claimed it vindicated the earlier belief. He said the dictum settles the matter and that I had no right to that belief, and that the evidence I now cite is bootstrapped and worthless.

I do not want the dictum refuted; I want it disambiguated. Set out the distinct precise principles it could be stating, varying at least along these dimensions: what counts as evidence (is evidence obtainable only by first believing admissible? does the expected practical value of believing count as a consideration bearing on sufficiency?); insufficiency for what (truth, knowledge, or acting at acceptable risk), and whether the threshold is stakes-relative; what kind of wrong is meant (moral, epistemic, prudential; blameworthy conduct versus a defective state of the person); what the quantifiers range over (all beliefs, all degrees of confidence, or only assertion and other manifestations, and only beliefs one could have avoided); and whether the norm applies synchronically, on the evidence available before one believes, or diachronically, so that evidence the belief subsequently generates can license it after the fact.

For each reading say whether it is near-trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or unstatable — the last if it presupposes a control over belief we lack — and whether it would actually do the work he wanted: does it rule out self-fulfilling trust, or only the case of stifling doubts to avoid an unwelcome conclusion? Conclude with which reading someone deploying the line to end an argument most likely intends, and which reading is the strongest one available to me.
