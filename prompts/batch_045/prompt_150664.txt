I teach an upper-level undergraduate option in philosophy of mind at a UK university: 16 students, one 80-minute seminar in a flat room with a projector, and no equipment beyond index cards, envelopes, coloured sticky dots, two dice and a screen. The group has already met the standard line of attack on epiphenomenalism — the thought that if a subject's being in pain plays no causal role in her saying or thinking "I am in pain", then that judgment is at best a lucky coincidence and at worst something she was never entitled to make. On the page they find it far too quick: someone always points out that a common cause can secure perfect correlation without the experience doing any work, the correlation looks as good as causality for epistemic purposes, and the objection appears to evaporate.

Design me a staged activity — a game with rounds, roles and scoring, not a discussion exercise — that makes them feel the difference between three things that are easy to run together: a report's being accurate; its tracking the state it is about; and its being produced by that state. The rules, the wiring and the scoring should deliver the problem; I do not want to lecture first, and nobody should hear the word "epiphenomenalism" (or "qualia", "phenomenal concept", "zombie") until the reveals are over.

The apparatus I have in mind, which you should develop or replace if you can do better: each participant occupies a station where a hidden colour-coded lamp or card fixes their current "state" in a way they cannot see for themselves but can report on, and a separate mechanism determines what they write down. Across rounds the wiring changes — state causes report; a single randomiser causes both; the report is generated from a list I wrote before class that happens to match; one participant's reports, unknown to everyone including themselves, are "orphaned" from their states entirely — and the group's accuracy stays at or near 100% throughout. I want at least one mid-round "divergence test", where I announce a wiring change and the scores do not budge, so that participants have to concede that nothing changed for them from the inside. I also want two or three students assigned as wiring inspectors who can see the diagram but never the states, so that the room splits into a first-person verdict ("of course my reports answer to my states") and a third-person verdict ("nothing flows from state to report"), and has to work out whether those verdicts are even in conflict.

Please distribute four distinct versions of the difficulty across the rounds rather than running them all at once: the counterfactual version (had the state been different, the report would not have been); the explanatory version (the accuracy is unexplained, or explained only by a harmonising conspiracy, and so is luck); the version where accepting the theory strips the subject of her grounds for continuing to trust the very reports she arrived with; and the screening-off version, where the question is whether introspection answers to the experience or to the experience's neural common cause, which is the reply a defender will reach for. Include a closing moment in which a participant who has just scored full marks is told that, in the round just finished, her reports came from a mechanism with no access to her states, and is asked what she would now stake on her next one.

Give me the design in the following form.

Three tables:

Table 1, "Run sheet", with columns: `clock` (elapsed mm:ss), `round`, `wiring_in_force` (what causes what), `what_participants_do`, `what_facilitator_does`, `what_it_should_feel_like_from_inside`, `what_can_go_wrong_here`.

Table 2, "Debrief map", with columns: `minute`, `moment_from_the_activity`, `which_objection_it_delivers` (one of the four above), `question_to_put_to_the_room`, `answer_to_draw_out`, `misreading_to_head_off`.

Table 3, "Roles", with columns: `role`, `n`, `what_they_can_see`, `what_they_are_denied`, `brief_as_written_on_the_card`.

And one JSON object with this schema:

`title` (string); `prep` (object with `minutes`, `materials`, `room_layout`, `prepare_in_advance`); `rounds` (array of objects with `id`, `name`, `wiring`, `instructions_read_aloud`, `scoring_rule`, `reveal_and_its_timing`, `target_feeling`, `first_aid_if_it_flops`); `scoring_scheme` (object describing a scheme with no money and no real prizes); `debrief_sequence` (array of objects with `step`, `move`, `prompt`, `landing_point`); `predicted_failures` (array of objects with `failure`, `why_it_happens`, `handling`); `irreducible_features` (array of objects with `feature`, `why_the_game_cannot_deliver_it`, `how_i_supply_it_in_prose`); `naming_script` (string: two or three sentences for the moment the name finally arrives).

Be frank in `irreducible_features` about what the activity cannot stage — in particular that in the game the state is publicly checkable in a way consciousness is not, that the wiring is manipulable by an experimenter in a way the actual causal order is not, and that any reply turning on constitution or grounding rather than causation cannot be modelled by changing who is wired to whom.
