You are a professional philosopher specializing in the philosophy of mind and language, who has also spent years writing technical parody — specifications, memoranda, compliance notices — in a deadpan register. Take a moment to plan carefully before you draft anything.

The target of your parody is a view and the practice that grows out of it: the assumption that meaning is a property a physical object carries on its own, so that a competent inspector could, by examining marks, grooves, or bits, certify how much meaning an artifact contains and what it says. Against that view you will use the distinction Douglas Hofstadter develops in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979) when he asks where meaning is located in a message-bearing object such as a phonograph record launched into space or an ancient inscription nobody can read:

- the frame message — whatever signals that the object is a message at all, that there is something here to be decoded;
- the outer message — the key that tells you which language, notation, or decoding scheme applies;
- the inner message — the content that appears only once a receiver with suitable machinery and background actually applies that key.

Hofstadter's point is that none of the three is a physical property of the object; each is relative to what the receiver brings.

First, in a short planning section of about 150 to 250 words that will not count toward the piece itself, do the following: list four or five genuine features of that three-way distinction which become absurd the instant a bureaucracy tries to make them measurable — the regress of needing a frame message to tell you that the frame mark is a frame mark, the way a decoding key is itself a message needing its own key, the fact that one and the same object is a message for one receiver and mere noise for another; then decide where your document will visibly break down, and pick the concrete artifacts your inspectors will be arguing over.

Then write the piece: roughly 700 words, formatted as numbered clauses, presented as an excerpt from the compliance manual of an invented standards agency — the Bureau of Intrinsic Semantic Content, or some equally drab name of your own devising — that issues Certificates of Meaningful Content for message-bearing artifacts. Requirements:

- The humor must come from getting the trichotomy right. A reader who knows the material should recognize that you understand it and should laugh rather than wince; a reader who does not should still follow the document and finish it having understood why the certification project cannot get off the ground.
- Exaggerate real features of the view and of bureaucratic standard-setting; do not invent fake features of either. Stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no authorial asides, and no explanatory note after the document ends.
- Include at least: a recursion provision that caps or surcharges "certification depth"; a clause requiring certified messages to ship with instructions for constructing their own reading device; a clause about the Bureau's own certification stamp; and a worked example in which one and the same physical object is both certified and rejected.
- Close with a short Annex A: a Nonconformity Report filed by a field inspector confronted with an inscribed bronze tablet in a writing system no one reads, at least one line of which is a marginal comment from an exasperated epigrapher who sees exactly what the Bureau is doing wrong.

Do not quote, cite, or allude to any real document; everything should be invented, and nothing in the piece should depend on a fact the reader would have to look up.
