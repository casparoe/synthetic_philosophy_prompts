I am marking the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on freedom and responsibility, and I want a second reader on one script before I finalise the marks. The seminar covered Dennett's use of the Sphex wasp to introduce "sphexishness" — behaviour that looks purposive from the outside but is in fact a stereotyped routine that does not track the features of the situation a reasoner would respond to (Dennett takes the anecdote from Wooldridge; the word itself is Hofstadter's) — together with reasons-responsiveness accounts of responsibility, including Fischer and Ravizza's guidance control. Below is the question as it was set and one candidate's answer. For the purposes of marking, assume the standard wasp anecdote is accurate; I do not want the mark to turn on disputes in the ethology about whether the loop really is endless.

The question (25 marks):

4.(a) [8 marks] The Sphex wasp drags its paralysed cricket to the threshold of its burrow, leaves it there while it inspects the burrow, and if an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches during the inspection, retrieves it and then re-inspects — the loop can be re-induced indefinitely. Explain why a compatibilist should not treat the threat of sphexishness as merely the threat of determinism restated. What exactly is she conceding, and what is she not conceding, if she grants that some human behaviour might be sphexish?

4.(b) [12 marks] Now consider "deliberative" sphexishness: an agent whose practical reasoning always runs through the same fixed sequence of considerations and terminates in the same verdict across a wide range of evidence, so that the rigidity lies in deliberation itself rather than in a peripheral routine. Does this pose a problem for accounts that ground freedom or responsibility in reasons-responsiveness? If so, is it the same problem as in (a) or a different and harder one? Say what the remedy would have to be, and whether the remedy is one a compatibilist can accept.

4.(c) [5 marks] What would have to be true for the worry about deliberative sphexishness to be practically inert — a philosophical curiosity with no bearing on our attributions of responsibility — rather than a live threat? Say which it is.

Candidate answer:

(a) The point of the wasp case is not that the behaviour is uncaused or random, but that it is insensitive to considerations that would matter to a reasoner. The routine is triggered and then runs off; when the experimenter moves the cricket, the wasp responds to the one cue it is wired to track and re-runs the whole sequence. Nothing in the wasp corresponds to "the cricket is already where it needs to be, so the inspection is unnecessary". So what makes the behaviour unfree is not its causal history in the abstract but the fact that the considerations which would justify stopping do not register. That is why the case is not just determinism over again: determinism is a thesis about every event having sufficient causal conditions, and it is perfectly compatible with exquisite sensitivity to reasons, whereas sphexishness is a failure of sensitivity, and one could imagine it in an indeterministic system too, if a chancy trigger set off the same rigid routine.

But this is also why the case is dangerous for the compatibilist. If a human action turned out to be sphexish, we would have a case in which the agent's behaviour is the mere unfolding of a mechanically fixed routine rather than the outcome of her reasons, and that is just what the incompatibilist has always said determinism entails. A routine that runs to completion whatever the agent takes to be a reason is not responsive to anything, so discovering that our own actions are like that would settle the dispute in favour of incompatibilism. That is why Dennett treats sphexishness as the most serious threat to his own position: it is determinism's sting made visible. What separates the wasp from a free agent is that no belief or desire of the wasp's enters into the causation of its behaviour at all.

(b) Deliberative sphexishness is the harder case, because with the peripheral version we can say that the agent's capacity for practical reasoning is intact and simply fails to be consulted, whereas if the rigidity is in deliberation itself there is no intact capacity left over to appeal to. There is also an epistemic asymmetry: the experimenter can move the cricket and watch the wasp loop, but there is no corresponding perturbation that would reveal rigidity in someone's reasoning, since we do not know which "antenna" to move. The structure of the problem, though, is the same as in (a). The verdict is produced by a routine that does not track the evidence, so the agent's beliefs and desires no longer explain why she judges as she does; and the remedy is therefore the one Dennett himself reaches for — the deliberative process must not be a mechanically fixed routine, and must be genuinely indeterministic, since a deterministic search through a fixed list of considerations is just the wasp's routine run inside the head.

(c) The worry is either visible to the agent or not. If it is visible, she can correct for it: she can notice that she always arrives at the same verdict and go back and check. If it is invisible, then it is a sceptical worry of the Cartesian kind, and sceptical worries make no difference to what anyone should do, since ex hypothesi nothing in our experience or in our practices changes. As Dennett insists, a difference that makes no difference is no difference. So deliberative sphexishness either is not really sphexishness or has no consequences, and neither branch undermines responsibility.
