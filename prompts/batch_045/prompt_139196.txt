I've spent years arguing about normative uncertainty on rationalist blogs and forums, and next month I'm finally presenting my own positive view to a live audience: a joint session of my city's rationality meetup and a university moral philosophy reading group. The room will be roughly a dozen longtermist-leaning effective altruists who happily multiply tiny probabilities by astronomical stakes and treat hesitation as a vice, three or four academic philosophers who know the moral-uncertainty literature well (the case for maximizing expected choice-worthiness, the bargaining-or-parliament alternative, and the problem of comparing choice-worthiness across theories at all), and two or three decision-theory and ML people who will mainly want to know whether the view is implementable.

My thesis: don't maximize expected choice-worthiness. Instead give each candidate first-order theory a say proportional to your credence in it, but under two constraints the standard models lack. First, a volatility discount: shrink a theory's say toward the credence-weighted average verdict in proportion to how much that theory's own verdicts swing across nearby courses of action. I call the motivation the parity-of-humility move — whatever epistemic humility justifies spreading credence over moral theories should equally justify distrusting any single theory's extreme magnitudes, since those are precisely the numbers our evidence can't pin down. Second, an evidential floor: a theory whose verdict on this decision turns on a consideration about which our total evidence is silent — far-future population size, the standing of merely possible beings, whether suffering's disvalue aggregates without bound — gets its say capped at the share it would win in a hypothetical long-run bargain among the theories. The selling point I claim is that this keeps the parliamentary model's resistance to a three-percent-credence fanatical theory dictating everything, avoids collapsing back into expected-value maximization the moment you need intertheoretic comparisons, and isn't mere timidity, because the discount shrinks as evidence about the magnitudes improves.

I expect a fight about whether the view collapses into maximizing expected choice-worthiness anyway, something in the dynamic-inconsistency or money-pump family, and the accusation that the volatility discount is just a de minimis credence cutoff in disguise; tell me which of these actually lands hardest and what would tell me that it had landed. Close with the objection I'm hoping nobody asks.
