I have been asked to lead a seventy-five-minute session on Lewis's triviality result for a graduate reading group that is roughly half philosophy and half psychology. The psychologists are entirely comfortable with conditional probability, Bayes, conditioning and the law of total probability, but have no philosophy of language beyond a rough grip on implicature, and at least two of them will arrive believing that if is the material conditional with pragmatic decoration. Last night I spent three hours arguing about this with a friend who works in machine learning, and I came away unsure which of his objections I should answer in the room and which I should park. He made three claims: that the triviality result is just the old complaint that material implication gives the wrong truth conditions, so any non-truth-functional semantics fixes it; that the equation tying the probability of a conditional to the corresponding conditional probability is an empirical finding about how confident people are willing to be in if-sentences, so a semantics that cannot accommodate it has been refuted by data; and that the whole puzzle dissolves if one treats conditional probability as primitive rather than as a ratio, leaving nothing for Lewis to prove. I take the third to be a serious position and the first two to rest on confusions, but I could not say crisply why, and the plan should fix that.

So: do not explain the triviality result to me. Plan how I should explain it to them, with a whiteboard and no handout.

Give me the order in which the components have to arrive and, for each, what it depends on: what they must already have accepted before a later piece has any force. My guess at the shape is that they first need a finite space and a probability-as-weight-over-possibilities picture, then the equation as a thesis about a single conditional and a single credence function, then the discovery that one can gerrymander truth conditions to fit that one case, then the demand that the equation hold for every credence function they might move to, and only then the step where the equation is applied to a credence function that has itself been conditioned on the conditional itself. Tell me if that order is wrong and where the load-bearing step is, since I want the collapse to feel like something they walked into rather than a rabbit pulled from a hat.

On the carrying example: my candidate is a single fair die that has already been rolled and covered, with A = the result is even and B = the result is six, so the conditional probability is one third while the material conditional would be true at four of the six outcomes. Ask them which outcomes make if even then six true; they should find that the conditional must be true at exactly two outcomes, that its truth is already pinned down inside the even region, and that all the work is therefore done by a gerrymandered choice among the odd outcomes; then perturb the weights, say by raising the probability of high faces, and watch the fixed truth conditions fail to track the new conditional probability. Is that the vehicle you would build the hour around? What must such an example have and what must it lack? My own list: a space small enough to count on a board, genuine probabilistic dependence between A and B, a substantial region where A is false, re-weightability over the same outcomes, and indicatives about an unknown but already fixed outcome rather than counterfactuals, causal language, or anything that invites a would have reading. Tell me what I have missed, and whether the second credence function should be a re-weighting of the same die or a genuinely different scenario.

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than merely rebutted at the end: that this is the material-implication complaint again; that the upshot is simply that conditionals are not true or false, when I take that to be one person's conclusion and not the theorem, the theorem being that one of a small package of theses has to go; that the psychological data settles the semantics; that the argument somehow damages conditionalization or Bayesian updating; and any reading of the word triviality that takes it to mean conditionals always get probability zero or one. Two people in the room will also push specific escape routes before I get to them: one will say that if is a restrictor on a modal rather than a two-place connective, so there is no conditional proposition for the argument to grip, and another will say that conditional probability is primitive and the ratio was always optional. Say where each belongs in the sequence, what I should concede to each, and what I should explicitly decline to adjudicate in seventy-five minutes.

Finally, the check. I want one question at the end that separates someone who has learned that material implication is bad from someone who has understood the dependency structure. Mine is: a student says, fine, I will say the conditional is true at the outcomes where A and B both hold and at exactly enough A-free outcomes to make the numbers come out for my current credences, and I will reshuffle which ones those are whenever I learn something new; what has that student done? Give me a better question if you have one, and say what a right and a wrong answer would sound like. And tell me what to leave out and say out loud that I am leaving out.
