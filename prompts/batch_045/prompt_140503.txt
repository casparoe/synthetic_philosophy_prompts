I am giving a 45-minute colloquium talk with roughly 30 minutes of questions afterward, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The audience, as precisely as I can describe it: about a dozen formal epistemologists who work in modal epistemic logic, dynamic epistemic logic, and belief-revision theory, and who mostly treat Fitch's paradox of knowability as having settled the fate of the knowability thesis decades ago and moved on; two philosophers of language and logic whose own research is on truth and the paradoxes, Tarskian hierarchies, Kripkean fixed points, and revenge paradoxes, and who will hear my construction as one more hierarchy with the same familiar shape; and one metaphysician who is sympathetic to the anti-realist uses of verificationism but who holds that propositions are language-independent abstracta and will press me hard if I look as though I am sliding from propositions to sentences. Assume several of them have taught the paradox and each has a well-rehearsed favourite response to it: restrict the knowability principle, go non-classical, or distinguish actualist from possibilist readings of the quantifier over propositions. Nobody in the room is hostile to formal epistemology as such.

The thesis, and the argument I will have on the board when the questions start. Step one: I read the knowability thesis as indexed to a language. Where L is an interpreted language an epistemic agent actually possesses, the principle says that every truth expressible in L is possibly known by someone. Step two: the Fitch instance for such a truth conjoins it with the claim that nobody knows it, and that claim deploys a knowledge predicate for L; since no language of even modest strength can contain its own knowledge or truth predicate, the Fitch instance for a truth of L is expressible only in some strictly richer language. No single indexed principle is therefore closed under its own Fitch instances, and the collapse is blocked not by restricting which truths are knowable but by an expressive gap between a language and the language that can say of its truths that they are unkown. Step three: I then take the gap away from myself. Consider a community that can iterate the move, adding to each language a knowledge predicate for the previous one, and can also take the union of the resulting hierarchy; the unrestricted knowability claim for that union reinstates the collapse. Step four, which is my positive claim: the knowability thesis has to be read as an open-ended scheme, a rule that takes you from any language you actually occupy to a commitment to extend, and not as a single proposition quantifying over all truths. Verificationism is a regulative principle with built-in self-transcendence, not a thesis, and I argue that this is what it always needed to be if it was to do anti-realist work. A corollary I will mention only if time allows: in a community whose members occupy different languages, the Fitch instance requires a single agent able to quantify over everyone's knowledge states, so a community can be knowability-complete while no member of it can state that fact.

What I want from you: seven or eight objections, ordered by how likely they are from this room multiplied by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, each phrased the way the relevant subgroup would actually phrase it rather than in your own reconstruction. I want to hear the modal logician asking what logic my indexed principle has and whether it is even a proposition-forming operator, the truth theorist asking whether this is just the revenge problem again, and the metaphysician asking what happened to propositions. For each objection give me a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a mark for whether I should concede it during the talk instead of in questions. Then tell me the two objections that have genuine force rather than dialectical force, the one reply that has to land for the position to survive the hour, and what I should put on the table as a cost in my opening five minutes. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks: my own guess is that it will be either the truth theorist observing that a hierarchy with no fixed point cannot host its own knowability claim and so cannot be the semantic theory I need, or the metaphysician asking in what sense I am still a verificationist once I have given up stating the thesis, but tell me if you think the real one is something else. No literature references; I know who to cite.
