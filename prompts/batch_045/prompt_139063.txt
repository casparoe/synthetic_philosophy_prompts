I should say at the start that I have no academic background — no history, no philosophy — and that I have come to the following view by reading popular history and arguing with myself about it. Please aim your answer at a careful general reader, and please don't give me citations, references, or a reading list: I can't check them, and they aren't what I'm missing.

The position, stated as carefully as I can manage, is this: some non-human things — a pathogen, a run of cold decades, a veil of volcanic aerosol in the upper atmosphere — deserve to be counted as historical actors in their own right, and not merely as stage, weather, or "the conditions under which the real actors acted." I do not want the strong version of that thought. I am not claiming that climate or geography settles which societies prosper, and I don't want to say that human decisions become less real just because a microbe or a cold decade was also in play. So I have tried to make the claim precise with a test of my own invention, and what I want from you is an attempt to break it.

My test has two parts. The first is a removal test: delete the candidate actor from the story and ask whether what remains is a genuinely different history, or merely the same history narrated in a different vocabulary. If the counterfactual history is one we would describe as the same events with different labels — the same wars, the same successions, the same migrations, only now attributed to "hard times" rather than to the famine — then I don't think the candidate was doing actor's work; it was doing scene-setting. The second part is what I call the answer-back condition: X counts as an actor only if what X does depends in part on what humans did, so that there is a loop rather than a one-way shove. Pathogens pass this easily — settlement density, trade routes, quarantines, and crowding change which organisms get the opportunity to spread and how virulent it pays them to be, and human responses then change the pathogen in turn. Climate passes weakly and with a long lag; human land use and, much later, human emissions feed back into it, but not on the timescale of any decision anyone made. Volcanoes fail it almost completely: nothing any human did affected whether the great eruption that dimmed northern summers in the 1810s occurred, or how much material it threw up.

That result embarrasses me, since the volcano is what started me on this train of thought in the first place. I would rather keep the verdict than the origin story, but I can't tell whether that is honesty or a sign that the test is rigged.

So: what would count against this position? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd especially like you to separate three things I keep muddling together. First, whether the answer-back condition is a real discovery or just a stipulation about the word "actor" that makes my conclusion true by construction, and relatedly whether removing it or weakening it changes anything I actually care about. Second, whether the distinction between actor and background condition survives the fact that everything we treat as background is also counterfactually relevant — the atmosphere, the length of the year, the fact of agriculture — since my removal test seems to have no principled stopping point. Third, whether anything follows from the label at all: is calling a pathogen an actor meant to change what explanations I accept, what I predict, or how I assign responsibility, or is it only a vivid way of saying that non-human causes are sometimes the difference-makers?

There are three worries I have already found on my own and can't settle, and I'd rather you engage them than repeat them back to me. One: on my own account, actorhood looks relational — it is a property of a cause together with the system it hits, so that the same eruption is an actor in a brittle, grain-dependent polity and a footnote in a flexible one — which seems to mean the germ was never an actor in itself, which is not what I wanted to say. Two, the mirror problem: if germs qualify because human behaviour shapes them, then human decisions are themselves shaped by germs, childhood fevers, and nutrition, so the "human" side of the ledger is partly non-human too, and I don't know whether that collapses the distinction or is simply true and costless. Three, a selection worry: the events we call history are chosen partly for being legible on a human timescale, so non-human causes may be edited in or out depending on narrative convenience, and my test may just be measuring that editing.

Tell me what a sympathetic defender of my view should concede as counting against it, not only what a critic would say; tell me whether there is any case my view predicts that a plain humans-plus-luck history does not; tell me which of the decisive defeaters have arguably already occurred; and tell me plainly if you think I have defined my way into a claim that nothing could dislodge, and whether, for a claim of this kind, that is fatal or merely embarrassing. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a well-described case in which the very same non-human cause produced opposite outcomes in two nearby and similar societies, since that would suggest the sign of the effect belongs to the receiving system rather than to the cause, and my whole way of talking would have mislocated the agency.
