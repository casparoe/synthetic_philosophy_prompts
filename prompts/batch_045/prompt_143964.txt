Hey — hoping someone can help me sort out an argument I've been stuck on for a couple of weeks. I have no philosophy background (I work in logistics) but I fell into this after I signed up for one of those savings apps where you pre-authorize a penalty if you skip your monthly deposit, and then started reading about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment. My brother-in-law, who reads a lot of this stuff and is extremely confident, sent me a long voice note laying out what he says is just the standard view, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it to my friends. Roughly, he said: "A Ulysses contract is a deal where you hand someone else the authority to stop you from doing what you'll later want to do — Ulysses told his crew to ignore him when he begged to be untied, and the crew is the whole point, because a rational agent can't tie itself up, it needs another will to hold the rope. It's basically the same trick as a constitution: the founders know that a future parliament or a future electorate will get swept up in some panic or fall for a strongman, so they lash the country to the mast with supermajority requirements and rights that are a nightmare to repeal. The logic is identical at both scales, so anyone who complains about the 'dead hand' of the past, or about unamendable provisions, is committed to saying Ulysses was irrational to have himself tied up — which is absurd, everyone agrees he was being rational. And it isn't even paternalism, because the people doing the binding and the people being bound are the same people at different times, so nobody is imposing anything on anybody. Philosophy basically settled this decades ago: precommitment is just what rationality looks like when your preferences shift predictably, and the only interesting question left is the practical engineering one of how tight to tie the rope."

That all sounds too clean to me, but I can't say where it goes wrong. Two things in particular bug me. First, my savings app doesn't involve anyone else's will at all — the penalty just fires on its own, no human decides anything, and I'd still call that a Ulysses contract, so his definition seems to build in something that maybe isn't essential? Second, the constitution case feels different in a way I can't quite articulate: I tie myself to the mast, sure, but my kids, and the people who voted against the thing, are also tied there by founders who are long dead. When I said that he replied that "the people is a continuing agent over time," which felt like a dodge, but I don't know why.

So concretely: can you go through his summary and tell me which bits are basically right, which are subtle misreadings or overstatements, and which a philosopher who actually works on this would push back on? Does his conclusion follow — if I grant that Ulysses was rational, am I really committed to cheering for entrenched or unamendable constitutional provisions? And one thing I made up myself that I can't let go of: imagine a constitution with a "decaying threshold" rule, where the supermajority needed to amend a provision starts at 90% right after it's enacted and then decays by, say, three points a year down to a floor of 50% plus one, so the founders' grip loosens on a schedule they themselves chose. Is that more Ulysses-like or less Ulysses-like than a fixed supermajority? My instinct is that it's more like the original story, since Ulysses expected to be let go once the danger had passed, but my brother-in-law says it's less, and I genuinely can't tell which of us is talking nonsense.
