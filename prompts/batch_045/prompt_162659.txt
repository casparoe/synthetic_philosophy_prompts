Hey — need a second pair of eyes on an argument I've been going in circles with.

A friend who works on wild animal welfare hit me with this at dinner and I cant put it down:

(1) The expected disvalue tied up in a population is: (credence that its members have valenced experience) × (number of individuals) × (expected suffering per individual per unit time, conditional on their having any).
(2) Insects outnumber vertebrates by something like five to seven orders of magnitude. I've seen a quintillion thrown around for insects alive at any moment; I'm not going to defend the figure, since anything above three orders of magnitude does the work.
(3) So even at a credence of 0.001 that insects have valenced states, and even if a given insect's suffering per unit time is only a hundredth of a vertebrate's, the expected suffering locked up in insects is orders of magnitude larger than the expected suffering in all vertebrates combined, where the credence is near 1 but the numbers are small.
(4) When one option reduces more expected suffering than another and nothing else distinguishes them, you should take it.
(5) So insect suffering dominates the priority list — not "is worth thinking about," but dominates, to where working on vertebrate suffering looks like a rounding error.

I basically believe that, or at least I can't say what's wrong with it. Here's where it turns into something I can't accept:

(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) uses anything specific to insects. Run it on soil mites and nematodes, which outnumber insects by another couple of orders; then on bacteria and archaea, which outnumber those by many more. For any population with credence p and number N, there's a smaller and vaster one with p′ and N′, and the swamping repeats as long as credence doesn't fall faster than numbers rise.
(7) Nothing in the argument guarantees that it does fall faster. Our credence that bacteria have valenced states isn't zero in any sense that would make the product zero, and "it's lower than our credence about insects" isn't enough — it has to be lower by more than the ratio of the numbers, and there's no floor on how big that ratio gets.
(8) But a line of reasoning that, every time you apply it, tells you to drop what you're doing and work on something smaller and more numerous, never delivers an answer. And (5) purported to deliver one.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

Things I've been chewing on and can't sort out:

- Maybe (1) is wrong, or at least incomplete: with insects the sentience question is basically one global hypothesis — either beetles have valenced states or they don't — so it isn't like per-individual coin flips that even out across a quintillion cases. The whole total swings on one thing I can't resolve, which makes the variance insane. But I don't see why that by itself gets me out of (5); expected value is supposed to cope fine with unresolvable uncertainty.
- Maybe the regress does stop, because of a third factor I left out of (1): as you go down in body size and nervous-system size, not only does credence fall and number rise, but the per-individual term plausibly collapses too — coarser temporal grain (fewer experiences per second), lower peak intensity, less memory or anticipation to suffer about. If per-individual suffering falls faster than numbers rise, the product peaks somewhere — maybe near insects, maybe well below them — and there's a stopping point after all, just an empirical one. I kind of like this, but it means (5) rests on a guess about how intensity scales with nervous-system size that nobody can presently cash out, which is a strange place for a moral conclusion to sit.
- Maybe (4) is where it breaks: total expected suffering in a population isn't the same as expected suffering you can actually move per unit of effort, and for insects the sign of our effect is basically unknown — we don't know whether anything we could do raises or lowers total insect-years of suffering. But then I'm not sure if I've refuted (5) or just attached a caveat.

I'd rather keep the thought that vast numbers can offset a low probability than end up committed to prioritizing nematodes over everything, and I definitly don't want to throw out expected value wholesale. I've also been toying with a hack: keep (1) but swap p for something like p², or discount p by how resolvable the hypothesis is — a "resolution-weighted" probability that gives less credit to hypotheses we have no way of ever checking. Is that a principled fix or just moving the bump in the carpet?

Anyway: name the step, say why it looked fine, and tell me what I'm paying on each of the exits I don't take.
