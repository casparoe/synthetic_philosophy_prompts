Suppose you are advising a five-person working group that has about fourteen months to produce the normative framework a large hospital system will actually use to decide who speaks for patients with advanced dementia, and on what basis. The board has already committed the system to a relational conception of personhood of the ubuntu kind — that who someone is, and what is owed to her, is partly a function of her relationships rather than only of her memories, preferences, or decision-making capacity — and the group is not to relitigate that commitment. Its job is to turn it into guidance a bedside nurse or an ethics consultant can apply on a Tuesday afternoon.

Three cases keep blocking the group, and I want them treated as stress tests rather than as side issues. Call them the thin-network case: the relationships that constituted the patient have dissolved and been replaced almost entirely by paid staff who have known her two years. The hostile-network case: her surviving relationships are real and thick, but they are also where the harm came from, or they carry obvious conflicts of interest. And the directive case: she left detailed instructions while capacitated, and the relationships that now constitute her point the other way.

Which three or four open conceptual questions should this group spend its limited time on, and in what order? I want the ordering defended rather than listed: for each question, what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is in fourteen months for generalist philosophers working with clinicians, and what evidence or argument would move it up or down the list. I also want to know which questions you would deliberately deprioritize even though they look important — the metaphysical-status question of whether relational personhood is really a claim about what constitutes a self or only about what we owe one another, for instance — and why settling them would not change the guidance.
