I am a novelist, and I would be grateful for a close reading rather than a survey of positions.

Let me give you the problem I am actually trying to solve. In the novel I am drafting, the narrator has moved into the house of a sister who disappeared some years earlier and was never found — no body, no confirmed sighting, no return. In the garden, at the back door, at the turn of the stairs, the narrator keeps encountering her. I want these encounters to carry real structural weight, and I have been drafting them in what I call the *unsettled disjunct*: the prose is built so that it is equally satisfied whether the sister is there to be seen or whether the narrator is producing her, and the book will never adjudicate between the two. A philosopher friend, hearing this, said something to the effect that on the view called disjunctivism there is no such thing as *the experience the narrator is having either way* — that a veridical seeing and a hallucination indistinguishable from it share no common mental kind — and that therefore, in withholding the disjunct, I may not be withholding anything. I want to know whether that is right, and whether it indicts my technique or only my account of it.

The reading I would like is of Henry James's The Turn of the Screw, which seems to me the great precedent for exactly this ambition, and which I take to be built on the same refusal. To make the request self-contained: the argument from illusion, as I understand it, starts from cases where things look or seem exactly as they would in a genuine Perception — an illusion, a mirage, a hallucination — and argues, often by way of a common immediate object of awareness such as a sense-datum, that the good case and the bad case must nonetheless be states of the same fundamental kind, differing at most in what causes them or in what, if anything, they go on to tell us. Disjunctivism denies that shared kind: it holds that the disjunction of seeing and of merely seeming to see is not to be analysed into a common core plus an extra relation, and, in its metaphysical form, that the two are different kinds of state altogether; in its epistemic form, that what one has to go on in the good case is not the same as what one has to go on in the bad. My questions are about where James's practice sits relative to that quarrel.

First, a wrinkle I would like you to take seriously before anything else. If the figures the governess encounters at Bly are real, they are mind-independent objects of a sort, however private to her and however uncorroborated. On most disjunctivist accounts, then, her relation to them should count as perceiving, not as hallucinating. Does that entail that the old quarrel between readers who take the apparitions to be real and readers who take them to be projections of the governess is, in disjunctivist terms, not a quarrel about her mind at all but a quarrel about the furniture of the world — and that the novella's famous undecidability is an undecidability about ontology that the philosophy of perception has quietly been treating as a question about experience? If so, what happens to the use both sides have wanted to make of the novella?

Second, the question of technique, which is the one I most need. James renders the apparitions with the full density of perception — the tower, the lake, the staircase — and does not modulate his prose for the bad case; the writing describes them in the same terms whether or not they are there. Does that literary neutrality presuppose precisely the highest common factor the disjunctivist denies, namely that there is a fully characterisable what-it-was-like-for-her detachable from which disjunct obtains? Or can it be defended as an indeterminacy in the *description* rather than in the state described — the difference between a text's declining to say and a state's having no determinate nature? I would like this argued both ways and then adjudicated, with whatever you take the stronger consideration to be, because the defence or the failure of James's neutrality is the defence or failure of mine.

Third, a device I am also using and would like named properly. I call it the *retrospective settlement*: a late event appears to decide what an earlier scene had been all along. At Bly the obvious candidate is the final scene with Miles, and the naming of Quint, on which readers have always leaned to settle the ghosts one way or the other. If a later scene settles the disjunct, does it go back and alter what the earlier experience *was*, or only what we are entitled to say about it? Is this a place where the metaphysical and the epistemic versions of disjunctivism come apart in a way a novelist needs to know about, since a novelist can put a settlement in the last chapter and readers will feel it as retroactive?

I would also ask you to say plainly where the reading fails. The argument from illusion, at least under that name, belongs to a philosophical generation later than James's, and his own vocabulary is one of visions and impressions rather than of sense-data; there is a real question whether a fiction can be held to a thesis about the metaphysics of experience at all, or whether reading it that way is a category mistake that flatters the philosophy more than it illuminates the book. I would rather you said so than produced a tidy result.

Finally, on the level of conviction: I would like you to tell me, at the end, what would change your mind about your central verdict — which consideration, whether a reading of a particular scene, a distinction you think I have collapsed, or a better account of what hallucination is, would lead you to abandon the reading — and to say where you think your own reasoning is likeliest to break, as distinct from where it is merely open to dispute.

One practical note: I am away from any library and from my own text, so please work from the broad shape of the novella as it is generally known, argue the points rather than cite authorities, and do not quote — I know the book well enough to follow you without it.
