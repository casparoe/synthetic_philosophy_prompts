I serve on a committee at my workplace that is redesigning how employees are enrolled in benefits, and over the past year I have had to work out, more or less on my own and without any training in philosophy, what I actually think about paternalism and nudging. I have ended up with the set of commitments below, and before I try to defend them in front of the rest of the committee I would like to know whether they are jointly tenable. I have a vague sense that two or three of them are pulling against each other, but whenever I try to locate the problem it moves, so I would rather not say where I suspect it is.

1. Means, not ends. I accept being steered when it makes it likelier that I reach the ends I already have, and I reject it when it substitutes someone else's judgment about what is good for me. I am aware that the line between helping someone get what they want and deciding what they should want may be doing more work than it can bear, but I cannot say where it fails.

2. No exploitation of quirks. An intervention is illegitimate if it works by getting me to act on a feature of my psychology that I would not, on reflection, want to be acting on. The cases I have in mind are defaults I stick with out of inertia, or because I read the default as a recommendation, or because dealing with it now rather than later feels costly.

3. Easy exit. I accept arrangements I can escape at trivial cost, and I reject bans, mandates and anything that materially burdens an option. Relatedly, I find the asymmetric case persuasive: an intervention that does a great deal of good for people who are prone to error, while costing people who are not prone to it essentially nothing, is especially hard to object to.

4. Sunset defaults, as I have been calling them. I have been pushing for defaults that expire: automatic enrolment would be set at the option most employees would choose if they thought about it, but every two years the default lapses and I would have to make an active choice before enrolment continues. My reasoning is that a default nobody ever revisits turns into exactly the thing that commitment 2 forbids, while a default you periodically have to re-confirm does not.

5. Publicity. I want to be told, in plain terms, what the intervention is and why it is set where it is, and still be free to choose otherwise. If a policy works only as long as people do not notice it, I do not want it.

6. Third-party benefit. I also support defaulting everyone into the renewable electricity tariff, even though the people who mainly benefit are not the people being defaulted. My reason is that nearly everyone says they want to do this and simply never gets around to it.

What I would like is an audit rather than an introduction. Please go through the list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are; rank the genuine tensions by how severe they are; and for each one say which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost me — in particular whether I would have to surrender a case I actually care about, such as automatic enrolment itself. Then tell me whether there is one revision, short of abandoning the whole position, that would clean up the most, and what it would commit me to elsewhere. If one of the commitments is unstable on its own rather than merely in conflict with the others, please say so separately. Please argue from the content of the commitments themselves rather than from what any particular author or school has said; I will not be able to follow references to a literature I have not read.
