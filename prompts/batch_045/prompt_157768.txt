I'm a novelist — three published books, the most recent in close third person — and I teach an occasional graduate workshop in fiction, so the vocabulary I actually think in is the vocabulary of craft: point of view, narrative distance, free indirect style, the difference between showing a character's state and asserting it, the discipline of scene, the way a later event retroactively changes the meaning of an earlier one, revision as re-seeing rather than polishing, and the fact that attention in a novel is finite and has to be budgeted across characters. Over the past year I have been reading about care ethics in a scattered way — mostly summaries and encyclopedia-length overviews, nothing I could call study — because a friend insisted it would help me make sense of the two years I spent as my mother's main carer while she had vascular dementia. I take myself to know the headlines: that moral thought should begin from relationships rather than from isolated agents choosing under principles; that the impartial, detached standpoint is a distortion rather than an achievement; that care is a practice with standards internal to it rather than a warm sentiment; that partiality toward those you are in a relationship with is a feature and not a vice; that care is systematically devalued, exploitable, and exhausting. What I lack is any felt grip on why anyone holds this, because I have met it in terms I do not speak.

So I want a systematic translation, not an explanation pitched at a beginner. Take each of the central care-ethics commitments — the relational starting point; the critique of impartialist morality and of principle-based moral judgment; the staged account of care that the literature seems to converge on, roughly: noticing a need, taking responsibility for it, performing the work competently, and checking whether it actually landed with the person cared for; the treatment of partiality; and the worries about exploitation and depletion — and restate it as faithfully as you can in my craft vocabulary, flagging at every step what you had to bend. I especially want you to work with the resources fiction gives you rather than the easy ones: the contrast between the omniscient narrator's view from everywhere and the embedded voice that cannot see past one character's attachments; the way a novelist's knowledge of a character is built up through accumulated scenes rather than applied from a rule; the practice, which I rely on heavily, of reading a scene back in the voice of the character whose scene it is to check whether the rendering has landed; and the economy of attention, the fact that I can make a reader care about three named people and not about a casualty figure. Then give me the residue report: which commitments carry over cleanly, which survive only after being distorted into something care ethics would not recognise, and which have no expression in craft talk at all — with your view, in each case, on whether the gap is a poverty in my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it sounds.

Two things I want you to take seriously rather than nod along to. First, the practice I just described — reading back in the character's voice to check whether the work landed — looks to me like an exact structural analogue of the care-ethics criterion that care must be answerable to the cared-for's uptake. But in fiction the character's voice is itself mine: I authored the uptake I then consult, so the check threatens to be self-confirming, and I suspect the same circularity is available to a carer whose framing of a situation partly shapes how the person cared for responds. I'd like you to say plainly whether that worry has real force against the responsiveness criterion or whether my analogue is misleading, and if it is misleading, where exactly it breaks. Second, the attention budget: my suspicion is that craft vocabulary can express care ethics' partiality beautifully and has nothing at all to say about how attention ought to be distributed across many others, so that care ethics has to borrow from the impartialist theories it criticises the moment it addresses institutions or triage. Tell me if that suspicion is right, or if it rests on a picture of care ethics as more anti-theoretical or more domestic in scope than it actually is. Work from memory and do not go looking for sources I can't check; but be explicit about where you are reconstructing a position you are not confident about, distinguish what the view commits its proponents to from what you find plausible in it, and mark the places where your translation is doing constructive work rather than reporting. And if the whole request rests on something confused — if, say, the two senses of attention or of uptake are a pun rather than a genuine structural parallel, or if care ethics already has a standard answer to the circularity worry that I have simply missed — say so directly instead of producing a polite translation of a muddle.
