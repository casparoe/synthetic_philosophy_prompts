I'm in the final year of an undergraduate historiography seminar — the course is organised around how narratives of the past get constructed rather than around any particular period — and the portfolio lets us substitute one "imitation" exercise for a conventional essay: a piece of mock-discursive prose in a real administrative or journalistic genre, submitted with a short note saying which feature of actual historical practice it exaggerates. I'd like to spend mine on the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present, and I'd be grateful for the piece itself.

What I want is a mock government consultation document — a call for evidence, 900–1,100 words — issued by an invented unit inside a national curriculum authority (something like an Office for Narrative Coherence), proposing a reform I'll call the trunk-and-branch curriculum: history to be taught backwards from the present, along the line of descent that actually produced the settlement we now have, with "dead branches" — defeated alternatives, abandoned programmes, movements that failed — pruned from the mandatory content and retained only as optional enrichment. Format it properly: numbered paragraphs, a short definitions section, one worked example, an equality-and-impact section, and a closing set of numbered questions for consultees. No footnotes or citations anywhere; I'll write the accompanying note myself.

The voice matters more than the gags. Deadpan civil-service register throughout: the document should be procedurally competent, internally consistent, and entirely unembarrassed — it should never wink, never hedge, never announce that it is being absurd. It must also not use the vocabulary of the debate: the words for the practice itself, and the terms for outcome-directed explanation and for present-centred judgement, may not appear anywhere in the document. Recognition has to come from the mechanism, not the label.

Three things the piece has to get right, because they're where a lazy version would go wrong.

First, the target isn't naive boosterism. It's the self-aware version: the move that says every narrative must select, that selection is unavoidably guided by what later mattered, and that we should therefore stop pretending and make the present-orientation an explicit, accountable, rule-governed procedure rather than a hidden one. The definitions section should separate three things the proposal claims to keep apart — using an outcome as a device for choosing topics, using it as part of the explanation of why earlier people acted as they did, and using it as a claim about what had to happen — and the document should insist, in so many words, that it adopts only the first. The comedy then comes from its sliding, without ever admitting it, into the second and the third. Give it a replacement for the two idioms of outcome-directed verdict that ordinary polemic uses (praise for being ahead of one's time, blame for being on the wrong side of it): a neutral-sounding technical score, say a temporal alignment rating, assigned by comparing an actor's stated aims with the settlement we now have. Have the document notice that this systematically down-rates people who could not have known about that settlement, and introduce a "fairness adjustment" that simply relabels them under a new technical term while leaving the verdict untouched.

Second, give the reformers the strongest argument actually available to them, and let it be an archival one: surviving records are unevenly distributed because record-keeping tracks administrative capacity, and administrative capacity correlates with who eventually won, so the archive has already returned a verdict, and pruning merely makes explicit what the sources had already decided. I'd like at least one paragraph where the absence of documentation for a defeated movement is treated as evidence of that movement's insignificance. Don't let the document be stupid about this — let the argument be good enough that I have to think about where it breaks.

Third, the reductio has to be internal and unnoticed. Include a pilot-evaluation paragraph reporting an unexpected result from a trial school: where alternatives are pruned, students stop treating the present as something anyone achieved or failed to achieve, and conclude that if the outcome was always coming, nobody deserves credit and nobody deserves blame. The document's proposed remedy should make this worse, not better — my suggestion is a licensed contingency provision, a small quota of officially preserved heritage alternatives, selected and annotated by the unit and designated non-load-bearing, so that the emotional payoff of progress is retained while the risk that anyone takes an alternative seriously is removed. It should not register that manufactured alternatives cannot do the work that real ones did, and it should close on a complacent administrative note about stakeholder confidence and the next phase of implementation.

Two constraints on content. The worked example should be a single real episode in which the defeated alternative is a matter of public record rather than a modern reconstruction, and in which the case for it was reasonable at the time it was made; and nothing in the joke may rest on a causal claim a specialist would contest, since the satire should survive even if the details of the example are contested. And if you want to use the fact that the party after which the practice is named did not itself endure, keep it to one line and don't let it carry the piece.

Aim the whole thing so that a historiographer recognises the mechanism being exaggerated and doesn't think it's a straw man, while a first-year with no background in the literature still finds it funny and can say what's being sent up.
