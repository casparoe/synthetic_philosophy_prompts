Putting together my position on the knowledge argument for a reading group and I want it stress-tested before I present, because I've got the feeling I'm paying for the same thing twice somewhere and I can't locate where.

Where I've landed:

1. Mary gains something genuinely new on release. I dont buy the move that she already knew everything and the apparent gain is just surprise, or a shift in what she's attending to.
2. What she gains is propositional knowledge, not know-how. The ability story fails because knowing what something feels like and being able to imagine / recognise / recall it come apart — you know what a toothache is like even when you can't call one up.
3. Physicalism is true, so there are no new facts. Her new knowledge has to be knowledge of an old fact in a new guise.
4. The guise is a phenomenal concept, and phenomenal concepts aren't acquirable a priori: no amount of reasoning from the complete physical story hands you one, you have to have had the experience. That isn't a lacuna in the physical description — it's just the kind of thing a phenomenal concept is.
5. The fading-Mary case: she sees red, then loses colour vision and with it any ability to imagine, recognise or bring back what red looked like. I want to say she still knows what red looks like afterwards — she'd truly say "I know what it looked like, I just can't picture it any more" — and I want that to fall out of 2 rather than be an extra claim I'm helping myself to.
6. What she learns is ineffable: she can't tell the other scientists what she learned, and it's not a vocabulary failure on her part.

Which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest fix for each? Rank them by severity — I don't want six equal-sized problems. Say what would have to give in each case, and which single revision would tidy up the most. If one of them is fine as it stands, say that; I'd rather you didn't flag everything.
