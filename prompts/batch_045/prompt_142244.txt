At work we're rewriting the internal guidance for incident writeups (I was a psych major before I drifted into ML infra, which is apparently why I got voluntold for this), and we keep going in circles over one sentence pattern. Someone writes "the model believed the user was in France," and then someone else redlines it into "the model's outputs were consistent with the user being in France," which is honest but completely useless when you're trying to explain to a support lead why their bot did something dumb. Ravi on my team thinks the redline is philosophy cosplay and that of course the model has beliefs, in whatever the only available sense of "has beliefs" turns out to be — he says around 0.7. I'm at maybe 0.2. My sister's a vet and keeps pointing out that nobody loses sleep over whether her dog really believes the leash means walk, and I don't have a good answer for why the model case is different, which is part of why I'm asking.

So: give me your credence that current frontier language models have beliefs. I don't mean anything vague about consciousness or moral status — I mean specifically that at least some of their internal states are genuinely about things in the world in the way a belief ascription says they are, such that "the model believes p" can be straightforwardly true rather than merely a useful façon de parler or a shorthand for a prediction. Before you give me the number, pin the proposition down yourself and say what would even count as this being settled, because I suspect there's no clean experiment that just decides it. Then decompose. Here are the sub-questions I'd want you to use or replace with better ones: (1) whether the intentional stance — predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires — is ever more than an instrument, i.e. whether there's a real difference left between "usefully predictable as if it believed p" and "believes p" once you cash both out; (2) the wrinkle I think people skip: the classic picture lets you in principle predict anything from the physical or design stance and take the intentional stance only when that's too expensive, but with a trained model nobody can actually derive its behavior from the weights in any usable way, so the intentional stance isn't one option among three, it's the only game in town — does being forced into a stance by our own computational limits count for or against its deliverables being true; (3) cheap satisfaction: these things were optimized to produce the continuations believers produce, first-person avowals included, so if the behavioral and verbal tests for belief are exactly what the training objective targeted, how much evidential weight does passing them carry, and does your number move at all if I tell you the model was fine-tuned never to emit "I believe"; (4) content indeterminacy: even granting belief-like states, how confident are you that we can pin a specific content like "the user is in France" onto a given state, given that charitable interpretation normally leans on a shared cognitive architecture and here there isn't one — is there a fact of the matter about which of several behaviorally equivalent ascriptions is correct; (5) the mediation question: how much of your number rides on some internal variable genuinely causing the downstream behavior across contexts, the sort of thing interpretability people go looking for, as opposed to being a post-hoc story we tell about outputs — give me a number for that, and a separate one for how likely we are to actually find out within, say, ten years of mech interp work; (6) how much of your uncertainty is really the worry that the question is ill-posed, that "has beliefs" is a word with a use that just doesn't extend cleanly to this kind of thing.

Combine them into the number, tell me which single term your final credence is most sensitive to, what one finding would move it the most, and whether you think Ravi and I at 0.7 and 0.2 are disagreeing about the world or about a word. Sections in this order, please: "The proposition" (a short paragraph, your pinned-down reading of what's being claimed, plus what would count as settling it), "My number" (one line, give me a single figure — a range is fine if you insist), "The decomposition" (the bulk of it, one short paragraph per sub-question with its number and a one-line justification, and tell me if you dropped or added any), "How it combines" (about a paragraph), "Where Ravi and I disagree" (about a paragraph), "What would move me" (about a paragraph).
