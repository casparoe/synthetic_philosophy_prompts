I want an exposure map for a position I have arrived at about polarization, and I would rather you not handle it gently. The position is not that polarization is exaggerated; it is that the specific belief that the other side argues in bad faith is, in the main, manufactured rather than observed, and that the manufacturing has three stages.

First, a sampling asymmetry. Inside your own coalition you meet the median member: colleagues, neighbours, the unglamorous local official. Across the coalition boundary you meet the tail, because attention economies pay a visibility premium to provocation and pay nothing to nuance. So the comparison your social environment performs for you is between your side's centre and their side's extreme, and it is performed before you have any occasion to perform it yourself.

Second, what I call the monitoring-mode effect. Out-party speech is consumed as threat intelligence, not as evidence: the question asked of it is what are they about to do to us, not is this true. Reading in that posture does not update you even when the content is good. This is why I think the echo-chamber diagnosis is close to backwards. The deficit is not exposure — exposure to out-party content is historically abundant — but the posture in which exposure is received, which means that increasing exposure can increase antipathy.

Third, a reciprocity ratchet. Extending good faith is worth it only if it is reciprocated, and you cannot observe the other side's type directly, only its behaviour under conditions you have partly helped create. Unilateral defection is cheap and self-confirming: my bad faith licenses yours, which confirms my original belief. A belief that was false when formed can become true by being acted on, which is why the first two mechanisms can be errors and the third can still be real.

Two stipulations intended to keep the view from being mush. By good faith I do not mean sincerity; most people on both sides say what they believe, and sincerity is cheap. I mean auditability: you would still hold the view if your side's standing did not depend on holding it. My claim is that failure of auditability is common, roughly symmetric, and largely invisible from the inside. And the view has a testable edge: I expect the share of out-partisans who can paraphrase your side's case in terms you would yourself accept to track perceived bad faith far better than any measure of issue disagreement or of raw exposure volume, and I expect interventions aimed at that paraphrase capacity to beat both fact-checking and exposure-building.

What I want is what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Not the objections a critic would raise from outside, but the ones a defender of my position should concede count against it. Separate what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward; say which of these things have arguably already happened rather than being hypothetical; and say what the view predicts that no rival diagnosis predicts, since a view that predicts nothing distinctive cannot be tested at all. I would also like you to tell me where I have insulated myself. In particular: if the reciprocity ratchet can absorb any observation of bad faith as something we caused, my view cannot lose, and I would rather be told that plainly than be protected from it — along with your judgment on whether such insulation is fatal for a view of this kind or an acceptable cost of a view about a self-confirming phenomenon. The place I am least confident is the separation of visibility from power: if the tail that collects the visibility premium is also the tail that sets agendas through primaries, donor bases, staff pipelines and platform incentives, then you are only seeing their tail stops being reassuring, because the tail is the part that acts. I fund cross-partisan dialogue work, so this is not academic for me: the three mechanisms point at incompatible interventions.
