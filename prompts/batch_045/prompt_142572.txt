I'm in a final-year seminar on predictive processing and the free energy principle, and I keep tripping over one sentence that gets repeated in that literature and in our discussions as though it settled things. Our seminar has more or less split into two camps about how literally to take it, and last week it was used to shut down an argument I actually cared about, so before I write my response paper I want to know what the sentence could precisely be claiming.

The case we were discussing was invented by our TA: two subjects in a hypothetical interface setup. In the first, an implant intercepts the optic nerve and supplies, at every moment, exactly the input the subject's generative model predicted, so precision-weighted prediction error stays at zero no matter what happens in front of him — the room could catch fire and his stream would still match his priors. In the second, a subject wears inverting optics so gradually introduced that she descends the error gradient and relearns; months later her predictions match the transformed input, error is again near zero, and, the part I dug my heels in about, she now sees the world the right way up again. My classmate's response was that the two cases don't differ in any interesting respect, because perception is controlled hallucination: in both subjects experience is generated by the model and not by the light, so there is no fact of the matter about who is perceiving and who is hallucinating, and my question was confused from the start. The same sentence got used later in the hour to wave off the dark-room worry, on the grounds that the objection only bites if you think the brain is trying to match the world, which on this view it isn't.

What I'd like is a disambiguation rather than a verdict in my favour. Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest — along the lines of: the platitude that expectation, context and learning influence what we see; the Helmholtz-flavoured claim that perception is inference or hypothesis-testing; the claim that the content of experience is supplied by the model's predictions rather than by the sensory signal; the continuity claim that perceiving and hallucinating are the same kind of state differing only in how tightly they are constrained by input; and whatever stronger reading — a thoroughgoing indirect realism, or something close to idealism — it would take to underwrite the claim that no perceptual state is ever genuinely about the world. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false or incoherent, what sort of evidence would tell for or against it, and, the part that matters for the paper, whether that reading would actually get my classmate his conclusion about the two cases and about the dark-room objection. If none of them does the work he needs, say so plainly and tell me what extra premise he would have to smuggle in to make the slogan carry the argument.
