At the investing-and-forecasting reading group I go to, people repeat that 'Kelly betting maximizes your long-run growth rate' as if that closed the discussion. It came up last week about a favourable-coin game: each round you may stake any fraction of your current bankroll at even odds, winning the stake with probability 0.6 and losing it otherwise — so the usual Kelly fraction is 20% — but with a hard floor: if your bankroll ever falls below 10% of its starting value, the game ends and you walk away with nothing. Someone said this shows that staking anything other than 20% is straightforwardly irrational, and that those of us who wanted a smaller fraction were 'paying a volatility tax for nothing'.

I'd like a disambiguation rather than a verdict. What distinct precise theses could that slogan be expressing — about which quantity is supposedly maximized (expected log wealth per round, the almost-sure asymptotic growth rate, the median or mode of the wealth distribution, the probability of being ahead after a fixed number of rounds), about which class of rival strategies it is compared against, and about what horizon and what sense of 'beats'? For each reading on that menu, say whether it is true, whether it is a near-platitude or a substantive result, and whether it would really license the step from 'not Kelly' to 'irrational' rather than merely to 'aimed at something else'.

Then say how the 10% floor interacts with all this. Does it leave the optimal constant fraction unchanged, does it make the optimal stake state-dependent rather than a fixed fraction of wealth, and in which direction does it push someone whose bankroll is currently close to the floor? Assume I know the standard derivation of the Kelly fraction, so don't re-derive it.
