I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on the ethics of killing and letting die, and I want to use Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* as my case text instead of another variation on the standard hospital cases. A friend and I argued about it for most of an evening after watching the film adaptation together, and neither of us moved: she maintained that the scandal of the novel is that the donation programme never kills anyone at all — it arranges for bodies to be fatally vulnerable and then simply declines to cure them, and everyone inside the book, donors included, colludes in treating that as innocence; I maintained that setting up a lethal threat and then not removing it is not an omission of any interesting kind, and that what Ishiguro is really dramatizing is how administrative distance converts killing into paperwork, so that no individual ever performs the act and the act nonetheless gets performed. We then discovered we couldn't say which of these the novel itself supports, or whether it deliberately declines to choose.

The distinction I've been working with in the seminar is a three-way one, not the usual two: (i) an agent who initiates a lethal sequence; (ii) a mere bystander who could rescue and doesn't; and (iii) what I've been calling a *constituted omission* — I'm coining the term — where the agent is causally responsible for the threat's being there in the first place, but whose final contribution is an omission to remove it. The live question in the seminar is whether (iii) is really a version of (i) wearing a disguise, and more generally whether the doing/allowing distinction does any independent moral work or is only a stand-in for causal responsibility, intention, and rights. I'd like a reading of the novel through that question: where would you place Hailsham and the programme as a whole — including Miss Emily and Madame, who built a humane school precisely so that the donations would go better — and where would you place Kathy herself, whose job is literally to accompany donors through their donations and completions and to make the process tolerable? I'm especially interested in the scenes that seem to me to do the most philosophical work: the official line that the artworks were meant to show the clones had souls, the rumour of deferral for couples who could prove they were in love, the scene by the beached boat where that hope is taken away, Ruth's attempt to make amends by giving Tommy and Kathy a lead, and the long stretch of Kathy's caring for Tommy as he donates and then completes. Does the novel take a position on whether what is done to these people is killing, letting die, or a third thing the distinction cannot see?

Two further things I want, and one thing I don't. What I don't want is padding: no plot summary, no quotation, no bibliography or reading list — I have the book in front of me and I want the argument. What I do want is, first, honesty about where the reading fails: my worry is that the novel isn't really about the act/omission distinction at all, since everyone completes eventually and the book may be deliberately letting the programme stand in for ordinary mortality, in which case pressing the distinction on it might be a category mistake, and I'd rather be told that than have the reading forced. And second, I want a closing claim about what the novel adds to the debate that the stylized cases don't: my sense is that it has something to say about how institutions distribute a doing across many hands until nobody does it, about the difference it makes that the victims narrate their own deaths without protest, and about whether the doing/allowing distinction might survive as a distinction about what an agent can be asked to feel or apologize for even if it collapses as a distinction about wrongness. I'd like you to take both the novel and the distinction seriously and tell me which of us, if either, the text vindicates.
