Me and a friend spent about two hours after dinner last night going round on the Löbian obstacle and got nowhere, and I've got to open the last session of our AI safety reading group this term and I'd rather do it with a poem than a slide deck.

Where we left it: I think the obstacle is structural, she thinks it's an artefact of demanding proof at all (her line: a real agent wouldn't sit around waiting for a proof, it'd act on expected value and just build the thing). Her fix for the obstacle proper is the descent one — each generation hands off to a slightly weaker system, which it can prove sound — and I think that just moves the bump down the ladder, since you can only ever step down and never sideways, so somewhere the chain bottoms out and whatever's at the bottom either stops improving or trusts without a proof. (I tried my own way out — don't prove it's safe, prove it's no worse than not building it — and she pointed out i hit the same wall one step later.)

Form: six stanzas, 6/5/4/3/2/1 lines, so the poem physically shrinks as it goes, and each stanza's closing line is inherited by the next stanza as its opening line with one word dropped — the child always gets the parent's last line minus a word. So the final line is a single surviving word, and it should be a word that obviously can't carry the weight (I keep hearing it land on "if", the whole chain bottoming out in a conditional that never gets its consequent, but use something better if you've got it).

Accuracy, because a few people in the room have actually read the tiling stuff and will wince otherwise: the agent isn't just unlucky at finding a proof, it's blocked — if it could prove "if I can prove the successor is safe, then it is safe", it would thereby prove the successor safe without ever having checked anything, and a system that helped itself to that move in general would prove every sentence, false ones included. The descent move isn't wrong though: you really can prove a weaker system sound, the hand-off does work, it only ever works downward. I want the poem to sit inside that and not pick a side, and I want it to still read as a poem for the two people there who don't know the math.
