I'm part of a small team writing the conduct code and the internal risk metric for a development organization that operates in roughly thirty countries, and we have got as far as discovering that the word corruption appears in every section of the draft and is defined in none of them. Before we look at any of the standard definitions on offer, I want the specification: what would a working definition of corruption have to deliver to count as adequate for our particular purpose — a code that a country director and a field officer have to apply on a Tuesday, and a risk score we then publish about the places we work — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I would like the cases, not just the labels: the phenomenon or hypothetical it has to handle, the distinctions it must not collapse (corruption versus clientelism versus patronage versus rent-seeking versus state capture versus ordinary theft; corrupt act versus corrupt official versus corrupt system; the need case versus the greed case), and the findings from anthropology and economics it is not allowed to contradict — that gift exchange frequently constitutes a relationship rather than distorting a pre-existing one, that enforcement is itself politically selective, that in some settings side payments move goods faster rather than slower.

Several requirements keep being presented to me as mandatory, and I suspect they are a mix of genuine desiderata and the prejudices of whichever camp is speaking: that corruption requires a public office, so a kickback to a purchasing manager inside a private firm is fraud but not corruption; that it requires an identifiable quid pro quo; that the gain must be private, whatever we then say about the official who steers contracts to her own ethnic group or her party and takes nothing herself; that there must be a loser, which seems to fall apart on the payment that buys a service the citizen was already owed, or the one that gets a nurse to turn up; that the standard must be identical in every country we operate in, which one of my colleagues says simply smuggles in the assumption that arm's-length impartial administration is the neutral baseline rather than one local ideal among others; and that whatever we end up with must be provable enough to dismiss or prosecute someone on. A different colleague has proposed a shortcut she calls the sunlight test — an act is corrupt just in case the people involved would not be willing to have it entered in a public register — and I do not think it survives contact with an official who would be delighted to publicise a patron's gift, or with a disclosed, budgeted, lawful fee for faster inspections, or with a practice the whole district knows about and approves of. I would also like to know whether the list has to cover corruption with no corruptible human in the loop at all: a procurement model whose training data encodes a decade of favouritism, or a vendor selected because he is somebody's cousin, where no individual exercised a discretion that anyone can point to.

So: sort the requirements I have listed into the ones that are non-negotiable and the ones that are a camp's preferences, add the ones I have missed, and give me the case behind each. Two things matter most to me. First, whether some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion that demanding courtroom-grade provability and demanding sensitivity to systemic or institutional corruption pull in opposite directions, and that demanding one cross-national standard and demanding something our own staff find legitimate and enforceable do too, but tell me if the real conflict is elsewhere, because if the list is jointly unsatisfiable that is itself the answer I should bring back to the team. Second, which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate definitions. You can score the two or three usual families of definition in a line or two each at the end if it helps, but the criteria are the deliverable.
