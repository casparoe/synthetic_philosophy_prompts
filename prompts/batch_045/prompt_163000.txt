I am second-marking scripts for an exam in a course on collective responsibility and historical injustice, and I want an independent read before I finalize the grades. Below are the question and one student's answer, verbatim.

QUESTION (20 marks)

In lectures given in 1945–46, published in German in 1946 and in English in 1947 as The Question of German Guilt, Karl Jaspers distinguishes four concepts of guilt — criminal, political, moral and metaphysical — and argues that a people as a whole cannot be guilty in the criminal or the moral sense, even though all citizens may bear political liability for the consequences of the acts of the state to which they belong.

Consider Ruritania. Eighty years ago its government carried out a campaign of mass killing. Today no perpetrator is alive. The state has a new constitution, has itself prosecuted the surviving collaborators, has completed the reparations required by treaty, and funds public acts of remembrance. Two further facts:

(i) The successor state still issues official apologies and makes further voluntary payments, and its officials say they speak on behalf of all Ruritanians.
(ii) Many present-day Ruritanians, who neither knew of nor approved the killings, report feeling guilty, and say they would feel this even if the state had never apologised.

Using Jaspers's four concepts:

(a) Can the apologies and payments in (i) be justified in Jaspers's terms without attributing guilt to present-day citizens as individuals? If so, under which concept, and why does that not collapse into the collective guilt he rejects?
(b) What can Jaspers say about the feeling described in (ii)? In particular, can metaphysical guilt explain it, given that on his account it is not tied to membership of a nation?

State explicitly how political guilt differs from moral guilt in his scheme, and what each of the four concepts takes to be its proper response.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

Jaspers's strategy is to separate four questions that ordinary language runs together. Criminal guilt requires acts that violate an unambiguous law and can be proved objectively; only a court can establish it, and its response is punishment. So no present-day Ruritanian is criminally guilty of killings performed before they were born. Moral guilt is equally individual: it concerns what I myself have done, it is judged by my own conscience in communication with those close to me, and its response is penitence and inner renewal. This is the heart of Jaspers's objection to collective guilt: criminal and moral guilt cannot be inherited or transferred, and to declare a whole people guilty is to treat persons as specimens of a group rather than as agents.

That leaves political guilt to do the work in part (a). For Jaspers the citizen bears the consequences of the acts of the state to which he belongs, and the response is not blame but liability: reparations, ceded power, institutional guarantees against repetition. The successor state can therefore apologise and pay without implying that any living individual is blameworthy. It is accepting the continuing consequences of the old state's acts, and the fact that the treaty obligations have been formally discharged does not end the matter, since liability for consequences is not a debt that one payment extinguishes. Nor does this collapse into the collective guilt Jaspers rejects: the question of what is owed is settled politically, between states and in the last instance by the power of the victor rather than by a verdict on anyone's character. The apology is best read as expressing, and the payments as partially discharging, the moral guilt of the citizens for whom the officials speak; it is a moral act performed for them by their representatives, and that is why the officials can speak for all Ruritanians at once.

The feeling in (ii) is harder, and here metaphysical guilt is the concept Jaspers needs. Metaphysical guilt is the solidarity of human beings as such: it makes each person co-responsible for wrongs committed in their presence or with their knowledge, and for failing to do what they could to prevent them. It is judged by God alone, and its response is not payment but a transformation of self-consciousness. Unlike moral guilt, it is not tied to what I myself have done, and this is why it is the one concept Jaspers can put in the place of the collective guilt he rejects: it makes sense of a guilt that no act of one's own has earned. And it is exactly what the case requires, because metaphysical guilt attaches to the community rather than to the individual and is carried forward into later generations, so that descendants can bear it for deeds in which they had no part. That explains why the feeling in (ii) survives the state's silence: the citizens' guilt is not borrowed from their government but inherited through membership.

One objection has to be met. If metaphysical guilt is universal, it cannot single out Ruritanians. But this misstates the case: the people in (ii) stand in a particular historical relation to the crimes, and a solidarity universal in scope need not be uniform in the intensity with which it is felt.
