Write me a sestina on the dispute between moral particularism and moral generalism: six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi, thirty-nine lines in all. The six end-words are promise, pain, weight, default, defeat, silence — promise and pain as candidate reasons, weight as what a reason contributes, default as the generalist's fallback, defeat as what undoes a default, silence as what is left where a principle should be. Permute them by the standard rule, each stanza taking the previous stanza's end-words in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3, and let the envoi carry all six, two to a line, paired in a way that shows they have not been reconciled.

The form has to do the philosophical work. The particularist claim I want embodied is holism about contributory reasons: a feature such as causing pain or breaking a promise does not carry a fixed valence from case to case; in one setting it counts against an action, in another it counts in favour, in a third it counts for nothing, and what makes the difference is what else is present. The returning, permuted end-words are the device for this: each one must do visibly different work in at least two stanzas, so the reader watches the same word arrive in a new position and pull a different way.

The generalist must also get her best case rather than a caricature: she grants the shifting, then says that a practice we can learn and teach still needs features with a default valence, counting as they do unless something defeats them, and that without such defaults there is nothing for a newcomer to acquire. Give the particularist a real reply to that too: the defaults are only the shape of our training, a record of what we were taught to notice rather than of what reasons there are.

Constraints: no philosophers' names and no technical vocabulary beyond the six end-words; the lines must work as poetry for a reader who has never heard the debate; no line longer than twelve syllables; and the envoi must leave the question genuinely open instead of settling it. What I am after is a poem that a philosopher who knows this literature would recognise as fair to both sides, and that everyone else would simply read as a poem.
