I've been arguing with a mate about whether lying is really worse than misleading, or whether we only think that because of the way philosophers tend to set the cases up. I need a matched pair of scenarios for a reading group I'm running next month, and I want the pair to come out the wrong way round for once: the misleading should be clearly the worse thing to do, not the lie.

Specs:

- Two scenarios, one paragraph each, both completely mundane. A workplace is fine, or a family thing. No spies, no courtrooms, no doctor withholding a diagnosis please. Same two characters in both if you can manage it.
- The hearer ends up with exactly the same false belief, does exactly the same thing as a result, and the downstream harm is identical. The speaker's motive is identical too, and fairly grubby — straightforward self-interest. So a plain consequentialist has to say the two are on a par.
- One case is a lie in the strict sense: the speaker says outright something they believe to be false. The other is pure misleading: everything literally said is something the speaker believes true, and the false belief arrives by implicature, innuendo, or selective truth-telling.
- The lie has to be a genuine attempt to deceive, not a bald-faced one, or the equivalence I want breaks.
- In both cases the hearer later tumbles to the fact that they were had. I think that's needed for the difference to show up at all, but you tell me if I'm wrong.
- The misleader has to come out worse, and it can't be because they had a nastier motive, caused more harm, or broke some explicit promise or rule. What I have in mind is this: the liar has put something on the record, so the hearer can point at the utterance afterwards, demand an account, warn other people, and the liar's own standing as an honest person is hostage to it. The misleader gives the hearer no foothold — they can deny, they keep their reputation, they keep the hearer's trust, and they can do the same thing again tomorrow. So the difference is in what the hearer is left able to do afterwards, not in what the speaker did. Say whether that's really the feature doing the work or whether you'd get there some other way.

Then take me through it:

- Give me the verdict of three views — a straight consequentialist one, the "assertion carries a special norm, lying breaches it, merely misleading doesn't" view, and the answerability / standing-to-complain thing I sketched above — and show that each verdict falls out of the theory rather than being baked into the scenario by me.
- Tell me which single feature of the pair is carrying the load, and what happens to the three verdicts if you strip it out.
- And tell me how someone who thinks lying is always the worse of the two would attack the pair — what exactly they'd accuse me of smuggling in.
