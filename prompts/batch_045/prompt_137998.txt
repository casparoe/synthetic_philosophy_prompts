A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Newton and Leibniz and about Darwin and Wallace—not about who really discovered what, but about whether, once the surrounding knowledge and techniques are in place, a discovery or invention is bound to be made by someone, and about whether it matters that it was made by these people rather than by others. We eventually set aside the historical cases and started arguing about present-day practices instead, and I would like you to reconstruct what those practices presuppose.

Consider first the U.S. patent system as it now operates. The rules moved from trying to identify the first person to invent toward awarding the patent to the first inventor to file an application. If two people independently invent the same thing, the second one cannot escape infringement liability by pointing out that they conceived and built the invention themselves; independent invention is not a defense to patent infringement. The patent is still open to challenge, however, through a derivation proceeding, which exists to ensure that the first filer did not simply take the invention from the other person rather than invent it independently. So the law polices derivation, but gives no weight to independent recreation.

Now contrast copyright. In copyright, if a second author, working in complete isolation, independently produces a work that is the same as or substantially similar to an earlier protected work, that second author is not treated as an infringer, and each author may have rights in their own independently created work. Independent creation is a full defense, and a source of rights, rather than something the law disregards.

Finally, recall how science and the public honor discovery. We still name theories, laws, and effects after individual people—Newton and Leibniz both have their names attached to the calculus, Darwin mainly and Wallace secondarily have their names attached to evolution by natural selection—and we give prizes such as Nobel Prizes to at most three discoverers even when the same idea was in the air and others were close behind. Credit seems to attach to the person who first made the result public, not to the secret first conception, and often to one name more than to the several who arrived at the same place.

What philosophical commitments about discovery, invention, authorship, and credit does this set of practices presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and where they are philosophically underdetermined so that several theories would license them.

In particular: does the patent rule—first-inventor-to-file, no independent-invention defense, but a derivation proceeding—presuppose that inventions are inevitable social products and that the identity of the inventor does not matter, or does it instead presuppose that the origin of the idea matters and must be policed, with first-to-file only an administrative proxy? Are these two commitments consistent within one legal package, or is the package a compromise between inconsistent views? Does the contrast with copyright show that the law implicitly holds two different theories of what is protected—a discoverable useful truth in the patent case versus an individual expressive act in the copyright case—or can a single underlying theory of authorship or originality explain both? And what does our scientific culture of naming and prizes presuppose: that discoveries are not inevitable, or only that the useful thing to reward is priority of publication rather than secret priority of thought?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with one of its rivals. If we came to believe thoroughly that discoveries are inevitable once the background conditions are present and that it does not matter who makes them, would patent law have to recognize an independent-invention defense, would copyright's independent-creation defense become a model for patents, would scientific prizes have to become anonymous or collective, or would nothing much change because the practices are already only about inducing disclosure and publication? Conversely, if we adopted the view that the individual discoverer is the source of the discovery's value and credit, would first-inventor-to-file, the refusal to let independent inventors use their own inventions, and the three-laureate limit become indefensible, or are they compatible with that view as well?
