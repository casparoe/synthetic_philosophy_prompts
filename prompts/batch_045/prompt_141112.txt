Hi — I've been drafted onto my university's public art committee (I'm an art history PhD student, which apparently makes me the aesthetics person) and I need a short memo I can circulate before next Wednesday, since we owe the donor an answer by end of term.

The case: an alumna will endow a gallery for work by Black artists with local connections, but on two conditions — the inaugural hang comes from her own collection, and the works go up "for their beauty alone": name, date, medium, no wall texts about politics, history, or what the artist was up to. Development is very keen.

The bit I can't get straight is Du Bois. In his 1926 essay on Negro art he says, more or less, that all art is propaganda and always must be, and that he has no use for art that isn't. Taken seriously that seems to cut against the donor's condition rather than for it: a hang with the politics quietly stripped out isn't neutral, it's propaganda for whatever the viewer already believes. But "everything is political" is too blunt to decide anything, so I want the memo to use a sharper test — here's my attempt: a work counts as propaganda in the good sense only if it addresses its audience as someone who could in principle be persuaded, i.e. it actually hands them something to argue with, as opposed to addressing them as a consumer to be moved or managed. On that test the donor's wall labels fail.

So: give me one page with a header and the recommendation up top — decline, accept, or accept with a counter-condition, and if it's a counter say exactly what it is and what we do if she refuses — then the reasons. Don't hand me three options and leave me to pick; commit, and say why the other routes lose. Also give the losing side its best day in court: one colleague thinks the whole idea of distinctively Black art is a hokum and that taking the money would do more for actual living artists than a purity spiral. And where you're relying on a contested reading of Du Bois, flag it in a line at the end rather than hedging the whole thing.
