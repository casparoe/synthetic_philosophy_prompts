I grow soft fruit on about forty acres, so almost every decision I make — when to spray, when to pick, whether to gamble on a dry week — turns on the weather. I have no formal training in any of this and have been reading about chaos on my own for a couple of years. Please describe the most important implications of deterministic chaos for how I should plan, and for whether planning is worth doing at all.

Two things I have worked out for myself, which I would like checked and then pushed further. First, what I call the flat-rate rule: if forecast error roughly doubles over every fixed stretch of time, then each additional significant figure I measure my fields and my weather with buys me about the same extra slice of useful lead time, however many I already have — so the tenth digit costs vastly more than the third and returns exactly as much, and past some point there is a wall that no quantity of better measuring gets through. Second, the part I cannot settle: my brother says that if the weather, and everything else, is deterministic, then what I decide makes no real difference to anything, and my instinct is to answer that being unpredictable in practice is what actually matters. I trust neither of us.

I would like the implications drawn for a working grower: what should I spend money on, how far ahead is it sensible to try to look, and what should I think about my own responsibility for a bad call I could not have seen coming. Please keep it concrete, and avoid equations and jargon I will not have met.
