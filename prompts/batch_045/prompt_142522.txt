I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and in three weeks I'm giving a departmental talk — forty-five minutes with twenty minutes of questions — on the species problem. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view; I already believe the view, which is exactly why I can't see its soft spots.

The thesis I'm defending is what I call role monism with mechanism pluralism. Species taxa, on my account, are the smallest lineages that respond to selection as a unit — I call these minimal responsive lineages. The role is single: being a unit of evolutionary response. The mechanisms by which a lineage comes to be such a unit are many: interbreeding, a shared selective regime, developmental canalisation, clonal descent in asexual groups, and whatever else turns out to do the work. So I'm a monist about what species are for and a pluralist about how that job gets done. Where two nested groupings both fill the role, I take the smaller; where a lineage fills it through more than one mechanism at once, I let that count as one species rather than a conflict.

Three moves carry the argument. First, the familiar catalogue of species concepts — interbreeding, ecological, diagnosable-phylogenetic, and the rest — is best read not as rival definitions of one word but as rival accounts of the cohesion mechanism, which is why each of them works somewhere and none works everywhere. Second, the standard hard cases — bacteria with extensive horizontal gene transfer, asexual lineages of hybrid origin, ring-shaped complexes where gene flow is continuous around the ring but the terminal forms don't interbreed — are hard because no single mechanism is present throughout, not because our concept is vague. Third, since people actually have to decide whether to attach a binomial to a specimen and whether a distinctive population is a unit worth legal protection, I offer an operational proxy: a lineage counts as minimal-responsive when its genetic composition would shift further under its own selective regime over a given interval than it would under gene flow from its nearest neighbouring lineage.

The room is small and I know who will be in it. There is a revisionary taxonomist with thirty years of morphological work on a beetle group, who has described a great many species by eye and regards genomic delimitation as taxonomy by other means; he will want to know whether the species he has named survive my account. There is a population-genomics postdoc who runs coalescent-based delimitation on genome-scale data and finds introgression nearly everywhere she looks, and who is inclined to think species boundaries are arbitrary points on a continuum. There is a philosopher of science who holds a straightforward pluralism about species concepts and thinks the species problem is a pseudo-problem generated by expecting one term to do several incompatible jobs. There is a visitor from conservation policy who cares only about whether my account licenses protecting a distinctive population as an evolutionarily significant unit, or quietly dissolves the thing she needs to protect. And the rest of the department works on philosophy of physics and general philosophy of science, so I should expect at least one version of: why should anyone outside taxonomy care whether this is settled? No one else in the room works on species.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it and in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each: a reply I can give in about two minutes, and one sentence on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks, and tell me which single reply has to land if the talk is to survive. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that goes where the account is thinnest, which I'd rather not name myself.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the objection or reply that decides the session. No preamble, and don't restate my view back to me.
