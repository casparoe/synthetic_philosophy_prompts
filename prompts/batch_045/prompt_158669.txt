I host a long-form interview series for a magazine on science and ideas, and next month I'm recording an episode with an archivist-scholar who argues that the central ethical failure of archives is not what they destroy but what they never entered. Her distinction is between silence — no record was ever made, because the person was never counted, never asked, never in the room where the paperwork happened — and absence, where a record existed and was then lost, weeded, or destroyed. She claims professional practice systematically collapses the two, filing both as a lacuna to be closed by further acquisition, and that this is why digitisation, which makes surviving holdings vastly more findable, tends to widen rather than narrow the asymmetry: everything that was already legible becomes more legible, and everything that was never written stays invisible while looking complete. Her remedy is what she calls counter-finding aids: descriptions written for documents that should exist and do not, each carrying an explicit confidence rating and a stated reason for the expectation, so that a researcher working the catalogue meets the missing as a described object rather than as nothing at all. She is very practiced at turning objections aside with the line that a conjecture labelled as a conjecture is not a fabrication.

Draft eight to ten questions that make that deflection progressively harder to lean on. I want at least one question on each of the following, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: first, how silence is to be told apart from absence in practice when the only evidence that something was never recorded is itself drawn from the records that survive, and whether her likely reply — that administrative routines leave traces of their own coverage — merely pushes the problem back a level. Second, the objection that rendering absence into a catalogued object launders conjecture into evidence once the finding aid is separated from its confidence rating by citation, indexing, or a downstream database. Third, whether power also works by over-production: the subordinate who appears everywhere in the archive as reported speech inside someone else's file, voluminously recorded and yet not present. Fourth, who gets to write the counter-finding aid, and whether the practice relocates interpretive authority to the archivist rather than dispersing it. Fifth, what would count as the project failing — historians ignoring the aids, or one of them sending a researcher confidently wrong. Close with a single question about whether an archive that described its own silences would still be recognisable as an archive, or whether it would have become something closer to an argument. Nothing about her career, her training, or her favourite discovery.
