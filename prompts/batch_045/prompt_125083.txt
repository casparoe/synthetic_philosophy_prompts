One thing I keep noticing about how these monument fights actually get settled: the figure gets pulled off the plinth, but instead of being melted down or smashed, the statue is carted off to a museum, sometimes with the paint and the ropes from the protest still on it, sometimes alongside a plaque about who he was and what he did, and the plinth is often left bare for a while rather than quietly refilled with something uncontroversial. Nobody involved seems to think the museum has thereby honored the guy, and nobody seems to think the empty plinth is saying nothing -- and yet the same city will take down one statue while leaving that same guy's name on a street, a school, or a building.

So what does that package of moves presuppose about what a monument even is? It looks like it assumes the same lump of bronze means one thing in a square and something totally different in a gallery, that the object has to be preserved rather than destroyed, and that leaving the space empty is itself a statement. Can you reconstruct the implicit theory of what monuments do, and separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with? I'm also curious whether the parts hang together: the museum move, the you-can't-destroy-it rule, and the empty plinth feel like they might each be riding on a different view -- honoring vs. teaching vs. keeping evidence of a past wrong -- and I'd like to know whether they contradict each other or can be had at once. Then: if we swapped in a much flatter view, say that a monument is just the state's speech, or just decoration, what would actually have to change in how we handle contested ones?
