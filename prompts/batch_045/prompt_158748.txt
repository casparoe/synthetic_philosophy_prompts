My friend Nate and I got into a four-hour argument last night about predictive processing and the free energy principle, and I went to bed annoyed because I dont think we ever disagreed about a single fact. Here is where we ended up.

Nate's line: whatever a system does, you can always write down some generative model and some priors such that what it's doing counts as minimizing prediction error, so the framework has no content — it's a redescription, not a theory. My line: plenty of respectable theories have that "you can always retrodict a fit" property — variational principles in physics, optimization-flavored explanations in biology — and they aren't empty; the content comes from the constraints you put on the model, i.e. what the generative model is allowed to be, what the architecture is, what plays the role of precision. Nate says: fine, name the constraints. And every time I try, he says that constraint was introduced after the data and so doesn't count.

Then there's the move that actually made us go in circles. I gave him what I thought was a hard test case — call it the surprise maximizer. Build a system explicitly engineered to seek out states it can't predict: an agent rewarded for visiting the highest-entropy corner of its state space, or a circuit wired so its predictions are systematically anti-correlated with its inputs. Nate says this is the counterexample and the framework can't handle it. And I caught myself making exactly the move PP people always make: the agent's own model predicts that it will go looking for surprise, so the surprise-seeking is itself expected, and once you score it at the level of expected free energy with a novelty or epistemic-value term in the prior, it's minimizing again. Nate's reply: that move *is* the problem, because it means no possible system is ever a counterexample.

My hypothesis, for what it's worth, is that none of this is a fight about the math or the data. I think it's a fight about what a theory is *for*: Nate is running a falsificationist standard where content = risky predictions ruled out in advance, and I'm running something more like a unificationist or research-program standard, where a framework earns its keep by generating new quantities to measure and a shared vocabulary across Marr's computational / algorithmic / implementational levels and across neuron / population / behavior. If that's right, more data can't settle it, because we're using different standards for which data would even be relevant.

But I'm not confident, and I want a diagnosis, not a survey. So:

1. Is my hypothesis roughly right? Or is there a hidden substantive question doing the actual work that we could in principle settle — e.g. whether real neural circuits do precision-weighted error signalling in the specific way the *process* version of the theory claims — a question both of us should care about regardless of our standards?

2. Take the surprise maximizer seriously. Is the "rescore it at the level of expected free energy with an epistemic prior" response a legit answer, a bait-and-switch, or a sign that the choice of level of description is doing all the work? Actually think about whether there is any system that could not be given such a description. And notice the further wrinkle: if you answer "a system only has a generative model insofar as it's the kind of thing that hangs together," then isn't the real content in the self-organizing / Markov-blanket part of the story rather than in the *prediction* part, which is the part the name advertises?

3. Is part of this just a level slip — the free energy principle stated as a claim about any system that maintains itself vs. predictive processing as a claim about actual cortical machinery — where Nate attacks whichever one is weakest and I defend whichever one is strongest, and the single label lets us each pick? If so, which of the two is even worth arguing about?

4. Given your diagnosis, what would progress look like? Not "more experiments," please. Would it be a derivation showing a class of systems that provably admit no free-energy-minimizing description? A worked-out case where the framework stuck its neck out, predicted something nobody expected, and either won or lost and was revised? Some formal result about when a redescription adds content at all? Or should I just expect this to stay stalled indefinitely — and if so, is that because it's hard or because it's ill-posed?

Please don't give me a reading list and don't summarize the literature. Nate and I both know the standard stuff — Friston, Clark, Hohwy, the usual Bayesian-brain framing — and going over it again is what got us into this arguement. I want you to argue for a diagnosis, and to say plainly if you think my hypothesis is wrong, or if you think the whole thing is mostly verbal.
