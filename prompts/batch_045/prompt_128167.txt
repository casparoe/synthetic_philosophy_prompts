I've got a column on cognitive science, and I keep circling back to the Sapir-Whorf thing because I can't work out why it never moves. Last month I chaired a panel with a lab psychologist and a field linguist: she kept saying show me an effect on a nonverbal task, no words anywhere in it; he kept saying that's not the claim, the claim is about which distinctions are readily available to you when you think. It struck me afterwards that her experiment couldn't have confirmed his claim even if it were true, and his claim may not be the sort of thing her methods could touch at all.

The same slide happens in every version of the argument I read. Someone shows that speakers of a language that makes you mark where your information came from are good at tracking who told them what, and by the time it reaches print it's become they inhabit a different conception of knowledge. Or the language doing the work is the lexicon in one paragraph and obligatory grammar in the next. My working theory is that the fight is definately more verbal than empirical: everyone more or less agrees on the data and they're using thought to name different things, so nothing could settle it. But maybe there's a genuine empirical question buried in the middle of it. Give me a diagnosis, not a lit review what kind of disagreement is this, and what would count as actually making progress on it?
