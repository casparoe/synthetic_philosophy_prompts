I spent most of my working life in analytical chemistry, largely on method validation and the evaluation of measurement uncertainty, and I have recently begun auditing a graduate seminar in epistemology. Last week the session turned to epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, and I offered an analogy from my own field in order to get clear about how the two views differ. It was received more warmly than I expected, and I have since come to worry that it was received too warmly.

In my field one does not say that a measurement result is simply correct or incorrect. One says that it is adequate or inadequate relative to a tolerance, and that the tolerance comes from the specification the measurement is meant to serve. A dimension reported as 12.02 millimetres with a stated uncertainty of ten micrometres is a perfectly serviceable result if the part has to fit a housing machined to fifty micrometres, and it is worthless if the fit is specified to five. The number and its uncertainty do not change; what changes is the tolerance, and the tolerance is set by what the result is going to be used for.

I proposed the following mapping. The measurand is the proposition under consideration. The result together with its uncertainty band is the subject's epistemic position — the true belief together with how well supported it is. The tolerance is the epistemic standard. Falling within tolerance is knowing. The specification, finally, is what fixes the tolerance, and here the two views seemed to part company: on the contextualist picture the specification belongs to whoever is assessing the claim, to the conversation they are in and the alternatives they have made salient; on the pragmatic encroachment picture it belongs to the situation of the person doing the believing, to how much it would cost them to be wrong. So raising the stakes can cost you knowledge while nothing about your evidence, your reliability, or the truth of what you believe changes by any measurable amount.

I then used the analogy to argue for a conclusion, and it is the conclusion I want audited. I suggested that the two views are not rival theories of knowledge at all, but rival answers to a single prior question about whose specification sets the tolerance, and that once the question is posed that way the dispute looks semantic: everyone agrees that there is an invariant quantity, the strength of the subject's epistemic position, and everyone agrees that a threshold has to be crossed before an ascription is true; they differ only about a parameter in the semantics of a word. I concluded that the debate is shallower than the literature makes it sound, and that the sensible thing — the thing a measurement scientist would do — is to replace both views with one relativized claim, of the form that the subject's true belief meets the standard set by such-and-such a specification, and to stop asking whether anyone knows simpliciter.

Three things now make me doubt the argument, and I would like them taken seriously rather than patted on the head.

The first is that in my field conformance does not entail truth. A result can lie inside the tolerance zone while the true value lies outside it, and one can be compliant and wrong at the same time; the whole apparatus of uncertainty exists precisely because of this. But knowing is factive, so falling within tolerance cannot be knowing, and I am not sure what in the source domain corresponds to the truth requirement at all.

The second is that when a tolerance tightens, nobody in a laboratory concludes that the measurement has thereby deteriorated. What one does is buy a better instrument, take more readings, or send the part elsewhere — that is, one improves one's epistemic position. The analogue of that on the epistemic side is advice to gather more evidence when the stakes are high, which is advice that no one in the seminar disputed. So either the analogy turns pragmatic encroachment into something trivially true, or pragmatic encroachment is claiming something the analogue does not capture, and I cannot tell which.

The third is a remark the seminar's instructor made afterwards: that my picture of an invariant magnitude plus an externally imposed threshold is not neutral ground between the two views but is itself one of the positions, or a third one, and that the analogy may be smuggling in the very claim under dispute. I did not follow the remark and would like it cashed out.

What I would like from you is an audit of the analogy as an argument rather than as decoration. Give me the correspondence table explicitly, including the roles I have not assigned — the instrument, the inspector, the customer, the application, the calibration chain — and say plainly which of my assignments are forced and which are arbitrary. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion I drew from those that are merely untidy, and in particular tell me whether the invariant-quantity-plus-threshold structure is something both views can accept or something only one of them can. Say whether the conclusion survives: is the dispute between contextualism and pragmatic encroachment genuinely shallower than it looks, does the analogy merely flatten a difference that shows up in cases I have not thought about, and if the difference is real, what kind of case would exhibit it and what would have to be added to the metrology picture to represent it. Finally, if there is a better analogy for the pair of views — I have seen writers reach for legal standards of proof, and I have my own candidate in the idea of a detection limit — tell me whether it does the separating work mine fails to do, and where it fails in turn.
