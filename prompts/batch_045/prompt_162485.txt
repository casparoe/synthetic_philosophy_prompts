I've been going round in circles on cognitive penetration of perception — the question of whether what you believe can change what you actually see, as opposed to what you say, judge, or reach for — and I'm trying to work out whether the debate is even empirically tractable, or whether every conceivable experiment leaves both sides a bolt-hole. What I want is a case that forces the disagreement into exactly one joint, so I can see whether there's any observation that would settle it.

Build me a scenario. One paragraph, somewhere ordinary — a psychophysics lab is fine, a kitchen table is better — but no scanners, no electrodes, no drugs, no brain implants, and no perfect anything. Two conditions (or two people) who differ only in an occurrent belief about the stimulus: same stimulus, same viewing conditions, same eyes, same hardware.

Design it so each of the standard debunking explanations is blocked in turn. It can't be spatial attention or eye movements. It can't be a post-perceptual judgement, a guess, or a demand-characteristics artefact. It can't be a difference in the peripheral input or in what's out there in the world. And it can't be plain perceptual learning or skill acquisition from exposure. For the judgement one, I'd like the difference in experience read off by something that isn't a verbal report — an aftereffect, adaptation transfer, whatever you think holds up — I'm not fussy so long as a normal lab could actually run it.

Then I want the residual disagreement to be a single joint, chosen deliberately, and I want you to say why you chose that one. My guess at the most promising candidate, take it or leave it: make the belief one the subject couldn't have got by looking — they have to deduce it from testimony about something not currently visible — and then run a retraction test, tell them the belief was false, and see whether the effect reverts within the same session, with the size of the push on experience tracking their confidence in the belief rather than how long they've been looking. If a different joint forces the issue more cleanly, use yours instead.

After the scenario: the verdict the pro-penetration side gives and why, the verdict the encapsulation/modularity side gives and why, and if you think a predictive-processing Bayesian gives a third answer that neither of the first two gives, throw that in. Then: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and the strongest non-ad hoc thing the modularist can say to wriggle out, plus the penetration side's reply to it. Steelman the modularist properly — I've read the usual blog posts on this and the sceptic always gets strawmanned. Finish by telling me honestly whether your case could ever actually settle the question, or whether it just relocates it one step back. Taboo the word see while you're at it, at least in the load-bearing bits. No papers, no authors, no reading recs — I cant follow any of them up, and I don't want a summary of a literature, I want the case.
