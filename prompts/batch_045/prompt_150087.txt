I am preparing a seminar on self-locating belief and found, on an unattributed popular-philosophy blog, the following summary of the Sleeping Beauty problem. I would like to hand something like it to my students, but I no longer trust my own judgment about where it goes wrong, and I am myself drawn to the thirder answer, which makes me suspect I am too ready to accept the parts of the summary that flatter it.

The summary runs as follows:

Sleeping Beauty volunteers for an experiment in which a fair coin is tossed on Sunday evening. If it lands heads she is woken once, on Monday; if it lands tails she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, and after each waking she is given a drug that erases her memory of it, so that every waking is subjectively identical. The question is what credence she should have, on waking, that the coin landed heads. Thirders answer one third, and the argument is just the principle of indifference: from where she sits there are three possible awakenings — heads-and-Monday, tails-and-Monday, tails-and-Tuesday — she cannot tell them apart, so she should divide her credence evenly among them, and only one of the three is a heads awakening. Halfers answer one half, on the ground that she knew all along that she would be woken at least once, so the fact that she is awake gives her no new evidence, and conditionalizing on nothing new leaves her credence where it was on Sunday. The same commitment forces a halfer to say that even if Beauty is told, on waking, that it is Monday, her credence in heads should still be one half. The betting argument then settles the dispute in favour of the thirders: if a bookmaker offers her even odds on heads at every waking, betting as if the chance were one half loses money in the long run, since she pays out twice when the coin lands tails and collects once when it lands heads, whereas betting as a thirder breaks even; and more generally a Dutch book — a set of bets each of which she regards as fair but which together guarantee her a loss — can be constructed against anyone who holds one half, which is what it means for a credence to be incoherent. Everything else in the literature is a variation on this. In the variant where the coin is not tossed until Monday night, after her first waking, the halfer is clearly right: on Tuesday she learns something she could not have known on Sunday, so that is a different problem in principle. And what the whole debate really comes to is a verbal dispute: thirders and halfers agree about every fact of the experiment and about every betting consequence, and 'credence' simply means something slightly different in each of their mouths.

I would like an accuracy audit I can act on. For each contested claim, tell me what a careful thirder would accept, what a careful halfer would accept, what is a legitimate simplification for a teaching context, and what is simply wrong. Please work through the following in particular.

1. Is the 'three indistinguishable awakenings, spread your credence evenly' presentation of the thirder case fair, or is it the straw version the halfer literature attacks? If it is unfair, reconstruct the argument a thirder would actually give, in the form a halfer has to answer, and say which premise the halfer rejects and on what basis.

2. Is keeping one half after being told it is Monday really a commitment of halferism, or only of one version of it? Derive what a halfer should say about the probability of heads conditional on Monday and conditional on Tuesday, and tell me whether that consequence is a genuine cost of the view or a bullet the halfer should bite.

3. Does the betting material settle anything, and is the claim that a Dutch book can be made against any halfer correct? What turns on whether the bet is offered at every awakening and settled per awakening rather than once per run of the experiment, and on whether Beauty knows how many bets she will face?

4. My instinct about the Monday-night variant is that nothing changes, since from Beauty's point of view the two experiments are the same, but I cannot show it. Run the argument with the toss moved to Monday evening after the first waking, and say whether the timing does any real work — in particular whether the thirder's key conditional probability becomes easier or harder to justify there.

5. If the dispute is not merely verbal, what is the substantive disagreement underneath it: about conditionalizing on centered evidence, about the link between credence and betting behaviour, about what accuracy requires of a self-locating credence? What sort of consideration could in principle settle it?

Finally, flag anything in the summary that is defensible but would mislead a student, and close with one short paragraph giving the corrected version of the summary you would actually hand out.
