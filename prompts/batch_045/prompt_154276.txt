Please explain what's actually at stake in the difference between a society collapsing and a society merely changing into something else. I keep going in circles on it and want the conceptual lay of the land, not a tour of sites.

Here's the version of the puzzle I get stuck on. Call a society's ceiling the most complex thing anyone in it can still pull off — not how well the average person lives, but the fanciest thing that can still be built, administered, or recorded there. Call its self-description the story its people tell about who they are and where they came from. Now compare:

(1) The ceiling drops hard within a couple of generations: palaces, writing, long-distance exchange, all gone. Population maybe down somewhat, but people seem to eat about as well and live about as long, and two generations later they still tell roughly the old story about themselves.
(2) The ceiling drops just as hard, population drops much more, and the self-description breaks too: nobody thinks of themselves as the same people anymore.

My intuition says (2) collapsed and (1) didn't, but on the ceiling measure they're identical, and its not clear to me why the self-description should get to do all that work — that looks like letting a people's own PR decide whether what happened to them counts as a disaster. And there's a mirror-image problem: cases where the ceiling goes up and the population crashes, or where every institution dissolves while material conditions improve. Collapse, or a success with an ugly distribution?

So: what is the concept of collapse for? Is it mostly descriptive, or is most of its real content normative — a marker for a kind of change we'd want to prevent? If it's the latter, is the collapse-versus-transformation argument secretly an argument about values wearing an archaeologist's hat, and what would we lose by just saying so? Examples from the Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Maya, and Rapa Nui are welcome, but use them to make the point rather than as a survey.
