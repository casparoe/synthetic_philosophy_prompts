You are a professional philosopher with long experience teaching upper-level metaethics, and you have been asked to write one question for a final exam. Please think carefully about the dialectic before you draft anything: in particular, spend a little time working out what the genuine crux is — the one move that separates a candidate who understands the structure of evolutionary debunking arguments from a candidate who has merely memorized the labels (tracking vs. independence, third-factor, companions in guilt) and can recite the standard back-and-forth. Drafting a question that can be answered by summarizing that back-and-forth would be a failure; the question should force a decision about which premise has to go.

The setting: a third-year seminar in metaethics. The exam is open-book, 90 minutes per question, and answers are capped at 1,200 words. The question is worth 20 marks. It must be fully self-contained: a candidate should be able to answer it well without consulting any reading, and the answer should not consist in reporting what some author said. (You may refer to argument-types by their familiar names, but nothing in the question should turn on attributing a thesis to a particular paper, or on recalling a title, date, or passage.)

To make the question non-recitable, build it around a stipulated case that candidates will not have seen. I have in mind something along these lines, which you should refine or replace as you see fit so that it does real work: a community whose moral judgments were produced in the first instance exactly as the debunker says — by selective pressures that rewarded fitness-conducive social responses and were indifferent to moral truth — and which then institutes a purely internal practice of filtering: recording occurrent moral judgments, discarding any that contradict other judgments the community is unwilling to surrender or that flip when the same case is redescribed, generalizing the survivors into principles, and using those principles to revise further judgments, over many generations. The filter never consults the world and contains no premise about what the moral facts are. The survivors are perfectly coherent, framing-stable, and systematic. The candidate should then be asked whether membership in that surviving set gives anyone a reason to think the judgments true, or any reason a debunker must take seriously — pressed to distinguish the two importantly different forms a debunking argument can take (the form that says our judgments were shaped by pressures with no tendency to track moral truth, and the form that says moral facts play no role in the best explanation of why we hold the judgments we do), since the filter looks relevant to one of them and inert, or worse than inert, against the other. A second part should then take the debunker's most natural retreat — restricting the claim to unreflective, affect-driven judgments and exempting the outputs of careful reasoning — and ask what the debunker would have to establish for that restriction to be non-arbitrary, and whether the restriction survives the observation that the capacity for practical reasoning is itself a product of the same selective history and operates on materials supplied by the very judgments under suspicion. The question should close by requiring a verdict: name the single premise you would give up if you had to make your position consistent, and say what giving it up costs you in first-order ethics.

Please deliver three things.

1. The question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including the full statement of the stipulated case and any brief instructions to candidates (word cap, what to do, what not to do).

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words. It should be written at the standard you would expect from a strong student in the time allowed — not a survey, but an argument that reaches the crux and commits itself.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth four marks each (total 20). For each point, state the specific move a candidate must make to earn the marks and add a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong — for example, by treating coherence as though it were obviously truth-conducive, by confusing an undercutting argument with an argument for the falsity of moral claims, by declaring the global version self-refuting and stopping there, or by treating cross-cultural convergence as though it discriminated between tracking and shared shaping. Finish with a short calibration note saying what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer at the margin.
