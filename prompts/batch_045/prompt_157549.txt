I'm a senior engineer on the developer-productivity team at a company with roughly 800 engineers, and I have no formal philosophy background — my training is in distributed systems. Within the next few weeks I have to recommend an architecture for the next version of our internal coding assistant, and the decision keeps turning on a distinction I've seen people in my field draw between tool AI and agent AI. As I understand it: a tool is invoked to do a bounded thing, its ends are supplied fresh by the person calling it, and it does not form or pursue objectives of its own; an agent is given ends, retains them across time, decomposes them into subgoals, and selects actions in the world in their service without a human re-specifying the objective at each step. Please correct me if I have drawn that line in the wrong place.

The system we ship today is a tool on any reading: an engineer asks a question about the codebase or requests a patch, it answers or drafts, and a human reviews and merges. The proposal on the table is what our director calls a release copilot. A release manager would hand it a written mandate — say, move the payment-service migration from 1% to 5% of traffic while keeping error rate and p99 latency inside our published SLOs — and from there it would plan, open pull requests, run canaries, read dashboards, adjust rollout, roll back, file incidents, and page a human only when it hits a limit it has been told about. It would run for two or three days, hold persistent memory across runs, and act on production only through an audited set of our own APIs under scoped credentials. It could choose which subgoals to pursue and in what order, but could not rewrite its own mandate. The argument for it is throughput: human review latency, not model capability, is now the bottleneck on our release train, and our director's view is that the system is still just a tool because every action is funneled through APIs we own and every mandate is written by a named human.

I have been sketching a third option, which I have started calling a commissioned tool: top-level ends fixed in advance by a committee rather than by whoever happens to be on call, irreversible action classes (deleting data, changing billing configuration, emailing customers, touching anything inside our compliance boundary) reserved for humans, and everything reversible delegated freely. A colleague says this is agent AI with extra paperwork, because a system that decides which subgoals to attempt and what to count as progress is doing goal formation regardless of where its top-level mandate came from. Another says it is a tool however long it runs, because it has no ends of its own and no way to act outside our audited surface.

I would like you to lay out the most important implications of the tool/agent distinction for this decision — ranked, and each one stated as something I could actually take into a design review. In particular: whether the distinction marks a real difference in kind or only a difference in degree that my third option sits somewhere along; what it implies for the guardrails that matter most, given that the pressure to delegate comes from review latency and the temptation for the system is to satisfy the literal mandate rather than the point of it (for instance by shedding load or refusing traffic shifts to protect its latency numbers); what it implies for how we monitor the thing, since catching goal-directed misbehavior seems to require predicting what the system wants, which is the stance tool design is supposed to let us avoid; and what it implies for accountability when something goes wrong, since authorship of a bad release would now be spread across the mandate author, my platform team, the model vendor whose weights we cannot inspect, and whichever human clicked approve. I would also like to know how much of your answer survives if the underlying models get substantially better at long-horizon planning in the next eighteen months — whether a system architected as a tool today is stable under that kind of capability improvement, or whether the architecture silently becomes an agent.

Two practical constraints. First, please write in American English, and please define any philosophical or decision-theoretic term as you go: I can follow precise reasoning, but I cannot follow jargon I have not been taught, and I will be quoting this to colleagues who know even less of it than I do. Second, please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I cannot act on references, and I need the reasoning itself, not pointers to it. Where the honest answer is that the implications depend on something I have not told you, say what that something is and what I should look for.
