hey — I'm writing a novel and im stuck on a scene.

Setup, so you have the world: near-future, a company sells a "fork" machine. A volunteer gets put into a superposition, the branches decohere, and the machine's calibration is public — say it's set so the squared amplitude on the "you wake up in the villa" outcome is 0.25, with the remaining 0.75 spread over outcomes where you wake up in your own bed and the only difference between them is trivia (wall colour, which song is playing). The company's consent form wants to say: "there is a 25% chance you will wake up in the villa." The scene is two characters arguing about whether that sentence is a lie.

Character one, the machine's designer, is an Everettian who thinks the sentence is fine: branching as such isn't something a rational agent has preferences about, and once you impose the usual rationality constraints on an agent who knows she's going to branch — indifference to pure branching, irrelevance of zero-amplitude outcomes, additivity across orthogonal components — her preferences have to be representable as expected utility with the branch weights, and those weights have to be the squared amplitudes. So 0.25 isn't a guess, it's the only coherent answer, and it's exactly what the volunteer should bet and plan with.

Character two, the auditor, also believes in many worlds but thinks that argument is circular — the constraints are already Born-shaped — and that in a theory where every outcome happens there's nothing left for "chance" to be about: you get a number telling you how much to care about a branch, not a probability that something will happen to you, and the consent form quietly trades on that confusion.

What I need: a real fight, both of them good at this. The auditor owes a positive story about what 0.25 is, if not a chance, and about why the form is misleading anyway; the designer owes an answer to the circularity charge and to "why not just count branches." Have them hit both the easy case (three equal-amplitude outcomes, where counting and the Born weights agree) and the uneven one (0.25 winning vs. two 0.375 losing branches, say), because the designer thinks the auditor's whole intuition is propped up by the easy case. Stagey please — people in a room, a bit rude, not lecturers. 700 words-ish, I care about the argument more than the length.

Last line, begin "Verdict:" and in one sentence say who won and why.
