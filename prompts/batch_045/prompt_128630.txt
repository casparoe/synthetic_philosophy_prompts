Hi — hoping you can help me think through something work-related before Thursday. I sit on the methods review panel for a regional hospital system (I'm the token non-clinican, I do the data side). When two services are arguing about which of them is actually safer, our panel rules on which numbers are allowed into the decision. We've built up a bit of a track record, and now we've got a case that feels different from all of them, and I want two things: what we should decide, and separately what we probably will decide.

Our precedents, roughly:

1. Sepsis order sets, two hospitals. Crude 30-day mortality favored Mercy. Split on a severity score computed from ambulance vitals — so, before anyone reached a hospital — Trinity was better in all four bands, and by more than the crude gap. We went with Trinity and said the crude number was just a fact about who gets sent where.

2. Diabetic foot ulcers, two protocols. Crude favored protocol 2; the reversal only showed up when you split on whether the patient got referred on to the wound center, which happens after the first treatment decision. We threw the split out and let the crude number stand.

3. Colonoscopy follow-up: someone split on whether the patient came back for a repeat visit. Thrown out — that conditions on a common effect of the things we're comparing.

4. Anticoagulation: a reversal appeared in one of about forty splits somebody tried. Thrown out, splitter has to be named in advance, a good story told afterwards doesn't count.

5. One case turned out to be a denominator error (observation stays counted as admissions at one site). No verdict, sent back for raw counts, and we now demand raw counts up front.

6. Ortho: overall A beat B, within each type of procedure B beat A, and within procedure-type-and-severity A beat B again. We went one level down and stopped; we said we're not going to chase the alternation down.

7. When the two arms swap ranks depending on which outcome you look at, we require a single primary outcome named in advance and we rule only on that.

New case. We're being asked to sign off on consolidating stroke care: close Northgate's stroke service and have dispatch send suspected strokes to Fairview. Last three years, all suspected-stroke arrivals: Fairview 7.4% 30-day mortality, Northgate 9.1%. Split by how the patient got there — ambulance vs walked in or private car — which is settled before anyone arrives (dispatch protocol plus patient choice), and which we've used as a splitter before and accepted: among ambulance arrivals, Northgate 14.2% vs Fairview 15.9%; among everyone else, Northgate 3.5% vs Fairview 2.9%. Northgate wins both. Fairview wins overall because it's the famous one and pulls in a huge pile of self-referred mild cases. Raw counts audited and clean. Mortality is the pre-specified primary outcome and they don't swap ranks on it inside the strata.

What's nagging me: arrival mode is pre-treatment in the data, but it's exactly what the policy would change. If we consolidate, dispatch changes, Fairview's case mix gets worse, and maybe it's door-to-needle times get worse with volume too. So the splitter isn't invariant under the thing we're actually deciding, and none of our precedents has that feature.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog, and how good is the analogy? Is "Northgate is safer, don't consolidate" the right ruling, or is the right ruling something closer to "these numbers don't answer the question you're being asked"? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle are we signing on to with each option? And what's the one further piece of evidence, or the hypothetical case, that would pry the two readings apart — I'd rather know now what to ask for than rule and get it wrong.

(Please don't bother with citations or pointers to the literature on Simpson's paradox — I can find those myself. I want the reasoning about our precedents and about this case.)
