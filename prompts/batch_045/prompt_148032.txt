Write an essay on whether prioritarianism really escapes the levelling-down objection, or whether the objection simply reappears once we consider choices made under uncertainty.

Set the view up as follows. Prioritarianism ranks outcomes by a sum over persons of a transformed wellbeing level, the transformation being increasing and strictly concave, so that a given increment of wellbeing counts for more the worse off the person who receives it. The view contains no separate term for equality and no term for the size of gaps between people; it is concerned only with how well off people are, weighted more heavily the worse off they are. Its best-known advertisement is that this lets it have everything the levelling-down objection was supposed to take away from egalitarianism: because it is increasing in every individual's wellbeing, it can never rank an outcome as better merely because the better off have been dragged down, and it never has to say that a world in which everyone is worse off but more equal is, for that reason, better.

Now consider a case in which identities are not fixed in advance. Ada and Bella are currently equally well off, and there are two options.

S: each ends at a level of 60 for sure.
R: a fair coin decides which of them ends at 130 and which ends at 10.

Four features of this pair are worth isolating before you argue about it. First, the distribution of wellbeing under R is the same whichever way the coin lands: one person at 130, one at 10. Nothing in the case turns on uncertainty about how unequal the outcome will be, and the view is indifferent between R and a perfectly correlated lottery in which both get 130 or both get 10. Second, each person's expected wellbeing under R is 70, against 60 for sure under S, and total wellbeing is 140 in every state under R against 120 under S. Third, with the square root as the transformation, the prioritarian value of S is about 15.49 and of R about 14.56, so the view prefers S: it prefers an option under which each person has a lower expected wellbeing and under which total wellbeing is lower in every state. Do the arithmetic yourself, and check how much concavity the verdict requires, since with a mildly concave transformation such as w raised to the power 0.9 the ordering reverses. Fourth, contrast this with the case in which the 130 and the 10 are assigned to Ada and Bella outright: there, a preference for 60 and 60 is just a transfer to the worse off, and raises no comparable difficulty. The problem, if it is one, is manufactured by the coin.

So the essay should settle at least these questions. Is the verdict in this case the levelling-down objection in a new guise, or a structurally different objection wearing its clothes? Note that nothing is levelled down here: no one is made worse off in order to reduce a gap; rather, an expected gain to everybody is declined. Does that difference matter, or is the underlying complaint the same? If the prioritarian wants to keep the verdict, they must reject something in the neighbourhood of ex ante Pareto, the principle that an option which gives every person a higher expected wellbeing is not thereby worse. Can they reject it, and on what grounds: because a person's good is not their expected wellbeing, because the coin means that no determinate person is benefited, or because what matters is who actually ends up badly off? If instead they want to keep ex ante Pareto, they can move to a version that applies the concave transformation to expected levels rather than to realized ones. That version never levels down and never violates ex ante Pareto, but it is indifferent between R and a sure 70 for each, and cannot register the claim that the person who actually lands at 10 has the stronger claim. Is that a fatal loss of grip on the intuition that motivated the view, or a correct trimming of it? You may also consider whether some intermediate view does better, but if you propose one, say what it costs.

Finally, decide. Does prioritarianism survive the case, and only at the price of giving up ex ante Pareto? Is the escape from the levelling-down objection revealed as cosmetic, with the objection migrating rather than disappearing? Or was the objection never really about Pareto in the first place, so that this case is no objection at all? Take one of these lines, or another you can state clearly, defend it against the strongest version of the alternatives, and be explicit about which premise you are giving up. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, keep the argument tied to the formal statement of the view rather than to slogans, and do not end on a non-committal note.
