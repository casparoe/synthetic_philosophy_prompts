Ok so I just got out of a three hour, slightly drunk argument with my friend Sam about scientific realism and the pessimistic meta-induction, and it ended exactly the way these always end, with both of us accusing the other of hindsight. I am the pessimist, more or less: I think the historical record gives us a real reason to expect our best current theories to be superseded too, and that the standard realist reply quietly applies a standard of success that past theories could never have met. Sam is a selective realist. Her view is that most of what is on the discard pile was never anywhere near as successful as our current theories, that what survives theory change is the mathematically load-bearing structure, and that believing in the unobservable stuff that does the load-bearing work is fine while believing in the rest is not.

We actually found a lot we agree on and then immediately started talking past each other, and I want the document we should have written instead of the argument. Four places where it broke down, and I want all four dealt with rather than smoothed over:

One, the units. The induction has to range over something, and we never agreed what. Whole theories? Individual posits like the ether or phlogiston? Explanatory claims? Research programmes including their instrumentation and experimental practice? Sam says the pessimist gets to pick whichever unit gives the ugliest looking history; I say the realist gets to pick whichever unit makes the survival rate look best. Neither of us could say what would settle the choice.

Two, retention. Every time I name a discarded theory, Sam points out that some of its machinery survived into the successor, and I say that is definately hindsight, you can always go back and find the piece that got carried forward. She says the retention rate is the entire ballgame and I am refusing to look at it. I proposed something I called the retention audit: for a discarded theory, list the specific bits that generated its novel predictions and ask what fraction of those bits, as opposed to the theory as a whole, are still doing work in the successor. She liked the procedure and then said it will come out high and that a high rate is evidence for realism; I said it will come out lower than she thinks and that even a high rate only shows we kept good instruments.

Three, comparability of success. I suggested we judge a past theory against the best available rivals of its own era rather than against modern absolute standards, otherwise the selective realist is unfalsifiable because no past theory can ever be as successful as a current one by construction. She says my version rescues too much and that what matters is the raw track record of novel predictions that came off. We could not agree on a rule, or on what to say about a theory that was enormously successful and then got demoted to a limiting case of its successor instead of refuted outright. I want a three way taxonomy here, something like refuted-and-abandoned versus absorbed-as-a-limiting-case versus kept-with-its-central-terms-reinterpreted, and I want each of those connected to what it is supposed to show, because I think the realist quietly leans on the middle category and I also think the middle category is doing more work than either of us admitted last night.

Four, approximate truth. I claimed it is a placeholder that lets the realist win in advance, since almost any amount of structural survival can be described as approximate truth. She claimed it is just what retention literally is. We went in circles for twenty minutes.

The thing I really want, though, is this: settle it forward. Both of us should write down now, in the document, a sample of currently successful theories plus an explicit rule for what would count as one of them being abandoned or demoted in forty years, and then each of us commits to a predicted proportion in advance. Sam predicts high retention and continued reference; I predict lower retention than she expects and at least a couple of central terms going the way of the ether. I also want, for each of us separately, what we would count as evidence against our own position, and what we predict the other person will say when the evidence comes in. And one more crux I raised that she thinks is a distraction: whether the record is any different in physics than in medicine and biology, where theories are messier and the discard rate feels higher to me.

Format it like this. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, the ones both of us would actually sign, and I mean non-vacuous ones: if a claim is something a total sceptic about science would also sign, cut it, it is not doing any work. Second, a disagreements table with a row per issue and a column for Sams formulation and a column for mine, each phrased so that person would put their name under it rather than feeling parodied. Third, a cruxes section: for each crux, the case, procedure or argument that both of us agree bears on it, what Sam predicts about it and what I predict. Fourth, a short section on which parts of this are merely verbal or definitional, if any are, and why. Fifth, a short aside where you tell us if one of our sub-arguments is just bad, but keep it separate from the document itself.

Two rules. Do not turn it into a verdict about who is right about realism, that is not what I am asking for and I will not use it. And do not pad it with citations, bibliographies or reading lists, or with appeals to what some famous person argued as if that settles it: we are two people in a kitchen, nobody can look anything up, and we will both be able to tell if you are bluffing. Just the formulations.
