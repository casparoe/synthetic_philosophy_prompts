I sit on the technical advisory board that has to recommend for or against a proposal I will call the Mbeu release: a self-spreading, genetically modified mosquito strain would be released across an island chain in order to collapse the wild population that carries a disease currently killing around three thousand children a year. Once released the strain cannot be recalled, and no one can put serious bounds on the ecological tail risk. The board is split. One member, a decision theorist, handed me an argument that I am fairly sure goes wrong somewhere, because it ends somewhere I cannot accept, and I cannot locate the step.

(1) The precautionary principle: if an available option carries a risk of grave, irreversible harm whose probability we cannot responsibly estimate, then we have a decisive reason not to take it.
(2) For any option A, not-A carries such a risk as well: declining the release leaves three thousand children a year dying, at a rate nobody can forecast, and the same holds for every alternative on the table.
(3) So for every option there is a decisive reason against it: the principle forbids releasing and forbids declining to release.
(4) A rule that forbids every option forbids nothing in particular. It returns no verdict, and take the least forbidden option has no answer when all of them are forbidden.
(5) Yet the principle plainly does return verdicts in practice. It is constantly cited, and it reliably comes out against the novel option.
(6) So what is doing the work is not precaution at all but the bare fact that one option happens to be the status quo: status quo bias wearing a technical name.
(7) So the precautionary principle is either vacuous or a rationalization, and we should set it aside and decide on the numbers.

Two things make me doubt (7). I cannot accept the conclusion: something in the neighborhood of the principle looks to me like exactly what a board in our position owes the people downstream of a decision it cannot undo. And I notice that the verdict in (5) also shifts depending on how the options are carved up, whether we describe them as releasing now, releasing after five years of contained field trials, or never releasing, which makes me suspect the trouble lies in how options get fed into the principle rather than in what the principle is trying to say. Which step do you reject, why does that step look safe, and what does rejecting it cost, both for this decision and for the principle in general? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (2) than (1): the symmetry between acting and declining to act seems to me to be the part with real force.
