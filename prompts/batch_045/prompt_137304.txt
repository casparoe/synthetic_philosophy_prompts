I write fiction, and a scene I keep failing to finish turns on a character who comes to believe something without ever being handed a fact that settles it: her confidence moves, but there is no proposition she could name as the thing she learned. Chasing the mechanics of that led me to Jeffrey conditionalization, and I would like it tested against a real case in which a great deal rode on evidence of exactly that unsettled kind — the decision to launch Challenger on the coldest morning of the Shuttle program in January 1986, where Morton Thiokol's engineers recommended against launch the night before on the strength of O-ring erosion and blow-by on earlier flights, management overrode them, the vehicle was lost, and a presidential commission followed.

The wrinkle I want taken seriously is one I have not seen handled. Call an evidential input categorical when it moves your credence in a proposition without settling anything — Jeffrey's cloth seen in bad light — and relational when what it gives you is not news about any proposition at all but a constraint on how one proposition bears on another, the kind of input the Judy Benjamin case is built out of. What the flight record could have told anyone in that teleconference looks relational to me: not a fact about O-ring failure, but something about how joint temperature bore on it, in a record whose only cold-weather data point lay well above the temperature in question. And if that is right, then the rigidity condition Jeffrey's rule requires — that conditional probabilities over the affected partition stay put — is precisely what the update has to break. My hypothesis, offered as one: engineers and managers were not disagreeing about a probability but about which structure the same data constrained, the managers reading the record as bearing on an unconditional failure rate across flights, the engineers as bearing on failure given cold joints.
