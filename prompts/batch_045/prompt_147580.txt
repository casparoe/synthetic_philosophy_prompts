I do a podcast and a newsletter on science-and-ideas stuff, and I've got this habit of agreeing to things on panels and then having to actually think them through afterwards. Last week, on a panel about autonomous vehicles, I heard myself say "well, obviously genuine moral dilemmas are possible" and then realised I can't say what makes a conflict a *dilemma* rather than just a difficult choice where one option is better and I don't want to admit it. So I've been putting cases in a notes app with my gut verdict next to each, and the pattern is definately wierder than I expected. What I want is a principle — or a short list of candidates — that fits the verdicts, tells me which ones it can't swallow, and gives me verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed. Please don't give me the literature: no names, no papers, no "so-and-so argues." I need to know whether there's a shape here or whether I'm just inconsistent, and I'm filing Friday.

Here are the cases, the way I actually have them:

1. An armed man tells a woman to pick which of her two children dies, or he kills both. My verdict: whichever she picks she does nothing wrong and shouldn't be blamed — but she should be wrecked by it for a long time, and I'd think less of her if she shrugged it off in a week. Also: if she refuses to choose and both are killed, that's worse, and I do blame her.
2. Two strangers are drowning, symmetrically situated, I can reach one. My verdict: not a dilemma at all. Save either one, coin flip if you like, no residue beyond ordinary grief.
3. Wartime: a young man who is the only person his mother has and who would be destroyed if he leaves, versus a resistance that genuinely needs him because there are very few who can do it. My verdict: this one feels like a real dilemma, and I can't tell you why it isn't just case 2 with better costumes.
4. I made two promises on the same day, knowing I couldn't keep both, and both people get badly hurt. My verdict: the real wrong was making the second promise; breaking either is also wrong; and apologising to the one I let down doesn't clear it, even if I picked the more important one.
5. I can pay for an operation that restores my own kid's sight, or pay for the same operation for three strangers' kids. My verdict: I'm not required to save the three, and I'm also not required to save mine; both are permitted, and that permission isn't just "anything goes" — it has something to do with whose kid it is.
6. A mayor authorises something that would normally be an outright violation of people's rights, because it's the only way to avert a much bigger disaster. My verdict: if it's genuinely necessary and proportionate, not wrong — but a mayor who does it briskly and feels nothing afterward is a worse mayor, and resigning instead of doing it is fine, maybe admirable, since someone else will make the call anyway. (This is the pair that keeps me up: in 1 refusing to choose is worse, in 6 handing the choice to somebody else is fine. Is there a difference or am I just inconsistent?)
7. An ICU doctor during a months-long shortage, allocating beds by a written protocol, shift after shift. My verdict: almost no moral phenomenology at all — it feels like paperwork — even though the stakes dwarf case 3. And the committee that wrote the protocol in advance did feel awful doing it, and I think they should.

The rough principle I've been gesturing at goes like this: a conflict is a genuine dilemma only if (a) whatever you do leaves a normative remainder, (b) the remainder isn't a debt you can discharge by an apology or a compensation payment, and (c) it isn't the kind of conflict you could have settled in advance by a rule you'd endorse without knowing which party in it you were going to be. I think (c) is the clause doing all the work — it's what makes 7 feel like paperwork and 3 feel like tragedy — but I also suspect it gives the wrong answer somewhere, maybe on 4 or 6. Test it, repair it, or replace it. One more restriction, which I want you to either honour or explain why it's cheating: I've only counted cases where the agent knows what's at stake; the ones where you're simply unsure which option is worse I've been filing separately as "fog" rather than as dilemmas, and I'd like to know whether that distinction is doing real work or is just me tidying up.
