My friend Dan runs a testimony reading group I've been going to for a couple of months — the reductionism vs anti-reductionism literature, though he point blank refuses to say which camp he's in — and he's very consistent about handing down verdicts on cases. His calls so far, all on whether the hearer *knows*:

- a colleague of ten years with a known track record of being right about this sort of thing says p, p is true, hearer has no worries: knowledge
- same colleague, except two independent reliable people have just told the hearer she's been misreporting this lately — and she's actually right this time: no knowledge
- total stranger at a station, but wearing a staff lanyard and coming out of a staff-only door: knowledge
- someone the hearer knows is a habitual exaggerator about this kind of thing, right this time: no knowledge
- a remark overheard on a bus, not addressed to the hearer, from someone the hearer recogises as an expert on the matter: knowledge
- a five year old the hearer knows is reliable about where the cat is, saying where the cat is: knowledge
- stranger on a train telling you the time when you know people on trains mostly guess and the carriage clocks are wrong, and he happens to be right: no knowledge
- someone who sounds completely drunk, is actually stone sober and reliable, and is right: no knowledge

Weirdly, in eight weeks we never once did the bog standard case — plain stranger, no badge, no track record, nothing suspicious, right about what they say. So I want to spring one on him Thursday, but with a wrinkle. Here it is: you find an unsigned handwritten index card pinned inside the map box at a trailhead saying the bridge at mile 4 is out and you should take the south detour. No name, no date, unfamiliar handwriting, nothing jokey about it. You've no idea who wrote it and no way to check before you'd have to decide. You do have the ordinary lifetime's worth of evidence that people who leave notes like that are usually right, and there's nothing at all pointing to this one being a mistake or a prank. And as it happens the bridge is out, the note was written that morning by a ranger who checks it every day, and the detour is fine.

Will Dan say you know? More usefully: what's the rule he's actually been running to get those eight verdicts? Also: which of his eight verdicts is the closest analog, is saying yes here going to commit him to the plain stranger case too, what exactly would he be committing himself to if he says no instead — and what's the one follow-up case I should bring along that would actually seperate the rules that agree on this one?
