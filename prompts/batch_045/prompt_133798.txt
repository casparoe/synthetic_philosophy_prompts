Fix the following claim about ideal rationality and call it U: an agent facing a decision should choose the act prescribed by the policy that maximizes expected utility from the standpoint of its prior, even when the evidence the agent has actually received makes some other act look better — as in Newcomb's problem with a near-perfect predictor, or in a counterfactual mugging where a predictor has already seen the coin land tails and asks you to pay anyway. I want your credence in U as a normative claim about rationality, not as a design heuristic for systems we might build, and I want the decomposition behind the number.

Give a rough probability and one line of justification for each of: (i) that the standpoint of the prior is even available to an agent that already has memories, or whether such an agent can only simulate what it would have committed to, and what authority that simulation has over it; (ii) that there is a principled line between the indexical or self-locating information an agent may still use and the worldly information U tells it to ignore, given that no agent can be updateless about everything; (iii) that the intuitive verdicts in the paradigm cases are evidence about what rationality requires rather than about what kind of agent we admire or would want to have around; (iv) that U is specified well enough to have a truth value at all, given that evaluating whole policies seems to require a treatment of logical counterfactuals that nobody agrees on; (v) that U survives its information-averse consequences, the cases where a U-style agent declines free evidence or pays not to learn something because learning it would change what its prior-standpoint policy prescribes.

Then show how the terms combine into your headline number, name the term that number is most sensitive to, and say what would count as settling the question: an agreed formal specification, convergence of intuitive verdicts, or something weaker than either. My own credence is 0.55; a colleague who treats the information-averse consequences as a reductio sits at 0.1. Tell me which of (i) through (v) our disagreement most likely reduces to, and what single argument would move your own number furthest.
