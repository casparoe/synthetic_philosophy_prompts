I write fiction, and the scenes I keep returning to are ones in which two people want incompatible things and have to go on living in the same valley, or the same marriage, or the same department. I have noticed that I have strong, unorganized opinions about which of these settlements are genuine — where both parties really do better than they would have done by fighting to a decision — and which are shams dressed up as fairness, where everyone gets a thinned version of what they wanted and calls it wisdom. I would like the principle behind the opinions, or told plainly that there isn't one. I have a working rule of my own and I suspect it is wrong; I would like it tested against my cases and either repaired or replaced.

My working rule: a settlement is a genuine gain from trade only if it divides the disputed matter extensively rather than intensively — each party gets undiluted say over some part of it, rather than both getting a diluted version of the same part — and each party ends up nearer to its own end than it would have been had the quarrel been fought to a decision.

Here are my verdicts. I have tried to state them the way I actually hold them, including the places where they pull against each other.

A. The two sisters. Their mother's house: one sister wants to keep it in the family and raise her children in it; the other wants it sold and the money given to a malaria charity. They sell, buy a smaller house in the same town, and place it in a trust that rents it below market to a family in perpetuity, with half the proceeds and all the rent going to the charity. Verdict: excellent. Both are better off than either would have been if she had simply won. This is the case by which I judge the others.

B. The highway and the marsh. A town divided between a four-lane bypass and preserving a marsh that a handful of people actually birdwatch in. The compromise offered at the hearing: a two-lane road, plus restoring thirteen acres of wetland five miles away. Verdict: bad, and I think worse than a coin flip. The birdwatchers never wanted thirteen acres of wetland function; they wanted that water, at that bend in the road, on Sunday mornings. And the road's friends get a road too small to carry the traffic it was wanted for. Note that this has the same shape as A — neither side gets what it wanted, both get something — and comes out the opposite way.

C. The department, first dispute. Ten historians, one line. Five want a paleographer, five want a specialist in print culture; neither faction cares about the other's field. The chair's settlement: the paleographer this year, the print-culture person the next, with a binding commitment and a penalty for reneging. Verdict: good. Waiting turns out to cost almost nothing.

D. The department, second dispute. The same ten people, the same year, now agreed that they need one hire in the long eighteenth century, but split between someone excellent on 1750–1789 and someone excellent on 1789–1815. The settlement on the table: hire a competent generalist who covers the whole span and is excellent on neither end. Verdict: bad. But I go back and forth, because the only alternative I can see is a coin flip, which feels arbitrary and wasteful in a way C did not. I would like to know whether I am wrong to dislike D or wrong to dislike the coin flip.

E. The log-roll. A coalition: one party wants religion out of the school curriculum, the other wants vouchers. Neither has any feeling about the other's issue. Each gets its way entirely. Verdict: fine, but it produces in me none of the warmth I feel about A. It reads as a deal, not a compromise, even though it is the textbook picture of gains from trade, and it satisfies both halves of my working rule perfectly. I suspect a correct principle will rank E very high and I find that I resent it.

F. The nun's hospice. A large donation is offered to a hospice on condition that it stop providing palliative sedation, which the donor regards as killing. The settlement someone proposes: take the money and sedate only the hardest cases. Verdict: monstrous, and not a compromise at all. There are offers that cannot be split, and the partial version is worse than either of the pure outcomes. I want a principle that explains why, without appealing to anything as cheap as the word sacred.

G. The imposed settlement. Two villages, a generation of feuding over water rights. An outside authority imposes a formula for dividing the water. Both villages think it is rigged against them, both resent it for thirty years, nobody is shot, and the farms on both sides prosper. Verdict: good, and I think good partly because neither side likes it. But this sits badly with A, where I took both parties' satisfaction as the sign of a real gain. I suspect I am measuring against a different baseline in the two cases — against the feud in G, against each side's own wish in A — and I would like that made explicit rather than smoothed over.

H. The valley, which is the case I actually need. My novel: an ageing shepherd and a rewilding ecologist are given joint management of a valley for ten years. He wants sheep on it; she wants the sheep off it and trees on it. They settle it themselves: sheep graze the lower valley, the upper valley is fenced and planted, and she helps him sell the wool at a premium under a conservation-grazing label. Verdict: I cannot settle on one. In chapter two I have him think the bargain was a good one; in chapter eleven I have her think it was a swindle dressed in a brand. I need to know which is honest, because I also need to know whether this settlement holds for ten years or comes apart in chapter nine, and I cannot write it properly until I know which of those is true.

Also: does whatever principle you give me reach cases where the parties' preferences are themselves changed by the settlement — where someone comes to want, and value more, the thing she only accepted because she had to? Half my plots are that, and it is not obvious to me whether a gain from trade should be measured against what people wanted before or against what they want after.

What I want, in this order. Tell me briefly, in prose, which clause of my working rule survives and which does the damage, and whether the damage is in the extensive/intensive distinction or in the baseline. Then give me a stated principle, with the case that motivates each clause and what would count against that clause. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, what it would cost me to keep that verdict anyway, and which single verdict you would preserve at the expense of elegance if you had to. Then three cases I have not considered, with the principle's verdicts on them, at least one of them a case where a compromise changes what the parties want, and at least one of them a single person dividing her own life across time — my protagonist spends her twenties in litigation paying for her mother's care, intending to write in her thirties, and then finds she no longer wants to write; I do not know whether that is a bargain honored or a swindle, and I would like the principle to say.

Finally, the thing a philosopher would not ask for and I do need: for each of A through H, a durability rating from 1 to 5 — how long the settlement holds before it comes apart — and, where it comes apart, the failure mode: which clause of the agreement fails first, and what the characters do the morning they notice. I need the valley to be honest, not merely tidy.

Please argue it out rather than sending me to a literature. I have no access to a university library and no use for a reading list or a set of citations; anything I cannot check, I cannot use.

Give the deliverable as a single JSON object with these fields:

principle — one sentence, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen.
clauses — array of objects, each with: clause (the condition), motivating_case (the letter and one-line description of the case that forces the clause), and falsifier (what kind of case would show the clause is wrong).
case_verdicts — array of objects for A through H, each with: case, my_verdict, status (accommodated, accommodated only after repair, or overturned), and reason (two or three sentences).
overturned — array of objects, each with: verdict, cost_of_keeping (what the principle would have to become), cost_of_dropping (what else I would have to give up).
baseline — object with: metric (the single baseline your principle measures gains against), and mismatch (which of my verdicts implicitly use a different baseline, and why).
new_cases — array of at least three objects, each with: case, predicted_verdict, and why; one must involve preferences changed by the settlement, one must be a single life divided across time.
hal_and_miri — object with: verdict (genuine gain or not, and say which), durability (1 to 5), failure_mode (which clause fails first, and what happens the morning they notice).
durability — array of objects for A through H, each with: case, rating (1 to 5), and failure_mode.
