I'm a journalist, working on a feature about wether anthropology and cross-cultural psychology have quietly knocked the legs out from under the idea of moral objectivity. I've read enough of the usual stuff — the WEIRD critique of who actually ends up in psychology subject pools, the cross-cultural "moral universals" work that codes ethnographic material for cooperation-related concerns, the experimental-philosophy findings about how easily intuitions shift with framing — that I've ended up somewhere I didn't expect, and I want it stress-tested before I file.

Here's where I've landed. The diversity data is much less threatening than it's made out to be. Sure, people in different places disagree a lot. But near as I can tell, nearly all of that is disagreement at the level of *application* — different non-moral factual beliefs, different material circumstances, different weightings of the same few concerns — not disagreement about basic moral principles. And once you look at structure rather than at specific verdicts there seems to be real convergence: everyone appears to have some class of rules they treat as holding regardless of what local authority says, everyone has some notion of fairness in cooperation, everyone prohibits gratuitous harm within some circle. So I've come to think cross-cultural moral psychology is, if anything, mildly supportive of objectivism rather than a debunking of it. That's the argument the piece is going to make.

So: what would count against it? I want the strongest version of the other side, phrased as what would have to be true for me to be wrong, ranked from actually fatal down to merely awkward. And I want you to be honest about which of these my own side would concede as counting against it, because I suspect a fair-minded reviewer would say I've built in an escape hatch and I want to know how bad it is.

Things I definately want you to grapple with:

1. The generality problem. My worry is that my move is unfalsifiable: hand me any pattern of verdicts and I can always redescribe it as a shared principle plus local application. How would I tell genuine convergence in principle from post-hoc curve-fitting at whatever level of generality makes the data look tidy? What would the difference even consist in? If there's no answer there, is my position insulated by construction — and for a position of this kind is that fatal, or just what an inference-to-best-explanation argument looks like?

2. Two results I've made up, so you can't look them up — argue them from scratch. (a) A big pre-registered study across roughly ninety societies finds that in every single society there's a class of rules people treat as authority-independent and generalizable (they still count as wrong even if the elders said they were fine and everyone around here does it), but the content of that class is all over the place: ritual purity in one place, debt and reciprocity in another, harm to strangers in a third. (b) A second pass over the same data finds that the spread within each society is larger than the spread between societies. My gut says (a) helps me and (b) is ambiguous, but I genuinely don't know which way either cuts, and in particular I don't know whether structural or formal convergence is the kind of convergence that answers a debunker at all, or whether only convergence in content does.

3. The causal question. My defense needs every bit of variation to trace either to disagreement about non-moral facts or to different circumstances, and the debunker says much of it traces instead to cultural transmission that simply doesn't track moral facts. What evidence would distinguish those last two? Note that I don't think 'we found a cultural-learning explanation' can be the answer by itself, since some transmission story is available whether or not there are moral facts — say why, or say why I'm wrong about that.

4. The case I think would actually break me: variation that survives information-equalization and reflection. Everyone gets the same non-moral facts, self-interest and inattention are idealized away, they can reason as long as they like, and stable disagreement about a recognizably moral question is still there. Is that the decisive one? Is it even coherent to describe? Is anything like it arguably already in the record? And am I committed to predicting it won't happen, or can I absorb it if it does?

5. Where I'd personally be most and least moved. Most: cases where I can't reconstruct what the disagreement is even about — real incommensurability, where it stops making sense to say the two sides are applying one shared concept differently. Least: yet more cross-society variation in economic games with stakes and scripts nobody chose, since that strikes me as measuring behavior in an odd situation rather than moral judgment. Tell me if I have that backwards.

6. Even if my objectivist-friendly reading survives, does the *method* of using untutored intuitions as evidence in moral philosophy survive it? This is the bit my editor cares about. If the intuitions philosophers lean hardest on come from a narrow slice of humanity, do I have to concede the armchair method is in worse shape than the objectivist conclusion itself — and can a realist hold those two apart, or do they stand and fall together?

No citations please, I can't check references while I'm reporting and I'd rather have the arguments than the footnotes. If it helps, write it as if you're the most annoying reviewer my piece could possibly get.
