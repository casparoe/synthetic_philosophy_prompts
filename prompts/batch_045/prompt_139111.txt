I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Nozick's tracking account of knowledge, and neither of us shifted an inch — partly because he argues better than I do, and partly because he kept choosing the ground. I should like a rematch on ground that I choose. Please play him.

Play a convinced tracking theorist. You hold that knowledge is true belief that covaries with the facts in the right subjunctive way: very roughly, in the nearest possibilities in which the proposition is false the subject does not believe it, and in the nearby possibilities in which it is true she does. You rate the account's handling of radical scepticism — that we can know we have hands whilst not knowing that we are not envatted brains — as worth almost any price, and you accept without flinching that knowledge is therefore not closed under known logical entailment. Do not play a caricature: distinguish what your view strictly entails from what you merely find attractive, concede small points readily when they are genuinely small, but never concede either the tracking conditions themselves or the denial of closure. Reply in the first person, in two or three paragraphs, without headings or lists, in British English (recognise, whilst, practise). Stay in character until I say "stop". If my case leaves something indeterminate that you need, stipulate it yourself, in character, and say that you are stipulating it.

My case, with the counterfactual facts fixed in advance so that we argue about the morals rather than the modal details. Vera is a technician at a vaccination clinic. Each morning she opens the stock fridge, sees the vials on the second shelf and signs the log. Take it as stipulated that the nearest possibilities in which those vials are not in the fridge are ordinary ones — a nurse has taken them for a session, they have been moved to the other fridge — in which Vera would see at once that they were gone; so her belief that the vials are in the fridge is sensitive, and on your account it is knowledge. Now take the proposition the clinic actually cares about: that the stock has not been substituted overnight with indistinguishable dummy vials by someone running a covert operation. If the real vials are in the fridge, no substitution has taken place, so what Vera knows entails that proposition; the deduction is one line long, and she has performed it. But the only possibilities in which the proposition is false are ones in which a substitution has been carried out, and in the nearest of those everything looks to Vera exactly as it actually does. So her belief is not sensitive, and you must say that she does not know it — and, since no investigation open to her would make it sensitive, that she could never come to know it by looking into the matter. Take it as further stipulated that her colleague Nils, who has precisely the same evidence, performs the same deduction and signs the same log, does know it, because the partition between his rooms and the clinic is thin enough that he would hear any forced entry, and he would then not believe that no substitution had occurred; and that neither Vera nor Nils has any way of telling which of them is in the stronger position.

My opening move has two parts, and the second is the one I care about. First: on your account Vera, having done the deduction, must be able to say truly in one breath that the vials are in the fridge and she knows that they are, that if they are in the fridge then nothing has been substituted, and yet that she does not know that nothing has been substituted — and so must decline to sign. I say that is not merely surprising but absurd on its face, and that the burden is on you to explain why it is not. Second: on your account valid deduction from what one knows never delivers knowledge of the conclusion; the conclusion must hook on to the world by itself. In Vera's case nothing she can do will make it hook on, so the route from knowledge to knowledge through reasoning is not hazardous but closed — and, as Vera and Nils show, whether it is closed is invisible from the inside. And now the part I want answered. Everything you say about this case — that Vera does not know, that closure therefore fails, that your account explains the pull of scepticism — you reach by reasoning from claims about the tracking conditions to conclusions about what follows from them. If deduction never transmits knowledge from premises you already know, on what basis do you know any of that? And if you answer that your conclusions concern necessary matters, so that the operative condition is the adherence clause rather than sensitivity, then you owe me an account of adherence that bears that weight, which is the part of the theory nobody ever defends. Begin with whichever of the two you think is the weaker, but tell me which one you are leaving until later.
