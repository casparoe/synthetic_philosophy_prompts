I'm about 60,000 words into a novel and I've written myself into a corner I can't think my way out of, so I'd rather you look at somebody else's book for a minute than at mine. Mine is about a man in his late thirties with early-onset Alzheimer's who, years earlier and in full possession of himself, wrote a letter saying that he did not want to be kept going once he could no longer recognise his daughter. That stage arrives. The problem is that he now seems... fine. He gardens, he laughs at the same stupid things, he recieves visits without distress, and the person who has to decide whether the old letter still binds is the daughter, who is thirty and whose life has been shaped by looking after him. I keep going back and forth on whether honouring that letter is respect or betrayal, and I think part of why I'm stuck is that I've been treating the request as a single thing when it isn't. So here's the kit I'vebeen using: first, authorship — whose reasons are actually in the request, given that a request like that is made inside a family, a cost structure, a set of exhausted people, and a healthcare system that would quite like the bed; second, uptake — what the person who complies is doing, since complying with a request to die can express respect but can equally express love, mercy, guilt, exhaustion, or a need to be the person capable of the hard thing, and those are very different acts wearing the same face; and third, something I've started calling the last-chapter problem, which is that in a story the ending gets to re-evaluate everything before it, so that a death freely chosen at the close makes the whole preceding life look like it was always heading there — which is often true of novels and, I suspect, sometimes false of lives.

So: read Me Before You, the Jojo Moyes novel (I've seen the film too), through that kit. It's the obvious test case and I keep dodging it. Will has money, a family, a set timeline, and a companion hired by his mother to improve his spirits; he has decided to go to a clinic in Switzerland and has given everyone six months, and Louisa's project in that time is to give him reasons to stay. I want a reading that takes the book seriously as an argument and not just as an illustration. Is Will's decision actually dramatised as his own, or does the book's machinery — the deadline, the mother, the hired companion, the trips, the letter, the money, the final scene of Louisa in Paris — quietly relocate the authorship of the request into other people's needs, including the reader's need for a shaped ending? Louisa is the protagonist and her arc is completed by his death in a way his would not be: does the novel know that, or does it launder the helper's interest into mercy? And what about uptake — is Frank... sorry, is Louisa's eventual acceptance of his decision meant to be the moral high point, and if so, what does that say about what the book thinks love requires?

I'd also like the honest parts, because I'm going to have to do the same thing in my own book and I'd rather not cheat. Where does the novel simply fail to imagine the alternative — the well-funded, well-supported life as a wheelchair user that its own premise has already paid for — and how much of its case depends on that absence rather than on anything it argues? What is the strongest version of the objection from disability-rights critics, and where, if anywhere, does the book answer it rather than just not engaging? Where does the reading break down, i.e. where does the book resist being read as an argument at all and insist on being a romance with an ending? And then the bit I actually need: what does the novel add to this debate that a philosophy paper can't — the texture of a request held over time, the difference between wanting to die as a judgement and as a stance, the seduction of a clean finale? Finish by telling me what that means for my manuscript: which scenes I'd have to write, and which of them would stop my ending from doing that last-chapter thing to my own character.
