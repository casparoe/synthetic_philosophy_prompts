Bit of an odd request, and feel free to tell me it's a bad question. I'm retired — thirty-odd years as a crystallographer — and for the last year and a half I've been sitting in on a philosophy of maths reading group at the university, mostly in the role of the person who asks the stupid question. Last week they did Hofstadter's MU puzzle and I have not been able to let it go.

For the uninitiated (and because you won't be able to look anything up, which is fine, I don't want you to): you start from the string MI and you're allowed four moves — add a U to any string ending in I; double everything after the M; replace any III with a U; delete any UU. Can you ever reach MU? No. The proof is that the number of I's in any reachable string is never divisible by 3 — doubling can't get you to a multiple of 3 and subtracting 3 can't either — while MU has zero I's, and 0 is divisible by 3. The room spent the session on what he calls jumping out of the system: shuffling strings inside, you can grind forever and never find out that MU is hopeless, but step outside, spot the invariant, and you're done in one line. Everyone seemed to treat that as a difference in *kind* between two kinds of thinking. I'm not buying it, and I want a number.

I've started cutting it up myself, probably crudely:

- **type 1 ("conservation")**: you stop searching inside and go looking for a quantity the rules preserve, or a partition they can't cross. What you get out is a short, machine-checkable certificate. For MIU it looks like it isn't just sound but *complete*: the reachable strings are exactly the ones whose I-count isn't a multiple of 3. I wrote a dumb script and it holds up to length 12 or so.
- **type 2 ("self-referential")**: you build the system a mirror. You map its strings into some other theory you already trust (later in the book the strings turn into numbers, M,I,U going to 3,1,0) and then make the system *as an object* the subject of the reasoning. This is where the interesting claims live — that a system can be made to talk about itself, that there are things true of it that it can't reach, that seeing the whole is the thing systems can't do and we can.

MU is won entirely by a type-1 move. So here's the proposition I want your credence on: **that the MU puzzle gives any real support for type-2 jumping-out being a genuine, distinct phenomenon** — as opposed to MU being a specially chosen *decidable* system in which a type-1 trick happens to be available and complete, so that the famous "step outside" is really a search over a small space of invariants that we just happen to be bad at doing by hand. Mine is about 0.25.

But I want the decomposition more than the number. Give me your credence and then take it apart: how likely is it that a fairly generic invariant-searching program would land the mod-3 invariant on a system this size? How likely is it that type-1 tricks suffice for "no" answers across that whole family of string-rewriting puzzles, given that the general version of the question is undecidable — i.e. how much of MU's tidiness is a selection effect in how the puzzle was picked? Would the lesson survive at all if MU had come from the undecidable part of the family? Is the felt "aha" evidence about kinds of thinking, or only about how the search feels from inside it? Are type-2 moves in principle unavailable from within? A number and a line of justification for each, then show me how they combine, tell me which term is actually load-bearing, and tell me what single argument or piece of evidence would move the headline number furthest.

Context for why I care: half my working life was noticing that a pile of reflections was systematically absent and thereby ruling out a whole space group without solving the structure. If that was jumping out of the system, fine — but it felt like a search, and these days a search is something you hand to a computer. And please dont tell me it depends on what you mean by jumping out of the system; I've handed you two definitions, so if it's the wrong cut, say so and make your own.
