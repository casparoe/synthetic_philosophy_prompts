I lead a four-person long-term policy evaluation unit inside a national audit office. Every two or three weeks we are handed a government proposal with effects that run past the next election and often past the next census — radioactive waste disposal, pension reform, energy infrastructure, fertility and family policy, large-scale housing and transport corridors — and we have about a day and a half to produce a short impact section that goes into a public report to a parliamentary committee, plus a one-page sign-off note to a director who has to defend it in person. None of my team has philosophy training; one has a statistics background. We cannot build population models beyond rough ranges, and we cannot look anything up beyond what is in the proposal and its annexes.

Our standard template asks three questions: who is made worse off, by how much, and can they be compensated or the cost offset? Increasingly we hit proposals where the honest answer to the first question is that the people who will bear the burden would not have existed at all if we had chosen differently — because the policy changes energy prices, labour markets, family timing, or where people live, and therefore changes who meets whom and which children are conceived. Under the alternative, other people would have lived instead. So the sentence our template wants us to write — that the policy harms future people — looks unavailable, and our drafts currently fudge this with vaguer wording that I do not think survives questioning.

I want a written procedure my team can actually run in the time we have. I do not need the intellectual history or references; I need an instrument.

Specifically, please give me:

A screening step that decides, in the time available, whether a proposal affects which people come to exist. Distinguish at least three outcomes — the policy is identity-locked (the same people either way), identity-scrambled (different people, roughly the same number, roughly similar lives), and identity-generative (a different number of people, or lives of a different shape). Tell us what counts as a sufficient answer at this step, what to do when the evidence only supports a probability rather than a yes or no, and what to do when the screen comes out mixed: identity-locked for the next twenty years, scrambled after that. Give us a materiality rule for that mixed case and say plainly what adopting it costs us.

A branching procedure. Where the policy is identity-locked, our existing person-affecting harm comparison is fine and I only want you to tighten it. Where it is identity-scrambled or identity-generative, the harm statement is unavailable, and the procedure must say what replaces it: what finding we are permitted to write, in what words, and what we must never write. In particular, address the temptation to write that these people would have been better off under the alternative — tell us whether that sentence is ever defensible and, if it is, under what description of the comparator.

Rules for the cases that break the clean branch, which are the ones that actually reach my desk:

Near-identity cases, where the policy changes a detail of origin — the month of a conception, which of two embryos, whether a birth is medically assisted — so that arguably the same person exists with a different life, arguably a different person exists. Handle the view that a person could not have originated from different gametes as one input among alternatives, and tell us what to write when we cannot settle it.

The mixed population case, where the same proposal makes identifiable living people worse off and also scrambles the identity of later generations. Give us a rule for combining a person-affecting finding about the living with a non-person-affecting finding about the unborn into a single sentence that does not equivocate between frameworks.

Cumulative sequences, where each decision in a programme is individually identity-locking but the programme as a whole scrambles identities. Tell us whether the procedure evaluates the decision, the programme, or the standing policy, and how to phrase the finding when the answer is the programme.

The asymmetry case, where a proposal both adds people and changes the quality of life of people who would otherwise not have existed, and quality is somewhat lower but every life is well worth living. Tell us where the procedure stops and hands this to a named person, on the grounds that the disagreement is about values and not about facts.

At each branch, separate three things explicitly: what the procedure settles, what it settles only after the office has committed itself to one contested moral frame, and what it cannot settle at all. Where a frame must be chosen — a person-affecting restriction, an impersonal total or average comparison, a threshold or sufficiency standard, a rights-based or practice-based ground — say who signs off, what the report must disclose about the choice having been made, and how the same proposal would come out under a different permitted frame. Include a stop rule that returns an explicit no-finding output rather than a fudge.

Then run the whole procedure on three proposals, showing the branches taken and the actual wording you would let me put in the report:

One, a choice between building a deep geological repository for long-lived radioactive waste now, at large immediate cost and with small expected releases far in the future, and continuing interim surface storage for another century, at lower immediate cost and with a larger expected burden borne by whoever is doing the monitoring then.

Two, a rise in the state pension age, which changes labour supply, savings and the timing of children, so that within roughly two generations the population is different, while also making identifiable people now in their fifties and sixties worse off.

Three, a proposal my team wants to approve and expects to approve: a publicly funded expansion of fertility treatment and neonatal care in a country with a low birth rate, which will lead to a somewhat larger future population whose lives are somewhat more strained — by congestion, fiscal load and competition for places — than they would have been under a smaller-population path, though comfortably worth living. Apply the procedure without softening it, and tell me what it returns even if the result is that our recommendation has to rest on a frame the office would repudiate, or that the only defensible finding is the no-finding output.

Finally, be straight with me about what the procedure cannot do. I want to know which questions in our current template have to be deleted outright rather than revised, where the procedure will systematically misfire on the proposals we actually get, and which part of the difficulty is not representable in a checklist at all and has to be carried by the director's judgment in the room.
