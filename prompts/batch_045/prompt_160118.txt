ok so hear me out: I think Clever Hans is basically a real-life instance of the "eliciting latent knowledge" problem, and I want you to run the case through that frame properly and then tell me where it stops working.

Facts as I remember them (correct me if I'm off, but don't look anything up): Hans was a horse in Germany around 1900 owned by Wilhelm von Osten, who showed him doing arithmetic, spelling, telling time etc by tapping his hoof. A commission under Carl Stumpf found no fraud in 1904, then Oskar Pfungst ran the careful tests around 1907: isolate horse and questioner, use questioners other than von Osten, vary with blinders whether Hans can see the questioner, and vary whether the questioner knows the answer. Result was roughly: correct most of the time when the questioner knew the answer and Hans could see him, near chance when he couldn't see him. Pfungst concluded Hans was reading tiny involuntary postural/facial cues that released tension at the right tap. Von Osten never accepted it and kept touring.

ELK setup I have in mind: you've got a system that's gotten good at predicting its input stream, you want a "reporter" that outputs what the system actually takes to be true about the world, and you're worried that a training signal made of human answers or approvals can be satisfied either by a head that reads out the system's own best guess about the world, or by one that imitates what a human would say. Both look identical on training data; they come apart exactly where the system knows more (or less) than the human demonstrator.

So: do the mapping carefully. Who or what is the predictor here, what's the latent variable, what's the report channel, and what are the two candidate "heads" in Hans's case? Is Pfungst's questioner-doesn't-know condition a genuine ELK technique, and if so what's the modern analogue — and what would it fail to rule out even if it passed? Then the parts it doesn't handle: Hans wasn't optimizing anything, there's no adversarial reward hacking, the cueing channel was perceptual and separable (blinders are an off switch nothing in an LLM has), and Hans had no model of the questioner's arithmetic, just of his body.

And the bit that actually interests me most: Hans's answers were true, and he did have a real skill — reading a person's posture. So what was his latent knowledge, the sum or the man's face? Does that show the phrase "what the system knows" is underspecified until we say what we want the report to track, i.e. that ELK needs a normative input it usually pretends not to need? Also: critics point out Pfungst never got Hans to give wrong answers by deliberately faking the cues. Does that matter for the analogy, or is it just a missing technique? End by saying whether this episode confirms the ELK worry, is an anomaly for it, or is a case the framework never really anticipated.
