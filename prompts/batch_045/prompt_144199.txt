I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on computational models of perception and action, and I've got stuck on a question about active inference that I keep going back and forth on, so I'd like a careful comparison rather than another summary of the framework.

As I understand the selling point: perception, action, and learning are all supposed to fall out of one imperative — minimize a bound on surprise, i.e. variational free energy — whereas in classical optimal control the agent minimizes a cost (or maximizes a reward) that is handed to it from outside the model, and perception is handled by a separate state-estimation step. The standard move is then to say that in active inference the goal is not an external cost function but a prior over preferred outcomes built into the generative model itself, so goal-directedness gets absorbed into the structure of the model rather than stipulated as an objective.

What I can't work out is how much of that is a substantive difference and how much is relabeling. Three sub-questions:

First, suppose I set up a partially observed Markov decision problem and solve it by model predictive control, with a cost that penalizes both deviation from a target trajectory and the expected entropy of my belief about the hidden state. Do I get literally the same behavior as an active-inference agent minimizing expected free energy under a prior preference over outcomes? If the two schemes are formally equivalent, what work is the variational framing doing beyond renaming the cost as a prior? And if they are not equivalent, I'd like you to point at the exact place where they come apart.

Second, on the standard telling, action reduces prediction error by making the world match the prediction, rather than by making the prediction match the world. But minimizing expected free energy means scoring counterfactual policies — sequences of actions the agent is not currently performing, whose sensory consequences it is not currently undergoing, evaluated over a future horizon. Doesn't that reintroduce precisely the kind of model-based, forward-simulating planner that the framework was advertised as replacing? What is left of the claim that perception and action are two aspects of a single process?

Third, a worry about precision. An agent can in principle reduce the prediction error it incurs either by acting on the world, by updating its beliefs, or by lowering the precision it assigns to the sensory prediction errors it expects — turning down the gain, as it were, or simply not sampling that stream of input. The last option looks far cheaper than the first two, and an agent that exploited it would present as more or less comatose: sitting in an unchanging environment with the gain turned down, and by its own lights doing perfectly well. I don't think this is quite the familiar dark-room worry, since the issue here is the agent's freedom to set precision rather than its choice of which state to occupy, but I suspect they are related. What in the framework rules this out? Is the answer just that precision parameters are themselves part of the generative model and carry their own priors, so that precision is optimized rather than freely chosen — and if so, doesn't that push the real explanatory burden back onto whatever fixes those hyperparameters?

Please use equations where they genuinely help, but always say in plain words what the equations are doing, and tell me what conceptual or empirical work the difference does if there is one, or what the active-inference framing buys you if there isn't.
