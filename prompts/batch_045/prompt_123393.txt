Hey — I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy and I'm finally writing the chapter of my thesis on accuracy-first (veritist) epistemology where you have to pick an inaccuracy measure, which I'd always assumed was the boring bookkeeping part and is now eating my life. I've got five candidates on my whiteboard and I want you to rank them. Set-up: a finite partition of worlds w_1 … w_n, c a credence function over it (not assumed to sum to 1, since the whole point is to see whether incoherence gets punished), and w_j the true cell. The candidates: (1) quadratic, sum over i of (c_i − δ_ij)²; (2) log, −log c_j; (3) spherical, 1 − c_j/sqrt(sum of c_i²); (4) city-block, sum over i of |c_i − δ_ij|; (5) one I invented last week because I hate that the log score lets inaccuracy blow up on fine-grained agendas — call it the soft-floor log with a knee at ε = 0.01: −log c_j when c_j ≥ ε, and −log ε + 1 − c_j/ε below it, i.e. log with the tail swapped for the tangent line at ε, which makes it bounded, continuous and still convex. My supervisor thinks (5) is cute and I think she is humouring me.

What I want: put the five in tiers (S/A/B/C is fine) for the job of being the measure of purely epistemic value in a veritist theory, one sentence each on why it lands where it does. Then score each 0–10 on four things I actually care about: strict propriety; boundedness; refinement-stability, meaning that re-describing the same doxastic state on a coarser or finer partition shouldn't change how inaccurate you're counted as being, since I don't want how bad it is to be wrong to depend on how many questions someone asked you; and whether the measure can underwrite the dominance argument for probabilism, i.e. whether for every incoherent c there's a coherent p that does better at every single world. Three cases I keep checking things against: the Fan, where you're flat over n cells and I track inaccuracy as n → ∞; the Coarsening, where one and the same state is written as (0.34, 0.33, 0.33) over three cells and as (0.34, 0.66) over two; and the Over-credence case, c = (0.5, 0.5, 0.5), where I ask whether the measure can even register the fact that your credences sum to more than one.
