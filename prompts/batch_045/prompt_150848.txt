Our journal's style committee has wasted two meetings on the gender-language section of our house rules, and in the middle of it I re-read Hofstadter's 'A Person Paper on Purity in Language' — the deadpan spoof of a pompous language columnist that he prints in Metamagical Themas next to that column about default assumptions and default images — and it made me think we're all arguing about the surface of a practice that rests on some theory nobody has ever written down.

Here is roughly what our rules say. Never use he, or man/men, or chairman, policeman, stewardess, for a person whose gender you don't know: use singular they, or recast in the plural. Always use the pronouns a person has said they want. But then the same document says it's fine — expected, even — to write 'the first female president', 'a male nurse', 'female founders': you mark gender exactly when the person departs from what readers will silently assume. And we killed 'mankind' and 'manpower' while leaving 'manhole', 'woman' and 'human' alone.

So: what view of meaning does this package presuppose? Our rules act as if generic he isn't really neutral, but I've also heard the defence that it is neutral in meaning and only tends to make you picture a man, and those feel like seperate complaints that would justify seperate reforms. Does banning it presuppose that the unmarked form literally means male, or only that it quietly defaults to a male image, or only that using it says something about the speaker? Does any of it presuppose that words shape thought in some strong way, or is it just about what a sentence does to a reader in the moment?

The bit I keep getting stuck on: the 'first female president' rule seems to lean on exactly the default the pronoun rule abolishes — it assumes readers do have a silent expectation to depart from, and that gender is a fact about someone a writer may flag — while the pronoun rule treats gender as the person's to declare. Contradiction, or two different jobs?

One member proposed a principle I've started calling the no-silent-default rule: never use a form for an unknown individual if that form also has a gendered use somewhere else. Is the practice actually committed to that, or does it just fit with it?

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, tell me which parts we strictly have to believe and which are optional extras, and say what would have to change if we decided the real problem was only silent defaults in imagery and nothing about meaning — would 'you guys' and 'nurse' be next, or is the practice already committed to not caring about those? And give the strongest version of the opposite view, the one where generic he was innocent all along.
