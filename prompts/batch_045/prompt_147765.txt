Consider the following hypothetical, which I will refer to as the Disk Case.

On a small island, five guilds — fishers, grain growers, potters, weavers, and metalworkers — trade with one another. For at least three generations, essentially all trade between guilds has been conducted with small red clay disks. Prices, wages, debts, and fines are all stated in disks. The disks have no use besides serving as payment: they are not ornament, not food, not tools, and no person or institution redeems them for anything. No new disks have been made in living memory, the total stock is fixed, and price levels have been stable for decades. The island has no government, no council with enforcement power, no army, and no tax collector; nobody issued the disks and nobody stands behind them.

What makes the case peculiar is the set of beliefs that keeps the practice going. Each guild accepts disks only because it believes that the next guild in a fixed cycle — fishers to grain growers to potters to weavers to metalworkers and back to fishers — treats the disks as sacred objects that that guild wants for their own sake, for use in its ceremonies. No guild treats the disks that way. Every guild's belief about the next guild is therefore false. But every guild's weaker belief — that the next guild will in fact hand over goods in exchange for disks — is true, and has been true for as long as anyone can remember. Nobody tests the stronger belief, because anyone who wants grain simply takes disks to the grain growers, who accept them. Twice in living memory a guild has discovered that the next guild does not revere the disks; on both occasions trade continued without interruption, because the disappointed guild concluded that the disks must be revered by the guild after next.

Please argue for the following position: the red disks are money in precisely the sense in which the U.S. dollar is money, and the Disk Case shows that a kind can be fully and genuinely socially constructed — realized, not merely believed in by mistake — even though every participant's explanation of why it works is false, no shared understanding of why it works underwrites the practice, nothing backs it, it answers to no authority, and it has no value apart from the practice itself.

In the course of making that argument, please do the following three things.

First, state as sharply as you can the general thesis about social construction that your argument commits you to: what, minimally, must be true of a population's attitudes, expectations, and behavior for a kind like money to exist among them? Say whether your thesis requires the participants' beliefs about one another to be true, and why or why not.

Second, confront the strongest objection you can construct: that the disks are not a social kind but a self-sustaining collective error, differing from a chain letter, a speculative bubble, or a fashion only in durability, and that treating them as money erases the distinction between a convention and a shared delusion. Explain why this objection fails, or else why it succeeds only against a version of your thesis that you can abandon without giving up the position.

Third, extend the argument to the cases of race and gender. If the disks are money, must someone who holds that race, or that gender, is socially constructed also accept that a system of classification can be genuinely constitutive of a kind even when the attitudes that sustain it rest on false empirical assumptions about what other people are doing and why? Or is there a principled difference — for instance, that money is a kind whose entire job is to circulate, whereas classifications of people purport to track something independent of our practices, so that error about them undermines the classification rather than constituting it? Take a stand on which way the argument should go, and defend it.
