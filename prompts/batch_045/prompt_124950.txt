I am a retired physical oceanographer, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science rather more seriously than I did while I was doing science. In a fortnight I have to give the after-dinner speech at a small workshop marking the retirement of a friend and collaborator of thirty-five years. He spent that career building box models: a handful of boxes standing in for an ocean that everyone in the room knows is not made of boxes. He was never in the least defensive about this. His line, repeated at conferences for three decades, was that a model you cannot falsify in the first five minutes is a model you have not yet understood.

My difficulty is that the two available ways of praising a career like his are both closed to me, and I would like the speech to be built around that fact rather than around a way of evading it. I cannot say his models were true, or nearly true, or on their way to becoming true, because he chose those assumptions knowing they were false and would remain false. But I also cannot fall back on the as-if defence — that an unrealistic assumption is harmless provided the predictions come out right — because his models were notorious for predicting badly, occasionally by an order of magnitude, while still being the reason the rest of us knew which measurements to take next. So the speech needs a third account of what a deliberately false model is for, and it needs to say it in a way that is praise rather than a lecture on the philosophy of modelling.

The distinction I have been trying out, and which I would like the speech to turn on, is between a falsehood you can pay back and a falsehood that is load-bearing. In the first kind, you can put the detail back in — add the friction, resolve the eddies, split the box in two — and the result degrades continuously, so you can see how much error you took on and where it went. In the second kind, the phenomenon you were after is simply not present in any more realistic version: make the assumption less false and the effect dissolves rather than degrading, so the false assumption was never a shortcut to the truth but the only place where the thing he was studying shows up at all. My own sense is that his best work is of the second kind, and that a scientific career worth celebrating consists largely in finding such places.

The audience is what makes this hard to write. Half the room are modellers who knew these models well and will notice at once if I quietly credit them with having been approximately true; the other half are his family and non-scientist friends, who do not know what a box model is and should not have to. Please write the speech: about eight minutes, warm rather than solemn, and structured so that the philosophical point about false models does the real work of determining what gets said about the career. The modellers should hear an actual claim about what the work amounted to; the rest of the room should hear why it was worth a life. Neither half should be condescended to, and nothing should be said about the models that he himself would have waved away as false.
