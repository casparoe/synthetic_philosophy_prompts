I work in formal epistemology, and the vocabulary I actually reason in is fairly narrow: credence functions over an algebra of propositions, conditionalization and its generalization to Jeffrey updating, coherence and Dutch-book style arguments, strictly proper scoring rules, and expected-utility maximization by a single agent whose reasoning we idealize by letting its computation run without bound and, more controversially, by granting it logical omniscience. Last week I sat in on a talk from the machine-learning side of the building where someone offered the following construction as a model of *ideal deliberation*, and I have not been able to stop worrying at it: a question is put to a person who, rather than answering outright, may split it into a handful of subquestions, hand each to a fresh copy of themselves, receive short written answers back, and then split those in turn if they like, up to some depth; the proposal (which that literature labels HCH) is then to treat whatever the root node says, as the tree is allowed to grow deeper and wider, as what an idealized version of that person would say — as the limit of deliberation, not just of effort. A colleague of mine brushed this off as "just the idealized Bayesian agent with a computational architecture bolted onto it," and I think that gloss is doing a great deal of quiet work, but I cannot yet say where.

So I would like a careful translation. Restate this construction in my vocabulary — credences, updating, coherence, scoring rules, expected utility, single-agent idealization — as faithfully as you can manage, and do not quietly substitute your own assumptions for the construction's. Two stipulations to make the translation possible: first, suppose that each node can also be asked to emit a number in [0,1] about a proposition and is evaluated by a strictly proper scoring rule, so that there is something credence-shaped at the leaves and not just text; second, suppose a readout convention, namely that the system's state of opinion just is the root node's report, and note explicitly when you are relying on that convention rather than on something in the construction itself. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which features of the construction have clean counterparts in my vocabulary (question-splitting, the copies, the depth limit, the growth limit)? Which translate only after being distorted — and say what the distortion is, and whether the distortion is smuggling in a normative claim the construction does not make? And which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? Two cases I want handled head-on rather than gestured at: (i) a node is permitted to ask, as one of its subquestions, what the whole tree of the same depth would say about the original question, which looks circular in a way I have no machinery for; and (ii) the claim that growing the tree approaches *ideal deliberation* at all, which in my terms would have to be a convergence claim about an estimator approaching some limit object in some topology, and I cannot see what that object is or what would count as convergence to it. Finally, tell me whether the residue indicts my framework or the construction: is the gap explained by formal epistemology having no way to speak about the decomposition of a reasoning process — treating reasoning as a black box whose internal structure is nobody's concern — or is it explained by this "ideal" being a different kind of thing from the Bayesian ideal, a limit of more help and more time rather than a limit of more computation and more coherence, so that calling both of them "ideal deliberation" is an equivocation?

Please don't point me to papers or give me a reading list; I have no library access at the moment and would only be guessing at references anyway. Structure your answer in four named sections, in this order: "Translation" (about 500–600 words), "Residue Report" (about 350–400 words, organized as what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion, and what has no counterpart), "Verdict" (about 250 words, on whether the residue is my framework's defect or the construction's), and "What Would Change My Mind" (about 150 words).
