I'm a neuro PhD student sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group, and I built this argument for last week's session and now I can't tell whether it's a genuine problem or whether I've just talked myself into something stupid. The reading group is split between the selected-effects / etiological side (Millikan-ish) and the Cummins-style causal-role side, and my argument says the two never actually disagree, which everyone agrees is the wrong result.

Two cases I'll keep appealing to.

Case 1, the dud: a heart with a malformed ventricle that can't pump blood. Everyone wants to say its function is still to pump blood and it's simply malfunctioning.

Case 2, the stowaway: a burrowing lizard with a bony chin plate that spread because males used it to shove rivals. The population moves onto fine dune sand. In the new habitat the plate happens to work beautifully as a kind of sled that stops the lizard sinking, and that effect is a big fitness booster — but nothing about sledding is part of the explanation of why the plate is there. Selected-effects people want to say: function is still shoving rivals, the sled thing is a lucky bonus. Causal-role people seem committed to saying its function now includes not sinking.

The argument:

(1) On the etiological account, a trait T has function F only because ancestors' T's doing F helped explain why T is here now.

(2) Selection can't respond to what a trait will do later, or to environments the ancestors never met. So what was selected was T's doing F in the environments actually encountered.

(3) But what a trait produces isn't a property of the trait on its own; it's a property of the trait together with the surroundings it operates in. Strictly, what selection picked out was the trait-in-that-environment producing F.

(4) So the function isn't T's, full stop — it belongs to the trait/environment pair. Functions are relativized: T has F relative to environment E.

(5) If it's relative to an environment, then relative to the environment the organism is actually in now, T's function is whatever T contributes now. In Case 2, that's not sinking.

(6) And in an environment where T contributes nothing useful, T has no function at all.

(7) So once you're precise about what selection acts on, the etiological account hands you exactly the causal-role verdicts with an environmental index stapled on. The history does no work. The two accounts are verbal variants of each other.

(8) That can't be right, because the whole selling point of the etiological account was that it gets Case 1 (the dud heart still has a function) and Case 2 (the stowaway doesn't have the sled function yet) in a way the causal-role account supposedly can't. So one of (1)–(7) has to go.

My own guess is that (2) is doing something sneaky with "respond to", but every time I try to say what, it collapses into something I already granted in (3). I'd much rather keep (1), (2) and the standard verdicts on both cases than accept (7), but I want to know what I'm paying and whether some weaker version of (7) survives.
