I'm a third-year internal medicine resident, and about six months ago our hospital started running a sepsis early-warning model alongside us — it just drops a risk score onto the patient's chart, and admin keeps telling us it's "one input among many." I've noticed I handle it in a totally lopsided way. If I've examined someone, settled into a fairly confident judgment that they're not septic, and then the model flashes high, I don't recombine the two things at all — I just demote myself to something like "eh, I've been wrong about this before," and order the lactate. But when the model is quiet and I *am* worried, I basically ignore the quiet and work the patient up anyway. My co-resident thinks none of this is philosophically interesting: she says higher-order evidence is just evidence, you feed it into the same update as everything else, and the "puzzle" is only that people are bad at arithmetic. I don't buy that, but I also can't say why. I started reading about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia on my last few nights off, so please don't spend your answer re-explaining the basic setup — the forecaster-and-calculator style cases, what akrasia is supposed to be — I've got that part.

What I actually can't get straight is a distinction I keep half-inventing and half-suspecting somebody has already named. Call a piece of higher-order evidence *transparent* if it tells you something specific about where your own reasoning broke: a colleague leans over and says "you're anchoring on the fact that she's thirty-one, look at the lactate trend." Call it *opaque* if it only tells you that reasoning like yours misfires at some rate, without pointing at the step — a base rate, a calibration statistic, the fact that residents miss sepsis about one time in N, or the fact that a trusted peer disagrees but can't say why. My gut is that these behave completely differently: transparent evidence seems like it can genuinely be folded back into the first-order question (I go look at the trend and change my mind about the patient), while the opaque kind seems to be the only stuff that actually forces akrasia on you, because there's nothing for it to hook onto in my first-order reasoning.

So explain what's really going on here. Is the transparent/opaque split doing any real work, or does it quietly collapse back into the plain first-order/higher-order distinction once you press on it? When the only higher-order evidence I've got is the opaque kind, what is it even to "respond correctly" to it — and can my lopsidedness (downgrading on a high score, ignoring a low one) ever be the right response, or is that just motivated reasoning wearing a lab coat? I'd like something reasonably concrete, and please write in American English.
