I'm running a reading group next week and I want a handout that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they're attached to. Can you help me build it?

First, take a breath and think carefully. You are a professional philosopher who has published on consciousness and on the metaphysics of mind, and you've supervised grad seminars like this before. Before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit about which distinctions in this debate actually do argumentative work and which are just terminological noise, and tell me in a couple of lines which ones you decided to build the tree out of and why. Then give me the finished instrument. Please don't just reproduce the structure of the standard encyclopedia overview — I've read it, and so has everyone in the room.

What I want is a decision tree / flowchart in plain text (ASCII is fine, or indented nodes) that takes someone from a starting commitment to a named position on whether there is phenomenal consciousness and what to do with the appearance that there is.

Concrete constraints:

- 8 or 9 branch nodes. Every node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or a concrete claim — a yes/no or a choice between two or three options — that a first-year grad student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. If a node is basically "do you think qualia are real?", rewrite it.
- Please build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of isms: start from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and then locate the actual positions in that space afterwards.
- Use at least three of these four cases, and feel free to tweak them:
  (a) the open-dashboard case: engineers rewire a subject's introspective system so that, while it is classifying some first-order state, the subject can simultaneously watch the mechanism doing the classifying and see that what it does is apply a coarse, categorical, self-ascriptive tag rather than detect any qualitative property. The subject then says "oh — there's nothing qualitative there after all." Have they discovered something, or have they just added a higher-order state that misrepresents the lower one?
  (b) the introspection-free subject: someone with intact first-order perception and intact reporting-from-the-outside, but no introspective system at all. Does anything go missing for them, and can the question of what's missing even be raised from outside introspection?
  (c) the swapped introspectors: two subjects' introspective mechanisms are exchanged, so each now classifies the other's first-order states. Does what-it's-like track the first-order state or the introspective mechanism?
  (d) the dimmer switch: a knob that continuously turns down the "seeming" while first-order discriminatory function stays fixed. Does the property itself fade, or only the representation of it? Can the knob reach zero with function fully intact?
  A fourth node I'd like woven in is a grain-mismatch problem: introspection represents its target as having a determinable character while the first-order state is maximally determinate — does the illusory content have a grain that no genuine property could have?
- 10 to 12 leaves. Each leaf gets a name (or "unnamed — as far as I know nobody defends this") plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Leaves I definitely want to be able to reach: full-blooded phenomenal realism of the hard-problem sort; the physicalist who keeps the realist intuition but gives an a posteriori or topic-neutral account; the Russellian-monist / panpsychist option; the representationalist who identifies phenomenal character with representational content; the quasi-phenomenal move where the illusion itself has attenuated, quasi-qualitative properties; illusionism that locates the misrepresentation only at the level of introspection versus one that says first-order perception is already in on it; the eliminativist/quietist who won't even grant that there's an appearance to be explained; and the error-theory-about-the-concept view that treats phenomenal discourse as defective rather than false.
- Two axes matter more to me than the others, so give them real nodes: (i) veridicality debt — does a view owe an account of what it would be for introspection to succeed, i.e. of the condition it says is unmet, or can it treat the appearance as brute? (ii) downstream/upstream — is the misrepresentation manufactured by the introspective system alone, or is it already built into first-order perception? I think (ii) is under-discussed and I want it to actually split some leaves.
- At the end: count up the answer combinations your questions permit and list the three or four most interesting ones that no actual position occupies, with one line each on whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered.
- Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and say what further question would separate them even if you think the honest answer is indeterminate.

Aim for roughly one page of dense text; no node commentary longer than two sentences. No citations or reading list — I've got that side covered, and I want the handout to be about the cases. Thanks!
