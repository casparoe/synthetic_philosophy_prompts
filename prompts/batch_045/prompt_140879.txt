I work on a platform team that owns a real-time allocator: for every request we serve, the system has a hard latency budget — 200 ms at p99 — to pick one item out of roughly ten thousand candidates, and the choice is binding, with no re-ranking downstream and no undo. Two of us have been arguing about what the system should even be trying to do, and the argument keeps resolving into a fight between two analogies. My staff engineer's position rests on the picture that bounded rationality is just optimization under a resource constraint: the mind, or our allocator, is a computer with finite time and memory; the normative spec is still maximize expected value; and heuristics, thresholds and early stopping are engineering compromises that approximate that spec. On his picture the only honest way to score us is regret against the offline optimum, and if we ever buy more compute the right move is to search harder and converge toward it. He uses this to argue that we should keep one scalar objective and treat our stopping rule as a knob controlling approximation error.

I pushed back after reading some Herbert Simon on a flight, and my version leans on a different picture: rational behaviour is shaped like a pair of scissors, one blade being the structure of the environment the agent is actually in and the other being the agent's computational limits, so you cannot grade a procedure by comparing it to the optimum of a problem the agent never had in front of it. On this picture a stopping rule is not a degraded optimizer but a mechanism matched to an environment, and there are environments — the stock example being an ant's path across a beach, which looks intricate only until you look at the sand — where more computation or more information makes outcomes worse. I use this to argue that part of the latency budget should go into choosing a better representation of the problem rather than into searching more options, and that we should stop treating the offline optimum as the benchmark.

Two wrinkles from our actual system that neither picture seems designed for. First, the candidate stream is not a fixed environment: it arrives from a counterparty who observes our behaviour over time and does better when we take certain items, so the order in which options present themselves is partly a response to our stopping rule — and a rule that says take the first item over the bar is a rule that can be fed. Second, we are considering making the bar itself adaptive, raising it after a run of easy wins and lowering it after a drought, which is roughly what I took Simon to be describing for human aspiration levels; but a colleague pointed out that a threshold that drifts with recent luck starts to look like the agent is maximizing something like score minus search cost, in which case we are back to optimization with the objective relabeled, and I cannot tell whether that objection is deep or merely verbal. I would like an audit of both analogies: what maps onto what in each, where each mapping breaks, which features each one quietly imports into a situation that lacks them, which of the breaks actually matter for deciding what this allocator should do, and whether the conclusions each analogy was recruited to support survive once the breaks are on the table.
