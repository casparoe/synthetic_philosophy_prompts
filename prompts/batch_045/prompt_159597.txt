I am a PhD student in philosophy, finishing a paper on the problems that go under the heading of embedded agency in the AI alignment literature — the difficulties that arise for a reasoner or agent that is itself part of the system it is reasoning about: that the agent is smaller than the world it models and so can never hold a complete model of it; that there is no clean Cartesian boundary between agent and environment, since the agent is made of the same stuff and its parts are themselves agents with their own sub-goals; that self-reference imposes limits on how far an agent can trust its own reasoning and its own decision procedure; and that an agent's model of itself is necessarily coarser than the system that holds it. I want to open the paper, and a graduate seminar I am helping to run, with a literary case rather than another toy example, and Stanisław Lem's 1961 novel Solaris has been pressed on me as almost too exact a fit. I distrust exact fits, so I want the reading and the resistance to it in the same breath. Take the novel, not the Tarkovsky or Soderbergh films.

What I want you to do is read the book as a dramatization of those problems, and then tell me where the dramatization distorts them. Four things I would like taken up specifically.

First, the state of Solaristics as Kelvin finds it: a century of observation that has degenerated into nomenclature — an enormous taxonomy of oceanic formations, no theory, and a standing prohibition on interpretation. Does the novel give a reason for this failure, or only assert it? And if it gives a reason, is it the same reason an embedded agent cannot bootstrap a complete model of a world that contains it, or is it a different, more parochial one about the poverty of human concepts?

Second, Harey (Rheya): not an apparition but a physically materialized person built out of Kelvin's own memories and his guilt, who knows what he knows and knows nothing where his self-knowledge runs out. I want this read as an externalized instantiation of his self-model — a subsystem that is a copy of the agent, built by the environment from an external record rather than by introspection, and therefore more accurate about him than he is about himself. Is that the right structure, or am I reading pathos into it?

Third, the unauthorized high-energy X-ray bombardment that precedes Kelvin's arrival, and the fact that its effect is not on the ocean but on the experimenters. Read it as intervention in an environment you cannot model at all: what actually changes is the agent.

Fourth, the close, in which Kelvin has his own brain activity recorded and beamed down at the ocean, and then waits. I want this read against the thought that the only thing an embedded agent can offer an environment that contains it is a lossy model of itself, expressed in the environment's terms — contact as self-disclosure rather than explanation — and against Kelvin's own remark that we are not looking for other worlds but for mirrors.

The claim I am tempted to defend, which you should push back on if it will not hold: that the novel's real thesis is that the binding constraint on contact is not theory-building but self-knowledge, and that Lem has thereby dramatized something the embedded-agency arguments treat as a technical nuisance and leave aside.

Then the resistance. Two objections of my own that I cannot settle: (a) the structural problems of embedded agency arise for any agent anywhere, whereas the novel stages them as an encounter with a single alien other, which may be why the fit looks so good and means so little; and (b) Lem said he made the alien an ocean precisely in order to defeat personification — and if that is right, my mirror reading may be doing the very anthropomorphizing the book is built to prevent, by turning an indifferent process into something that wants something from us. Tell me which of these bites, and whether they are one objection or two.

Finally: what, if anything, does the novel add to the philosophical debate that the formal arguments do not — the phenomenology of living inside the failure, maybe, or something more? Keep plot summary to the minimum needed to fix the scenes, do not quote the text, and if you think Solaris is the wrong book and I should open with something else, say so plainly and name a better one, with the reason.
