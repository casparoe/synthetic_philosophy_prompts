I sit on the program committee of a private foundation whose stated mission is to increase the number of people who lead flourishing lives, and at our next meeting we have to choose between two five-year grants of identical size. The vote is deadlocked in a way I have come to think is philosophical rather than empirical. Grant A funds contraceptive access and voluntary family planning in a district of roughly 900,000 people; the foundation's own modelling says it would avert about 11,000 births over five years, mostly births to women who report not wanting another child now, and that it would raise measured wellbeing among people who exist either way — maternal health, household earnings, schooling per child. Grant B funds IVF and fertility care in the same district, where staff estimate roughly 1,200 additional births over five years to people who want children and cannot otherwise have them. The staff economist scored both grants on wellbeing-adjusted person-years and got nearly the same figure: the lives averted under Grant A would, on our own data, have been well above the local average, while the lives created under Grant B are expected to come out slightly higher still, though the two estimates sit inside each other's error bars. A complication that keeps resurfacing is that many of Grant B's patients would eventually have conceived naturally, so the children born under the grant are not merely earlier versions of children who would otherwise have been born; under the grant, different children exist.

The deadlock tracks two positions. One bloc argues that the beneficiaries of Grant B do not exist independently of the grant, so there is no one whom the grant makes better off and no one whom declining it leaves worse off; on the person-affecting asymmetry, Grant B's value is close to zero and Grant A wins comfortably on its benefits to women and existing children. The other bloc replies that Grant A does nothing the mission statement names — it makes nobody's life flourish — and points out that the first bloc's principle, applied consistently, would tell the foundation to stop counting most of what it currently counts. A third trustee has floated a compromise rule: count benefits to people whose existence depends on the grant, but only to the extent that their expected wellbeing exceeds a threshold set at the district median, and cap the contribution of such existence-dependent benefits at a fixed fraction of any grant's total score. Separately, our board chair has raised the repugnant conclusion about the mission statement itself, noting that maximizing the number of flourishing lives seems to imply that a much larger population of lives just barely worth living would be better, and has asked whether the committee should propose an amendment.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund one grant, split the money, fund both with conditions, or decline both and reopen the search — with a rationale a colleague who disagrees could actually answer, and it should state whether you accept, reject, or revise the threshold-and-cap rule and why. It should also give the committee's honest view on whether to recommend amending the mission statement, and it should give the strongest version of the losing position rather than only arguing for the winner.
