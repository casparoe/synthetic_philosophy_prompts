I am a novelist, and I am trying to make one scene in a manuscript philosophically honest rather than merely moving. The scene is a hearing: a woman who arrived at seventeen, fleeing a militia her father had informed on, was refused, never left, and has now spent eleven years in the same city — she works nights on a hospital ward, sings in a church choir, and is the person her neighbours ring when a boiler breaks. What I need from you is the argument, not the atmosphere.

Compare two accounts of what her claim consists in.

The first I will call the danger-anchored account: an asylum claim draws all of its force from the peril the claimant flees, so that its content is roughly "shield me from that," it is discharged by any state able to do so, and it thins out and eventually expires as the peril recedes. The second I will call the accrued-membership account: over years of actual residence a person becomes a member of the political community she lives in, woven into roles, obligations and mutual expectations, and membership of that kind generates a claim in its own right — one owed by that community in particular, not discharged by safety somewhere else, and not extinguished when the original peril fades.

Set the two side by side on four questions. First, what each implies about who counts at all: whether either account can accommodate the many people who leave for reasons nobody designed to punish them — drought, the collapse of a livelihood, the slow disintegration of order — without stretching a notion like persecution past the point of usefulness. Second, who must take such people in: whether the duty, on each account, is owed by a specific state or is a duty that any capable state may discharge on everyone's behalf, and what follows for returning a claimant to a "safe" third state to which she has no connection whatsoever. Third, the wrinkle my manuscript turns on: the eleven years exist because the state took eleven years to decide. May a state point to the staleness of a danger it was itself slow to adjudicate, or does its own delay alter what it owes? Fourth, what is left of each claim when the original peril has genuinely ended but the ties have not.

Then tell me whether the two accounts are really rivals. It may be that accrued membership is best read not as a competing theory of asylum but as a second and independent claim that begins where the first leaves off; if so, say what turns on reading it that way rather than the other.

Argue from the structure of the views. Do not give me a reading list, authorities or citations: I have no way to check them, and they would not help me write the scene.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
