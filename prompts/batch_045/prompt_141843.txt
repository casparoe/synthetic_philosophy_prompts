I convene the first-year Philosophy of Biology seminar at my college, and the week on "what is life?" always goes the same way: everyone agrees that a bacterium is alive, a pebble is not, and a virus is a cheat, and then they spend fifty minutes asserting their definitions at one another without ever locating the disagreement. So this year I want to replace the discussion with a handout built as a dichotomous identification key of the sort you find at the front of a field guide — numbered 1a/1b couplets, every couplet a single question that can be put to any candidate entity, each answer leading either to a further couplet or to a leaf, and each leaf a named position on the definition of life together with one sentence on the case that position is most embarrassed by. Please build me that key: ten to twelve couplets, seven to nine leaves, written so that a student with school-level biology and no philosophy can work through it. Please also build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first give me the couplet questions in order, with a note on which combinations of answers anyone actually occupies, and only then staple the names on. The leaves must include the astrobiology-community working definition ("self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution" — that sort of thing, described in your own words, no quotation needed), a metabolism-first or autopoietic-organisational position, an evolution-first or replicator position that is substrate-neutral, a genealogical or lineage position on which what matters is descent rather than capacities, a position requiring terrestrial carbon-and-water chemistry, a pluralist or cluster-concept position, and a deflationary or eliminativist position on which "life" has no sharp extension at all; add any others you think are missing. I want at least three answer-combinations marked as unoccupied, each with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody lives there (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored), and at least one pair of leaves flagged as genuinely inseparable by this set of questions, with the extra couplet I would have to smuggle in to prise them apart. No couplet may settle its question by using "alive", "organism" or "natural selection" in a way that presupposes the answer, since that is precisely what begs the question.

I also want the key tested rather than merely asserted, so please run the following cases through it in a table and tell me where each one lands. A cell whose genome was chemically synthesised from a digital file and "booted up" inside a host cell whose own genome had been removed. A lineage of cells that can no longer make their own ATP or copy their own DNA, because a machine built decades ago does both for them and has been drifting in its settings ever since, while the cells still repair themselves and respond to their surroundings. A bacterial spore sealed in a salt crystal with no detectable metabolism, revived after a very long interval. A clonal fungal network spreading continuously through a forest floor, metabolically active throughout, with no bounded body and never reproducing as a whole. A mule, and separately a sterile worker ant. A population of self-replicating computer programs that have been competing for processor time and paying for their own electricity for years, subject to mutation and selection. A virus, and separately a defective viral particle that can replicate only when a helper virus is present. A candle flame, a crystal growing in a supersaturated solution, and a self-copying RNA molecule in a cell-free broth. A heart muscle cell taken from a person ten years ago, still contracting in a dish, never dividing again. An insect and the bacterium that lives inside it, neither able to survive without the other. Flag which of these you think are anchor cases the key simply must get right, and make sure at least two of them come out with a verdict most people would reject — I want my students to see the key break, not just to see it work.

Two closing requests. First, no references, reading lists or citations at all: I cannot check them, the students will not follow them, and I would rather have your own formulations than a bibliography. Second, at the end add a short note on how to run the thing backwards: if I wanted a "which definition do you actually hold?" version that sorts people rather than cases, which three or four answers of mine would give me away, and what should I do about the respondent who notices the pattern of the key and answers strategically?
