I sit on the committee that maintains our regional mass-casualty triage protocol, and I have lost the thread of the argument about what it commits us to. The rule sorts casualties into four colour-coded groups: people with life-threatening but survivable injuries are treated first; the walking wounded are asked to step aside and are treated last, even when closing a bleeding wound would take a minute; those whose injuries cannot be survived given the resources on hand are tagged expectant, given comfort care only, and deliberately passed over so that the same resources go to others. So the least injured and the most severely injured are both pushed to the back while the people in the middle go first.

One colleague says the rule is straightforwardly prioritarian — the view that a benefit matters more the worse off the person who gets it is — and that this is the whole story. Another says prioritarianism is refuted by the levelling-down objection, since if the worse off mattered more we would have to welcome making the better off worse off; so what we are really doing, he says, is maximising lives saved, which is exactly why the expectant are abandoned. A third proposal on the table would make the weighting explicitly comparative: fix each patient's weight by their rank in that incident's severity ordering, and apply that weight to setbacks as well as to benefits, so that a loss falling on someone better off than the rest counts for less.

What does the protocol as it stands strictly presuppose about distributive value, and what does it merely fit with — does its priority track how badly off someone is, the size of the benefit treatment would produce, or a threshold of saveability, and do the expectant and the walking wounded settle which? Does anything in the rule rest on comparative facts about the other casualties rather than on facts about each patient, and would that make a difference? Can the levelling-down objection reach the first colleague's view at all, given that it scores outcomes by a weighted sum of individual well-being, or only the comparative variant — and if the comparative variant escapes it too, does it inherit the objection in some nearby form? Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
