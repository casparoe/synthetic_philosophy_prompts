Regulators who set "safe" exposure limits for pollutants and food additives basically never use probabilities. They take the highest dose at which the test animals showed no effect, then divide by a round factor of ten for extrapolating from animals to humans, another ten because people differ in sensitivity, maybe another ten if the data's thin or only a lowest-effect dose was found, and whatever comes out is the limit. EPA describes its own reference doses as estimates whose uncertainty spans about an order of magnitude, and then publishes a single number anyway. Meanwhile the same agencies, on the benefit side of the very same decision, are happy to multiply point estimates together and do expected-value cost-benefit analysis.

What view of probability and uncertainty does that practice presuppose? Is it committed to something like: here we only have a set of admissible distributions rather than one, so we should pick the option that does best under the worst of them? Or is it all reconstructible by a Bayesian with a hierarchical model, heavy tails and a loss function, so the practice never really needed imprecise probabilities? And does it presuppose that "safe" is a threshold concept at all, rather than a matter of degree we've chosen to cut off somewhere?

Two follow-ups. First: does the practice presuppose that more evidence narrows the uncertainty? I half-remember something about sets of priors where conditioning on new evidence can actually widen your interval instead of narrowing it — dilation? — and if that's right, "run another rat study" isn't obviously doing what the practice assumes it's doing. Second: if the imprecise view were taken seriously, what would these agencies have to stop doing? Stop issuing single best-estimate limits, admit that some regulatory options just can't be ranked against each other, or quit doing expected-value arithmetic on the benefit side for consistency's sake?
