I'm a software engineer — I run the data platform team at a mid-size consumer app company, and my only philosophy background is some half-finished online course material — and I've been invited to give a thirty-minute talk with fifteen minutes of questions at a joint session of a sociology department's stratification workshop and a philosophy of science reading group. I need help preparing for the question period, because I can tell I'm walking into a room where four different people will be annoyed with me for four completely different reasons.

The audience, as precisely as I can describe it: two quantitative life-course demographers who fit age-period-cohort models all day and who think popular generational discourse is a carnival-mirror version of what cohort analysis can actually support; one historically oriented sociologist who works in the classic European literature on generations and who draws a firm line between a cohort that merely occupies a shared position in historical time and a generation that has become an actively self-aware historical formation, and who thinks the Boomer/Millennial/Gen Z vocabulary has thrown away everything that made that tradition interesting; an analytic philosopher of science who works on natural kinds and on homeostatic-property-cluster accounts, and who is attracted to the idea that species are historical individuals rather than classes; a dozen-odd graduate students; and one colleague from my company's people-analytics group who has built real dashboards around generational segments and will defend them as decision-useful regardless of what the metaphysics says.

My thesis, briefly: generational labels are not natural kinds and not even respectable social kinds in the homeostatic sense — there is no cluster of co-occurring properties and no mechanism holding such a cluster together. What the labels actually pick out is what I call an exposure bundle: for people born inside a given window, a large set of historical exposures tends to travel together simply because birth year is a shared cause of when each of them was encountered — what the entry-level labor market looked like in the year you started looking for work, what the media environment was like during your adolescence, which technologies arrived while you were still cheap to retrain, which diseases and which recessions and which wars were salient at which age. On this view, "Millennial" names a relation to a shared historical particular rather than a set of shared intrinsic properties — closer in kind to "everyone who was on call the weekend the payments pipeline went down" than to "gold." The boundaries are stipulations: the cut years are conventional, they get revised, and different institutions stipulate them differently. That conventionality, I want to argue, is no more damning than the stipulation of where a storm begins, and it does not make the exposure bundles unreal. So: promiscuous realism about cohorts as exposure bundles, eliminativism about cohorts as kinds. And instead of the usual hand-waving about correlation and causation I want to offer a concrete test — a cohort variable earns explanatory standing only if its effect survives replacing birth year with actually measured exposures, and I propose calling the failure to survive that substitution "exposure collapse." I also want to concede up front that the labels travel badly: the same cut years applied outside the country whose history generated them pick out nothing.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do to the thesis rather than by how interesting they are, each phrased the way that person would really phrase it — I want to recognize the question when I hear it, so please do not sand off the jargon. For each objection: a reply I could give out loud in about two minutes, plus a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which ones I should simply concede during the talk itself rather than waiting to be asked, on the theory that conceding early costs me nothing and buys credibility with the demographers, and tell me which one or two I should hold the line on even if it costs me the room. Please also give me: the question I am privately hoping nobody asks (my guess is something about whether the exposure-bundle story can distinguish a cohort effect from an age effect at all, given that exposures are themselves age-graded — tell me if you think the dangerous one is somewhere else); anything in my setup that the philosopher of science will think I helped myself to without argument; and the objection from the people-analytics person, which I expect the room to laugh at and which I suspect is actually the hardest to answer. Assume I have read nothing in any of these literatures and cannot look anything up before the talk, so if you need to invoke a position, describe it in a sentence rather than naming it and expecting me to recognize the name. Give each objection a short two- or three-word tag in capital letters, and end your entire response with one final line, with nothing after it, in exactly this form: \answer{TAG OF THE OBJECTION MOST LIKELY TO SINK THE TALK}.
