My opening move. The grounding objection is the one I cant get past: before any decree there is nothing in the nature of a free creature that makes it true that she *would* choose A rather than B in circumstances C, so there is nothing for God to know. So suppose we soften the doctrine: what God knows prior to any decree is not what each possible creature would freely do but a set of objective propensities — in C, this creature has a 0.7 propensity toward A. Propensities look groundable in a creatures character and dispositions in a way a brute counterfactual isnt, and expected value still gives God something to work with: he can rank feasible histories and choose the best. But then providence is a gamble — he can favour the odds that this person freely accepts grace, but he cant secure any particular outcome, which is most of what middle knowledge was invented for. Is that trade an improvement, or does it throw away the whole point? And are you anyway already committed to something like it, once you grant that character makes some choices easier for a creature than others? If your answer is just the standard move that the "would" is not a "will" and needs no grounding, or that the objection would sink mere foreknowledge too, tell me why that isnt a dodge.
