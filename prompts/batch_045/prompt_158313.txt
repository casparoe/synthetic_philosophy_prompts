**Question.** Explain Kant's distinction between acting merely *in accordance with duty* and acting *from duty*, and explain why he holds that moral worth attaches only to the latter. Illustrate with his own examples of the shopkeeper and of the person who, by temperament, feels no sympathy for others and yet helps them. Then consider this case: Miriam visits her difficult and ungrateful aunt in a care home every Sunday. She is genuinely fond of her aunt and enjoys many of the visits; she also believes firmly that she has a duty to visit; and she would go on visiting even if she stopped enjoying it. (a) Suppose that what actually gets her out of the door on most Sundays is the enjoyment, though the thought of duty is present and would have sufficed alone. (b) Suppose instead that she honestly cannot tell, on reflection, whether it is affection or duty that moves her. Does her action have moral worth in each case? Finally, explain Williams's "one thought too many" objection and say how seriously it damages the Kantian doctrine of moral worth. [25 marks]

**Student answer (reproduced exactly, typos and all):**

For Kant, acting in accordance with duty means doing what duty requires, whatever your motive; acting from duty means acting *because* duty requires it. Kant's claim is that only the second kind of action has moral worth, and that moral worth is not a matter of what the action brings about. It depends on the maxim — the principle on which the agent wills the action — rather than on the end achieved, since the same outward act can be done from very different principles. This is why Kant thinks the person who helps others while feeling no sympathy at all, purely out of duty, has a worth that is in a way higher than the worth of the naturally sympathetic person who helps because she likes helping; the second person's action is right and even admirable, but its worth is only conditional on a temperament she did not choose.

Kant therefore thinks that an action has moral worth only when the agent acts against her own inclinations. This comes out most clearly with the shopkeeper: the shopkeeper who gives honest change to a child because honesty is good for business acts in accordance with duty but not from it, and so his action has no moral worth at all. Indeed it follows that from the moral point of view the honest shopkeeper does nothing morally right — his action is on a level with the dishonest shopkeeper's, since in neither case is there moral worth. Kant's point is that helping someone because you want to is not the same as helping them because you ought to, and only the latter is morality proper.

In Miriam's case (a) the answer must be that her visits have no moral worth. She is moved by affection, the affection is what actually explains her getting in the car, and she would have visited anyway even if the thought of duty had never crossed her mind. Moral worth requires that duty be the sole motivating reason, so a merely accompanying inclination destroys it: the fact that duty would have been enough if affection had failed is irrelevant, because what matters is what actually motivated her. In case (b), where she cannot tell which motive is operative, Kant's view leads to a problem: he locates moral worth in the maxim rather than in observable behaviour, but he also holds that we can never get fully behind our own covert incentives by self-examination. It follows that no agent can ever tell whether any of her own actions has moral worth, and the same goes for everyone else's actions, which shows that the notion of moral worth cannot do the work Kant wants from it.

Bernard Williams's "one thought too many" objection is an objection to exactly this feature of Kant's view. Williams argues that we cannot know our own motives, so if the moral worth of an action depends on which motive the agent acted from, then an agent who stops to ask herself whether she is acting from duty has already had one thought too many. His conclusion is that we should stop assessing actions by their motives and assess outcomes instead. The objection does have some force against Kant's doctrine, but it does not touch the separate question of whether Miriam deserves credit or praise for what she does, which is a question about the agent rather than about the action, and which I think we should assess by whether she reliably gets things right over time rather than by what moved her on a particular occasion.
