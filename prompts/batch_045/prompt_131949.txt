I run the weekly challenge session at a small grantmaking foundation: five analysts, each assigned to tear holes in somebody else's funding case, and I write the one-page note that goes to the board under my name. Roughly twice a month a case stalls because two people are arguing past each other in a way that more evidence won't fix — typically over what counts as evidence at all: one analyst holds that nothing that hasn't been replicated in a randomized trial is evidence, another that a mechanism plus a field visit is. I've tried the usual rationalist toolkit on these — steelmanning, double crux, asking what would change your mind — and it fails in a specific way: it presumes the disagreement bottoms out somewhere arguable. The answer I get back is often "nothing", and "nothing" is ambiguous between a refusal to engage and the honest report that the claim in question is load-bearing for everything else the person is doing.

Which is why I've been working through Wittgenstein's late notes on certainty, published after his death as On Certainty, and the literature that calls the propositions at the bottom of that structure hinges: not known, not inferred, not doubted, but the frame inside which knowing, inferring and doubting have the sense they have — closer to the riverbed that the water runs through than to a belief floating in the water, though the riverbed can shift over time. Please turn this into a procedure my team can actually run: twenty minutes alone at a desk, in writing, with no philosophy training, on a claim that has deadlocked a dispute, and in a form I can audit before the note goes out.

What I need is the tests, in the order they should be applied, for sorting a stalled claim into (1) an ordinary empirical question whose evidence test the two sides simply haven't specified, (2) a genuine clash between frameworks that no evidence will settle for the people holding them, and (3) a hypothesis someone has insulated from criticism by treating it as though it were a hinge — together with what to do in each case (send it back for a number and a date, escalate it to the board as a scope-and-values decision, push back and demand the argument), what counts as a sufficient answer to each test, where the sequence stops early with a firm verdict, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of commitments rather than facts.

Then run it on three cases: the randomized-trial-versus-mechanism stalemate above; an analyst who refuses to put any probability on a low-base-rate catastrophic risk on the grounds that any number would be spurious; and my own standing rule that we only fund interventions whose impact can be measured, which I expect the procedure to treat less kindly than I do. Finally, tell me honestly what a procedure like this cannot do: where it will misfire, which of its verdicts I should be most suspicious of, and how it might end up as a convenient vocabulary for people to stop arguing earlier than they should.
