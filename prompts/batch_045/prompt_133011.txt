I am writing a paper on the reference class problem and have backed myself into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I do not need pointers to the literature; I need the faulty step isolated.

(1) A probability claim about a single case, say that this patient's operation carries a twenty per cent risk of a serious complication, gets whatever warrant it has from the frequency of complications in some class of cases to which the patient belongs.

(2) Every case belongs to indefinitely many such classes, and they characteristically yield different frequencies: all operations, operations of this kind, operations of this kind on patients of this age and co-morbidity profile, and so on down to classes with a single member.

(3) Nothing about the patient, and nothing in the frequency data, singles out one of these classes as the correct one. Which class is used is settled by what we already know and by what the estimate is wanted for.

(4) So a single-case probability is always a probability relative to a class, and the class is supplied by us rather than dictated by the case.

(5) Nor can the data adjudicate between rival class-choices. Take two actuaries working from the same database who divide it into different, cross-cutting partitions and who, for the same patient, quote 0.2 and 0.8. Each can be perfectly calibrated over a long shared run: each is right in the cells they themselves use exactly as often as their own numbers say. Calibration is the only external check we have on a set of probability estimates, and no calibration check on that shared run will declare either of them wrong.

(6) If the class is supplied by us and nothing in the evidence settles which class to supply, then the statistics never constrain what we should expect in the particular case. They can only be made to yield whatever answer the choice of class was shaped to produce.

(7) But (6) is not confined to medicine and insurance. Any induction from a sample to what we should expect next, including the inductions that give us the frequency data in (1) and the calibration records in (5), requires treating the sample as drawn from one population rather than another. So the reference class problem, taken at face value, yields global scepticism about statistical evidence and thereby pulls the ground out from under the considerations that established it.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Why did the step look sound? If you think the chain is valid as it stands, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what giving it up commits me to. If it can be helped I should like to hold on to (3), since the thought that the class is supplied by us rather than read off the case is doing real work in the paper.
