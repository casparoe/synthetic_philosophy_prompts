I work as a research assistant on a book manuscript about institutional failure in classical Athens, and I need to classify an episode using the senior author's scheme before she rules on it herself. She sorts each episode into one of two boxes: a failure of the demos, or not a failure of the demos. The second box is reserved for episodes she assigns to bad luck, to the constraints of the moment, or to the manoeuvres of individual leaders, rather than to the judgment of the Assembly or the courts. Her verdicts so far, with the one-line descriptions used in the manuscript and the occasional note she has attached to them:

- Mytilene, 427, the first decree: the Assembly, on the day it learned of the surrender, voted to put every adult male Mytilenaean to death and enslave the women and children, and sent a trireme with the order — a failure of the demos.
- Mytilene, 427, the next day: a second Assembly heard Cleon for the decree and Diodotus against it, rescinded it by a narrow margin, and a second trireme overtook the first — not a failure.
- Melos, 416: the Assembly refused the Melians' offer to stay neutral, took the island, killed the men and enslaved the women and children — not a failure.
- Pylos, 425: with the Spartan hoplites cut off on Sphacteria, the Assembly, pressed by Cleon, refused the peace Sparta offered and voted to hold out for more — a failure of the demos.
- Sicily, 415: the Assembly voted a large expedition against Sicily and then, at a second Assembly, granted the generals what they asked for over Nicias' warnings — a failure of the demos.
- Arginusae, 406: the Assembly tried the victorious generals as a single body, refused them separate trials, and condemned those who had returned to death notwithstanding the storm that kept them from the wreckage; it repented afterwards — a failure of the demos.
- Socrates, 399: a jury of 501 convicted him by a substantial but not overwhelming margin and, after the penalty phase, sentenced him to death — not a failure; her note is that this was a jury weighing a charge brought by private prosecutors, not the Assembly deciding policy.
- The peace of 404: the Assembly accepted Sparta's terms — the Long Walls and the wall of Piraeus down, the fleet surrendered save a handful of ships, the exiles restored, Athens to follow Sparta's lead — not a failure; her note is that there was nothing left to refuse.
- The ostracism of Hyperbolus, usually dated to 417 or 416 and attributed by our sources to a deal between the followings of Nicias and Alcibiades, after which the institution quietly lapsed — not a failure; her note is that the mechanism did what it was for and the dishonesty was the leaders'.

The unruled case is 411, after the destruction of the Sicilian expedition. An Assembly held outside the city walls, at Colonus, voted to replace the democracy with a government of a narrow body of citizens — the Four Hundred, with a broader Five Thousand projected — on the representation that only a change of constitution would bring Persian money, and that the fleet at Samos depended on it. Our sources record that opposition was not heard: those who tried to speak against the motion were silenced or worse, and the fleet, when it heard what had been done, refused the new government and went on running the war under its own democracy. The Four Hundred fell within a few months, and no Persian alliance followed.
