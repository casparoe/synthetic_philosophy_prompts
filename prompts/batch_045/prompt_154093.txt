I'm building out an argument for a reading group and it has come loose in my hands: it seems to prove something I can't accept, and I can't locate the step that does the damage. It runs:

(1) For any consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory F strong enough for a modest amount of arithmetic, Gödel's construction yields a sentence G_F that F neither proves nor refutes, and G_F is true.

(2) So take any F offered as a theory of my own mathematical capacity. I can construct G_F, see that it is true, and note that F cannot prove it. No such F captures me, and I am not a formal system. This is Lucas's move.

(3) What makes (1) work is that F is rich enough to encode claims about its own proofs, so the construction finds a fixed point: a sentence that, under the intended decoding, speaks about itself. Note that G_F is strictly a sentence about numbers; it counts as self-referential only relative to a coding scheme supplied from outside.

(4) The further claim, Hofstadter's in GEB, is that an "I" is precisely a structure of this kind: a tangled hierarchy in which a system's representation of itself is among the things it operates on, and self-awareness is what such a loop is like from the inside. (2) and (3) then reinforce each other — the feature that makes formal systems incomplete is the feature that, at sufficient complexity, makes a system a subject.

(5) But the fixed point in (3) belongs to the formalism, not to a mind. The diagonal construction requires no awareness on anyone's part, and G_F is there whether or not anyone reads it. So if (4) is right, every theory to which (1) applies — Peano arithmetic, the proof-checker on my laptop — already has the loop that (4) says constitutes self-awareness.

(6) Hence: either Peano arithmetic and my laptop are self-aware, which is absurd, or (4) is false, the Gödelian analogy is a metaphor that explains nothing about consciousness, and (2) is left standing as a claim about mathematical capacity with no bearing on minds at all.

Name the step you would reject and explain why it looked sound enough to get past me, then price the alternatives rather than just listing them. Two things I want addressed explicitly. First, (2) seems to need more than undecidability: to see that G_F is true I seem to need to know something about F's reliability — consistency at the very least — and by Gödel's second theorem that is exactly what F cannot establish about itself; there are even consistent theories that prove their own inconsistency. I can't tell whether that is a genuine gap in (2) or only the same point restated. Second, can the analogy in (4) be repaired by distinguishing the formal system's coding-dependent, syntactically fixed-point self-reference from whatever self-reference a mind has, or does that distinction empty the analogy of everything that made it attractive? If forced I would keep the mathematical steps (1) and (3) over either big conclusion, and I have no settled preference between (2) and (4).
