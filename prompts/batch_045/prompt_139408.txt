My friend and I ended up in about a three-hour argument last night — we'd gone out for dumplings after someone's birthday and it somehow went there — about whether humanity is likely to still be around in a million years or whether we should expect the whole thing to wrap up fairly soon. Here's the move he kept making. Of all the people who will ever live, he said, I happen to be one of the very earliest: I'm somewhere around the hundred billionth or whatever it is, out of however many there will turn out to have been in total, and if that total were going to be astronomically huge, it would be a wild coincidence for me to find myself this close to the start of the list. So the total probably isn't huge, and that means the end probably isn't that far away. I pushed back on whether "I" could even have turned out to be one of the later ones, and on what kinds of thing get counted as people in the first place, and we went round on that for ages, but that isn't what's been bugging me since. What's been bugging me is the shape of the argument, and I don't have a word for what I'm seeing. Everything it uses about me is something I've known, roughly, since I was about ten: that I'm alive now, in the early twenty-first century, at a point where the number of humans who have already lived is some particular large number. Nothing happened last night that I didn't already know, and yet the argument seems to demand that I treat that fact as a discovery and revise what I expect about the future. On top of that, the verdict seems to slide around depending on when in my life I happened to first hear the reasoning — if I'd of come across it at twenty it would have pointed at one total, and if I only get round to thinking about it properly at sixty it'll point at a bigger one — which makes it feel like the conclusion is about my reading history rather than about the species. A friend who did one philosophy of science module said it reminded her of the puzzle where a theory gets credited with evidence that everyone already had in hand before the theory was thought up; she mentioned something about Einstein and Mercury, I think. But that doesn't seem quite like my case, because there the point is that the observation was sitting around and then a new theory came along that happened to explain it, whereas here there is no new theory at all, theres just a new argument, and the fact doing the work is a fact about me in particular rather than about the world in general. And I also notice the same style of reasoning feels completely boring and unobjectionable elsewhere: if I've been standing at a bus stop for twenty minutes, or I walk past a theatre and want a rough guess at how long the run will last, reasoning from "I turned up at some more or less middling moment" just seems like common sense, and nobody feels swindled.

So what I'm after: does the thing I keep tripping over have a name? The thing where an argument's entire evidential force comes from a fact the person already fully believed, where the fact is about that person's own position in a sequence rather than about the world in general, and where the upshot depends on when they got round to thinking about it? Or is it not one thing at all but two or three that I'm running together? And then there's the separate question of why the bus-stop version feels fine and the species version feels like a trick — is that a real difference in the reasoning or just me being inconsistent? Please don't just hand me a label and call it done: I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, which distinctions nearby do the real carving, how it differs from the closest things that are already named, and where the borderline cases fall — including whether any of the worries I listed above are actually bad worries rather than good ones. And write it as plain continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs like you were emailing me back about it.
