my mate sits on the ratings panel for one of the big democracy indices — the ones that score countries every year and put out those "backsliding" warnings — and I've been trying to reverse-engineer what rule they're actually running, because there own calls don't look consistent to me. Here's their track record:

- Hungary after 2010 (new constitution written on a two-thirds majority, electoral law rewritten, media authority restructured): backsliding, no hesitation.
- Poland 2015–2018 (constitutional tribunal first, then the ordinary courts): backsliding — even though every step went through parliament and was signed by the president, and even though some of what they replaced was itself pretty unaccountable.
- Turkey after 2016: *not* backsliding. Their reasoning: it's below the line already, that's a consolidated autocracy, and their category is supposed to be decline *inside* a democracy, so Turkey gets filed under a different code.
- Israel, the 2023 judicial overhaul as originally tabled: backsliding.
- Mexico under AMLO, 2018–2024, including the judicial reform at the very end: not backsliding. Reasoning given: large democratic majority, everything through congress, and a lot of it removes unelected veto players rather than adding power to the executive.
- US, 2020–2022: no finding at all. Two panel members argued the indicators simply didn't move; the chair apparently worried that issuing a ruling would read as taking sides in a partisan fight.

The case in front of them now is the one I genuinely can't call. Call it Varnia. Two years ago a three-party coalition won 62% of the seats in an election everyone certified as free and fair, campaigning explicitly on taking back the courts, the public broadcaster and the electoral commission from what it called a captured establishment. Since then, on party-line votes with almost no committee scrutiny and one fast-track procedure, it has: moved the appointment of most judges from a self-governing judicial council to a parliamentary committee; given the culture ministry control of the public broadcaster's budget and board; and replaced the electoral commissioners with parliament's nominees. In the *same* package it abolished the upper chamber's veto over ordinary legislation and cut the signature threshold for citizen initiatives, and it made registration automatic and extended voting to a diaspora that polls strongly against it. On paper it's all constitutional. The constitutional court upheld it 6–3 — but the coalition had filled two of the nine seats by then. The changes are split between ordinary law, repealable by a simple majority, and the constitution, which needs 60% to amend and they have 62%. Judges appointed under the new system get 12-year terms. The opposition is fragmented and polling badly, the coalition's own numbers are down fifteen points, the next election is in eighteen months and still scheduled, and there's no evidence yet that anyone intends to stay if they lose.

What I want is not really "is Varnia backsliding," I want the rule. What rules fit all six of those earlier calls about equally well, what does each of them say about Varnia, and which would you bet they land on — and why? I've got a feeling the interesting bit is that the Hungary/Poland stuff was hard to reverse while most of what Varnia did is reversible on paper but has that twelve-year personnel tail, so if that distinction is doing work in their track record I want it named.

And then the part I can't settle: which single further fact about Varnia, or which small variant of the case, would best tell the competing rules apart? I can imagine a version where they lose the next election and hand over power without incident, and a version where the only thing that changes is that the package contains *just* the upper-chamber and initiative pieces with nothing touching courts, media or the commission. Do either of those separate the rules cleanly? If their precedents really do underdetermine it, say so and tell me what they'd be committing to with each ruling — because at this point I'm half convinced "democratic backsliding" is just the name we give to institutional reform by a government the commentator dislikes, and I'd like to know whether the concept has more spine than that.
