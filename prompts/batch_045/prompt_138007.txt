I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group — four graduate students, a retired mathematician, and a visiting postdoc — and we have been working through the conceivability argument against physicalism for about eight months now. We are completely stuck, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the standoff, not a verdict on who is right.

Here is where we are. The gap people among us put it like this: you can specify the physical and functional structure of a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person down to the last synapse, and it still seems like a further, open question whether there is anything it is like to be that duplicate; and that openness does not feel like the openness of ignorance, it feels like the physical facts are simply not the kind of thing that could settle it. The no-gap people reply that this is a fact about our concepts rather than about the world: we have two independent ways of picking out the same state, one by introspection and one by causal or functional role, we cannot establish a priori that they converge, so a gap opens in our knowledge that no a priori reasoning can close — which makes it an epistemic gap, and makes the inference from I cannot derive it to it is not necessitated the very inference that already fails for the standard a posteriori identity cases involving water. The gap people then say that this reply is not an answer but a policy: it is a general scepticism about conceivability that would defuse any conceivability argument whatsoever, and the physicalists bite that bullet without noticing. The no-gap people retort that the alternative is an equally general policy on which conceivability is decisive unless someone first produces an independent argument for necessity, which just relocates the whole dispute. Neither side has moved since about February.

Two things make me suspect the real structure is somewhere else. First, we started experimenting with partial cases instead of full ones: is it conceivable that a physical duplicate of me lacks exactly one experience — the specific smell of coffee, say — while every other experience remains intact? Our intuitions fragmented in a way that surprised me. Some of us find that perfectly conceivable. Others report an all-or-nothing intuition, as we have started calling it: if the duplicate shares all the functional organisation, then either the whole phenomenal field goes or none of it does, and subtracting precisely one quale is not even describable. Second, our mathematician keeps insisting that the argument has the logical form of a consistency claim and wants to know which modality is in play and whether both sides are using the same one. My own hypothesis is that the eight-month impasse is really two hidden disagreements: a meta-modal one about whether conceivability is evidence of possibility at all, and an unacknowledged one about the granularity of phenomenal properties — whether consciousness is one global property or a bundle of many local ones — which is what is silently driving our divergent verdicts on the partial cases.

Is that diagnosis right, and if not, what is? I want your honest account of what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, a difference about which cases count as data, a hidden empirical or formal question, two questions going under one name, or a badly posed question — argued from what each side actually says rather than surveyed, and then, given your diagnosis, a concrete account of what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that we should expect no progress and why. Please answer in a single paragraph of no more than 400 words, with no headings or bullet points; I would rather have one sharp claim and its reason than a map of the territory.
