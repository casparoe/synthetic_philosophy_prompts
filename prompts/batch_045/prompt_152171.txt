Two colleagues of mine — a psychologist and a computer scientist — spent most of a department dinner arguing about a case that has been circulating in our field, and I was the only one at the table who stayed out of it. I genuinely could not tell who won, and they asked me to referee.

The case: a research group built a text-only conversational system trained on the complete surviving correspondence, diaries, unpublished drafts and published work of a historian who died three years ago. His widow and two of his former doctoral students each held a week of text conversations with a remote interlocutor. Each session was blind: they were told that any given session might be with the system or with a live human who had spent a month studying the same archive, and at the end of each session they had to say which. Their accuracy came out at roughly chance. Call this a *personalized* imitation game: the target is not "a generic human" but one specific dead person, and the interrogators are the people best placed to know him.

Their exchange went as follows.

(1) The psychologist opened by saying this is a far stronger result than anything that has come out of the ordinary, generic version of the test, where the system just has to sound like some human or other. The target class is narrower and more demanding, and the interrogators have the sharpest discriminative apparatus available — years of intimate familiarity. If a behavioural criterion for mind is going to be satisfied by anything, it is satisfied here.

(2) The computer scientist replied that this isn't an imitation game at all. The game's structure requires a comparison: the machine's mimicry has to be set against the behaviour of the real thing under the same conditions. The real thing is dead, so there is no control condition; the only comparator on offer is the interrogators' memory, and memory of a dead spouse or mentor is precisely the kind of thing that idealizes, fills gaps, and cooperates. A test whose outcome depends on how badly the interrogator wants to be fooled tests the pair, not the system.

(3) She answered that the motivational objection is a design flaw, not a refutation. Run the same set-up with interrogators who have the opposite motive — a hostile biographer, a journalist looking for a scandal, a litigator testing whether the thing could be deposed — and require *both* sympathetic and adversarial interrogators to fail. Motives are a parameter you can control for; Turing's own prediction was statistical for exactly this kind of reason: a machine good enough that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 percent chance of identifying it correctly after five minutes of questioning.

(4) He conceded the point about motives but said it actually makes his deeper worry worse: the verdict is always budget-relative. Turing's five minutes and 70 percent weren't incidental — he knew that interrogation of unbounded length and cleverness will unmask any finite system. So the criterion is never "behaves like a person," it is "indistinguishable within some resource bound," and no finite bound is privileged over any other. A behavioural criterion of mind that is relativized to an arbitrary budget is a moving target, not a criterion.

(5) She said budget-relativity is not a special problem for machines. Interrogate a human for long enough about their own inconsistencies, their forgotten commitments and their confabulated memories, and they too will eventually be caught out — but nobody takes that to show that humans lack minds. Turing's thresholds were a workable empirical standard, not a metaphysical claim, and demanding a privileged budget is demanding something no behavioural test of anything could deliver.

(6) He granted that, then shifted ground: behaviour, he said, can at best cover intelligence, not consciousness. What anyone actually cares about in this case — whether there is something it is like to be the thing at the other end of the chat, whether the widow is talking to her husband or to a very good portrait of him — is exactly what no behavioural test, however personalized, is even in a position to address.

(7) She replied that this just changes the subject. Turing replaced the loaded question with an empirical one on purpose, because the original question was, in his view, too empty to argue about; you cannot fault the replacement for failing to answer the question it was introduced to displace. If the objection is only that the test stays silent about consciousness, then the test has not been shown to be defective — it has been shown to be about something else.

(8) He then pressed what he called his strongest card. Grant all of that, he said: the personalized case is where the familiar lookup-table worry bites hardest rather than least. A mimic of one person is, almost by construction, a system of narrow coverage — an archive plus a retrieval-and-recombination mechanism tuned to one idiolect — and its whole training objective was to minimize the difference between its outputs and his. So its success is close to circular: you optimized for indistinguishability from him, and then you treat indistinguishability from him as evidence of mind. And the psychologist's reply that the widow's questions were unrehearsed only pushes the demand back one step, to finite generalization over more novel inputs, which is still just more behaviour.

She answered that a lookup table of all possible conversations is not physically constructible, that what matters is systematic rather than tabulated success, and that an idiolect-specific test is at least a harsher novelty test than a generic one. They went round this track twice more and neither moved.
