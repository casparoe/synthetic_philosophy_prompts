Two friends of mine got into it at a dinner thing on Sunday and I've been chewing on it since, so here goes. Ines mentioned she's been routing a chunk of her donations to a fund that pays for very high-fidelity simulations of ordinary people living in the early 21st century — ancestor-simulation style compute — and when Toby asked what the mechanism was supposed to be she openly admitted there wasn't one: no causal influence of any kind runs from the simulation's choices back to hers. Her case was that the two decisions are still correlated, because whatever later civilization runs those sims will have a decision procedure similar enough to hers that, conditional on her funding the sims, they reward cooperators, so she's effectively choosing for both of them at once. She called this acausal trade and said the right price is the swapped-shoes price: pay whatever the other side would have paid if it were standing in your shoes, no more. (1) Toby said this is the twin prisoner's dilemma dressed up, that correlation isn't causation, and that if the sims either already exist or never will, she's just burned money that could have gone to bednets. (2) Ines replied that he uses evidence-based reasoning himself all the time — he's a one-boxer on Newcomb's problem, and he thinks keeping a promise when literally nobody is watching matters — so he can't object to outcomes being correlated with your action in principle, only to this instance. (3) Toby conceded the correlation is coherent in principle but said real trade needs both sides to actually gain and needs some way of verifying the other side held up its end, and that here verification is impossible: either her action is already in the past, in which case the sim has no reason to reward it, or it hasn't happened yet, in which case it can't commit to rewarding it — so the trade unravels from both directions. (4) Ines said inspection replaces enforcement: the sim-side can read her decision procedure the way you'd read source code, so it doesn't need to wait for the action or trust her, and the same goes in reverse, which is why she thinks the swapped-shoes price is the natural one. (5) Toby then said that just turns it into a fancy Pascal's mugging — pick a huge enough payoff and a tiny enough probability and you can rationalise funding literally anything, including two contradictory things. (6) Ines answered that a mugging has an unbounded ask and no symmetry, whereas here the other side faces the exact same decision she does and the price is capped by what she'd pay in its position — though she did concede she has no idea how you'd estimate, from the inside, how correlated two decision procedures actually are, and Toby jumped on that as the whole thing being unpriceable. One more thing that got tangled in: at one point Ines said "for all I know I am one of the simulated people," and Toby said that was a completely different argument and a worse one, because if you might literally be the sim then you're not trading with anyone, you're just hoping you drew the lucky ticket — and they kind of talked past each other on that for a while.
