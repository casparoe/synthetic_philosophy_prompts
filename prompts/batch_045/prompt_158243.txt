I convene a small fortnightly reading group on decision theory — three philosophers, an economist, and a machine-learning researcher — and we have now spent two sessions on a pair of cases that the five of us keep treating as a matched set without anyone having argued that they are. To fix our terminology: the first is counterfactual mugging, in which a demonstrably reliable predictor tells you that it has already tossed a coin with a one-in-a-hundred chance of heads, that in the heads branch it would have handed you $10,000 conditional on predicting that you would hand over $100 if asked in the tails branch, that the coin in fact came up tails, and that it is now asking for the $100. The second is the XOR blackmail letter, sent by a stranger with an excellent model of your decision procedure, who sent it if and only if exactly one of two things is true: you will send him $1,000, or you have a rare condition that will otherwise cost you $10,000 to treat. Nobody in the room wants to give up either intuitive verdict — pay the mugger, refuse the blackmailer — and nobody has produced a formulation that delivers both without in effect naming the case it is being applied to.

The claim I want a number on is conjunctive: that there exists a single principle which (a) is stated without naming either case, without any clause keyed to being simulated or predicted, and without a similarity or stakes threshold tuned to get the answers right; (b) returns pay in the mugging and refuse in the blackmail; and (c) also returns a verdict in a third case we invented, which splits us three ways. Call it the resampling mugger. Everything is as before except that in the heads branch the $10,000 goes not to you but to a stranger whom the predictor drew from a large pool of people whose decision procedures are, on a behavioural similarity metric it shows you, roughly 70 per cent similar to yours — same training, same deliberative habits, plus a short list of named stable differences — and the payment was conditional on that stranger's predicted willingness to pay. In the tails branch you are the one asked for the $100, and nothing you do can causally reach the stranger or the money. Two of us say pay, because your output is still informative about a procedure very close to your own; two say the operative reason in the original case was strict identity of procedure and lapses the moment similarity drops below identity; one says the case is underdescribed until we know whether the named differences are ones the predictor's condition is sensitive to. I would count the conjunctive claim vindicated if someone writes such a principle down and all five of us agree it is not ad hoc, and refuted if someone shows the dilemma that paying the resampling mugger commits you to paying blackmailers of the XOR shape. My own credence is 0.35, held mostly because of that suspected dilemma.

Give me your own number, as a single figure, together with its derivation: name the sub-questions the conjunctive claim decomposes into — I would guess they include whether the reason to pay in the original mugging rests on strict identity of procedure or on graded correlation between procedures, whether graded correlation of that kind is even a coherent object, whether sensitivity to it is exploitable by an adversary who knows your procedure, whether exploitability is a decisive objection or merely a cost to be weighed, and whether the demand for a single unified principle is the right demand at all — assign a probability and one line of justification to each, and show how they combine into the total. Then give me a sensitivity note saying which single term the total is most exposed to and roughly where the total would land if that term flipped; the one argument or consideration that would move your number most, and in which direction; and the term you are least confident about. Please commit: no it-depends-which-decision-theory-you-accept hedging and no survey of the live options — pick the number, defend the decomposition, and say plainly where you think it is most likely to be wrong.
