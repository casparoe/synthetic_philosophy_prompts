I've been given two seminar slots on a first-year module on social epistemology and I've decided to be a bit reckless and build them around Arthur Miller's *The Crucible* — most of them did it at school, which cuts both ways, since they think they know it and they'll have forgotten that Hale turns up already half-convinced and that Danforth is basically structurally incapable of admitting he backed the wrong horse. The obvious reading is almost too easy and I want to get past it: the court runs on a credibility deficit for the accused and a credibility excess for the girls, and the whole vocabulary of witchcraft is a shared interpretive resource that makes some experiences articulable and others unsayable — so you get the standard two categories, the identity-prejudicial deficit and the gap in the collective resources for making sense of things, illustrated almost too neatly. The bits I actually want help with are two wrinkles. The first is something I've started calling *credibility capture*: the thought that what's wrong with Salem isn't just that some people are disbelieved, but that the credibility economy is rigged so that only certain propositions are hearable at all, which means speakers are rationally pushed toward saying whatever will be believed — Tituba works out within a scene what the room needs to hear, and thereafter confession is the only utterance the court can take in, while a true denial costs you everything. Is that genuinely a third thing, or is it just the two standard categories stacked together and given a name? And does it show that credibility itself, the thing the theory treats as the remedy, can be the instrument of the harm? The second is a student who insisted last week that Danforth is a victim of testimonial injustice too — his informants are liars, nobody will tell him the truth, and his office is precisely what makes him un-tellable. I think that's a category error but I couldn't say why quickly enough to satisfy her, so I'd like the honest version: whether epistemic injustice can run uphill, whether the theory is quietly built to describe deficits in the powerless and has no room for the way power insulates, and whether Danforth's failure is really a matter of credibility at all or of something else the theory should also be tracking.

What I want is a reading that's faithful in both directions — that uses the concepts to make specific scenes do work I hadn't seen, and also tells me where the play resists the frame and I'd be distorting it by pressing. I have in mind Tituba's confession, Abigail's performance in the court, Rebecca Nurse refusing to confess even though it would save her, Proctor's torn-up confession at the end, and that awful exchange where he wants Elizabeth to tell him what to do and she won't; I'd be grateful for other moments too, but I don't have the text to hand so please don't quote at me, just describe. Two things in particular I keep going back and forth on. One: Miller wrote the thing as an allegory for McCarthyism, and I can't work out whether that helps or hurts — does casting it as a story about state terror and self-interest lose the specifically epistemic wrong, or does it add something, that the damage is done to a community's stock of concepts and not only to individual knowers? Two: the ending seems to me to sneak in a claim the theory should reject, since Proctor ends up opting for a truth nobody can corroborate over a statement the court would find perfectly credible, and the play treats that as a kind of victory. Is that a coherent moral for someone who thinks knowledge is social all the way down, or is the play cheating?

So: what would you actually do with the two hours? Which scenes would you put in front of eighteen-year-olds, what question would you set on each, and what would a good answer have to get right — including, ideally, where you'd expect a smart student to go wrong. And in your own words, does the play add anything to the philosophical debate that the standard examples don't, or is it only ever an unusually vivid illustration?
