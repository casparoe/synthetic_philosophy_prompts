My department does a fake "induction pack" every year — last year someone forged a set of medieval guild statutes, this year it's my turn and I want to do a utilitarian institution: the Bentham College of Felicific Compliance, housed in a half-scale Panopticon with the auto-icon parked in the lobby and a laminated "greatest happiness of the greatest number" motto over the reception desk. My contribution is meant to be a mock internal circular, Form 7(b), "Preliminary Estimate of Felicific Return (Sexual)": a document staff are supposedly required to file for approval before embarking on a romantic or sexual relationship, deadpan HR-voice, maybe 700-800 words, laid out as numbered clauses with a scoring table and two worked examples at the end, plus the little marginal notes / cross-references that a real compliance form accumulates.

The bit I actually care about is that the jokes come from getting the doctrine right rather than from cheap stuff. Bentham's sexual ethics is the target: he argued in an unpublished essay that homosexual acts should be decriminalised, he thought a lot of sexual wrongdoing was what he called a fictitious offence (harm to nobody, offence only to taste), he derided the appeal to "unnaturalness" as so much ipse-dixitism and the principle of sympathy and antipathy, and he held that the pains and pleasures of reputation — the popular or moral sanction, disapproval and gossip and self-approbation — are real pains and pleasures and have to go into the sum like any other. So please build the form out of those, exaggerated but not invented: the seven dimensions of the felicific calculus (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent) turned into an actual scoreable spreadsheet with weights and a worked total; a clause solemnly ruling that "reasons of delicacy," "unnaturalness" and "the offence given to persons of a particular persuasion" score zero, since they are not pain but antipathy; a clause on self-regarding acts where the only sanction available is admonition; the classification-mania (sub-classes, brackets, a footnote system that refers to clauses that don't exist); the conviction that once the numbers are in, the practical question is settled, and that the arithmetic can be done in advance of the act.

Two things should do the real reductio work. Worked example A should have a brief, mutually enjoyed fling outscoring a long, dutiful, joyless marriage, with the form's commentary staying entirely approving and straight-faced. Worked example B should be where the moral sanction eats the whole calculation: the identical act scores differently depending on whether anyone finds out, and the Office ends up officially recommending concealment as the happiness-maximising option, perhaps with a note about scheduling to minimise expected gossip — that's the real reductio, since counting reputational pain as real pain genuinely pushes a Benthamite there, and the form should not notice that it has done anything odd. Somewhere there also has to be a clause on absence of consent, and I want it handled with visible strain rather than a gag: let it be reduced to a very large term in the pain column, and let the surrounding prose betray that a spreadsheet is a thin way to register what's at stake, without winking at the reader or making light of anyone's safety. Nothing graphic or lurid anywhere — the comedy should be procedural and conceptual. No real named people, living or dead, as the butt; the doctrine is the target, and a Bentham specialist should ideally read it and think the satire was fair rather than a strawman. No citations, footnotes to editions or quotes from Bentham, and please don't rely on anything you'd need the web for — I'll be reading this offline.
