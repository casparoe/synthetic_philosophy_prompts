hey — heres the brief. I'm putting together a small zine thing and I need one more piece for it. Write me an agony-aunt column in the voice of a Saturday-paper magazine: warm, brisk, slightly exhausted, the sort of writer who signs off with a passing reference to a recipe or the weather. Call her Aunt Effie. Headline plus 600-700 words, and I'd like the whole thing in British English.

The catch, and the whole point: Aunt Effie is a completely convinced epiphenomenalist about conscious experience, and she has to stay consistent about it for the entire column. Concretely:

- She never claims to know that anyone at all is conscious — not her readers, not herself, not the dog in the third letter. No warm aside reassuring us that of course she has feelings too; if she ever does something like that, it has to be the joke rather than a slip.
- She can't write anything that implies her column is going to cause anyone to do anything. So the advice has to be advice-shaped while doing no actual recommending. If you want her catching herself mid-imperative and rewriting it as a description of what people of a certain sort tend to do anyway, that's very much the spirit.
- She can't let a report of pain be caused by the pain. She's allowed the standard move that the felt hurt and the belief about it share a neural cause, so the belief still tracks things — but I want her to notice, in her own voice, that this leaves it unexplained what makes the belief about the hurt at all, and that it certainly seems to her as though she is writing this column because the letters upset her, which on her own view cannot be what's happening. The self-stultification needs to be in there, and it should be funny without ever being named as such.

Three letters, roughly: one from a widower asking whether anyone can ever tell what grief feels like from outside, and whether his wife's feelings have now simply stopped; one from a reader whose boyfriend keeps telling her the column refutes itself, who wants to know whether she should be worried (use him to get in the one bit of technical vocabulary in the piece, and let Aunt Effie handle it badly); one from a lab technician whose participant keeps pressing the button marked sharp, and who wants to know what the button presses are actually evidence of.

Then the bit I actually care about, and its invented, so you'll have to work it out rather than recognise it: the last letter is from someone who has just been told she has a newly described neurological condition — call it occult sensory dissociation. On the scan and in behaviour everything is exactly as it should be: the nerves fire, the reports come out on time, she winces at the right moments. But on some days, her neurologist says, the experiences themselves quietly switch off for a few hours, and nothing in her behaviour, her speech or her own entirely sincere reports gives this away. She wants to know how anyone could ever find out, and whether it matters. Aunt Effie's answer should be one a real epiphenomenalist could actually give — and the punchline I'm after is that on her own view this isn't a rare diagnosis, it's just what is already true of everyone, every day, and always was. Land that without her ever saying it flat out.

Close with an editor's footnote in a different voice (someone trying to keep the magazine out of trouble) and a teaser for next week's column. The target is the view, not any person, living or dead, so no philosopher gets named or impersonated. No citations or further reading, and nothing that requires looking anything up. Typos in the letters are welcome; sloppiness in the philosophy is not.
