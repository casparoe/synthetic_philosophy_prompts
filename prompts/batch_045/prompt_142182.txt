hey — I'm putting together a ~3000 word essay for a reading group on al-Ghazālī's causal scepticism and I'd rather have the argument kicked around now than after I've spent a weekend writing it. I'm not an Arabic reader, I'm working off translations, and everyone in the group knows the Tahāfut better than me, so I definately want the interpretive bits stress-tested. Notes below.

Setup: I think two seperate claims get run together under the word occasionalism, and the essay is about pulling them apart.

- NEC: no created thing has genuine causal power; God (or an intermediary) is the only real cause of anything.
- ANN: the connection between a created cause and its effect is not metaphysically necessary — God could produce the effect without the cause and the cause without the effect.

The fire-and-cotton discussion is my example. My reading is that it actually contains two moves: (1) a conceivability point — you can suppose the contact without the burning, the drinking without the quenching, the decapitation without the death, so nothing in the ideas themselves forces the connection; and (2) a point about agency — fire has no will, it isn't an agent, so it can't properly be said to produce anything.

Claim: only (1) is an argument about necessity, and it supports ANN at best. (2) is what's actually load-bearing for NEC, and it rests on an independent and very strong premise, roughly that causation requires will. So the vivid part of the discussion and the argumentative part aren't the same part.

Second claim, and this is the bit I'm most attached to: ANN is cheap. Any theist who thinks God institutes natures he can suspend already has it, and a Hume-flavoured regularity theorist gets ANN with no theology at all. So ANN can't be what's distinctively at stake. If the exciting conclusion is NEC, the conceivability material is basically a detour.

Third: I also want to complicate the direction of entailment. I'd assumed NEC trivially implies ANN, but it seems to me now that it only does if you add that God's willing is contingent — a necessitarian occasionalism where God's decrees couldn't have been otherwise would restore necessary connections between creaturely events while still denying creaturely power. That's a fun wrinkle but I'm unsure I've got it right.

Fourth: the regularity cost. NEC owes an account of why fire reliably burns. The usual answer is that it's God's custom or habit. I want to press that this has (at least) three readings, and none is comfortable: a descriptive reading (custom is just a summary of what God has done, so we get no reason to expect tomorrow's fire to burn and every sunrise is a fresh coincidence), a normative reading grounded in God's wisdom or self-consistency (in which case necessity hasn't been eliminated, it's moved upstairs — God's character is now doing the work the philosophers assigned to natures), and a brute reading (there's no explanation, which makes occasionalism a scepticism about causal explanation rather than a rival theory of it).

Fifth, the induction tu quoque, which may be my favourite bullet but may also be a cheap shot: the occasionalist says observed constant conjunction never establishes necessity. But to get from fire has always burned cotton to fire will burn cotton tomorrow, the occasionalist has to run an induction over God's behaviour — which looks like a worse induction, not a better one, since it needs more instances, it predicts a mechanism you have no acquaintance with, and it has to generalise across possible divine interventions. So the occasionalist's everyday epistemics lean harder on the very inference they say can't ground necessity. Question is whether there's a non-inductive route available (revelation, an innate grasp of divine wisdom, something else).

Sixth: the explanatory cost, as opposed to the epistemic one. Take the engineer who says the bridge failed because the bolts sheared. That's informative because causes are the knobs you can wiggle to change outcomes. On NEC the true causal story is a divine volition, which you can't access, which tells you nothing about what to change, and which is compatible with any outcome whatsoever. So NEC renders special-science causal claims either false or mere shorthand. I think this is the strongest objection available to us now, and I want to flag honestly that it's anachronistic — al-Ghazālī wasn't trying to underwrite an engineering practice.

Seventh, the constructive bit I'm least sure about: a small scorecard. A causal theory should preserve (a) counterfactual dependence of effect on cause along the actual sequence, (b) the usefulness of causal claims for intervention, (c) singular causation, one-off cases with no repetition. My guess is ANN passes all three, NEC gets (a) only by deriving it from decrees, fails (b), and struggles with (c). Upshot: the defensible Ghazālian thesis is ANN, and NEC is the part people remember because it's vivid but the part that can't pay its explanatory bills.

What I'd like from you:

1. Is the NEC/ANN split (my labels — I assume the literature has other names) a real distinction, or does one collapse into the other in a way I'm not seeing? Especially: is my wrinkle about NEC not entailing ANN without divine freedom actually right?
2. Is the induction tu quoque fair, or does it beg the question against the occasionalist?
3. Is there a fourth horn on the custom dilemma I'm missing? I have a vague sense that classical occasionalists reach for something like general volitions to avoid acting by one-off decrees — if that's a real move, does it rescue the view or does it just make the laws the real causes under another name?
4. Which bullet is weakest, and which is most likely to be something twenty people have already said? Be blunt if one is just confused.
5. Anything I should be careful about given my audience — in particular, is it flatly wrong to call him an occasionalist in the first place, and does my reading of the two moves in the fire example ring true to how that passage actually goes?

Don't bother with style or structure comments, and please don't suggest readings or citations — I'll chase those up separately once the argument is in shape. Just pick it apart.
