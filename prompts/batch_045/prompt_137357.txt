I am applying for a nine-month research fellowship that funds a single project on the basis of a proposal of no more than two pages, and I need you to draft that proposal for me.

The topic is whether brain size — or any purely quantitative neural property — can be morally relevant in its own right, as opposed to being merely evidence for the psychological capacities that most people think do the moral work. I want the proposal to be scoped so that it does not discuss variation in brain size among human populations or any use of the topic in that vicinity; I say this partly for ethical reasons and partly because that literature would swallow the project. The cases I care about are cross-species comparison, change within a single organism's life, and artificial or composite systems: brain organoids, gradually replaced or uploaded nervous systems, and interfaces that functionally couple two brains.

My half-formed idea, which I would like you to sharpen rather than restate, has three parts.

First, metric-dependence. Brain size is not one variable. Brain mass, total neuron count, cortical or forebrain neuron count, neuronal density, synapse number, and brain mass scaled against body mass all come apart somewhere in the animal kingdom, and nervous systems differ in how centrally their neurons are located. So a claim about the moral relevance of size has no determinate content until a metric is fixed, and my suspicion is that the choice of metric silently imports a prior about which neural features matter — sheer number of processing units, packing density, ratio of central processing to sensory and motor demand. If that is right then the size question may be a disguised form of a different dispute rather than an independent moral consideration, and the interesting work is in saying what that other dispute is.

Second, the counting problem. Any criterion that assigns status by summing a quantity over a nervous system seems to be sensitive to how we carve the world into individuals: separated hemispheres, dicephalic cases, nervous systems whose neurons are largely distributed rather than central, two brains functionally coupled by an interface. If status is a function of a summed quantity, then fusing two subjects into one larger system appears to create moral value that neither had and dividing one appears to destroy it, with no change in what anyone undergoes. I want to know whether this is a genuine reductio of additive quantitative criteria or only a special case of the familiar problem of counting subjects that every theory of moral status faces anyway — and if it is the latter, whether it still tells us something specific about quantity as opposed to capacity.

Third, the persistence problem. Neural quantities change within a life, through development and through atrophy. If status tracks a quantity directly, then one persisting subject's status rises and falls, and the grounds of our obligations to it change with it. Perhaps that is acceptable, but I want it stated as a cost rather than quietly assumed away.

What I need from you:

1. A precise statement of the question, with the scope exclusions built in rather than appended.
2. An account of why the obvious answers leave it open — in particular, the view that quantity is only ever a proxy for capacity, the view that imperfect proxies are still what we must act on under empirical uncertainty, and the view that some quantitative property is intrinsically relevant because it tracks how much experience or how much valenced experience there is.
3. The argument I would actually develop, including a decision about which of my three puzzles carries the weight and which are supporting material.
4. A method and a month-by-month plan for nine months. This is a conceptual project, not an empirical one; say why the conceptual route makes progress here and why the obvious alternative — waiting on better comparative neuroscience — does not.
5. The possible conclusions, what a deliverable looks like (I am planning two journal-length papers, or one if the project narrows), and an explicit failure condition. My worry is that every formulation of the question collapses either into capacities all the way down or into a general puzzle about counting subjects, in which case the project says nothing specific about quantity; tell me how I would test for that early rather than in month eight.
6. An honest scope assessment for a nine-month project.

Two standing constraints. Do not give me citations, references, or a bibliography; where you need to invoke a position that exists in the literature, describe it in a sentence instead of naming its authors, and I will fill in the citations. And if any of my background empirical assumptions about nervous systems look wrong or contested to you, say so and tell me what would have to change in the proposal, rather than building the case on them.

Please use these sections, at roughly these lengths: Question and scope (250 words); Why the standard answers do not close the question (350 words); The argument and which puzzle carries it (400 words); Method and month-by-month plan (300 words); Possible conclusions, deliverables and failure condition (250 words); Settled, contested and speculative (250 words). In the final section, separate what most people working on this would simply grant from what is genuinely contested among them, distinguish both from what is my own framing rather than anything in the literature, and flag any point where the proposal's promise depends on a claim you are not confident is true.
