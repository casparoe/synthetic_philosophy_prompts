I convene the committee that sets the written comprehensive examination in moral philosophy for our doctoral candidates, and I have been asked to contribute one question on karma — understood specifically as a theory of moral desert, and not as soteriology, cosmology, or a causal story about rebirth. I need a question that cannot be answered by producing a competent summary of the doctrine.

The failure mode I want to design against is the one we saw repeatedly last year. Candidates explain that karma links act to consequence across lives, distinguish the karmic claim from fatalism, gesture at the tension between retribution across rebirth and the Buddhist denial of a persisting self, observe that the Sanskrit root means action rather than fate, and stop. That earns a pass and tells the committee nothing about whether the candidate can do philosophy.

My own sense is that the crux is what I have started calling the Provenance Problem. Desert claims, on most accounts, require that the desert-base be attributable to the subject who is supposed to bear the desert: I deserve to suffer only if the wrong for which I suffer is somehow mine. Karma appears to supply a desert-base (a wrong done by an earlier person) and a bearer (a later person) while offering, at best, causation rather than identity between them, and, at worst, no psychological continuity at all — many karmic traditions do not promise memory of the earlier life. So either karmic suffering is retribution visited on someone who is not the agent, which is straightforwardly unjust, or it is merely a causal consequence befalling a numerically distinct person, in which case no desert is involved and the moral content of the doctrine evaporates. A candidate who can say why this is a genuine dilemma, and can then either press it or reconstruct the doctrine so that it survives, has understood something.

To force the issue I have drafted a stipulative variant that the question should present as a hypothetical, not as a historical position. Call it the Doctrine of Transferred Merit: suppose a suitably advanced agent can deliberately take on the karmic debt of another, so that the other's suffering is remitted and the advanced agent bears it instead. In some devotional strands something like this is loosely imagined, but I want it stipulated cleanly as a philosophical option, with the details supplied in the question. It is dialectically useful because it separates two things candidates routinely run together: whether desert can be inherited at all, and whether desert is the kind of thing that can be voluntarily assumed by a party who lacks the desert-base. A candidate who thinks karmic transfer is obviously incoherent should have to say whether it is more incoherent than the ordinary, untransferred case.

What I would like from you:

1. The question itself, written for a ninety-minute closed-book essay. It must be self-contained: a candidate with no Sanskrit or Pali, no textual training, and no access to any source should be able to answer it from the stipulations given. Include the Transferred Merit variant, stated precisely, and one further case of your own construction — not drawn from any canonical source — that discriminates between a desert reading of karma and a consequentialist or quasi-legal reading in which the mechanism merely produces good states of affairs or administers a system.

2. A model answer of roughly eight hundred to nine hundred words that takes a determinate position rather than surveying options. It should appeal to nothing a candidate could not reconstruct unaided; if you want to invoke a canonical formulation of the doctrine, paraphrase it and say that you are paraphrasing. I do not want textual exegesis doing any argumentative work here.

3. A marking scheme out of twenty marks, organised around five moves that an excellent answer must make. For each: the marks available, what distinguishes an answer that earns them from one that partially earns them, and a single line on the characteristic way candidates go wrong — for instance, treating the no-self doctrine as a conversation-stopper, or sliding from the claim that a later person is causally connected to an earlier agent to the claim that they are the same moral subject.

4. A short note on how the question should handle the moral-luck dimension. Karma is often presented as dissolving luck by extending the moral ledger across lives; a good candidate should be able to say whether it dissolves luck or merely relocates it, since being the continuation of a bad person is itself something one does not choose. Tell me whether you would make that a sixth marked point, fold it into one of the five, or leave it as an unmarked distinction the examiners reward in borderline scripts.

Where you disagree with my framing of the Provenance Problem, say so briefly and rewrite the question around your own crux instead — I would rather have a sharp question than a polite agreement with my draft.
