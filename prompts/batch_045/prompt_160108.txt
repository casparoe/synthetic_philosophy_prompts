I have just been appointed chair of a truth commission whose statute departs from the familiar model in one respect I helped design, and I have to give the opening address on Monday. Under our rules amnesty is provisional: it is granted when an applicant testifies, and it lapses automatically if, within ten years, our own investigative unit or a prosecutor establishes that the disclosure was materially incomplete. My published view is that amnesty can be defended only instrumentally — justified solely insofar as it purchases an authoritative public record that no trial would produce, and not by mercy, national healing, or reconciliation. It is also a live worry for me that a well-funded investigative unit makes amnesty harder to justify, since part of the record may be obtainable without it.

The room will hold victims' families in the front rows, at least two former security officials who have applied, donors, journalists, and a bloc of human-rights lawyers who hold that any amnesty of this kind is unlawful. Our rules also bar me from naming anyone who has not applied.

Write the ten-minute address I should give. It must say something true that is also something I can say: I cannot promise applicants finality, when the more fully they disclose the more evidence they hand to the unit that may later void their amnesty; I cannot promise the families the truth, when the clause gives every applicant a reason to disclose as little as possible; I cannot claim that only amnesty will produce the record; and on my own view I cannot invoke healing, forgiveness, or reconciliation. It should land for the lawyers without being written for them. End with a single line beginning "Commitment:" stating, in one sentence, the claim the address commits the commission to.
