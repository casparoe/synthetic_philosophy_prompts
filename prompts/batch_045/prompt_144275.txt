I direct a small municipal archive in a city of about ninety thousand, and I came to the job from a history PhD rather than from an archives program. My staff is me, one part-time technician, and a rotating set of volunteers; we get thirty to forty offers of material a year and can properly process perhaps eight of them; we are moving buildings in eighteen months and have been told to plan on leaving behind roughly a third of what we hold. Our existing holdings are what you would expect from a city that grew around a rail yard: deeds and plat maps, tax assessor rolls, council minutes and departmental correspondence, the papers of two founding families. The people who make up most of the city now — renters, recent immigrants, a large Ghanaian Pentecostal congregation, the families who pass through the housing court — appear in those papers almost entirely as entries someone else generated: a name on a summons, a line in a ledger, a photograph taken from the sidewalk. I take the point that archives are not neutral and that the shape of a collection is an effect of power rather than a residue of chance. What I cannot work out is what follows from that on a Tuesday morning, when a box is on the table and I have perhaps half a day to decide whether it comes in, and how it gets described if it does.

What I want is a written procedure my team can actually run, not a set of principles. It should cover appraisal (do we take it, do we decline, do we take it with conditions, and when do we take only a description of it rather than the thing itself) and description (what we are obliged to say about a collection in the finding aid beyond what it contains). The part I care most about is that it should treat gaps as objects to be documented rather than lamented: I want my staff to be able to distinguish, in a repeatable way, a silence that was never created from one that was destroyed, from one that survives somewhere we cannot find it, from one that exists in a form or language we cannot render, from one we ourselves are creating right now by declining or deferring; and I want each of those to have its own test, its own standard of evidence, and its own line in the catalogue record — call it a silence note — written in language a twenty-year-old volunteer can apply to a box without a seminar in theory. I also want the procedure to say, explicitly, where it stops with a no, and where it must stop and hand the question to our community advisory panel because the issue is one of values rather than of fact. Two further wrinkles: the archive's own paperwork (retention schedules, destruction logs, the files of past refusals) is itself a record of the archive's silences, and I want to know whether the procedure should capture that; and one of my board members has proposed that we simply correct the imbalance by actively collecting from under-recorded communities, which I half suspect reproduces the same asymmetry in a friendlier voice, since the material would still enter on our terms, under our description, in our building. Please commit on that dispute rather than describing both sides.

Then run the procedure, at whatever length is needed, on three real-shaped cases. First: the records of a property management company, 1974 to 2003 — eviction files, rent ledgers, maintenance complaints, a few photographs — in which tenants appear only as defendants, debtors and complainants, offered to us by the founder's daughter on the condition that the reading room be named for her father. Second: a box of maybe two hundred audio cassettes from that Ghanaian congregation, services, counselling sessions and committee meetings from 1989 to 2004, donated by a departing pastor without the knowledge of anyone recorded on them, in a language none of my staff speaks, and including discussions of congregants' immigration status. Third: the papers of a long-serving city councillor, forty linear feet, offered with a small cash endowment attached, which would consume essentially all of our processing capacity for the next year and would fit our existing holdings perfectly. I expect the procedure to give me an answer I dislike in at least one of these; give it to me anyway and say so. Where you are making a judgment call dressed up as a step, mark it as one; and commit to the rule you would actually adopt and why, rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to choose — including, at the end, an honest note on what this procedure cannot do, and which of your steps you would be least surprised to find yourself revising in three years.
