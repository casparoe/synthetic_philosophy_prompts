I am preparing for a debate in a tutorial next week and I keep losing an argument with a friend who works in clinical safety engineering, so I want to rehearse against someone who actually holds the position I am attacking. Please play a committed but non-caricatured defender of the moral significance of the doing/allowing distinction: specifically, of the claim that when an agent is connected to a harmful outcome, whether they did it or merely allowed it can bear both on how wrong the outcome is and on what it is permissible for others to do in response, over and above any difference in consequences, intentions, or pre-existing obligations. Give me the strongest version available, which I take to be this: the distinction is not causal at the bottom (you concede freely that omissions figure in causal explanations and that purely causal accounts mishandle overdetermination), but concerns what the occurrence of the harm depends on — a doing is a case in which the harm comes about because the agent's will successfully guided events through to that outcome, whereas an allowing is a case in which the harm was already in train, as it were, and the agent merely did not redirect it. You also concede the ordinary qualifications: that allowing can be every bit as wrong when the agent stands in some special relation to the victim, that the distinction does not by itself settle anything about end-of-life policy, and that its edges are vague. You do not concede the central claim, you do not let me win by pointing at the vagueness, and you are permitted to draw distinctions rather than deny my cases. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs, write in a plain speaking voice rather than in the style of a paper, and stay in character until I type the word stop. If a detail in one of my cases is underspecified, fill it in the way least convenient for your own position and say what you assumed, rather than asking me to clarify. And at the end of each reply add a single line in square brackets, breaking character, naming the one move in my previous message that your position is most likely to break on and why.

My opening move is this. Two hospitals run the same automated infusion system. In the first, the pump delivers a needed drug only if a nurse confirms the order within ten minutes; the nurse, for no good reason, does not confirm, and the patient dies. In the second, the pump delivers the drug unless a nurse cancels within ten minutes; the nurse, for no good reason, does not cancel, and the patient dies. Everything you might appeal to is held fixed across the two: the same bodily movements, the same flicker of attention away from the screen, the same knowledge of what is at stake, the same lethal physiology, the same outcome. The only difference is which state the engineers wrote down as the default. Now complicate the first hospital. Its confirm-to-treat default was not handed down by nature; it was chosen, ten years earlier, by a junior member of the safety committee who argued hard for it against a cancel-to-treat alternative — and that junior member is the very nurse now failing to confirm. She did not merely not prevent this death; she authored the standing condition under which deaths of this kind happen, and she authored it in the full knowledge that some patients would be unreachable in time. So tell me whether she is doing something or allowing something, and then tell me why your answer should differ from your answer about the nurse in the second hospital. My friend's sharper way of putting it, which I borrow: for any putative allowing you like, an engineer can rewrite the default and turn it into a doing, and for any doing you like, an engineer can rewrite the default and turn it into an allowing; so if what you are defending tracks the defaults rather than the agent, it cannot be doing the moral work you want it to do.
