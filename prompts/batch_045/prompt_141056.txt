ok so I'm in this undergrad seminar on the history of violence (joint history / politics thing, mostly reading about whether things have gotten less bloody over time) and I keep getting hung up on how the numbers get built rather than on the big claim itself. Maybe you can help me see what my prof's measurement setup is actually committed to.

Here's the practice as we use it in class. For everyday killing we always use a rate: homicides per 100,000 people per year, taken from whatever the national statistics and coroners recorded. So a village of 500 in 1300 with one murder a year counts as way more violent than a modern city, because the denominator is tiny. But for wars we always use absolute totals: "this war killed about X million people," full stop, never divided by world population or by the number of people living anywhere near the fighting. And the war totals only include direct deaths — people who starved or died of disease because the war wrecked the food supply get coded on the death certificate as famine or epidemic and drop out of the violence number entirely. Also within the homicide stats, soldiers killed in battle, people executed by the state, and people who die in police custody each get handled differently depending on the jurisdiction, so the same kind of death lands inside or outside the count depending on who's doing the recording.

Then, to make cross-era comparisons fair, our prof invented this thing he calls "violence-adjusted life years" (VALY): you weight each death by how many years the person would probably have had left, so a famine that kills infants can score worse than a battle that kills twenty-year-olds, even with fewer bodies.

But he also insists there's one real fact of the matter about whether violence has declined, and that all these measures are just imperfect approximations of it.

So: what theory of what violence is, and of what makes a killing bad, does this whole bundle presuppose? It feels like it's mixing a view where the bad thing is your risk of being killed, a view where the bad thing is the life-years you're deprived of, and a view where the bad thing is someone intentionally killing you — are those consistent with each other here, or is the practice quietly switching views depending on which number it wants? Which parts does the practice strictly require and which does it merely go along nicely with, and where is it underdetermined so that several quite different theories would all license it? And concretely: what would have to change in the numbers if we dropped his implicit view and rebuilt them on each of those rivals — and would we even still be measuring one thing?
