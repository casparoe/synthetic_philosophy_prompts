I'm drafting a short piece on why "I never technically lied" is a bad defence, and before I write it I want to know whether my own position on lying vs. misleading actually hangs together. Here's the package, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

(1) Lying = you assert what you believe false, aiming at the hearer believing it. Misleading (what I've seen called paltering: literally true sentences picked because of how they'll land) is getting someone to a false belief some other way.
(2) The difference is real and isn't just consequentialist in disguise: lies are pro tanto worse, and not merely because they're riskier or more likely to blow up on you.
(3) It's still a lie, and still wrong, if the hearer sees straight through you and you knew in advance they would.
(4) The best story I have for (2) is about assertion specifically: when you assert you explicitly invite someone to take your word, you stake your credibility, you put yourself on the record in a way you don't when you merely leave them an inference to draw.
(5) But in ordinary life paltering is basically as bad as lying, and the test I keep leaning on is what I call the transcript test: replay the exact sentence to a neutral third party who knows the conventions of the setting; if they'd say "she told him the false thing," it's a lie in all but name and just as bad; if they'd say "well, strictly speaking she told him the truth," it's a lesser wrong.
(6) No duty to volunteer: selling a bike I know has a cracked frame and saying nothing when the buyer never asks is permissible, even knowing they'd walk away if they knew.
(7) Lying to the person at the door asking where my friend is, surprise parties, bluffing in poker — all fine.
(8) Perjury law ought to be able to reach the technically-true answer that was built to mislead.

What I want is an audit, not an essay. Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — I'd bet at least two of these are fine together and I just haven't seen why, so don't just flag everything. Order the real tensions by how bad they are for me, and for each one say what specifically has to give and what giving it up would cost the rest of the package. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. I genuinely don't know which of these I'd drop.

One case that makes me nervous and that you should use if it's useful: a reference letter written in the standard faint-praise code of my field reads as damning to insiders and as a warm letter to everyone else, so the transcript test seems to give different verdicts depending on who the third party is.

Two more things. Be explicit about how confident you are in each call, and about where you're reconstructing a live dispute rather than reporting something nobody argues about. And if the request itself is confused — if, say, the transcript test is just a relabelling of something that cant do the work I want, or two of my commitments aren't independent claims at all — say that plainly instead of politely auditing me.
