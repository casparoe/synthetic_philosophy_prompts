I built this argument for a reading group on free will and now I think it proves way too much, but I've been staring at it to long to see where the move is. Would like a second pair of eyes.

Setup is the standard Frankfurt-style case. Jones deliberates, decides to vote for the candidate, and votes, for his own reasons. Black wants Jones to vote, is monitoring something that reliably indicates what Jones is about to decide, and would have forced Jones to vote had Jones been about to decide otherwise. Jones never gives any indication; Black does nothing at all. Jones is intuitively morally responsible for voting, and intuitively he could not have done otherwise — so the principle of alternative possibilities (you are responsible for what you do only if you could have done otherwise) is false.

Here is where I take it further than I think it should go.

1. Jones is morally responsible for voting even though he could not have done otherwise.

2. The natural explanation of 1: Black is idle. Every item in the actual causal history of the voting — Jones's reasons, his deliberation, the way his decision produced the action — is exactly as it would have been in a world with no Black in it. So facts that obtain only in counterfactual circumstances cannot affect how responsible an agent is. Call this Idleness.

3. Notice what actually removes Jones's alternatives. Not anything Black does — Black does nothing — but the fact that Black *would have* acted. What makes it true that Jones couldn't have done otherwise is a purely counterfactual guarantee: in every nearby possibility in which Jones is heading for a different decision, something steps in and the vote happens anyway.

4. Now generalise. Idleness says merely counterfactual facts are responsibility-irrelevant. By 3, the absence of alternative possibilities is, in the Frankfurt case, nothing but a merely counterfactual fact. So: the absence of alternative possibilities is responsibility-irrelevant, period — not just in Frankfurt cases, but wherever it shows up, and whatever produces it.

5. Run 4 on the cases where we pre-theoretically want the couldn't-do-otherwise excuse to work. The bank teller with a gun at her head. The subject acting under post-hypnotic suggestion. The person in the grip of an addiction. The patient with a tumour compressing her frontal lobe. The sleepwalker. In each of these the excusing feature is standardly put by saying she couldn't have done otherwise. By 4, that is no excuse at all.

6. So either all of these people are fully responsible for what they do, which seems absurd, or 4 is false.

7. But denying 4 looks like it costs me the Frankfurt case itself, because 4 is just Idleness plus 3, and Idleness plus 3 is exactly what makes the Frankfurt case work. And denying Idleness just is going back to the principle of alternative possibilities. So it looks like the argument against PAP, if it works at all, doesn't only refute PAP: it refutes every excuse that trades on the absence of alternatives, which is a much bigger and much less plausible claim than the one people sign up for.

I don't want to accept 5, and I dont want to give up 1. So something in the middle has to go and I can't tell which.

Two variants I made up as stress tests, in case they help you localise it.

Black-lite: same case, except Black is unreliable — say he'd have intervened in about 70 percent of the nearby possibilities where Jones is about to decide otherwise, and in the rest he'd have been distracted and done nothing. Does Jones now have alternative possibilities, and if they come in degrees does his responsibility come in degrees too?

White: the mirror image. In the actual sequence there is a mechanism that prevents Jones from doing otherwise — imagine the alternative action is blocked, the way a locked door blocks it. White stands by and would have unblocked it if Jones had been about to try. Nothing in the actual sequence involves White. Does Jones have alternative possibilities after all, in virtue of a purely counterfactual enabler? If yes, then having alternatives is just as cheap as lacking them, and if no, I want to know why counterfactual enablers don't give alternatives while counterfactual interveners take them away.

What I'd like: name the step you'd reject, explain why it looked fine when I wrote it down, and price the alternatives — does rejecting it put PAP back on the table, or is there a version of the actual-sequence view that survives and still keeps 1? And tell me what your diagnosis predicts about Black-lite and White, because if it doesn't say anything crisp about those two I don't think I've understood it yet.
