My reason for caring is not paradox-mongering. Here is my current candidate list, which I expect you to add to, subtract from, and reorder:

1. The structural question I most want settled: is the preface really the same paradox as the lottery? My own suspicion is that it is not, and that the usual "both are just closures plus fallibilism" line papers over a real disanalogy. In the lottery you are given a known partition (exactly one ticket wins) and a known base rate over its cells, and the winning proposition competes with every other cell for a single outcome. In the preface you are given neither: the claims in the book do not compete for a single error slot, there is no partition over which an error rate is defined, and the judgment that something in here is wrong is a global reliability estimate rather than a base rate. If that is right, the lottery is the tractable, possibly dissolvable case and the preface is the load-bearing one. Is the disanalogy real, or is there a unified treatment I am missing?

2. The version I actually care about, which I have not seen handled: call it the paradox of the literature. No single agent holds all the claims, nobody writes or believes a preface, and the errors surface asynchronously years later, one erratum at a time. Does removing the single believing subject change the normative question, or merely relocate it? Is there a group-level analogue of the standard move of splitting first-order belief from a second-order hedge, and if there is, does it have any bite for action rather than for assertion?

3. The diachronic preface: the author who learns in year six that some particular claim was false. I suspect the interesting content is really about revision policy under discovered error, not about the synchronous co-tenability of attitudes, and that treating it synchronically has quietly mis-set the whole literature's sense of what a solution would look like.

4. Whether conjunction is doing any work. Can the argument be reconstructed with a single-entailment step, or is closure under conjunction essential? If it is essential, the paradox may be narrower than it is usually made out to be.

5. The individuation question: is there a level of granularity at which the claims are described such that the paradox evaporates, so that the tension is an artifact of how we cut up the content rather than a fact about rationality?

6. A scoring-rule approach: if a corpus is evaluated under a proper scoring rule, is there pressure that dissolves the paradox in practice, on the grounds that you do best by standing behind each claim individually while never staking anything on the conjunction?
