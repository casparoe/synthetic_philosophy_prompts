I serve on my school's academic integrity committee, and a case we heard last month has left me with an argument I cannot finish.

A teacher asked a student whether she had passed the answer key for a test to anyone. She replied, "I didn't send it to anyone." That was true as far as the words went: she had dropped the file into a class group chat rather than sending it to an individual, and she knew the teacher would hear her answer as a denial that she had distributed it at all. Our committee charged her with dishonesty anyway, and when she objected that she had not lied, one of the teachers answered that deliberately misleading someone is morally no better than lying to them. I voted with the committee and I still think that is right. But when I tried to write down why, the argument ran straight past the conclusion I wanted.

(1) The wrong of lying does not consist in the falsity of what is said. Someone who passes on a falsehood he honestly believes is not lying and is not guilty of the same thing. What makes lying wrong is that you aim to leave someone with a false belief, and usually succeed.

(2) That feature is fully present in misleading of the group-chat kind. The hearer ends up with the false belief, the speaker intended it, and the speaker controlled it. The only difference is the route: a false assertion on one side, a true assertion plus an inference the speaker expects on the other. If the wrong-making feature is identical and the route changes neither what the victim gets nor what the speaker was after, then misleading is exactly as wrong as lying.

(3) It follows that the route cannot matter at all. So deception by staging — saying nothing while arranging the evidence: sliding a phone under a notebook during an exam, clearing a browser history, wearing another student's lanyard to look enrolled — is as wrong as lying, since it aims at the same false belief by other means.

(4) And if the aim and the resulting false belief are what matter, then leaving someone with a false belief I could remove at no cost is the same act done slowly. A classmate is walking to the wrong exam room because the timetable moved overnight; I know, I could call out, and I stay quiet so that she misses the test and I place higher. On the account in (1), I have done what the liar does.

(5) So silence is as wrong as lying. That cannot be right. Nobody owes every passer-by a correction, and a conduct code that punished failing to correct a stranger's timetable error as severely as lying would be absurd. Yet (4) is reached from (3) by what looks like exactly the move that carried me from (2) to (3), and I cannot point to the difference.

Three further things make me distrust my own tidiness. First, if the wrong lies in producing a false belief, then a deception that never lands — I hide the phone and no invigilator ever glances at my desk — is not wrong at all, which is hard to accept, since attempted lying seems wrong whether or not anyone is fooled. Second, some silence is not really silence: when a teacher asks a whole room a question, staying quiet is taken as an answer, and if that convention is doing the work, then (4) and (2) may not be different cases after all. Third, a friend points out that if (2) is true, the familiar escape hatch closes: someone who answers a murderer at the door with a literally true sentence he knows will be misheard is doing precisely what (2) says is as bad as lying.

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than (4). I want the group-chat student and the phone-hider to be answerable, and I do not want to be a liar for not correcting a stranger. So: which step do you reject, and is there a principled line between the staging cases in (3) and the silence cases in (4), or does the line actually fall somewhere else, between (1) and (2)? Whatever line you draw, I would like an honest account of its costs. Does it let the group-chat student off? Does it let the phone-hider off? Does it make refusing to correct a false belief cost-free even when the refusal is cruel? And does it leave any room at all for the person at the door?
