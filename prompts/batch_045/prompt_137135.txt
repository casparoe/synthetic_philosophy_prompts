me and my friend Sam spent like three hours last night arguing about the foreign-language translation requirement for PhD students in our department — I said scrap it, nobody can tell me what it's for, and he hit me with Chesterton's fence and said I had it exactly backwards: that not being able to see the point of a thing is a reason to leave it standing, not a reason to tear it out. He's been reading Burke on the side so I also got a lot of inherited-institutions-carry-more-wisdom-than-any-one-person's-reason. I definately lost the argument, but I don't think I lost it for a good reason, because I still can't tell what the fence line is actually claiming.

The version we were both throwing around is the popular one: "Don't ever take a fence down until you know the reason why it was put up." I looked it up afterwards and Chesterton's actual passage (in The Thing, 1929, the essay "The Drift from Domesticity") says something noticeably different and weirder — the intelligent reformer answers: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it." Seeing a *use* isn't the same as knowing the *reason it was put up*, and "I won't let you" is a claim about who may stop whom, which is a different claim again.

So give me the menu: what are the distinct precise things this line could be saying, from the trivial to the wild? Here are the fights I want settled, but add readings I haven't thought of:

- How many senses of "know why the fence is there" are in play — the intention of whoever erected it, the causal reason it's still standing, the function it currently serves, being able to predict what breaks when you remove it? Which one is the principle demanding? If it's the first, it goes dead in the cases I care about: I roughly know why the language requirement was put up (nineteenth-century philology, German academic prestige) and I think that reason is dead. If it's the predictive one, the principle just collapses into "get evidence before you act," which isn't conservative at all.
- Whose ignorance triggers it? Mine, the department's, some relevant expert's, humanity's collectively? And is it a first-personal rule about what I should do, or a licence for someone else to stop me, the way Chesterton's wording suggests?
- The bad-reasons problem: plenty of fences were put up for reasons that were bad at the time — excluding people, protecting a guild. Does "know why" also require the reason to have been a decent one? If yes, the fence principle is just "have good reasons" in a fancy hat. If no, it cheerfully protects every known-bad institution on the planet. Is there any version that escapes that dilemma, or is the dilemma the answer?
- Symmetry: does it apply to absences? Is there a presumption against putting up a fence where there has never been one — Chesterton's gate, if you like? If not, what does the asymmetry between acts and omissions rest on, and can the principle supply it without borrowing from somewhere else?
- Gameability: "go away and think until you can see the use of it" is passable by confabulation — a reasonably clever person can invent a function for anything in ninety seconds. So either the test is nearly free to pass and has no bite, or it has to be made falsifiable (say what will go wrong, then we check), which quietly converts a conservative rule into a hypothesis-testing rule.
- Packaging: the principle is stated one fence at a time, but nobody reforms one thing at a time. Does applying it sequentially give a different verdict from applying it to a whole package? If so, which application is the right one?

For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, substantive but contested, or actually false — and then the bit I really need for round two with Sam: which readings would do the work he wanted them to do, i.e. would actually block scrapping the requirement *given* that nobody in the department can name a current use and the historical reason looks obsolete? My suspicion is that the readings that are true are too weak to help him and the ones that would help him are false, but I want that argued, not asserted.

Last thing: be upfront about where you're shaky. For each reading you defend, say what would change your mind about it — which argument, or which fact about our case, would flip you — and where your disambiguation is most likely to be wrong or to be missing a reading entirely. I'd rather see which parts are load-bearing than get something that sounds smooth and even.
