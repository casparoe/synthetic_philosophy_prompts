I've spent the last eight months as a junior legal assistant to the amnesty committee of our truth commission (not the South African one — a much smaller thing, set up after a five-year counterinsurgency, and I'm the person who drafts the recommendation memos, so I'm the one who has to make our past decisions look like they add up to a rule). I care about this more than I expected to, because the lists at issue in the case I'm drafting now included people from the town my dad's family is from.

The statute is the standard trade: amnesty from prosecution and civil suit in exchange for full disclosure of offences committed with a political objective. I know the general shape is borrowed from elsewhere, but what I need to work with is our own track record:

- A low-ranking fighter who confessed to torching a market stall during a coordinated attack and named his commander and two others: granted.
- A mid-level commander who confessed to ordering the killings at the Kaleri roadblock and gave hours of detail, but refused to name the regional politician who authorised it — said he'd carry the responsibility himself: refused, disclosure incomplete.
- A rebel finance officer who never carried a weapon, just moved money and forged movement papers: granted.
- A prison guard who beat a detainee who had been sleeping with his wife, at a time when guards beat detainees routinely and with political cover: refused, private motive, ordinary crime.
- A field commander who ordered the shelling of a barracks; forty-one civilians died in the housing compound next to it; he says he didn't anticipate that; two members dissented but amnesty was granted, on the view that disproportion doesn't bar amnesty where the objective was political and the disclosure was full.
- An applicant who confessed to four incidents; a fifth surfaced in someone else's testimony and he admitted it when confronted: refused, even though the fifth incident was the least serious of the lot.

New case. The applicant was an intelligence analyst for the state. He compiled monthly lists of suspected sympathisers — names, addresses, who they met. He had no role in detentions, which were run by a different directorate. People on his lists were detained, and over about two years at least thirty of them were killed, two from my dad's town. He has handed over his notebooks and the archive, named his three superiors and the head of the directorate that ran the detentions, and says he cannot name who actually did the killings because he genuinely doesn't know. He also says that when he started he understood the lists to be for surveillance — that he thought he was preventing attacks — but admits that by the second year he knew a listing usually meant detention, and that detained people often didn't come back.

What do you think our committee does with him, and what rule does that commit us to? I can see at least two rules that fit all six precedents and split on him: one that's basically about political objective plus telling everything you yourself know, and one where amnesty tracks whether the harm you contributed to was something you could foresee and were proximate to, so that a man who thought he was compiling a watchlist is closer to the prison guard than to the finance officer. There's maybe a third reading of the disclosure condition too — that it isn't about what the applicant knows but about whether the commission can reconstruct the whole operation, in which case his not knowing the killers is itself a disclosure failure even though he isn't hiding anything.

Tell me which of those you'd bet on, which precedent is the closest analog and why, what principle we'd be adopting with each choice, and what further case would actually separate the rules — my guess is something involving an applicant with a genuine memory gap, but I'd like your version.
