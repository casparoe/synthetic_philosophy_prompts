Q3. Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness holds that phenomenal properties are causally inert with respect to the physical world. Yet subjects form highly specific and apparently reliable judgments about their own phenomenal states — that the afterimage is fading, that this pain is throbbing rather than stabbing. (a) State the argument that gets called "the paradox of phenomenal judgment" (Chalmers's label, I think, from The Conscious Mind) as strongly as you can, and say exactly what it is supposed to show about epiphenomenalism. (b) Then assess this reply: "There is no paradox. The experience E and the judgment J about it share a neural cause N; J is caused by N, not by E, so E need not do any causal work. And since E supervenes on N, J would have been different had E been different, so reliability is secured without causation." Does that reply succeed, and if something is left over, say precisely what it is.

Student answer:

Epiphenomenalism is the view that phenomenal properties do no causal work — Huxley's image of consciousness as the whistle on a steam locomotive, which makes a noise but drives nothing. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is basically Jackson's knowledge argument in a new costume: the point is that if experience had no effects on us, we could never come to know anything about it. Put more sharply, epiphenomenalism is self-refuting: to state the view one needs the concept of a phenomenal property, but concepts are only acquired by being causally acted on by the things they are concepts of. If phenomenal properties never act causally, nobody could ever have acquired the concept, so nobody could ever have asserted epiphenomenalism. Since the view has been asserted, it is false.

The common-cause reply shows that this is too quick. Let N be the neural state, E the experience, and J the judgment "I am now in pain." N causes E and independently causes J. The correlation between E and J is then fully explained by the common cause, so E's causal inertness is consistent with the accuracy of my judgments about my experience. And because E supervenes on N with nomological necessity, had E been different, N would have been different, and so J would have been different: J counterfactually tracks E. A judgment is reliable just in case it covaries with the facts it is about, so reliability is secured with no causal influence from E at all. Note that this concedes nothing to the interactionist: causal influence and counterfactual dependence come apart here, and it is the latter that does the work the paradox seemed to demand.

The residual worry — that the epiphenomenalist cannot explain how J manages to be about E rather than about N — is not a real worry. The contents of mental states are fixed by their causal-functional role, and J's role is settled wholly by N and the subject's physical history. What else could aboutness consist in? So the reply succeeds and the paradox dissolves. What looked deep was only the causal exclusion problem reappearing: someone who tacitly assumes that explanation must be causal will think there is a gap, but the epiphenomenalist is not obliged to grant that assumption. Nor need we posit any special faculty of introspective acquaintance; J is produced by the same mechanisms as any other judgment about ones own body, and its reliability is just counterfactual covariation. Epiphenomenalism survives the paradox intact.
