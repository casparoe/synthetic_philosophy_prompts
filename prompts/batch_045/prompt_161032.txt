I'm a novelist and I've written myself into a corner. There's a scene in my draft where a character who works in philosophy of physics explains the hole argument to her brother (also a novelist, natch) over about four pages of dialogue, and I need to actually get the machinery right so that a physicist reading it doesn't wince.

So please walk me through the argument properly. The bit I keep getting wrong is what the conclusion is even supposed to be. I assumed it was "spacetime points aren't real, relationism wins," but someone who knows this literature told me that's a caricature, that the real force comes from determinism plus haecceitism, and that there's a move called sophisticated substantivalism that's meant to dodge the whole thing.

Three things I need pinned down, in plain language but without flattening them into slogans:

1. Why determinism is load-bearing. What happens to the argument if the laws aren't deterministic? Does it collapse entirely, or does it turn into a different complaint altogether — something about underdetermination rather than indeterminism? My character needs to know what she's giving up if she concedes the laws are stochastic.

2. Whether sophisticated substantivalism — keep the points, but deny they have primitive identity, so the shifted model isn't a genuinely distinct physical possibility — is a stable position or just structuralism in a costume. And if it is structuralism: can you run a hole-style argument against the structure itself, or does the shift eat its own tail somehow? I have a feeling its here, not in step 1, that my character should get stuck, and I want to know why.

3. Does the hole have to be a hole? That is, does the diffeomorphism need to be the identity outside some region and non-trivial inside, or does any old active diffeomorphism generate the same problem? My instinct is that the "hole" part is doing real work, but I can't say what work.

Running analogy, if it helps: my narrator insists the events of a story are pinned to fixed chapter-slots, and someone slides every event three chapters later through an empty stretch, leaving every relation between events intact. Use that if it's useful — but do tell me where it breaks down, because I'd definately rather cut the scene than publish something dumb.

No citations or reading lists, please; I can't check anything and neither can my readers. End with a single one-sentence summary, as its own last line beginning with Verdict:, of what my character is actually claiming — the sentence I can put in her mouth.
