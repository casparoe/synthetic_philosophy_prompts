Hey, hoping you can help with something I have to actually hand to people next month.

I sit on the board of a regional legal-aid nonprofit — about forty staff, we do evictions, benefits denials, a bit of family law, and we turn away something like six in ten of the people who qualify because there simply aren't enough hours in the week. We're voting in three weeks on whether to roll out a drafting tool from a vendor I'll call Briefwell: you feed it the intake notes and it produces a first-draft petition, demand letter, or discovery response. On the vendor's demo it's genuinely good, and the failure mode is the nasty one — text that reads perfectly and is subtly wrong about a deadline or a legal standard, the kind of thing you only catch if you already know the area. The staff are split in a way that maps oddly onto seniority: our newer attorneys and most of the intake team are strongly for it (their line is that the queue is the moral emergency, and that agonising about our own fulfillment while a client sleeps in her car is definately a form of self-indulgence), while the senior litigators and the paralegals' committee are strongly against it. The paralegals' objection isn't mostly about jobs — it's that drafting is where judgment gets formed, and that sitting in review of text you didn't write, with your name on the filing and the liability on you, is a thinner and different activity from writing it. Our main funder, a state access-to-justice commission, has also let us know unofficially that a decent slice of next year's grant depends on showing we've adopted this kind of technology, so doing nothing isn't costless either.

What I want from you is a decision memo I can circulate to the board and then, unchanged, to staff — one document, not a board version and a staff version. It should take a position: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if it's the last one I want the conditions to be real and dated — which case types, what phase-in, who reviews it at the six- and twelve-month marks, what specifically triggers a rollback — rather than the sort of pilot-and-monitor language that means nobody ever decides anything. It also needs to say what we owe our own staff either way. If the tool comes in and the paralegal role quietly becomes person-who-checks-the-machine's-work, I'd rather we name that out loud and either redesign the role into something with actual judgment left in it or admit we're cutting it, instead of letting it happen by drift. And it should be honest about who is answerable for the errors, which our board has so far managed not to discuss at all.

The part I'm stuck on, and the part I'd like the memo to reason about rather than gesture at, is whether the preservation of meaningful work is something a body like ours is even allowed to weigh. I've been arguing with a friend who says obviously not: we're a charity funded to serve clients, our duty to staff is to pay them properly and give them notice, and if we think meaningful work is getting scarce we should go fight that in tax and labour policy rather than making someone wait three weeks longer for her eviction answer. I keep half-agreeing and then not. The thing I can't get past is a distinction I made up and can't fully defend: there's the product (the brief gets filed either way), there's the craft (someone spent years learning to do the thing well), and then there's what I'd call the office — being the identifiable person who stands behind a document and can be asked why it says what it says. Automation can leave the first two more or less intact and still hollow out the third, and my hunch is that the hollowing is where the real harm lives, but I honestly can't tell whether that's an insight or just my own profession's preferences in a nicer coat. So give me the straight version: a recommendation with reasons a skeptical paralegal and a skeptical client could both respond to, the strongest case for whichever side you're not recommending, and your own verdict on whether the craft-and-office worry is status anxiety dressed up. Assume nobody reading has time for footnotes — no citations or reading suggestions, and keep it under about 1,200 words.
