I'm in a final-year seminar on epistemology and aesthetics, and I keep running into the same move. Whenever someone suggests that we can learn whether a work is good by being told so, our instructor reaches for the wine analogy: you can be informed a hundred times that a wine is balanced, complex, and worth the money, but until you taste it you don't really know anything — and the same goes for a painting, a symphony, or a building. The point of the thing is the experience itself, and no report, photograph, audio guide, high-resolution scan, or generated verbal reconstruction can hand you the experience. She then uses that to carry a fair amount: that aesthetic beliefs formed on testimony aren't knowledge in the fullest sense (our reading calls the underlying thesis the acquaintance principle), that criticism and museum education are at best invitations to go look, and that aesthetic disagreement is therefore harder to resolve by appeal to authority than disagreement about, say, the age of the building.

What I can't shake is that the analogy may be doing the opposite of what she wants in at least two places. First, wine looks like the case in which we defer to testimony most heavily of all: people buy on numerical scores, on a merchant's or a sommelier's word, on region and vintage, and they treat that as a perfectly good substitute for tasting it themselves. If that's the model, expert aesthetic testimony transmits just fine, and she has picked a source domain where her own conclusion is false. Second, some of the works we've discussed don't seem to have the feature the analogy turns on: a piece that consists only of a set of instructions or a short text, where being told the content looks like being given the work; an installation built around smell, which is awkward for me because I lost most of my sense of smell after an illness last year; and a performance piece that burned down with the warehouse it was stored in, so that documentation is all anyone will ever have. If the analogy is right, I have no route to aesthetic knowledge of the smell piece, and after the fire nobody — the artist included — can know anything about the burned work, which strikes me as a strange result for a principle to have.

So I'd like a proper audit rather than a verdict. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the wine, the palate, the tasting note, the trained expert, the novice, the vintage — and say which parts of the mapping are actually bearing the weight. Then sort the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions she draws from the ones that are noise: whether testimony about structural or formal properties and testimony about value come apart; whether the wine case is really a case of deference rather than of first-hand experience; whether a description, a reproduction, and a guided walkthrough differ in the relevant way; and whether "you can't be told" is a claim about evidence, about the value of the resulting mental state, about what's required for the judgment to be genuinely yours, or about the risk of getting it wrong. Does the acquaintance claim survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if so in what restricted form? And is there a better analogy than wine — I've been toying with the critic as a travel guide, who can tell you where to go and what to notice but can't hand you the trip — or does that one quietly import the same conclusion? I'm not after reading recommendations; I want the mapping and where it breaks.
