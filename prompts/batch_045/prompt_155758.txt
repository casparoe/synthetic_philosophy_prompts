I'm writing the final paper for my metaethics seminar, and I want to use the cross-cultural fairness data constructively rather than sceptically: the large multi-society ultimatum-game and costly-punishment samples, where mean offers and punishment rates turn out to track things like market integration, community size and religious participation. The debunking arguments built on that variation seem to me to contain an unremarked resource. If a population's response is explained by a cultural or economic cause, then we know how much of it to distrust — so I propose regressing each population's responses on those covariates and discounting each intuition in proportion to the share of its variance the covariates explain, treating whatever variance is left as the epistemically clean part of our moral evidence. WEIRD subjects get discounted heavily, small-scale societies much less, and realism survives with a hard core of moral judgment that no cultural cause can be shown to produce. Two things I can't settle. The residual variation is still mostly between societies rather than within them, so should I weight it by how typical a society is, or by how many people live in societies like it? And is the residual really the morally reliable part, or just the part nobody has found a cause for yet — and is there a clean way to argue that whatever survives the filter tracks the moral facts?
