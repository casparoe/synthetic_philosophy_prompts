I got into an argument at a family barbeque about whether you should always save the greater number, and I've been turning cases over in my head for a week and can't get them to line up. I'm not a philosophy person at all. I dispatch for a volunteer ambulance service, which is probably why it got under my skin, but I'm not asking what I should do on shift — I want the rule, or to be told there isn't one. Here's where I land, going with my gut.

- One stranger drowning on the left, five on the right, I can only get to one side. The five. Not even a question.
- Same thing but the one on the left is my kid. My kid, obviously. I've been treating that as a seperate question and I'll come back to it at the end.
- Five people each lose a thousand dollars, or one person loses five thousand. No difference to me. Genuine tie, flip a coin.
- Five people each lose a finger, or one person loses a hand. I'd stop the hand first, and I think I'd still say that at a hundred fingers, or a thousand. Being outnumbered doesn't seem to help the fingers at all.
- One person drowns, or ten people each lose a leg. Save the one person. Also at a hundred legs.
- Five people go blind, or one person dies. Don't know. Ten blind, or one dead? Twenty? I don't know where it flips and I don't trust that there's an answer in me to find, which worries me more than the ones where I have a firm verdict I can't justify.
- One person who will drown for sure, or ten people who each have a one-in-ten shot at drowning. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on. Said one way — one certain death against ten possible deaths — I go to the certain one. Said another way — on average one person dies either way, but if I go to the ten I might get lucky and save every one of them — I go to the ten. I hate that the description moves me around that much.
- Five people each lose a couple of years off the end of their life, or one person loses forty years. I go to the one person. But five people each lose ten years, or one person loses forty — now I go to the five. So somewhere between two years apiece and ten years apiece it flips, and I have no idea where or why.
- One person drowning for sure, or ten people drowning for sure who are all 85 and sick, while the one is 25. I save the 25-year-old. Which I notice sits badly with the thought that a life is a life.
- Everybody's facing the same thing, one life each, and instead of picking I run a lottery so all six get an equal shot. This feels fair to me in the money case, like a cop-out in the drowning case, and actively gross when it's my kid in the water. So either my reaction to the lottery is telling me something real about how equal the claims are, or it's just an echo of whatever I'd already decided. Can't tell which.
- Last one: the options are save person A, or save persons A, B, C, D and E. Obviously the five. Every rule should get this one, and if yours doesn't, it's broken.

The rule I half believe, said out loud: what each person stands to lose counts once, and you can add people up, but only when what's at stake for each of them is the same kind of thing at roughly the same size. Then you just count heads and more wins, even by one. But when one person is looking at something of a worse kind than anything anyone in the group is looking at, that wins however big the group gets — the group can't make up the difference by being bigger.

I can already see this thing doesn't know what to do with my blindness case (what kind of thing is blindness next to death — bigger, smaller, and how would anybody ever tell?), and I think the risk case breaks it too, because I can't decide whether a one-in-ten shot at drowning is the same kind of thing as drowning for sure or a different and smaller one. And the 85-year-olds case is the same kind of thing by my own rule — death is death — and I still don't count them the same.

So: can you state something precise that does better, and say which clause of it comes from which of my cases, and what it says about the blindness one? Please also hand me a case I haven't thought of where the serious candidate rules come apart, and say which one you'd back. I'd much rather have a clean rule that costs me one or two of my gut calls than a messy one that saves them all, so say plainly which of my verdicts I'd have to give up. And tell me whether the my-kid thing is really a different question or the same one in a hat. If the honest answer is that some of these are just two different values in me that were never going to add up, say that instead — but then tell me which ones, and which one I should stop trusting.
