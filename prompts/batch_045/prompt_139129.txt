I work on a modelling team at a public health agency, and we have had literally the same argument so many times that I've started keeping notes on it, because it never goes anywhere.

Here is the setup. We have a workhorse epidemic model. Everyone on the team agrees its assumptions are false: the population is treated as homogeneously mixing, contact between any two people is assumed memoryless, the mixing structure is fitted from a single season of survey data, and there is no behavioural response anywhere in it. It also predicted the last two waves pretty well out of sample, better than anything else we had.

Half the team says: that's the whole story. The assumptions were never meant to be true, the model earns its keep by predicting, end of discussion. One guy calls it the F-twist. The other half says: it gets the right numbers for the wrong reasons, so we have no idea what happens when we actually *do* something, because the things we would do — closing venues, targeting by age, shifting contacts from workplaces to homes — are exactly the things those fitted parameters encode, and the parameters are not the sort of thing that stays put when the world changes under them.

The detail that makes it worse: we also have a second model, built with households and workplaces instead of homogeneous mixing, and it is also false in about six obvious ways. The two models agree closely on forecasts. They disagree sharply about which of three interventions to spend money on. And that disagreement has not moved anybody in either direction, which I find weird.

My hypothesis is that this isn't a disagreement about assumptions at all, and that each side is scoring the model against a standard the other side never accepted: one against "does it say what will happen next," the other against "can I use it to decide what to do." If that's right the fight is mostly about what models are *for*, and it should dissolve once you say which question you're asking.

But I want to know whether that's too easy. Is it actually a real dispute in disguise — a hidden empirical question about how stable those fitted parameters are across regime shifts, which we could in principle settle? Is it partly verbal, in that people mean different things by "assumption" and by "works"? Or is the modellers' side resting on something like a bedrock methodological intuition — predictive success is the only evidence there is — that nobody can argue for or against?

Please diagnose it using what each side in my description actually says, not a tour of the positions on idealization in the literature. And then, given your diagnosis: what would count as progress? I'd especially like to know whether there's a concrete test that would settle something. A colleague has proposed a "swap" exercise — deliberately refit the same model with a structurally different false contact process, then see whether the forecasts survive while the intervention ranking flips, and treat the flips as evidence about which uses the model can bear. Is that the right kind of thing, or is it just going to generate another round of the same argument? Or is this impasse just a permanent feature of doing science with models you know are false, and nothing should be expected to end it?
