I'm finishing a chapter on the ethics of price regulation and I keep getting stuck on something that isn't really about whether these policies work. It's about what the shape of them seems to assume.

Take the common core of city rent stabilisation (I mean the stylised package, not any one statute): the cap binds only while the sitting tenant stays put, and when they leave the landlord can generally reset to market; a close relative who has been living there can inherit the tenancy; the owner can often evict in order to move themselves or a family member in; and new construction is usually exempt for some years. Minimum-wage regimes, at least the ones I know, are built almost the other way round in every respect: the floor attaches to the person rather than to the slot, so a brand new hire gets it on day one and there is no "vacancy" reset at all; but there are lower rates for younger workers and apprentices, and enforcement is mostly complaint-driven.

What I want is the normative reconstruction, not the incidence stuff — I've read enough demand curves. What view of the thing being protected would make this the natural shape to hand both policies? The rent rules look like they protect incumbency itself, or the tenant's grip on a particular place; the wage rules look like they protect the person against a market, whoever they are and however long they've been there. But then why is it fine for a new tenant to pay market and not fine for a new hire to be paid less, and why does one regime have a "you can reset when there's nobody in situ" rule and the other doesn't, while the other has a "you can be paid less if you're young or still training" rule and the first doesn't?

The thing I've been kicking around, which may be nonsense: call it disruption-sensitivity. The thought is that a price becomes regulable only where paying more would force someone to abandon a life they've already built around it, which would explain vacancy decontrol very neatly and also why the exemption is for new build. Is that something the practice actually presupposes, or just one story it happens to fit? If it is the right story it generalises wildly — renewal caps on insulin, on broadband, on tuition for continuing students — so why is housing basically the only place we do it, and would a "portable cap", where the tenant carries their discount to the next flat, be the more coherent version of the same idea? What would break if we did that?

Also: the new-build exemption seems to presuppose that prices are signals we should mostly preserve, and the youth and apprentice rates seem to presuppose that a price is a transfer that can be adjusted to what someone deserves or produces. Does that mean the two regimes quietly rest on inconsistent views of what a price even is, or can one theory hold both?

And I'd like to know which parts of all this the practice strictly needs versus which merely sit comfortably with it — where it's underdetermined, so that a couple of quite different theories would both produce exactly this architecture. I don't need pointers to papers, I need the argument laid out.
