Something about ordinary mathematical practice has been bugging me for a while and I want you to reconstruct what it actually presupposes.

Here's the practice. In a normal analysis or algebra paper, if you want to establish that there exists an x with some property P, you're allowed to suppose there is no such x, derive a contradiction, and stop. Nobody asks you for the x. My first-year analysis TA did the famous one: there are irrational numbers a and b such that a^b is rational — look at √2^√2. If it's rational, take a = b = √2 and you're done; if it's irrational, take a = √2^√2 and b = √2, and you get 2. Done. The proof never tells you which pair works, and everyone treats it as a complete proof of existence.

But the very same people, in the very same journals, will refuse to accept "there exists" in a numerical analysis or algorithms paper. There they want the object itself, plus a bound or an error estimate, and referees bounce papers that don't hand it over. So there are two standards inside one practice, and I can't tell whether that's an actual inconsistency or just two different jobs being done.

Three wrinkles I can't fit together:

1. We now actually know that √2^√2 is irrational — transcendence theory, the Gelfond–Schneider theorem, gives you that it's transcendental. So one of the two branches is the true one, and the proof does as a matter of fact point at a witness. But only for a reader who knows that theorem; the proof itself never says. So is "constructive" a property of the proof, or of what the mathematical community happens to know, or of what was in the prover's head? I keep going back and forth on this, and it seems to matter for the whole debate.

2. There's an anti-realist / verificationist line I keep running into (Dummett and that crowd, I think — don't worry about naming names or getting the attribution right) which says roughly: if the meaning of a mathematical statement is fixed by what we're able to recognize as verifying it, then helping yourself to excluded middle over a predicate we have no way to decide is unmotivated, because you've let truth outrun any possible verification. If that's right, then ordinary classical practice is quietly resting on a semantic view — every mathematical statement determinately true or false, independently of us — that it never states and maybe couldn't defend. But is that really required, or is it just the story that happens to fit? I've also heard something that pulls the other way: for statements of the shape "for every x there's a y such that R(x,y)" with R decidable, a classical proof can often be unraveled mechanically into an actual program, even when the original proof used excluded middle all over the place — which would suggest classical mathematics secretly has constructive content exactly where anyone cares, and so doesn't need the realist story after all. Is that true? And if it is, does it defuse the worry or only relocate it to the statements that aren't of that shape?

3. Proof assistants: my impression is that in Lean you get excluded middle and choice more or less by default and a lot of the big mainstream formalization effort happens there, whereas in Agda or Coq you'd have to add them by hand as assumptions. If that's roughly right, is it a sociological accident, or does it tell us something about what mainstream mathematics actually needs?
