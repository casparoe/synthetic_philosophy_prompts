I hold a two-clause norm of assertion that I arrived at by trying to repair what I take to be the knowledge norm's blind spot, and I want it stress-tested rather than validated. Call it the Reliance Norm: S may assert that p only if (i) S is in a position to know that p, and (ii) S is warranted in believing that her audience's treating p as settled on her say-so would not predictably leave them epistemically worse off than if she had stayed silent. Clause (i) is just the knowledge norm and is meant to do its usual work — lottery propositions, Moore-paradoxical utterances, guesses dressed up as statements. Clause (ii) is where I am going out on a limb: it is meant to explain why it can feel improper to assert something you know to an audience you know will misuse it (the true but easily misread statistic offered to a crowd mid-moral-panic; the specialist's hedge-free claim to lay readers who will hear more confidence than is warranted), and also why institutional assertion is strange — a committee, a lab, or these days a language model can issue a flat claim that no individual member knows, and yet we do not react to it the way we react to a person guessing.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure, not a survey of the field. Which observations, arguments, or cases would actually count against it — and, crucially, which of those are inherited from clause (i) and so land on the plain knowledge norm as well, and which are specific to clause (ii)? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what a defender of my view should concede as counting against it before any critic presses the point. My own guesses at the soft spots: that the audience-relativity makes the permissibility of what I say depend on facts about your gullibility, which is the kind of feature that usually gets a proposed norm into trouble; and that clause (ii) is doing either no work or all of the work, in which case I do not really have a distinct view. Does it misclassify any ordinary, non-philosopher's assertion as impermissible — I care much more about that than about contrived cases? Does it collapse into the truth norm, into some pragmatic-encroachment story about knowledge itself, or into straight consequentialism about speech? And has the vagueness of clause (ii) insulated it so thoroughly that nothing could falsify it, and if so, is that a defect in this particular proposal or just the normal condition of proposing a constitutive rule?

Some background so you can pitch the answer: I have been reading this literature on the side for years, in the rationalist-blog-and-forum tradition where the knowledge norm is close to the house view, and I have come to find the standard arguments for it too quick — they feel like they settle the question by stipulating which cases are the telling ones. Assume I know the standard moves; do not spend time establishing that knowledge-norm views are popular or that truth and belief norms are on the table. Go straight to where my formulation is exposed. On priors: I am moved more by cases than by considerations of theoretical elegance, and I would count "it cannot distinguish itself from its rivals" as more damaging than "it has awkward implications in bizarre situations."
