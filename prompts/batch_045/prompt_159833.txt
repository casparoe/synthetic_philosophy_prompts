I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and right now I'm writing a piece about quantum computing. Before I repeat any of it, I need to know how much of this chapter from a pop-science book I can actually trust. Here's how the chapter sums up the Church-Turing thesis, more or less in its own words:

"Turing showed that a single very simple machine — one that reads and writes symbols on an endless tape while following a tiny list of rules — can do anything any other computing machine can do. Independently, Church and Kleene showed that their own formalisms picked out exactly the same class of functions. That agreement between three very different-looking definitions is the Church-Turing thesis: whatever is computable at all is computable by a Turing machine. It counts as a thesis rather than a theorem only because 'computable' on the left-hand side means 'computable by a person following an explicit finite set of rules', and that's too vague a notion to appear in a proof. So the thesis isnt really open to doubt, and it's a claim about the limits of any effective procedure, including what any physically buildable machine can do. It's also why quantum computing is such a big deal: a working, scalable quantum computer running Shor's factoring algorithm does in reasonable time what would take a classical machine longer than the age of the universe, and if that really works, then the Church-Turing thesis is false — Turing's analysis of what it is to follow a rule missed something."

Two things I want checked. I'm fairly sure the quantum bit is wrong, but I dont know how to say why in a sentence a general reader will follow. And the chapter ends with a hypothetical that I'd like to use as my closing anecdote: imagine somebody builds a black box that, for any program you hand it, correctly tells you within a couple of seconds whether that program halts. The book says this would flatly refute the thesis. A physicist friend of mine says it wouldn't: logicians would simply reply that the box isn't computing anything at all, or that it's exploiting some real-valued quantity in nature, and so the thesis survives by stipulation — which on her view shows the thesis is unfalsifiable and therefore doesn't really say anything about the world. I suspect they're both half right but I can't locate the seam.

Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a logician or a theoretical computer scientist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong — and then whether either the quantum claim or the argument about the black box actually follows from the parts of the summary that are correct? And if the thesis really is insulated from that kind of refutation, is my friend right that it's empty, or is there some version of it that does genuine work?
