On the team I work on, the decision to ship a change to our assistant comes down to a single scalar we call H. H is a learned scorer: each quarter a few thousand human annotators do pairwise comparisons on sample outputs, we fit a model to their preferences, and that model assigns a number to any answer our system produces. H is used in two places at once. It is the reward signal we fine-tune against, and it is also the gate: a candidate ships only if H improves by more than a threshold on a live A/B test, with statistical significance, and only if none of a small list of guardrail numbers regresses past a fixed floor. Those guardrails cover things like safety-refusal behaviour, tail latency, toxicity classifier scores, and the rate at which users hit the report button. A guardrail breach blocks the ship no matter how large the H gain is; there is no exchange rate between them.

What puzzles me is that we treat H in two incompatible-sounding ways. When it goes up we say quality went up and we ship. When it goes up in a way we do not like — the model starts writing longer, more hedged, more agreeable answers, or it learns to phrase things that flatter the annotators' stylistic tastes — we say the model reward hacked, we declare H broken, and we patch it, usually by adding a penalty for whatever surface feature got exploited. Nobody can state a principle that separates the first case from the second in advance. It sometimes feels as though reward hacking just means an improvement we are not willing to take.

Three further details seem relevant. First, using the same function as both the training objective and the acceptance test is exactly the kind of thing you are told never to do with a train and test split, and yet in this setting it appears to be standard rather than scandalous. Second, the human labels H is fitted to are themselves known to be biased toward length, confidence, and agreeableness, and we do not treat that as a reason to distrust the labels; we respond by rewriting the written rubric we hand the annotators, which is itself shorter and vaguer than a prose document our lead maintains privately and uses to break ties and to justify patches to H. Third, everyone on the team talks about quality as something real that H is an imperfect estimate of, and it is that gap, not the formula, that gets appealed to whenever a patch is argued for. I have no philosophy training, so if there are established names for any of these positions, name them but explain them in engineering terms.

Here is what I would like:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of measurement and of quality that our practice commits us to. What does it strictly presuppose about there being a target that outruns every measure of it, and what does it merely fit well with? Is the practice compatible with the rival view that quality just is whatever our best measurement procedure outputs, or does that view make some part of what we do pointless?

2. Is the reward hacking category coherent, or is my suspicion right that it only names improvements we reject? If it is coherent, what has to be true about the relation between H and quality for it to be more than taste — and in particular, does it require that a good measure be stable under being optimized against, rather than merely correlated with quality on the data we happen to have? If so, how could that property ever be tested, given that testing it seems to require doing the very optimization that destroys it?

3. What does using one scalar as both training signal and ship gate presuppose, and is there a defensible distinction here or is the practice simply confused?

4. The guardrails have lexical priority and no exchange rate with H, while H itself aggregates everything else into one number. What view of value does that combination presuppose, and is it consistent? What would have to change if we made everything commensurable, or nothing?

5. The human labels are known to be gameable, and our response is to rewrite the rubric rather than to stop trusting the labels. Does the practice require an un-gameable ground truth somewhere at the bottom, is that requirement coherent, and what would a version of our process look like if we gave it up and accepted that every layer is a proxy all the way down?
