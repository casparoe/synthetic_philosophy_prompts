I run a monthly thing at my local library — we call it Thinky Thursday, mostly the same 12 to 16 regulars, ages from about 20 to 75, nobody with any philosophy training including me, 90 minutes, one topic a month. Last month we did the trolley thing and it was a bit of a flop tbh: everyone just took turns stating their opinion and nobody moved an inch.

This month I want to do something on that story about the digger wasps. The way I remember it: the wasp stings a cricket, drags it back to her hole, leaves it on the doorstep, goes inside to have a look around, comes back out and pulls the cricket in. If you move the cricket a few inches while shes inside, she drags it back to the doorstep, then goes inside and checks all over again — and you can keep doing that to her, apparently over and over. I read it in a book a couple years ago, it might be exagerated, doesn't matter much for what I'm after.

What I want people to feel — and I should say that last time I stood at the front and explained the vocabulary first and everyone just handed my own words back to me, so I really don't want that — is a distinction I've been chewing on by myself. I think there might be two different things we mean by "she could have done otherwise." One is: if the world had been different, she'd have done something different. A thermostat does that. The other is: she can notice that she's going round in circles and change what she's doing *because* she noticed, not because anything outside her changed. I've started calling the first one "sensitivity" in my notes and the second one "answerability," and my guess is the wasp has a lot of the first and maybe none of the second.

But here's the bit I can't shake and that I actually want the session to land on. Any rule you write down for how to notice you're stuck and what to do about it is itself just another rule. So then you need a rule for revising that rule, and a rule for that one, and you can climb levels forever without ever obviously arriving at anything that is free rather than just more complicatedly rigid. I've been calling that the escalator. It might be a load of nonsense — say so if it is.

Can you design me a game for it? Rules, materials, a timeline across the 90 minutes, how to split people up, and stuff that costs about nothing — I've got masking tape, index cards, a bell, some boxes and about twenty dollars. My rough idea, which you should absolutely throw out if you've got better: small teams write an instruction sheet on cards with numbered steps; then a different person has to carry it out following only what is written, not what they think was meant; and I go around being "nature" from a secret list of little disruptions I've written in advance — I move the cricket, I block the hole, I put a second cricket down next to the first one, I swap in something too fat to fit through the entrance. Then round two they get to rewrite their sheet to fix what broke, and I run the same tricks but shifted slightly, and round three they get someone shouting advice from the side, but only three words at a time. Something along those lines. I want the room to walk into the problem, not be told about it.

Then give me a debrief that maps it back onto the ideas: which moment in the game was the sensitivity part, which moment was the answerability part, where the escalator actually showed up if it did, and where people will walk away convinced they found freedom when all they found was another layer of rules. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it — I'm guessing someone will flat out refuse to follow their own sheet, or one team will just write "use common sense" on the card and break the whole thing — and how I handle that without turning it into a lecture.

And be straight about what the game simply cannot do, the part where I have to stand up and say it in my own words.

One last thing I care about: the wasp's loop works almost every single time out in the real world. It isn't stupidity, it's reliability that only falls apart in a weird situation somebody set up on purpose. If everyone goes home thinking the wasp is just dumb I'll be annoyed, so build that in somehow. And if it can end on a real puzzle about us rather than about the wasp, that would be perfect — I'd like people leaving unsure whether the gap between them and her is one big thing or a hundred small ones.
