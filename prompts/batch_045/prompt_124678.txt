Two people at my rationality meetup got into it for like an hour last night over this and I gave up trying to referee, so I'm outsourcing. We've all read more LessWrong than is probaly good for us, which is context for why anyone thought this was worth arguing about.

Marcus — another guy in the group, not one of the two fighting — wants to run a real-money thing at the next meetup. Everyone fills out a 200-question values-and-politics quiz; he pairs each person with their nearest neighbor in answer-space (he says the typical top match is around 85% agreement, and nobody's partner is a stranger). Then everyone plays a one-shot anonymous prisoner's dilemma against their partner, for actual cash: both cooperate, $30 each; both defect, $10 each; you defect while your partner cooperates, you get $50 and she gets $0, and vice versa. He also floated a second variant for the same 40 people: a $4000 pot that gets paid out to a single claimer, but only if exactly one person claims it by noon. Zero claimers or two or more, nobody gets anything.

The argument was about what the correct play is and why:

(1) Jonas: cooperate. Forget the fancy version for a second — even on the dumb, purely statistical version, with these payoffs cooperating pays iff the probability your partner makes the same move you do is above 5/7, i.e. about 71%. An 85% match clears that. But the real point is that the correlation that matters is logical, not statistical: the argument for cooperating with a copy of yourself isn't "copies tend to do the same thing," it's that there's one decision procedure being run twice. And you can't coherently hope to be the unique defector — if defecting is right for you it's right for her.

(2) Rae: the 85% is agreement on a quiz, not probability of matching moves. You've swapped in a different quantity. What you actually need is P(she cooperates | I cooperate), and a values quiz doesn't hand you that. What drives one-shot PD cooperation is whether someone has accepted a particular argument about it, and quiz similarity barely touches that. Worse, shared exposure to an argument doesn't even reliably push people the same way: if a third of the room thinks superrationality is obviously right, a third thinks it's obviously a sucker move, and a third has never heard of it, "she reads the same blogs I do" moves P(match) almost not at all. And "you can't hope to be the unique defector" is only a coherence constraint at perfect correlation; at 85% the branch where you defect and she cooperates is a real branch, and it's the $50 branch.

(3) Jonas: two things. The base-rate worry is weak because partners aren't random draws — nearest-neighbor matching out of 40 selects hard, so the population base rate for one-shot PDs isn't the relevant prior. And "you can't have 85% of a copy" is too quick: the threshold calculation shows superrationality doesn't need identity, it needs the match probability above 5/7, so it degrades continuously rather than dying the instant you perturb the setup. Also, look at Marcus's second variant. Nobody in that room thinks "I should be the unique claimer" is a reason to claim. The superrational move there is to randomize — press with probability 1/40 — which gives roughly a 37% chance that anybody gets anything, about $37 in expectation per person. But even that is bad next to conditioning on a public signal: everyone agrees in advance that the claimer is whoever's badge number equals tomorrow's high temperature in Central Park mod 40, and you collect the whole $4000 for sure, $100 expected each. That's the real lesson — superrationality isn't about reasoning your way into your partner's head, it's about latching onto a shared signal so you land on a correlated equilibrium. The survey-match pairing is that kind of shared signal. So cooperate.

(4) Rae: the temperature scheme only works if it's common knowledge that everyone follows it, and with 40 semi-anonymous people in a group chat it isn't; if you think even two people will freelance, your own best move changes, and the whole thing unravels. And it's worse than that: the scheme has no teeth, because a non-selected person who spoils loses nothing personally — the pot dies for everyone else, but she was getting $0 either way. So it's only weakly incentive compatible, and if there's any chance the selected person flakes, everyone has a reason to claim as a backup and you're back to two-or-more. Anyway the variant-2 move is a different animal from variant 1. Agreeing in advance to a rule that picks one winner is a coordination device. What's at issue in the PD is which decision theory is correct. If I can bind myself to a rule in the PD case I'd just bind myself to cooperate — that's precommitment, not superrationality, so your analogy smuggles in enforcement and then credits the enforcement to the decision theory.

(5) Jonas: fine, grant all that. You still have to pick a move, and "cooperate" is what you'd want everyone in your epistemic position to do, and not being certain she does it doesn't make defecting any less of an attempt to be the unique defector.

(6) Rae: that last bit just restates what's under dispute. "What you'd want everyone in your position to do" is universalizability with a decision-theory costume on, and it only spits out cooperation because the game is symmetric — in a PD where the two players' payoffs differ slightly, superrationality gives no answer at all, which is odd for something claiming to be the right decision theory.
