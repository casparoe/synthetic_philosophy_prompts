I'm in a small reading group this term on the unity of the proposition — me and two other grad students, no instructor, so we're basically unsupervised and none of us really knows the literature. I picked up one of those big-ideas popular metaphysics books to get oriented before my week, and I want to know how much of it I can safely repeat out loud. The chapter on relations says roughly this (reconstructed from memory, so the wording is mine):

"Bradley pointed out that you can't assemble a proposition out of a pile of parts. Take 'Desdemona loves Cassio'. If the proposition is just Desdemona, Cassio and loving, you've got a list of three items, and a list isn't the kind of thing that can be false. So you need something extra that ties them into a single claim. But the tie is itself just one more item, so it too has to be tied to the first three by something else, and that has to be tied as well, and so on without end. Every step adds a new constituent and never delivers any unity, so the proposition ends up having infinitely many constituents — a metaphysical explosion, which is what makes the regress vicious. Bradley's conclusion was that relations aren't real and reality is one undifferentiated whole, wich is why nobody has been a Bradleyan for a hundred years. Russell and Moore simply replied that relations obviously exist, and the problem went away. What survives is the modern, purely semantic version of it: how do the meanings of words combine into the meaning of a sentence? And that one has been solved — say the proposition is ordered, a structure with slots and fillers rather than a heap, and the regress stops, because a structure is already one thing. There's a leftover question about what makes a true proposition true, i.e. about facts, but it has the same answer."

He then floats his own view: stop building the proposition out of parts at all, start from the whole act of judging, and treat the parts as abstractions from it, so unity is contributed by the act rather than by any constituent. He says this is basically Bradley's own answer and that it costs you nothing, since propositions still come out true or false.

Two things I'm suspicious of but can't put my finger on. The "infinitely many constituents, therefore vicious" bit: I thought the standard complaint is that the regress doesn't explain anything, or that each stage reintroduces the very unity it was meant to account for, and that some infinite regresses are harmless — so counting entities might be the wrong objection entirely. And the "just make it ordered/structured" bit: I have a feeling ordering either needs its own connector or just relocates the problem one step back, but I can't reconstruct why.

Can you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what a specialist would accept, what's a standard simplification, and what's just wrong? In particular: is it fair that Russell and Moore brushed the argument aside rather than engaging it; is the author sliding from the technical sense of 'proposition' as truth-bearer into the everyday 'proposal' sense anywhere; and does collapsing the unity problem into compositionality, and then into facts, conflate questions that are normally kept apart? Also: is the "act of judging" move a real option with a name, or does it just push the regress somewhere else — and if it does work, what does it cost you?
