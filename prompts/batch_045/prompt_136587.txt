My brother-in-law works as a court clerk and he got me into an argument at Thanksgiving that I still cant settle. Its about oaths.

Here's what puzzles me. When someone gets sworn in as a witness, the ritual is insanely strict about the *words*. You have to actually say them out loud. Nodding isn't enough. Holding up your hand isn't enough. My brother-in-law says he's seen testimony get thrown out because the oath was never properly given, and that a perjury charge can collapse on exactly that technicality. So the form matters, in a way it almost never does anywhere else in law.

But the very same system seems totally indifferent to the *inner* part:

- You can affirm instead of swearing if invoking God bothers you, and nobody asks what you believe either way.
- A notary isn't supposed to check whether what you signed is true. She checks your ID, watches you sign, checks nobody's twisting your arm, and stamps.
- In federal filings, my brother-in-law says, you can skip the ritual altogether: you sign a declaration at home that ends with a line saying you know lying has consequences, and it counts the same as if a clerk had sworn you in a courtroom.
- And if you secretly cross your fingers, or say the words while thinking 'I don't mean this,' you're bound anyway. There's no defense of *I didn't really swear it*.

So: fussy about the mouth, carefree about the mind. Now the part that started the fight. His county is looking at a self-service kiosk for this. You walk up, you pick swear or affirm, a recorded voice plays the formula at you, you push a button, and the county's position is that you are now duly sworn, just as if a deputy clerk had done it in person. His boss says it's obviously fine, it's the same words and the same consequences. His coworker says it's obviously a travesty and that something real is lost, and she gets genuinely angry about it.

I can't tell who's right, mostly because I can't tell what any of this is *for*. An oath isn't a statement — it doesn't describe anything, so it isn't true or false. It isn't quite a promise either, since what you'd be promising is to tell the truth, which you were already on the hook for. It seems to do a third thing: leave the sentences you were going to say anyway completely unchanged, and change what's at stake in saying them. So:

1. What has to be true about what words can do for the strict-about-form / indifferent-to-belief combination to be coherent? Is that one view, or two unrelated ones stacked on top of each other?
2. Which parts of the practice does that view strictly require, and which parts are just inertia that would survive replacing it? Would a flat 'the oath is nothing but a credible warning about consequences' view license all of it, kiosk included? If it does, what would actually have to change in the practice?
3. Is the coworker's 'something real is lost' the kind of thing that's true or false here, or is the practice underdetermined enough that two different theories of the oath would both sit fine with the kiosk, meaning neither of them is wrong?

My brother-in-law also says the thing that makes an oath work is that you're *calling something to witness*, and that even the secular version keeps the shape of that. I suspect that's the sort of thing people say because it sounds deep. But I couldn't say why it's wrong, if it is.

Last thing: I'd rather be told what's shaky than handed a tidy story. Where is your reconstruction most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind — what's the strongest thing the kiosk's defenders could say that you haven't already written off?
