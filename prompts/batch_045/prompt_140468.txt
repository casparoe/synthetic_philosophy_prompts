So this got personally relevant in a dumb way: in March I got appointed to fill out a vacancy on our township's zoning hearing board. Three people, meets once a month, and I have no legal training at all — I run a small print shop. Our soliciter handed us a one-page list of "rules for reading the ordinance," and I've been chewing on it for six months because the first rule seems to collide with the rest of them. Roughly, it says:

(1) Start with the words. If the words are clear, that's the end of it — don't go asking what the drafters were thinking or what problem they were trying to fix.
(2) But if taking the words plainly gives an absurd result, you don't have to follow the plain reading.
(3) Read the whole ordinance together, and don't read any word or clause as doing no work.
(4) The specific provision beats the general one.
(5) If it's genuinely ambiguous, tilt toward the property owner.
(6) If it's ambiguous, also tilt away from any reading that would raise a constitutional problem.

And it's not abstract for us, we've got two live ones. One: the setback rule says no structure may be built within 25 feet of a side lot line, and a resident bolted a 14-foot steel sculpture to a concrete pad in that strip. Is a sculpture a structure. Two: a section from the 1970s bars any trailer, boat, or unregistered vehicle from being kept in a front yard, and somebody is living in a tiny house built on a trailer chassis that is registered and insured.

What I want from you:

- What theory of what a legal text *is* — of meaning, or of who gets to say what the rule is — does this bundle of rules presuppose? In particular, can (1) and (2) both be true at once? It looks to me like (1) says the meaning of the rule is what the sentence says, and (2) says the meaning is what a reasonable reader would take the drafters to have been driving at, which are different theories. Reconstruct whatever single view makes the whole list hang together, and tell me if it can't be done.
- My pet theory, which I want you to either sharpen or knock down: I think (2), (5) and (6) have nothing to do with meaning at all. They're about who eats the cost of a badly drafted ordinance — the point is to force the supervisors to go fix their own mess rather than having us quietly repair it at the back of the room, and "absurd result" is just a way of saying we'll step in only when the fix is too obvious to argue about. Does the practice strictly need that story, or is it one of several stories that happen to fit? And what would the handout look like if you rewrote it deliberately around that idea?
- Related: when we depart from the clear text, are we doing one thing or three — deciding the text doesn't really say that once you have the full context, deciding it does say that but we won't enforce it as written, or deciding to enforce it and just wave a flag at the drafters? Does the handout conflate those, and does the conflation matter?
- The bit I can't get past: rule (3) — don't treat any word as surplus — seems to presuppose one careful drafter who never repeats herself and never writes belt-and-suspenders. Our ordinance is a patchwork of amendments from 1974, 1998 and 2011 written by three different solicitors, and it definately has redundancies. Does the practice require us to pretend otherwise? Is that pretense the same kind of animal as the law's reasonable person, and does it matter that everyone knows it's false?
- And be honest about the underdetermined parts: I suspect a hard textualist and a moderate purposivist could both sign most of this handout and only really diverge in one or two spots. Tell me which spots, and what each of them would have to give up.

Don't cite cases or name judges at me — I have no law library, no Lexis, and no way to check you, so it would just be noise. I want the reconstruction, the disagreements, and where the practice is philosophically looser than it looks.
