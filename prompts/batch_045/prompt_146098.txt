I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term and I've got myself stuck on the function debate in a way that is now actively interfering with studying for the midterm. We've been given the two standard stories — the etiological or selected-effects one, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, and the causal-role one, on which a trait's function is whatever it contributes to the capacities of the system it sits in, relative to some decomposition of that system — and instead of picking a side I tried writing down my own verdicts on a list of cases, hoping the pattern would tell me which one I actually believe. It doesn't; it seems to tell me that I believe both, inconsistently, and I'd like to know whether there's a principled position in the neighborhood that saves most of what I've written down.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can state them. (1) The heart's pumping blood is its function; the heart's making the thumping sound that a midwife listens for is not a function, even though it is useful to someone. (2) The degenerate, skin-covered eyes of a cave population that has been in total darkness for a very long time and is under no selection on vision at all still have the function of seeing — they simply can no longer do it; I want to say the same about a human appendix that really does nothing now. (3) A one-off somatic mutation in a single animal's heart muscle, not heritable and with no selection history whatsoever, that makes contraction stronger and helps the animal survive — I want to say that does have a function, and this is the verdict my study group says I have to give up. (4) A novel protein fold that arose by one lucky mutation, was never screened by selection, and happens to buffer a chemical the organism needs buffered: I don't want to say its function is buffering, it's just a fortunate accident; but in a lab population where that same buffering protein has been under strong selection for a couple of hundred generations I'm happy to call buffering its function. (5) A structure originally selected for one job (stiffening a limb) that populations now use for a completely different job (stripping leaves off branches), where doing the new job well now makes the difference between more and fewer offspring but the structure's shape hasn't changed at all — I go back and forth on this one, and so does everyone I've asked. (6) A gene-drive element that biases its own transmission and spreads through a population while making its carriers worse at flying: the function of the element is to get itself transmitted, and I want to say both that this is a genuine function and that it is a malfunction from the organism's point of view. (7) A tumor has no function, and a tumor's secreting a hormone that happens to keep the patient's blood pressure up is not a function either, even though it is currently helping. (8) A coloration pattern that drifted to fixation in a small island population and, since a predator arrived recently, happens to camouflage its bearer: I don't want to call camouflage its function yet — but I can't say what would have to happen for it to become one, or when.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is broken, is something like: a trait has function F only if the trait's doing F figures in the right kind of explanation of why the trait is there, where the explaining can be done either by a history of selection or by a currently operating mechanism that maintains it. Please test that against my eight cases, tell me where it overgenerates or undergenerates, and repair it into something precise enough to have teeth — or tell me plainly which of my verdicts has to be overturned and why, and what the simplest principle that saves the rest would cost me. I'd also like to know what the repaired account says about three cases I haven't listed: a gene that designers inserted into a synthetic cell because it was needed for division, in a lineage that has since been evolving in the lab for thousands of generations, where nobody has identified what the gene does; a unit in a trained neural network that measurably contributes to the network's competence; and the moment, if there is one, at which a drift-fixed trait's useful effect turns into a real function. If you think no single account can hold all this together and that the right answer is that function ascriptions are always relativized to a system or a level, I want to hear that too — with an account of which level my verdicts are implicitly switching between, and when.
