hey — I'm a postdoc in political theory and I've ended up defending, slightly more strongly than I intended, a view about merit in selection (hiring, college admissions, promotion, prizes, the usual cases) that I now want someone to try to break, because I have a nagging feeling I've quietly built it so nothing could count against it. Roughly the view: merit is always role-relative — to deserve a position just is to be the candidate expected to best serve that role's constitutive aim — and from that I get a divide I quite like. Roles with a single, relatively uncontested aim (a commercial pilot, a bomb-disposal technician, arguably a research fellowship judged purely on output) admit a genuine meritocratic justification; roles whose aims are essentially contested (a university, a police force, a supreme court, a hospital board) don't, and there "the best qualified candidate" is doing something other than it advertises: either smuggling in one contested specification of the aims as if it were neutral, or laundering the advantages of whoever is already winning. I've bolted on two extra bits that I'm not sure are load-bearing: (i) the ratchet — merit criteria drift over time to match the traits of whoever currently succeeds, so merit-talk systematically stabilizes existing hierarchies; and (ii) aim-drift — because who gets selected partly determines what the role's aims become later on, the counterfactual "how well would this candidate serve the aims" isn't well defined even in principle, so the account can't be rescued just by better prediction. Please act as a political philosopher who works on desert and distributive justice and has read around the meritocracy literature, and think carefully: start by brainstorming maybe six to eight independent things that could count against this before you rank anything at all, and don't just give me the objections people standardly make against meritocracy — give me the ones that bite on my version specifically.

What I want out of it: defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one an honest note on whether I, as a committed defender, would concede it or whether I'd dismiss it as smuggling in a rival assumption. Include the uncomfortable categories — a case the view can't describe, a consequence I'm committed to that's unacceptable, a result showing the whole thing collapses into something I was trying to avoid (Rawlsian fair equality of opportunity? luck egalitarianism about effort and circumstance? some qualified-lottery proceduralism?) — and be explicit about whether that collapse is itself a defeat or just a redescription. Also tell me which of my two bolted-on bits the core actually needs, and whether dropping the ratchet saves everything else. And then, bluntly: has the move to "essentially contested aims" made the view unfalsifiable, and if it has, is that a real problem for a view of this kind or just what normative views are like? I'd definately rather hear the strongest version against me than a comforting one.

Then run it on three cases I can't make up my mind about: military officer selection (arguably contested aims, yet the intuition that we must pick on merit is very strong there — a case where I might have to bite a bullet I don't want to bite), organ allocation (pretty clear-ish aim, and yet we resist pure efficiency scoring and add urgency, age, queue position — so is that a counterexample to my divide or a case where the aims were never as single as they looked?), and blind orchestral auditions (a case where tightening merit criteria famously changed who got in, which I want to claim as evidence for the ratchet and which I suspect someone can turn straight around on me). I'd be most moved by two kinds of thing: a role with genuinely contested aims where our merit-selection intuitions are rock solid, and any empirical finding that would actually discriminate — e.g., if criteria set under one contested specification of a role's aims still predicted performance well under rival specifications, or if qualified-lottery selection in contested-aim roles reliably degraded something measurable independent of the aims in dispute.
