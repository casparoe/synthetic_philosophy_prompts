I'm a CS PhD student, and on Sundays I'm in a tiny reading group (four of us) that has been picking very slowly through the market-based "logical induction" framework for reasoning under logical uncertainty. I keep trying to explain an idea out loud and failing, so I wrote it up as a short blog post, and I'd like you to read it the way a referee would and tell me where it breaks. Its meant to be informal — a post, not a paper — so I probably care whether it's interesting at least as much as whether it's right. Here's the draft:

**Targeting computation: no-Dutch-book is a theory of prices, not of thinking**

*Setup.* Someone who is unsure whether a program halts, or whether some identity holds for all n below 10^9, is unsure about something no event in the world could settle. Classical Bayesianism has trouble here. A coherent prior already assigns probability 1 to everything provable, so conditionalizing on a newly found proof is a no-op — which misdescribes exactly the phenomenon we care about, namely that finding a proof is what changes mathematicians' minds. (I've also seen the stronger claim that no computable prior can be coherent at all over a language this expressive. I've never been able to reconstruct the argument and I won't hang anything on it, but I'd like to know whether it's true.) Either way, logical uncertainty looks like it needs its own theory rather than being a special case of empirical uncertainty.

*The target.* The market-based reply replaces coherence with a negative condition: don't be exploitable. Let prices on sentences evolve as a computable deductive process runs; if no continuous computable trader can make unbounded profit at bounded risk, you get the goodies — convergence on sentences the process eventually settles, calibration, learning to trust the process. I think this is the right kind of move and I don't want to dispute it. I want to say it answers a different question than mine. My question is: which computation should you run? Under empirical uncertainty you largely can't choose what you learn next; the world decides. Under logical uncertainty you choose the lemma, the search depth, the conjecture to test. The natural proposal is a logical value of information: rank candidate computations by how much resolving them could change what you do, times how likely they are to change it, and work down the list. I claim this directive is not merely expensive to follow but ill-posed, for a reason specific to logical uncertainty:

(a) Whether resolving φ would change your action is itself a question of the same kind. It depends on what the rest of your deliberation would output under φ versus under ¬φ, and that is a fact about a computation you haven't run. So applying the rule requires already having answered the sort of question the rule was supposed to schedule.

(b) So the rule is self-referential in a way empirical VOI isn't. You need priorities before you can compute, and computing priorities is itself a computation that needs priorities. A fixed meta-level budget schedule doesn't escape this; it relocates it one level up.

(c) Sketch that the regress isn't just an artifact: take any computable priority policy π, and build a decision problem whose payoff turns on some φ where φ's relevance to the decision only becomes visible after more relevance-checking than π performs before deciding whether to compute φ. The agent that spends extra effort estimating relevance first beats π. Diagonalize over π and no computable priority policy is optimal.

*What instead.* Move the unit of assessment from prices to policies. Call an agent's effective opinion about φ whatever its own deliberation policy would output about φ given its remaining budget; require self-endorsement, i.e. the opinions the agent acts on are already its effective opinions, so deliberation is transparent to itself at the level of what it acts on. A theory of logical uncertainty is then a theory of which computation-plus-action policies are admissible, and no-Dutch-book becomes a constraint on one component. Two remarks I'd like checked: it isn't sufficient (a defender will point out that the traders do their own computing, so the market is forced to price in whatever is computable — fine, but that's a limit statement; it says nothing about order or budget allocation, and my deadline is what makes order matter), and it isn't necessary either (the criterion is asymptotic, but deliberation has a deadline, so being Dutch-bookable on a timescale longer than your horizon looks free).

*The crux, where I'm least sure.* Self-endorsement invites Löb-style trouble: a system that fully trusts its own output — if I would conclude φ, then φ — proves everything by Löb's theorem. The probabilistic weakening, trusting the process's calibration on the sentences it actually inspects rather than each individual output, seems to block that, but I can't tell whether it also closes the regress in (a)–(c) or just pushes it inside the definition of effective opinion.

What I want, in this order: (1) accuracy — is the setup claim right, is (c) sound or hand-waving, does the Löb objection actually bite the weakened version; (2) whether the self-endorsement proposal is even coherent, and if not, the nearest coherent version of it; (3) interestingness — am I attacking something anyone actually holds, or reinventing something standard in worse words. Be blunt about which parts are just wrong versus under-argued.
