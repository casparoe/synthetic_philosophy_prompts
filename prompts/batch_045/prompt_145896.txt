I'm a software engineer by training and trade — ten years on distributed systems, no formal philosophy background — and I've been invited to give a talk next month in the decision-science seminar series at a business school. Format is a short paper (20 minutes) plus a long Q&A (about 25), and I'm much more worried about the Q&A than the talk. I'd like you to build me a prep sheet.

The thesis I'm defending: the money-pump argument is a weaker argument for diachronic rationality than it is usually taken to be, because it smuggles in an unrealistically strong adversary. The standard argument gives the exploiter perfect advance knowledge of the agent's future preferences and then walks the agent around a cycle, extracting a guaranteed loss. I want to replace that with what I call a *bandit money pump*: an adversary who observes only the choices the agent actually makes, who has to discover the cycle online, and who pays a small cost each round it offers a trade. For such an adversary the right measure of harm isn't "was a sure loss extracted" but regret — the agent's realized payoff against the best fixed non-binding plan it could have announced at the start and then failed to stick to. My punchline is that consistency of preference across time is sufficient but not necessary for immunity to this kind of exploitation, so the normative weight of the money pump falls on a no-regret condition, not on a consistency axiom.

The case that motivates it, and the one my simulator actually produces: *band-limited intransitivity*. Suppose an agent's comparisons are perfectly transitive except when two options fall within some just-noticeable difference δ of each other; inside that band the relation is allowed to cycle (A preferred to B, B to C, C to A, all by margins under δ). Such an agent violates the textbook consistency requirement, but my runs suggest an adversary who doesn't already know the cycle can only extract loss that grows sublinearly in the number of rounds — something like the square-root-of-T behaviour you get from online learning — because each exploratory trade costs the adversary something and reveals only a little. So: an agent can be cyclic, known to be cyclic by anyone who reads its whole preference graph, and still be effectively unexploitable in the only sense that seems to matter, and I want to say it isn't irrational in the way the money pump is supposed to establish. If you like systems analogies: I'm arguing that requiring a total order on preferences is like requiring strong consistency, when what the money pump really motivates is the much weaker guarantee that no scheduler can drive the system into unbounded debt.

The audience, as I understand it: roughly thirty people. Most are judgment-and-decision-making psychologists and behavioral economists from the business school — they run experiments on present bias, intertemporal choice, and self-control; they think in terms of treatments and effect sizes; several of them have built careers on the claim that real people are systematically time-inconsistent and worse off for it. Two are philosophers of action who work on weakness of will and intention; they will care whether I've described what an agent has reason to do or merely what an agent can be made to lose by. One person from the ML group will be there and is likely to be my friendliest questioner, possibly too friendly. And there's an econometrician who comes to everything and will ask whether any of this is identified from choice data. Nobody in the room is a decision theorist by trade, so the technical apparatus will be new to them but not hostile.

What I want from you:

The eight or so objections I'll actually get from that room, ordered by expected damage (likelihood times severity), not by logical order — and explicitly not the objections I'd get from an online rationalist audience, which I can already predict.

For each one: phrase it the way the person raising it would phrase it, in their vocabulary, and then give me a one- or two-sentence plain-English gloss so I can follow it, plus a short note on who raises it. Then a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede outright in that reply, what I should refuse to give ground on, and — most usefully for me — a note on where my reply is soft or where it quietly depends on an assumption I haven't defended. I'd rather walk in knowing which of my answers are load-bearing and which are hope.

Also: tell me what to concede before anyone asks, in the first two minutes of the talk, to take the sting out of the room. My instinct is that I should grant immediately that present bias is real, measured, and costly, and that nothing I say questions the data — but tell me if that concession costs me the thesis.

And give me the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks. My own candidate is the one where the deadline-person points out that if exploitation by a *computationally bounded* adversary is the standard, then whether an agent counts as irrational depends on what is efficiently computable, so my "norm" is really a contingent fact about algorithms and hardware wearing a normative disguise — which would mean I haven't found a norm at all, I've found a benchmark. If that's the right one, tell me what I'd have to say if it does get asked; if there's a worse one lurking, tell me that instead. I suspect the philosophers of action have one I'm not seeing.

One last thing: assume I can't look anything up between now and then and that I don't know the literature's proper names for these moves. Where an objection has a standard label or a standard reply in the decision-theory literature, describe the move in a sentence rather than naming it — I'll recognize the shape even if I can't cite it, and I'd rather not drop a name I can't pronounce correctly.
