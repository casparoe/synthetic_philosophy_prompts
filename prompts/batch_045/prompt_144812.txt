I am a sixth-form student at a state school in London, in the final year, and I am doing a directed-reading course in early modern philosophy with one teacher who knows Berkeley properly. I am writing a 1,200-word paper for a national essay competition judged by academic philosophers, and I want it to do something more than restate familiar material. My question is what George Berkeley would say about a kind of thing that did not exist in his lifetime, and I would like you to reason your way to an answer rather than tell me what is in the secondary literature.

Here is the case. Suppose a persistent online world — call it *Ashfall* — that players walk around in. Two of its regions matter for my argument, and I have deliberately built them so that they differ in exactly one respect.

In the first region, terrain is generated on demand from a stored seed. The geometry of a particular hillside is nowhere present as data until a player's client asks the server for it; when the request comes, the server computes the hillside, sends it, and discards it. But the generator is deterministic, so there is always a definite answer to what the hillside would have been had anyone gone there: the same request always returns the same hillside, and one could in principle compute what is there without ever visiting. Assume also that no one ever inspects the logs, and that before any player arrives, no human being has any sensory experience of that hillside at all.

In the second region — call it *the Glass Coast* — there is no seed. When a player enters, a language model composes the terrain on the spot from the surrounding context. Before anyone enters, there is no answer at all to what the Glass Coast is like: not an unknown answer, but no determinate one.

What I want from you is a considered prediction of Berkeley's verdict on both regions, argued through, with the following conditions.

First, treat them separately, and identify which structural feature of each is doing the work. If you think the difference between them makes no difference to him, say why.

Second, I do not want the answer to collapse into the remark that God perceives everything anyway. That move is available to him in both cases and would flatten my paper. Tell me what work the divine mind is actually supposed to be doing: whether the continued existence of an unobserved object is, for Berkeley, a matter of God's ongoing perceiving or of God's causing ideas in orderly succession according to laws, and which of those the two regions pull apart.

Third, engage the step everyone calls the master argument — the instruction to try to think of a tree existing unconceived, and the observation that in performing it one only succeeds in conceiving it. Say whether the generated-on-demand case gives Berkeley a sharper way to press it, or whether it exposes the gap between I cannot perform that act of conception and that state of affairs cannot obtain. And note that Berkeley also denies that the mind can frame abstract general ideas: a rule or an algorithm is not, for him, the sort of thing that can be before a mind in the way a coloured patch can. Say whether that doctrine helps him with the seeded hillside or costs him.

Fourth, tell me whether the server, the client, or the generating model could count as a perceiver on his terms, and why.

Fifth, mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond anything Berkeley actually wrote, and say what would make such an extrapolation faithful to the spirit of his system rather than merely convenient.

Finally, give me the strongest objection my teacher is likely to raise to the whole exercise, and how a Berkeleyan should reply to it.

Please write about 900 to 1,100 words, in a register suitable for a paper read by professional philosophers: precise, no hand-holding, no summary of Berkeley's life or times. Do not give me citations, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading — I have no way to check them, and the competition forbids them. Argue from the content of the position itself.
