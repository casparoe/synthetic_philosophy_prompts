I'm writing a short piece for a general-audience philosophy magazine on Arrival (Villeneuve, 2016, from Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life) read against Jackson's knowledge argument, and I want the reading tested rather than asserted.

The mapping looks obvious, which makes me suspicious of it: Louise Banks acquires a new representational system, and once she has it the scenes she initially takes to be memories of a dead daughter are revealed as foresight. Nothing in the world changes; her access to it does. That reads as a dramatization of the representationalist reply — Mary gains no new facts, only a new way of representing facts she already possessed, which is close to where Jackson himself eventually landed.

What I want is the three-way distinction made precise and then applied to her case specifically: Mary gains new non-physical facts; Mary gains only abilities and no new facts (the ability hypothesis, Lewis and Nemirow); Mary gains new phenomenal concepts, new representational vehicles for old contents, obtainable only by acquaintance. What does each predict about Louise, and on which of them does her transformation count as gaining knowledge in the sense the argument needs?

The wrinkle I most want pressed: the order is reversed. Louise gets the vehicle first — a non-linear script — and the new mode of experiencing follows; Mary gets the experience first and the concept afterward. Call the reversed case the linguistic-Mary variant. If a notation could in principle deliver the phenomenal concepts without the experience, then the acquaintance constraint doing the real work in the phenomenal-concept strategy fails, and with it much of the argument's appeal. Is Arrival evidence that this is possible, or is the language better understood as installing a cognitive capacity, which pushes back toward the ability hypothesis?

Two places I expect resistance, and I want them named rather than smoothed over: Louise's relation to the future is presented as lived and affective rather than propositional, which may support a genuinely phenomenal reading; and the film rests on a very strong linguistic-relativity premise that most philosophers of mind and language would reject. Does the reading survive weakening that premise to something defensible? Finally, does the film's practical register — whether foreseeing an outcome undermines choosing it, and her choice to have the child anyway — move the philosophical dial, or is foreknowledge and agency a separate problem that merely shares a structure?
