You are a philosopher who works on the metaphysics of welfare and the philosophy of pain, and I would like you to reason carefully rather than quickly: before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm every step of the argument below that could in principle be rejected, then choose one, explain why that step looked solid enough to fool me, and finish with a short ledger of what each of the other exits would cost me theoretically. I am not after reassurance that my friend is wrong; I am after the hinge.

The argument comes from a friend I argued with for three hours after a seminar last week, and it begins from pain asymbolia: patients who, after certain kinds of brain injury and in older neurosurgical case reports, are said to retain the full sensory character of a painful stimulus — they can say that it is burning, or stabbing, or cramping, and can localise it and rate its intensity — while reporting that it no longer hurts them, that it does not distress them, and that they feel no inclination to withdraw or escape. Grant the case reports for the sake of argument; I do not want to dispute the neurology, and my friend insists the argument works even if the phenomenon is rare or imperfectly described. His chain was this:

(1) Asymbolia shows that the sensory-discriminative component of pain — location, intensity, bodily quality — is separable from its affective-motivational component: the unpleasantness, the distress, the urge to stop and get away.
(2) In these patients the sensory component remains, and the subjects are not, by their own reports or by any observable measure, made worse off by it. So whatever makes pain bad is located entirely in the affective component.
(3) Therefore the sensory component of pain is in itself prudentially neutral, like the taste of bitterness or the feel of pressure: it carries no value either way, and there is no reason as such to prevent it or to avoid it.
(4) So there is no prudential reason to prefer an intervention that abolishes the whole pain over one that leaves the sensation fully intact and strips off only the unpleasantness. If the second intervention were cheaper, safer, or easier to deliver, we should prefer it.
(5) Since the components are separable, they are recombinable: nothing in the neighbourhood of (1) to (3) rules out the very same sensory character arriving with a positive affective sign. Masochistic enjoyment is offered as evidence that this is actual and not merely conceivable.
(6) But then the sensory character of pain is unused hedonic real estate. A life — or a hospital, or a world — in which every painful sensation keeps its full intensity and comes with positive valence is better than one with fewer pains and more neutral states; preventing injury, disease and tissue damage has no prudential point except through affect; and the rational policy is not fewer injuries but more sign-flipping. The ideal life is maximal sensory intensity with the valence inverted.
(7) That is absurd, and not merely surprising: no view of welfare worth having says we should keep the burns and flip the sticker, or that the badness of being injured is a detachable property a drug could peel off while leaving behind everything we actually cared about. So one of (1) through (6) is false.

I want to keep (1) and (2). The distinction seems clinically real, and it is what drew me to a pure valence view of welfare in the first place — the view that only the felt unpleasantness or pleasantness of an experience matters — and I would rather give up almost anything than that. I have tried two exits and been talked out of both. I said the subjects' reports concern motivation and distress rather than prudential value, and that someone can be harmed and not mind, just as a severely depressed person can be harmed and not mind; my friend replied that if nothing is bad for the subject then there is nothing left for a theory of welfare to be about, and that I am smuggling in desires or an objective list by the back door. I then said the sensory character cannot really be intact once the affect is gone, and he replied that this is a verbal stop: if I build unpleasantness into the definition of pain then (1) is empty and the clinical reports become unintelligible. Where is this going wrong — which step do you reject — and what does rejecting it cost me?
