I used to be a research assistant for an archaeologist — call her M — who is writing a book on the c-word, and I am now doing a master's and drafting a chapter that leans on her framework, so I want to pin down what rule she is actually running before I build anything on it. Her verdicts so far, as she has given them in talks and in the two chapters she has let me read: the Mycenaean palace world around 1200 BCE, collapse without hesitation, palaces and script and long-distance exchange all gone and nothing on that scale rebuilt for centuries; the Classic Maya southern lowlands, collapse, with her line that what ended was a political system rather than a people, since Maya people are obviously still there; Rapa Nui, not a collapse, she thinks the ecocide story is one we told about them and that the statue cult gave way to a different one; Norse Greenland, collapse, even though the last scrap of writing from there is a wedding in the early 1400s and after that just silence; the western Roman Empire, not a collapse, too slow and too much carried over; the Inca, also not a collapse, because conquest is something done to you; the Mesa Verde region around 1300, not a collapse, people relocated and Pueblo descendants describe it as a migration rather than an end; and the Soviet Union in 1991, collapse, on the grounds that what disappeared was the future people could imagine. I cannot get the Rome verdict and the USSR verdict to sit together under any single rule I try, since Russia kept the archives and the seat and most of the ministries, and the Inca and Greenland rulings seem to undercut her own conquest line, which makes me think the real rule is something other than what she says out loud.

The case she has not ruled on, which is meant to be the last chapter: a coastal town of about eight thousand that over roughly thirty years, with large majority support at every referendum, moves most of its housing and businesses two kilometres inland after a bad storm surge, abandons its port, lets the old site return to tidal marsh, merges its municipality into a neighbour an hour away, and loses its maternity ward and its local paper to bigger places further off. Population is slightly up, incomes are up, nobody who wanted to stay was forced out, the town is unusually pleased with itself, and it has a published adaptation plan running to 2050. Structurally it is clearly less complex than it was: fewer institutions, fewer specialised roles, less energy and fewer goods flowing through it, and an archaeologist looking at the debris in five hundred years would call it a collapse without thinking twice. So what does she say, and what should she say if she wants to be consistent with herself? I can see three or four rules that fit her track record about equally well — a straight complexity-loss rule, a rule about whether the people living through it experienced catastrophe, a rule about whether the participants themselves narrate an ending, and something stranger about the time horizon over which a community can still plan and commit — and they come apart sharply on this town, so tell me which one is really doing the work in her verdicts, what each predicts here, which you would bet on and why, and which is the closest analog among her precedents: the relocations, or the abandonments. Then the thing I actually need: name one further case that would separate the surviving rules from each other. My candidate is a case where nothing material falls apart — all the buildings and bureaucracies intact — but everyone involved says their world ended, and I want to know whether that is the right probe or whether I am missing a better one. And one more thing I keep going in circles on with my flatmate: if a future archaeologist with only the pots and the foundations would say collapse, and the people living through it would not, is collapse for her something that happens to a society or something that happens to evidence about one?
