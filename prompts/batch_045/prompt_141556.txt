I'm a novelist, and the book I've been working on for three years is set largely in the offices of a provincial transport authority in a country that is quietly deporting a minority population. The two characters I keep getting stuck on are a section head and his deputy. The section head is, in my draft, a scrupulous man: he keeps a careful diary, worries about whether he has done the job properly, learns the regulations by heart, and on two occasions has written memos arguing for better food on the trains, on grounds of efficiency and of the paperwork it would save. He has no animus against the people he is shipping; when pressed he says he has never met one. His deputy is the opposite — he volunteers for the work, stays late, and says things in the canteen that make it clear he enjoys it. The legal system of the country I've invented convicts them of the same offence, but it also holds a second hearing to determine whether the defendant acted from conviction or from mere conformity, and it sentences the conformist more severely, on the stated ground that the man who does this without hating anyone is the more reliable instrument and therefore the more dangerous one. I didn't design that second hearing to be philosophically loaded; it simply arrived while I was drafting. But the more I try to write the prosecutor's closing argument, the more I realise I don't know what the institution is committed to.

So here is what I want from you. Take two features of real legal practice as I understand them, and reconstruct the implicit theory of culpability they commit us to. First: in ordinary criminal law, motive is said to be irrelevant to conviction while intention is central — a killing done for a pitiful reason is still a killing — and yet the same systems enhance sentences for offences motivated by hostility toward a group, which seems to presuppose that a worse will makes for a worse act. Second: the doctrine of crimes against humanity, and the famous trial that Arendt covered, treat obedience to superior orders and the absence of personal hatred as no defence at all; and the well-known reading of that defendant treats the salient thing about him as precisely the absence of anything recognisable as a malignant will — an emptiness, a reliance on stock phrases, an inability to consider the matter from anyone else's standpoint — so that what is punished looks less like a bad maxim than like a failure to have any maxim at all. Is that one theory or several stacked on top of each other? Is the thoughtlessness reading genuinely in tension with the hate-crime enhancement, or can a single account of blameworthiness generate both — and if so, how exactly does it get from a failure of attention to a harsher sentence?

I'd also like the reconstruction separated out by strength. Which parts of the pattern does the practice strictly presuppose, in the sense that the verdicts and sentences would be unintelligible without them; which parts does it merely sit comfortably with, such that some rival theory of responsibility would license exactly the same outcomes; and where is the practice simply underdetermined, so that the law could go either way without inconsistency? Finally, the practical half, which is the part I actually need for the book: if my fictional jurisdiction really meant what its second hearing says, what would have to change in its procedure that it currently doesn't do — in its mental-element requirements, in what counts as evidence of a state of mind, in the way it questions defendants about what they were thinking or failing to think, in the defences it would have to abandon? And symmetrically, if it abandoned the thoughtlessness claim and treated the enthusiastic deputy as straightforwardly the worse man, what would it lose? I'm not asking for sources or suggestions of further reading; I just want the implicit theory made explicit, its weak joints marked, and the consequences for courtroom procedure spelled out in enough concrete detail that I can put it into a prosecutor's mouth.
