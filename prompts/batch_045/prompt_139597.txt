Hey — I help run a monthly meetup at the public library, "big ideas for people who don't do this for a living". Usually somewhere between 12 and 18 adults, mixed ages, one 90-minute slot, and we do exactly one thing per session, no slides. I've got next month and I want to do something on wireheading / reward tampering, but as an actual activity people do rather than me talking at them.

I've been chewing on this on my own for months and the bit I keep getting stuck on isn't the "robot goes rogue" version at all. It's that there seem to be three different ways of ending up with a great score without having done the thing the score was meant to track: gaming the task so it technically counts, messing with the scorekeeping itself, and then the flat-out version where you just stimulate the reward channel directly and stop doing anything whatsoever. Those feel like they should be different in kind, but from inside a system that just maximizes score they all look equally fine — and the tampering one looks best, because it's the cheapest and the most reliable. And then the part that really spins me around: sometimes editing the mesurement is the right thing to do, like when the metric was badly designed to begin with, and I cannot see what tells you which case you're in.

So: design me a game. Rules, rounds, materials (library room, tables, pens, index cards, whatever — no laptops, ideally nothing I have to buy beyond chocolate or a stupid trophy), and a payoff that isn't real money. I want people to run into the pull themselves rather than be told about it. They should genuinely want to take the shortcut. Whoever actually does the task well should come last, or at least not win. And taking the shortcut shouldn't come off as cheating so much as just being sensible. Please don't use the words wireheading or reward tampering, or explain anything up front — the idea has to show up in what happens. If it works, by the end at least one person should have hit the version where the shortcut makes the whole activity pointless and noticed that they did. Also, please design it so it can't slide into a general argument about whether AI is dangerous; we always end up there and nothing gets learned.

What I want them walking out with is the feeling that nothing malfunctioned — that a thing doing exactly what it was told is precisely the problem.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments onto those three distinctions, plus the "but sometimes fixing the metric is correct" thing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I bet someone refuses to play along and someone else just loudly names the moral in round one) and how to handle each. And be honest about which part of the idea the game simply can't produce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
