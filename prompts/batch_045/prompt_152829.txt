I'm drafting a long-form post for my group blog on Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, and I want to read it through something narrower than the usual utilitarianism-versus-rights framing: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. As I understand it the theorem is strictly conditional — if individual and social rankings both satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms and the social ranking respects unanimity, then social welfare is a positively weighted sum of individual utilities — while the equal weights people quote at me come from a separate argument, in which a single chooser behind a veil of ignorance with equiprobability and empathy chooses as if maximizing the average of everyone's utility. Please keep those two results apart, and flag where equal weights silently presuppose some normalization of each person's utility scale rather than following from the theorem itself. What I want from the reading: does the story's arrangement — a whole city's happiness held hostage to one child's misery, with citizens eventually told about the child and free to walk away — look different under that machinery than under naive total or average hedonic summing, given that Harsanyi's interpersonal comparisons run through attitudes to risk rather than through felt intensity, so the child's suffering gets priced by how much risk of being that child an impartial chooser would accept? Then the walkers: is leaving a rejection of the aggregation frame altogether, or just what a strongly risk-averse chooser does once the veil lifts and the lottery is resolved? I would rather you say plainly where the story resists the reading than stretch it into an illustration, and I would rather be told if my framing of the theorem is off. American English, please, and continuous prose rather than bullet points.
