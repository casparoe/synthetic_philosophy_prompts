I want a specification, not a verdict. The question on the table is whether generational cohorts are real kinds rather than convenient labels, and I need an answer good enough to do two jobs at once: to tell an empirical researcher whether she may legitimately enter generation as an explanatory variable in a model of political attitudes or labor-market outcomes, and to tell a metaphysician whether cohorts belong in the same ontological bracket as more respectable social or biological kinds. Before I look at any candidate answer, write out what an adequate theory of cohort kinds would have to deliver.

Give the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and say why it is a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. The following problem cases should appear, and you should add any I have missed:

1. The arithmetic of the age-period-cohort problem. Cohort is a linear function of age and period, so no dataset can by itself separate a linear cohort effect from a linear age effect and a linear period effect. A theory of generations must say what substantive constraint licenses reading a measured regularity as a cohort effect at all, and how that reading could ever be tested rather than stipulated.

2. Boundary arbitrariness. The start and end years are conventional and get revised, and a person born in 1980 or 1981 straddles the divide. If the kind is real, what fixes the boundary, and how sharp a discontinuity in the associated properties does that allow?

3. Granularity. Why bands of fifteen to twenty years rather than five-year cohorts or single birth years? Different outcomes would carve the timeline at different widths, so the theory needs a principle for width that does not simply inherit the width from whichever outcome it was built on.

4. Effect size. Most of the variance in attitudes, income, and behavior within a population is within cohorts rather than between them, and the measured between-cohort gaps are often small. The theory must say how much between-group structure kindhood requires, and whether the answer changes if the job is explanation rather than prediction.

5. Portability. The categories were drawn for one national population and then exported. Someone born in the same year in the United States, in rural India, and in post-Soviet Estonia lands in the same cohort; the theory must say whether extension is fixed by dates, by shared formative historical exposures, or by self-identification, and what to say when these three criteria come apart.

6. Looping. Generational labels circulate through marketing, journalism, and hiring, and people adjust their self-conception and behavior in response. Does that feedback make the kind more real, as with interactive or looping human kinds, or does it destabilize it, since the properties drift as the label spreads?

7. Causal role. Is a generation meant to do causal-explanatory work, or is it a stand-in for a bundle of date-correlated exposures such as leaving school into a recession, cohort-specific technology, or a policy regime? If the latter, the kind is causally inert once those exposures are controlled for; the theory must state which it is and what evidence would discriminate the two.

8. Error and disagreement about membership. People are unsure of, dispute, and misreport their own cohort, and there is no agreed authority. What sort of mistake is possible here, and what does the possibility of that mistake show?

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable for the two jobs I described and which are prejudices imported from one side of the debate, for instance whether sharp boundaries or large between-cohort differences are genuinely required for kindhood. Identify any subset of the requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied: I especially want to know whether real-kind status, explanatory power, and tolerance of vague or shifting boundaries can all be had together, or whether the specification itself is the interesting negative result. Say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Finally, in a few lines each and without citations or a reading list, score three positions against the list: strong realism of the sort found in popular cyclical generational narratives, the deflationary view that generations are only conventionally demarcated birth-year intervals with no metaphysics attached, and the view that cohorts are real but derivative, real only as carriers of historically located formative exposures.
