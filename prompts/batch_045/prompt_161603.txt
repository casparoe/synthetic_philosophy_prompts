The setup: there's a foundation here that hands out a handful of small grants a year — a few thousand quid, six months, one day a week — to people doing independent scholarly work outside universities, and I want to pitch them a project on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. I know the shape of it: three ways of picking a 'random' chord of a circle give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 for the same question, all three are right given their own method, and the usual moral is that 'pick a random chord' was never a well-posed instruction. The responses I've half-read all seem to try to break the tie — insist on the method that respects the symmetries you really care about, or maximise entropy or whatever — and that feels like it dodges the bit I actually find interesting.

My half-formed idea, and this is where I need you: nobody samples from a continuum. Every chord generator you or I could write is finite — floats on a grid, pixels, whatever — so the paradox as a claim about a limit is really a claim about which finite procedures converge to what. Two things I want at the centre:

(i) What happens at finite resolution? Do the three classical methods correspond to three natural finite schemes, do reasonable discretisations converge at all, and do they converge to the same thing? My guess is that plenty don't, and that the paradox survives taking the limit rather than being dissolved by it.

(ii) A distinction between comparative and absolute questions about random chords. 'Is this chord longer than that one?' looks like it comes out 1/2 regardless of scheme, just by symmetry, whereas 'is this chord longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?' is exactly where the 1/3 / 1/2 / 1/4 split appears. So maybe the live question isn't which method is right but: for which questions about random geometric objects does the choice of sampling scheme matter at all, and is there a clean classification (ordinal versus questions that invoke a fixed external reference)? I genuinely can't tell whether that's a theorem waiting to happen or whether five minutes of measure theory kills it. Be honest if it's the second.

Plus a sentence or two on why a foundation should fund an engineer with no philosophy credentials to do this rather than an actual philosopher.
