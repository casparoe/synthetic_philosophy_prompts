I have spent about fifteen years designing and debugging distributed systems: sharding and replication strategies, service decomposition, retry and idempotency semantics, consensus under partial synchrony, and the standing argument about when a problem should be expressed as a stateless fan-out with a cheap reduce and when it needs a long-lived stateful pipeline that refines a single answer over many round trips. That vocabulary is the one I actually think in. I have no formal philosophy or machine-learning background; I read about AI alignment as a hobby.

In that reading I keep meeting a certain ideal, usually written HCH. As I understand it: a question-answering scheme in which a human — or a model trained to imitate one — may answer a question by breaking it into subquestions and delegating those to fresh copies of the very same scheme, recursively; each copy sees only the subquestion it is handed and returns only its answer; nothing learned in one call carries over to any other except through the text that travels down and up the tree; and the scheme is meant to be a fixed point of this amplification, so that adding another layer of delegation would not change what comes out. People invoke it not only as a recipe for building systems but as a picture of what deliberation itself is, as though careful thinking just were this: recursive decomposition into questions small enough to answer, recombination of the answers, with the continuity of a single thinking subject contributing nothing that the tree does not already contain.

I want that picture — HCH as an account of deliberation — restated in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then I want the residue report.

To fix the target vocabulary: recursive call graphs and the depth-versus-width tradeoff; pure functions versus stateful actors; memoization and caching, and what a system loses when it is forbidden to cache; idempotence and retry safety; fan-out/fan-in versus iterative refinement; interface contracts and what may cross a service boundary; the points at which non-derived input enters a system (sensors, user input, configuration) as opposed to what the system computes for itself; cost models, tail latency, and unbounded recursion; Byzantine versus crash fault models; and the difference between a system's returning a value and anyone's standing behind that value.

Please map the following features of the ideal one at a time, and for each say whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no counterpart in anything I know:

1. Self-similarity — every node is the same agent at the same level of capability, so there is no architect outside the system writing the decomposition; the decomposition is produced by the system it decomposes.
2. Statelessness — no node remembers another node's work or its own earlier calls, so everything that carries over must be encoded in the question text handed down and the answer text handed up.
3. The fixed point — amplification is supposed to have converged, so that calling the scheme on a question it has already answered adds nothing.
4. The base case — the recursion has to bottom out somewhere, in a single unaided judgment on a question small enough to answer directly.
5. The costlessness of delegation in the ideal — the tree is not billed for depth, and the idealization seems to say nothing about what deliberation is for or what it ought to cost.
6. The assumption that every copy wants the same thing and reports honestly — no node is strategic, and the question at the root is simply given rather than authored by anyone inside the system.

Two things in particular I want from the residue report.

First, in my world a computation that is a fixed point of its own refinement is either trivially idempotent or already converged, and a system that may not cache is a system deliberately forbidden to learn from its own past. Human deliberation seems to be neither: I can reopen a question I already answered well and land somewhere different precisely because I am no longer the deliberator I was, and a committee that reopens a settled question is not merely repeating work. Say plainly whether this is a limit of my vocabulary — something about agents with histories that functional decomposition genuinely cannot express — or whether it shows that the ideal has defined deliberation as the special case in which reconsideration is uninformative, and so has helped itself to the answer.

Second, there is a distinction I use constantly and cannot locate in the ideal: the difference between a system's returning a value and anyone's endorsing it. My vocabulary has health checks, service-level objectives, error budgets, and on-call ownership for this, but all of them presuppose a specification given from outside and a party who wants the service to work. Tell me what in the picture is meant to play that role, what becomes of it when the root question is itself generated by the same machinery, and whether the residue here indicts my vocabulary or the ideal.

Close by naming the single feature that distorted the most in translation, and by saying whether you think that distortion is a defect of distributed-systems language or a sign that deliberation is doing less work in the idealization than it appears to be doing.
