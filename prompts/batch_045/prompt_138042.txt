My friend Nell has spent the last four years making a variable font — it's called Kestrel, it has a single axis she calls "voice", and it slides every lowercase letter between a monolinear, single-storey skeleton and a high-contrast double-storey form, so the "a" at one end looks like the a you'd scrawl in a notebook and the one at the other end like something chiselled into a monument. The catch — and this is the part nobody at the party agrees about — is that at 50% along the axis you get shapes nobody has ever drawn: the in-between a isn't a blend of two a's, it's a shape with no history at all, not a compromise but a new thing. She's releasing it on Friday at a small party, maybe sixty people: a dozen type designers who will catch every reference to grids, masters, interpolation, the platonic a, glyph-versus-letter, the whole bit; two investors from the foundry; her family; her old calligraphy teacher, who is eighty-six and taught her to write with a broad nib; and a guy from the letterpress shop down the road who mostly wants a free drink. I'm giving the toast, four or five minutes. It can't be a lecture on the metaphysics of letterforms, and it definitely can't be one of those things where I say she finally "got the a right" — she'd hate that, she doesn't think there's any such thing as getting the a right, and I half agree with her, which is why I'm stuck.

Here's the bit I've been chewing on since we argued about it in the pub last month (this is the Letter Spirit question, as our group chat has been calling it ever since somebody brought it up last year: what on earth makes every "a" an "a"?). Nell's view — I've started calling it contrast essentialism, which is my coinage not hers, and she says I've got it slightly wrong — is that the essence of a is not a shape at all but a job: the a of a typeface is whatever glyph occupies the slot marked out by not being the o, not being the d, not being the e, not being the n. So "is this really an a?" always turns into "does it still hold its place in this particular web of distinctions?" I find that attractive and I also think it's too cheap: if the essence is just the role, then presumably almost any blob could be the a provided you redrew the other twenty-five letters to contrast with it, and that seems clearly false — the two-lobed bowl-and-stem thing keeps turning up across a thousand unrelated typefaces and centuries, and a pure slot theory has no room for the fact that it keeps turning up. So: write the toast, aiming for something that works for the designers without losing her mum and the letterpress guy, and don't paper over the disagreement — I'd rather the toast admit it doesn't know than smuggle in a position Nell would reject. Then, after the toast, add a short note (a few lines, not an essay) giving your own verdict on whether there is anything that makes every a an a — shape, role, family resemblance, whatever — naming the one line of the toast that most depends on that verdict, and saying what would change your mind: which argument, or which thing you'd want to look at, would make you go back and rewrite it. Flag as well anywhere you think you've leaned on a contested reading of Nell's idea, since I'd rather cut a line than have the designers roll their eyes at a position I've misdescribed. Typos are fine, keep it warm — she's my friend and its her night.
