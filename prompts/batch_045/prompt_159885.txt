I am a senior and I run my school's philosophy club: 16 to 18 members, one 75-minute after-school block, a projector, a phone speaker, no budget, and no need for anyone to look anything up. I want a session built around the puzzling asymmetry between finding out for yourself that a work is good and being told that it is good by someone perfectly reliable. The claim I want them to feel is not that second-hand reports are unreliable; it is that something is still missing when the report is excellent, the verdict turns out to be right, and you have never met the thing yourself.

What I need is a session in which they collide with that gap rather than have me describe it. A few constraints, which you should treat as design requirements rather than suggestions:

The participants should have to act on testimony first and meet the object only later, and the action should have a consequence they actually live with. My plan is to have the club choose, on the basis of reports alone, the piece of music that will close our last meeting of the year, or the image that becomes the club's page header; tell me which of those works better and why, and how to make the commitment feel real rather than hypothetical.

I want the gap to be measurable, not merely discussed. My idea is to separate two stakes. The first is predictive: a secret ballot on which of three unseen pieces the group as a whole will rate highest once they finally hear them, scored against the group's own later ratings, so that deferring demonstrably pays. The second is endorsive: each member must decide whether to put their name to one sentence defending the winning piece in public, or to stake a song of their own on it, or to defend it against a hostile questioner. The interesting outcome is the person who bets confidently and then will not sign the sentence. If that is the right instrument, tell me how to make the second stake bite without turning into a humiliation ritual; if it is the wrong instrument, give me a better one.

Two kinds of report should be in circulation and should feel different to receive: verdict reports, which say which piece is strongest, and taste-match reports, which say which piece a given member will like given what they liked before. I suspect the second kind goes down much more easily, and I want that difference to show up in what people do, not in what I later point out.

Reports should come from an absent source with genuine authority, so that the reliability of the testimony is not itself in question and nobody in the room has privileged access. No vocabulary up front: they should not hear words like testimony, acquaintance, or aesthetic until the debrief, and there should be no lecture before the activity.

Please give me materials and sourcing, including how to get three short pieces that essentially nobody in the room already knows and how to verify that; the report packets, written so that they are usable verbatim; a minute-by-minute timeline including the reveal; the scoring; and the debrief, mapping specific moments in the session onto the specific parts of the puzzle, and separating the thought that second-hand judgments fail to be knowledge from the thought that they fail to be something you can honestly say or stand behind.

Finally, be frank about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure, and which part of the puzzle the activity simply cannot stage, so that I know what I will have to say myself.
