I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the trials of the defeated German and Japanese leaders after the Second World War, at Nuremberg and Tokyo. It is often said that these were just the winners punishing the losers under rules invented for the occasion, and I don't want to wave that away; but my own reactions, case by case, are messier than either dismissing the trials or endorsing them wholesale. Here they are.

Charging the top leaders with starting aggressive wars: right, even though no individual had ever before been convicted of a crime by that name, and their own legal systems treated the decision to go to war as a sovereign right.

Charging them with the extermination camps: obviously right.

Charging a general who burned villages in reprisal under a written order from above: right.

Charging the clerks and railway officials who moved people to the camps and say they did not know where the trains were going: right for some of them, wrong for others, on grounds I cannot quite state, something about what each was in a position to find out.

Charging a factory director who used deported civilian labour, when every belligerent, including the powers sitting in judgment, used forced labour on a comparable scale: I want to convict him, and I also want to say that a court which convicts him while its own charter forbids it from looking at us is corrupt. I cannot see how both can be true.

Charging officials of the defeated side over a colonial practice that was lawful where they did it, routine in the victors' own empires, and not thought criminal by nearly anyone then alive: wrong, I think. But I cannot see why this is not just the first case again, since aggressive war was routine too and was also claimed as a right.

My tentative principle: such a prosecution is legitimate just when what the defendant did was already wrong by a standard he could have recognised, and when the side doing the prosecuting would accept the same rule applied to itself, as I would accept our own commanders being tried for firebombing cities before a court that could really reach them.

Does that hold together? Where it breaks, which of my verdicts should I give up? And what does the best version of it say about two cases I have not settled: a tribunal created by a treaty signed by winners and losers alike, applying a statute drafted after the fighting ended, and a prosecution brought fifty years later, once the defendants' own country has itself adopted the law it is being tried under?
