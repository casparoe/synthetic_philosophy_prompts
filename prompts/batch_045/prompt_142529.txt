I've been trying to work out my position on the lying/misleading distinction, and before I write it up I'd like an audit of whether the package actually hangs together. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Lying is pro tanto morally worse than merely misleading, holding fixed the speaker's aim (getting the hearer to believe something false), the false belief the hearer in fact forms, and all downstream consequences.

(2) The best explanation of (1), as I see it, is hearer-side and epistemic rather than assertion-theoretical. When you mislead, what you said is true and the truth is recoverable from it by reflection, so you leave the hearer an epistemic resource; when you lie, you leave him nothing to work from. The asymmetry lies in what you leave the hearer, not in what you were trying to do and not in any special norm governing assertion as such.

(3) I also want to say that the identity and normative status of a speech act cannot vary with the contingent psychology of whoever happens to be listening: same sentence, same intentions, same act, whether addressed to someone who reads implicatures fluently or to someone who does not.

(4) I hold that lying to a young child and misleading a young child are equally bad, since a child cannot recover the implicature; the asymmetry I claim in (1) does not show up there.

(5) I hold that a defendant who answers a direct question under oath with a literally true sentence chosen precisely to misdirect is morally on a par with one who answers falsely, even though — as I understand it — the law draws the line at literal falsity and will not treat the evasive answer as perjury.

(6) In the murderer-at-the-door case I hold that you may simply say your friend is not home, and that this is not even pro tanto worse than the evasive alternative; you do not owe the murderer a misleading truth.

Which of these really pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as though they were all equally bad, and for each say what exactly has to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I also want to know whether any single revision would clean up more than one of them, and whether any of the apparent conflicts dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely — I suspect at least one does, but I cannot tell which. This should be an audit of my own set, not a survey of the literature or a set of reading suggestions: argue from the cases and from what each commitment commits me to. Please write in American English.
