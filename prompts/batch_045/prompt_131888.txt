The essay is about whether you can take someone else's word for how good a work of art is. I have reconstructed the two positions myself, so please treat both as my characterization rather than as anyone's official view.

DRAFT: Can You Take Someone's Word for It?

1. Start with an asymmetry. If I say "the train leaves at 9:04" and you ask how I know, "my sister told me" is a perfectly good answer. If I say "that album is a masterpiece" and you ask how I know, "the critic told me" sounds bad in a way the train answer does not — not merely uncertain, but slightly shameful. Call this the asymmetry datum. It is the thing any account has to explain.

2. There seem to be two broad positions. Call the strict one the acquaintance view: testimony cannot put you in a position to judge that something is beautiful, moving, or masterful; the most it can do is get you in front of the work, or tell you that you would probably end up liking it, or point out features you would otherwise miss. Call the permissive one the inheritance view: testimony can in principle justify an aesthetic judgment just as it justifies other beliefs, subject to the usual conditions about reliability, independence, and the absence of defeaters. The acquaintance view explains the shame datum neatly and has trouble with the obvious fact that we rely on critics constantly. The inheritance view has the mirror-image problem.

3. My first claim is that both sides have been looking at the wrong kind of sentence. They both assume that what a critic offers is a verdict: "it's a masterpiece," "it's thin." But a great deal of useful critical speech is not verdict-shaped at all. "Watch the drummer, not the singer." "Read the second chapter twice before you judge the first." "Listen to how the bass comes in a beat late." Call this orientation testimony. It hands you no verdict; it changes what you are able to notice. When it works, the appreciation you end up with is entirely first-hand. So the acquaintance view can accept orientation testimony without strain, and much of what looks like deference to critics is really orientation-seeking: we skip to the passages they name and check for ourselves.

4. My second claim is that even genuine verdicts can be learned from without being inherited. Suppose I have lived with a hundred records that a certain critic has also written about, and my first-hand verdicts have matched hers about nine times in ten. From that track record I can build a kind of mapping, so that her verdict on a new record is evidence for me about a further fact: that I myself will, after some number of listens, judge it good. That is learning from testimony, but it is not deference. I do not believe because she believes; I believe because her verdict is evidence about my own future response. If this is right, the acquaintance view is too strong as usually stated, since testimony really can be evidence for an aesthetic claim, and the inheritance view is right for the wrong reason, since what testimony gives me is not someone else's judgment taken over but information about myself.

5. Here is the part I am least confident about: the shame datum. My account predicts it, or so I want to say, because what we want from a work is first-hand appreciation, and calibration-knowledge openly advertises that I am not speaking from acquaintance. But someone could reply that the shame is explained just as well, or better, by the acquaintance view, on the grounds that I do not really know at all. And there is a third option: the shame might be about a norm of conversation rather than about knowledge — we dislike citing authorities for matters of taste because it carries an implication that we are parroting someone. I am not sure how to decide between these three, and I do not know whether that indecision is a problem for the essay or an honest place to stop.

6. Now the case that made me think the whole debate might be slightly mis-set. Call it the rehearsal case. I play a new record once and think it is shapeless noise. A critic whose taste has matched mine on nearly everything we have both heard says it is the best thing released this year. I keep playing it. Around the tenth listen I hear what she heard, and now I think it is the best thing released this year too. Two things about this are worth noticing. First, my original verdict was as first-hand as a verdict can be and was still wrong by my own later lights, so the acquaintance view cannot say the trouble with inherited verdicts is that they are unreliable. Second, it is natural to describe what happened as: her verdict was addressed to a listener at her stage of readiness, and I had to be brought to that stage before I could occupy it myself. That suggests a thesis I will call readiness-relativity: aesthetic verdicts are implicitly about the work as encountered by a perceiver in a given state of readiness, so a verdict can only be inherited across matched readiness, and calibration is how we estimate whether our readiness matches. On this reading the acquaintance view is best stated as the claim that you cannot inherit a verdict across unmatched readiness — which is very close to something the inheritance view could also accept, and so part of the dispute begins to look verbal. The cost, which worries me, is that readiness-relativity makes it very easy to explain away aesthetic disagreement, and we lose the sense in which two people arguing about a film are arguing about the film.

7. What I think follows: the asymmetry datum is real but is not evidence that testimony is epistemically inert in aesthetics; it is evidence that we want first-hand appreciation and know that calibration-knowledge is a substitute for it. The interesting question is not whether testimony can justify aesthetic belief but what kinds of thing critical speech passes along, and my answer is orientation, calibration, and only then verdicts.

2. Is calibration really not deference? Give the strongest version of the objection that I am still trusting the critic's verdicts, just one step removed.

3. On readiness-relativity: is it a substantive thesis or a cheap way of dissolving the debate? What would the acquaintance theorist say against it, and is there a version of it that is not merely verbal?

4. Paragraph 5 is where I am weakest. Does the shame datum support my account, support the acquaintance view, or support neither? Should I keep it, cut it, or move it?
