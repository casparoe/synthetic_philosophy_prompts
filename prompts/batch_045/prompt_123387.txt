Sorry if this comes out half-baked. I teach early modern metaphysics and I only know the ethics side of the house secondhand, from a reading group a few of us run in my department (we meet in a pub, which may be part of the problem). Every year or so the same pattern comes up, in the readings and in departmental life, and I've started to think its the same pattern, but I can't find a word for it. Three instances. (1) Two colleagues in different departments each push through a big curricular reform against their dean's advice. One looks great two years later because a demographic blip happened to swell enrollments in exactly that area; the other's reform looks like a disaster because the state changed a funding rule halfway through. At the retro the first is described as someone with the courage to take a principled stand, the second as someone who won't listen — but the actual trait, the pig-headedness about the reform, is the same in both, and everyone in the room can see that. (2) Two junior people send near-identical papers to the same top journal. One draws a sympathetic referee and gets it in; the other draws a hostile one, gets desk-rejected, and spends eighteen months revising. Ten years later the promotion files read "publishes in the leading journals" and "aims too high, wastes time" — and here's the bit that interests me most, the second description has by then leaked into wholly unrelated judgments: the same people who say he aims too high also quietly stop trusting him on the budget committee and on hiring. (3) The person who got lucky seems to end up believing the flattering version about herself. A former student of mine took a genuinely reckless gamble on a research program that paid off; she now describes her earlier self as "brave," and that self-description visibly changes what she's willing to try next, so the luck seems to have become a real feature of her practical reasoning.

What I think I'm seeing is two things pasted together, and I can't tell whether they're one thing. First: when the outcome-driven judgment gets challenged, nobody gives it up; instead the voluntary bit of the conduct gets relocated. The act gets described more broadly — not "he drove home that night" but "he decided to go out drinking at all" — or more narrowly, and in the lucky case it shrinks to something like "he got lucky" — and the relocation happens in whichever direction protects the original reaction. Second: the residue then travels sideways into assessments that have nothing to do with the original act at all. It's obviously in the neighbourhood of the famous discussion of the two drivers, one of whom hits a child, but it isn't quite that, because in my cases nobody is disagreeing about how much the agent controlled. The silent shift is about which bit of behaviour we're counting as the thing she did, and about where the resulting judgment lands afterward.

So: does this have a name? And I really don't want a bare label and a reading list — I've had enough of people saying "oh that's just X, go read X." I want the carving. Are the relocation part and the sideways-travel part the same phenomenon, or two that merely travel together? What are the nearest established concepts — I'd guess there's something on the action-theory side about how fine- or coarse-grained a description of an action is, something on the ethics side about luck, and maybe something in psychology about judging by outcomes rather than by decisions — and how does the thing I've described differ from each of them, and which, if any, actually fits? Give me the edge cases too: when is re-describing someone's conduct more broadly perfectly legitimate rather than a symptom of this pattern? My guess is it has something to do with whether the broader description is one the agent would have accepted in advance, but I'm not sure. I'd also like a case where the pattern is absent even though all the surface features are there. And note the asymmetry in my examples — the unflattering version seems to survive being debunked while the flattering one collapses the moment you point at the luck — and tell me whether that asymmetry is essential to the thing or a separate fact about us. If nothing established fits, coin the term and define it tightly enough that I could walk into my department meeting on Monday and actually use it.
