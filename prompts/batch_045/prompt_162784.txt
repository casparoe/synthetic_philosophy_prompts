Hi — I'm a PhD student in political theory (my dissertation is somewhere in the neighbourhood of state responsibility for foreseeable harms, which is apparently why I got drafted onto this thing), and I sit on the emergency-classification panel for a mid-sized humanitarian coalition. We have to decide how to label one unfolding crisis before the appeal goes out next week, and I'm the person who has to write the memo. The case: a real, measurable rainfall failure across the northern highlands of Bhera — three failed seasons, satellite data and the national met office both confirm it, so nobody is disputing that there is a genuine food-availability drop. But the mortality is almost entirely concentrated among transhumant herders in one district, because grain prices roughly tripled while livestock prices collapsed; farming households two valleys over, with the same rainfall failure, are coping. Meanwhile the government kept its grain procurement quota running to honour an export contract, restricted movement of people and animals into the district citing an insurgency in a different province, turned away the journalists who tried to get in, and sat on its own agriculture ministry's early-warning memo for about six weeks. It is also midway through a fiscal programme that caps its spending, and half the cabinet would probably say they were just managing a budget. So: is this a natural disaster, a political crime, or something else, and what do we actually put on the appeal?

The room is split in ways I can't adjudicate by instinct. Our emergencies coordinator wants a 'natural disaster' label because it unlocks the pooled disaster fund, keeps the government cooperative enough to let our convoys through, and donors give generously to droughts; she thinks calling it a crime gets us expelled within a month. Our advocacy director wants 'man-made famine' and an explicit call for accountability, on the grounds that the drought explains a harvest shortfall but not who died, and that if we describe it as weather we launder a set of decisions. Our lawyer notes that 'crime' invites questions we can't answer — who exactly, with what intent, under what jurisdiction — and worries about making claims we'd retract later. Everyone keeps gesturing at the usual examples (Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, the Great Leap Forward), but they're being used as slogans rather than as arguments, and my sense is that this case doesn't map cleanly onto any of them, since the trigger really is climatic even if the mechanism of death looks like an entitlement collapse. I think the thing I most need is a cleaner set of distinctions than the room has: whatever separates the cause of the shortfall from the cause of the mortality, whatever we should be comparing the government to, and whether 'crime' can be made to work when the state's failure looks more like culpable indifference under fiscal constraint than like a plan.

So: draft the committee's decision memo, addressed to the board, that recommends one concrete label and one line to take publicly — with whatever conditions, caveats, or sequencing you think are needed (e.g. disaster framing now, accountability findings later, or vice versa) — and give reasons the people in the room could actually push back on rather than a lecture. Don't give me a bibliography; the board won't read one. Please engage the strongest version of whichever position you're rejecting — I'd guess that's the access-and-funding argument if you go with 'crime', or the laundering/decriminalisation argument if you go with 'disaster' — and be explicit about which factual uncertainties are load-bearing for your recommendation, since we could in principle send someone to find them out before Thursday. And add a short closing section on what would change the memo's mind: what finding about the government's information, intentions, or alternatives would flip the recommendation, and where you think the memo's reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
