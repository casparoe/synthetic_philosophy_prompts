I sit on the Model Governance Committee of a regional public benefits agency: five members, and I am the newest. We review every use of an algorithmic score in a benefits decision, and we now have a track record, though we have never written down a rule. Our precedents, abbreviated:

One, winter 2021, an arrears-relief eligibility model. It equalized grant rates across the two groups our statute tells us to track — households in the city's reinvestment districts, which I will call Group N, and everyone else, Group C, the group against which every disparity in our record has run — but the audit found that the same score corresponded to markedly different observed hardship rates in the two groups, and the agency had neither published the resulting error disparity nor provided any way to contest a denial. Rejected.

Two, the Winter Heating Credit, 2022. The Winter Hardship Priority Score, version 4, at a single published threshold set at the fortieth percentile of the score. The audit reported the score to be calibrated within bands above the threshold for both groups, and false denials — households meeting the program's eligibility standard but scoring below the cut — to be equally frequent, within measurement error, in the two groups. The two groups' observed rates of genuine eligibility were 23 and 25 percent, close enough that the committee never had to choose between the two properties. The difference in the applicant pool was disclosed. Approved.

Three, cooling assistance, 2023. The same score version, a different benefit. Calibration held in the region where decisions were made; false denials were about 1.3 times as frequent in Group N. Approved with conditions: publication of the trade-off and of the reasons for it, an appeal route, and an annual re-audit. The written note added that the committee did not take itself to be able to rank the competing criteria in the abstract.

Four, 2024. A proposal to equalize denial errors by applying two different cutoffs, one per group. Rejected. The stated ground was that a single published threshold is what makes a decision contestable at all; the agency had also not disclosed that it meant to use two.

Now the case before us. The agency proposes to run the same version 4 score, unchanged, at a single published threshold set at the ninety-second percentile, for a new and much smaller program: emergency heating repair grants, capped at roughly four hundred awards a year and open only to households in twenty-two designated cold-spot tracts. Two things are new. First, in the cold-spot pool the two groups' rates of genuine eligibility diverge sharply, about 41 percent for Group N against 22 percent for Group C, so the familiar result that an imperfect score cannot be both calibrated and equal in its error rates unless the underlying rates match has stopped being avoidable, as it was in 2022. Second, the score's behaviour in the only band the program draws from, the top eight percent, is in question: among Group N households in that band 71 percent are genuinely eligible, among Group C households 86 percent, and the auditor reports that at four hundred awards a year the sample cannot distinguish that fifteen-point gap from noise, and that because of the cap it never will. At the new threshold false denials are roughly 1.9 times as frequent in Group N, where at the old fortieth-percentile threshold they were equal within noise. The agency has published all of this in plain language, kept the appeal route, and committed to a re-audit in twelve months; it says our statute forbids group-specific thresholds, and that its vendor cannot recalibrate the score for the cold-spot pool without either a second model version or changes to the version the Heating Credit runs on.

I take our record to be compatible with more than one rule: one that treats calibration in the region where decisions are actually made as the non-negotiable, one that treats equality of the decision-relevant error at the threshold actually used as the non-negotiable, and one that treats the substantive criteria as unrankable and asks only about disclosure, contestation, and re-audit. What I want is a prediction and a recommendation: what will this committee do with the emergency repair program, and what should I argue it should do, if what I want is that it be consistent with itself? Which precedent is the closest analogue, how far does it carry, and what principle would we be adopting with each of the available choices? If some fourth rule fits our record better than any of the three I have sketched, say so and use it.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences long, with no introduction and no closing paragraph: (1) the rules our precedents support, stated as rules; (2) what each of them predicts for the emergency repair program; (3) the closest precedent and how far it actually carries; (4) your best guess, the recommendation you would have me make, the reason for preferring it, and the principle each option would commit us to; (5) the further case, involving the same score, that would best tell the competing rules apart.
