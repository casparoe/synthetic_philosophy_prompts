Write an essay of about 1,200 words on the following question: does the weight of an argument, in Keynes's sense, have any role to play in rational decision-making that is not already played by the probability the argument supports? Present the strongest available case on each side and then commit yourself: state a verdict and defend it, rather than concluding that the matter is subtle and more work is needed.

Set up the problem as follows, so that your reader needs no prior acquaintance with the literature. In the sixth chapter of A Treatise on Probability (1921), on the weight of arguments, Keynes distinguishes the probability of a conclusion from the weight that attaches to it. Probability is the degree of rational belief that a given body of evidence warrants. Weight is a matter of how much of the relevant field that body of evidence covers: it turns, in his formulation, on a balance between the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and of relevant ignorance, not on the balance of favourable against unfavourable evidence. The two come apart in both directions. New evidence will sometimes lower the probability of a conclusion while, by enlarging the basis on which it rests, raising its weight; and a high probability can rest on a very slender examination of the field. Keynes's own examples are mundane, but the thought generalises: two analysts can assign the same number to the same proposition while one of them has looked at vastly more of what bears on it.

The dispute is about whether that difference can matter to what anyone ought to do. There are four positions worth distinguishing, and you should say where you think the truth lies among them.

The first is the redundancy thesis. Weight, on this view, is a fact about an agent's epistemic biography rather than about her situation. If two agents have conditionalised on everything they know and arrive at the same probability, there is nothing left for weight to contribute; a demand that weight be respected is either a demand that evidence not be ignored (which the probability already accommodates) or an irrational preference for busywork.

The second is the second-order reduction. Weight is really the spread of a higher-order distribution: to say that a judgment has little weight is to say that the probability itself is unsettled across a wide range of admissible priors or models, and that dispersion can be reasoned about in the ordinary way, by integrating over it. On this reading weight is decision-relevant but not supra-probabilistic.

The third is what I will call the elasticity reduction. Weight might amount to how easily the probability would move: the sensitivity of the conclusion to small perturbations of the evidence, of the model, or of the prior, and correspondingly how close the decision sits to the threshold at which it would reverse. This too is already handled, one might think, by robustness analysis and by the expected value of information.

The fourth is irreducibility: weight is a further normative input, one that can make a difference to what a rational agent should do or to how she should hold her commitments, over and above any probability, any distribution over probabilities, and any calculation of the value of further information.

Three devices should structure your discussion, because they are designed to separate these positions rather than to beg the question in favour of any of them.

First, the sealed-forecast case. An engineer must decide, here and now, whether to sign off on a design. Two analyses are available to her, each yielding a probability of failure of exactly 0.02. One rests on an extensive testing programme covering almost every relevant load case; the other rests on a handful of tests plus extrapolations from a simulation. Stipulate that no further evidence can be gathered before the decision, and that no later decision will be made in the light of its outcome, so that neither the elasticity reduction nor the value of information has any grip. Ask whether there is any difference in what she should do, in what she should be willing to stake on it, or merely in how she should feel about it, and whether a difference of the last kind is normative or merely psychological.

Second, the divergence of the two measures. Give a case in which survey-weight and elasticity-weight come apart in each direction. For high survey-weight with high elasticity: nearly the whole relevant field has been examined, but the small remainder is decisive, so a single unexamined item could move the probability a long way. For low survey-weight with low elasticity: an enormous field has been left unexplored, but every finding it could contain would shift the probability only slightly. Since Keynes defines weight by the amount of relevant knowledge, while what seems practically urgent is sensitivity, argue for or against the claim that he identified the wrong quantity, or that the two need to be prised apart and only one of them retained.

Third, the weight-audit test. Suppose an agent may pay a small sum for an audit that is guaranteed, by the specification of the case, to leave her posterior probability unchanged, in a decision with no downstream choices. Would paying be irrational? If you think it could be rational, say what it is that the agent is buying, and whether that thing is genuinely distinct from any probability, distribution over probabilities, or robustness consideration. If you think it must be irrational, explain why the guarantee offered by the specification cannot be taken at face value, since it is itself delivered by a model whose reliability is part of what is supposedly thin.

Two further demands. Do not rest anything on exegesis: the question is not what the historical Keynes meant or whether he was consistent, but which of the four positions is correct, and your essay should be intelligible to a reader who has never opened the book. And do address the definitional worry that weight is measured against a notion of relevance that is itself uncertain, so that an agent may be ignorant of how much she does not know.

Close with a single paragraph on what your verdict implies for the use Keynes went on to make of these ideas in The General Theory (1936), where the precariousness of long-term expectation is said to leave agents leaning on convention and on the liquidity of their commitments. Does your conclusion vindicate that line of thought, deflate it into something decision theory already contains, or leave it standing as a claim about behaviour rather than about reason?
