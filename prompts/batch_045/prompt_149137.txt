I am working through Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz’s *Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz* (written in March 1691 and first published in 1700), and there is a passage whose argumentative logic I am struggling to reconstruct. The passage is often read as part of Sor Juana’s defense of women’s education, but on its face it seems to concede the opposite: that a woman’s learning is superfluous, even harmful, and that the appropriate response is to hide or extinguish one’s understanding. The original Spanish text of the passage runs as follows:

"El escribir nunca ha sido dictamen propio, sino fuerza ajena; que les pudiera decir con verdad: Vos me coegistis. Lo que sí es verdad que no negaré (lo uno porque es notorio a todos, y lo otro porque, aunque sea contra mí, me ha hecho Dios la merced de darme grandísimo amor a la verdad) que desde que me rayó la primera luz de la razón, fue tan vehemente y poderosa la inclinación a las letras, que ni ajenas reprensiones --que he tenido muchas--, ni propias reflejas --que he hecho no pocas--, han bastado a que deje de seguir este natural impulso que Dios puso en mí: Su Majestad sabe por qué y para qué; y sabe que le he pedido que apague la luz de mi entendimiento dejando sólo lo que baste para guardar su Ley, pues lo demás sobra, según algunos, en una mujer; y aún hay quien diga que daña. Sabe también Su Majestad que no consiguiendo esto, he intentado sepultar con mi nombre mi entendimiento, y sacrificársele sólo a quien me le dio; y que no otro motivo me entró en religión, no obstante que al desembarazo y quietud que pedía mi estudiosa intención eran repugnantes los ejercicios y compañía de una comunidad; y después, en ella, sabe el Señor, y lo sabe en el mundo quien sólo lo debió saber, lo que intenté en orden a esconder mi nombre, y que no me lo permitió, diciendo que era tentación; y sí sería."

For orientation, a loose English paraphrase (not a translation, and not to be quoted) is that Sor Juana says writing has never been her own judgment but an external force—she could tell them with truth, *Vos me coegistis*, you compelled me—that from the first light of reason she has had an overwhelming, divinely given inclination to letters that neither others’ reproofs nor her own reflections have overcome; that she has asked God to extinguish the light of her understanding, leaving only what is needed to keep His Law, since others say the rest is superfluous in a woman and some say it is harmful; that, not obtaining this, she entered religion in order to bury her understanding and hide her name, even though the exercises and company of a community were repugnant to the freedom and quiet her studious intention required; and that her attempt to hide her name was not permitted, being called temptation—and so, she implies, it would have been.

My difficulty is understanding how this apparently self-suppressing narrative functions as an argument for women’s education rather than as a capitulation to her critics. Please help me unpack the logic by addressing the following crux. When Sor Juana says she has asked God to "apague la luz de mi entendimiento" and reports the view that the rest "sobra, según algunos, en una mujer; y aún hay quien diga que daña," is she endorsing that assessment, or is she reporting it as the opinion of her opponents in order to expose its cost? How does her insistence that the inclination to letters is a "natural impulso que Dios puso en mí" bear on that question? And what work is done by the closing detail—that she entered religion in order to "esconder mi nombre," but was refused because it would be "tentación"—in turning a story of attempted self-erasure into a claim that women’s learning cannot be licitly suppressed?

To make this more precise, I have been playing with two interpretive hypotheses, and I would like your assessment of them (or a better alternative). First: perhaps Sor Juana is distinguishing between the private, God-given desire for knowledge and the public act of writing, which she frames as externally compelled ("fuerza ajena" and "Vos me coegistis"). On this reading she can grant that further learning is not necessary for salvation while still insisting that the desire for it is from God and therefore not to be extinguished. Second, and more speculatively: perhaps her prayer to have her understanding extinguished is itself an instance of the "tentación" named at the end—an attempt to bury a divinely given talent—so that the failure of that prayer and the refusal of her wish to hide her name together imply that suppressing women’s learning is spiritually illicit rather than praiseworthy. I am unsure whether either reading is textually responsible, and I especially want to know how to read the relation between "fuerza ajena" / "Vos me coegistis" and the claim that the inclination is a "natural impulso que Dios puso en mí." Please go through the relevant clauses and explain which interpretive moves the Spanish syntax and rhetoric support, which they do not, and what, if anything, both of my hypotheses are missing.
