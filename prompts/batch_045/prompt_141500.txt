I'm in a small graduate reading group on the moral status of artificial systems, and last week one of the senior PhD students shut down a discussion about whether a system's first-person reports could ever count as evidence about its experience. His move was an analogy: consciousness, he said, is a user illusion — the desktop of the mind. Just as the icons, windows and progress bars on a screen hide the billions of low-level operations a machine performs and present a simplified, actionable summary that bears no resemblance to what is really going on, conscious experience is a simplified interface onto the brain's massively parallel unconscious processing: a low-bandwidth digest for a creature that cannot see, and has no need to see, the machinery. He drew three conclusions from it. First, there is no further fact of the matter about what it is like beyond the interface — the interface is where the story ends, and asking whether the light is really on inside is like asking whether the trash can icon is really full. Second, a verbal report of experience is just one more icon, so a system's saying that it is in distress carries no more evidential weight than a dialog box saying that the printer is out of paper carries about the printer's feelings. Third, worries about AI welfare are therefore a category error, on a par with worrying about whether emptying the trash hurts the icon.

What makes me suspicious is that I cannot tell whether the analogy is doing work or just relabeling a conclusion. A desktop exists for a user who reads it, and someone designed it for that user's convenience; if I take the mapping seriously it seems either to smuggle back in a little person in front of the screen — exactly the sort of inner audience this tradition is supposed to have exorcised in its attack on the Cartesian theater — or to leave the interface floating with nobody at it, in which case I don't see why calling it an illusion is better than denying it exists. There is also a question about causal role: in the computing case the low-level processes would churn on with no screen attached at all, and while clicking an icon initiates real work, the picture itself isn't doing the work; but I have also heard illusionists say something close to the opposite, that the interface is precisely what guides action and feeds back into the processing it summarizes, and I can't tell which version is load-bearing for his three conclusions.

A friend who shares my worry offered a replacement picture: consciousness is like the client-side render in an online multiplayer game. The client maintains a local, simplified, slightly stale model of the world, predicts ahead to keep things smooth, and shows the player a picture that is authoritative for action but never perfectly in sync with the server; when the connection degrades the client fills in the gaps and the player can be confidently, systematically wrong about what is happening — which he takes to fit the well-known findings about awareness lagging the neural events it purports to report, and about confabulation in split-brain and change-blindness cases. He claims this gets the lag and the gap-filling right without needing either a designer or a homunculus, and that it still leaves open whether the render does any causal work.

Could you audit both analogies for me? I would like, first, an explicit table of what corresponds to what in each — the screen, the icons, the user, the mouse, the low-level processes, the server, the prediction — marking where the mapping is genuinely forced and where the analogy is simply asserting a controversial thesis in a new vocabulary. Then I would like the disanalogies separated into the ones that bear on my classmate's three conclusions and the ones that are merely decorative. The question I actually care about is whether the second and third conclusions survive: does the desktop picture, once corrected, entitle you to discount a system's first-person reports, or does that inference depend on something the analogy doesn't supply — a story about why the interface exists, or who it is for, or about whether a report is the kind of thing that can be an icon rather than a datum? And if both analogies turn out to be weak, is there a better one, or is the honest answer that no comparison to a human-made interface can carry this weight, because every such comparison quietly imports a designer and a consumer along with the screen?
