So I've been arguing with a friend about heterophenomenology — the Dennett-style method where you treat a subject's reports as a text to be interpreted, suspend judgement on whether those reports are authoritative, and build up a description of the subject's heterophenomenological world without ever helping yourself to first-person authority. She says its just behaviourism with extra steps. I've been defending it, and my defense has converged on one load-bearing claim that I'm much less sure about than the rest of it, so I want to poke at that claim specifically rather than at the method in general.

Call it the segmentation claim: you can decide what counts as a *report* — as opposed to a bit of task performance, a guess, a confabulation, a memory-driven reconstruction, or an artifact of how the question was framed — without first committing to a theory of what reports are about. Concretely: suppose a subject in an attentional blink or change blindness setup says different things depending on whether you ask a yes/no question, ask for a 1-to-7 vividness rating, or force a choice between two displays. I want to say the method can triangulate across those formats, weigh reaction times and confidence ratings and metacognitive sensitivity, and settle on a description of what the subject took themselves to experience, all while staying neutral on whether that description is really a description of experiences or only of judgements about experiences. If segmentation fails, then the whole method looks to me like it's smuggling in a substantive theory of consciousness under cover of a claim about method, and my friend is basically right.

So: what would count against the segmentation claim? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a heterophenomenologist would themselves concede from the ones they'd swat away as resting on a conflation of method and metaphysics. Things I'm already chewing on, tell me if they're the good ones:

- the regress worry: fixing what a report is seems to require assumptions about the etiology of the report (that it was produced by the thing it reports on, not by guessing, memory, demand characteristics, whatever), and those assumptions look theoretically loaded in exactly the way the method promised not to be. Is that a real regress, or just the ordinary situation of any measurement procedure?
- whether there's a version of the segmentation claim that's trivial (reports are whatever the subject says; no problem, nothing loaded) and a version that's substantive (reports can be validated as reports *of* something), and whether the method needs the second while officially only claiming the first. If so, is that a fatal gap or a healthy division of labour?
- the silence cases: subjects with absent or degraded report dispositions — infants, non-human animals, blindsight-ish cases, dreamless sleep, anaesthesia, split-brain patients. Does the method's having nothing to say there count against segmentation, or only delimit its scope?
- the LLM case, which is the one I keep coming back to: a system that produces fluent, internally consistent, cross-format-stable first-person reports, where we have no independent grip on the mechanism producing them — or we have total grip, and it isn't the kind of mechanism any theory of reporting would recognise. Does the method's inability or refusal to adjudicate there count against it, or is that a case where it correctly declines to answer?

Also the meta-question I actually care about most: is the segmentation claim, or my defense of heterophenomenology generally, in danger of being qualified into unfalsifiability — e.g. by replying that if triangulation across formats can't settle it, then there's just nothing determinate to settle — and if it is, is that definately a problem for a thesis about *method* in the way it would be for an empirical thesis? I'd personally find one concrete case where the method's own rules deliver a verdict that's demonstrably the wrong verdict about the thing we care about more persuasive than any amount of regress-spotting.

Last thing: tell me what would change your mind about the ranking you give, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to be going wrong. I'd rather have the weak point flagged than a tidy answer.
