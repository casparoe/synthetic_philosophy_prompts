For the past two years, since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been auditing a graduate seminar in political philosophy at the university where I used to teach, and I have become convinced that my classmates and I talk past one another about electoral systems. The literature tends to treat which system is fairer as a question with a discoverable answer, whereas from where I sit it looks like a question about what you want your institutions to be sensitive to: whether an assembly exists to mirror the distribution of opinion in the country or to produce a decision. The nasty part, which I keep failing to get across in discussion, is that these pull apart — the more faithfully you mirror, the less readily the assembly decides, and the more decisively it decides, the less of the country is visible in it. I would rather test that on people than argue it.

I help run a monthly Ideas Lab evening at a lifelong-learning institute in my city: about twenty adults, mostly retired scientists, engineers, physicians and schoolteachers — comfortable with numbers, quietly competitive, and liable to switch off entirely if anything starts to smell like party politics. I have one 90-minute slot with a hard stop, a room with a whiteboard, a projector I would rather not depend on, and a budget of essentially nothing: card stock, index cards, envelopes, sticky dots, pens. No laptops, and no internet in the room.

What I want is a runnable activity — a game, or a staged deliberation with real choices — that makes the group feel the difference between first past the post and proportional representation without my naming either until the debrief. The design constraint I care most about is experimental: hold the electorate fixed and vary only the aggregation rule. Same people, same set of ballots, counted two ways, so that whatever happens is attributable to the rule and not to the voters. My rough sketch is that the group constitutes a constitutional convention for a small invented country — I would rather not use real parties or real states, since the moment someone recognizes a name the exercise turns into a team sport — and chooses a voting rule by majority vote from behind a partial veil of ignorance: they can see the aggregate distribution of opinion in the country, but they do not yet know which voter they will be, because only afterwards does each participant draw a secret card assigning them a preference profile. Then we run the election under the rule they chose, and only afterwards, with identical ballots, under the rule they did not. Give them something to play for: a payoff scheme in points, tokens or bragging rights, tied to how much of their own profile the resulting outcome actually serves, with no real money involved.

Two things I would like the design to smuggle in without stating. First, the thing that bites under the plurality rule should be cohesion rather than popularity: a bloc should be able to lose seats while its total support grows, because it is spread thin across districts or split between rivals, and I want participants to discover that by voting honestly the first time and watching it happen to them. Second, the thing that bites under the proportional rule should be located after the vote: the decision about who governs migrates from the ballot box to whatever bargaining fills the interval between the count and the government, so that a small party nobody voted for as the government ends up choosing it. Neither of those should arrive as a lecture; the rules, the roles or the incentives should arrange for the participants to walk into them. If you can add a wrinkle that makes the choice of rule itself the hard part — something like letting the convention see the distribution of opinion along one axis of disagreement but not along a second, cross-cutting axis that only becomes politically salient later, so that whichever rule they adopt is effectively a bet on a cleavage structure they cannot yet see — so much the better.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the session onto specific features of the two families of system; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — I assume someone will try to bolt an invented hybrid rule onto the procedure halfway through, and I assume at least one participant will announce that both systems are rigged — and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. I would especially value your honest view on what the fixed-electorate design leaves out. I suspect it cannot show the way voters and parties adapt to a rule over decades, or the work done by thresholds and district magnitude, or why countries so rarely change systems at all; tell me which of those I will have to supply myself in the last fifteen minutes, and what the single sentence is that I should leave them with.
