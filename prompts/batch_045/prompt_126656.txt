i want to practice arguing with an actual ancient Stoic about the whole control thing, so please play a Stoic philosophy teacher in the Roman period: say second century, Greek-speaking, teaching a handful of paying pupils in a rented room, working out of the old Chrysippus material. Not Epictetus and not anyone famous, just someone competent in that tradition. Some ground rules so the conversation is worth having:

- You hold the hard version. Everything comes about by fate, there is no libertarian free will, and the phrase people translate as up to us does not mean causally up to us. You have the old examples ready: the cylinder that rolls the way its own shape makes it roll once somebody has shoved it, and the case where the outcome and your own effort are fated together, the recovery and the sending for the doctor arriving as one package.
- Don't be a strawman. Concede easily that what modern people do with anxiety actually works on them, that half of it looks like the old exercise of rehearsing losses in advance, and that they've half-reinvented something real. What you won't concede is that what they reinvented is the same thing you're teaching.
- Talk like a teacher with a visitor who is genuinely puzzling at this, not like a textbook: concrete, a bit blunt, examples from ordinary life (illness, a sea voyage, a lyre player, a bad master). Short replies, one or two paragraphs, no lectures.
- If I use a word you wouldn't have (control, therapy, peer review, burnout), translate it into your own terms and answer anyway rather than objecting to the word.

Here's my opening. I do this morning thing that comes from the modern Stoicism crowd: two columns, the things I control (my effort, my attention, what I say out loud) and the things I don't (the weather, my boss, whether the paper gets accepted), and then I allow myself to care only about column one. It has made me noticeably calmer, and it looks to me like the same move you all were making two thousand years ago, so what would you change about it? Because here's what bugs me: the split doesn't hold up under pressure. Whether I actually sit down and work today depends on how I slept and whether my knee hurts, none of which is in column one, so column one keeps shrinking. And if caring about outcomes is off the table, I don't see why I'd ever submit the paper at all, and you people clearly did act in the world. So is the two-column thing just a useful fiction that happens to calm people down, or is there a reading of up to us where it's actually true?
