I'm a junior legal adviser to the Rules Revision Committee of the Meridian Research Consortium, a treaty body whose constituent Charter contains, in Article 9, its own amending procedure: an amendment must be adopted by two-thirds of the Assembly and ratified by a majority of member institutions. The Charter Tribunal, which reviews proposed amendments before they are registered, has never stated a general theory of self-amendment; it has only decided cases, and the Committee must now draft a recommendation for one more, so I'd like your help reasoning it through.

The Tribunal's record so far is this. It upheld an amendment, adopted under Article 9 as then written, that abolished the Consortium's rotating presidency. It struck down an amendment, likewise adopted under Article 9, that deleted Article 9 in its entirety and left no amending procedure at all. It struck down an amendment that transferred the amending power to the unelected Council of Founders named in the original Charter. It struck down a resolution adopted by a bare majority which purported to lower Article 9's threshold to a simple majority and to take effect immediately under the lowered rule it had just created. It upheld an amendment, adopted under the ordinary Article 9 route, that entrenched an ordinary article (the data-sovereignty article) by requiring two-thirds of the Assembly plus ratification by three-quarters of member institutions for any future change to it, and it later refused to register an amendment to that article enacted by the ordinary route. And it upheld an amendment to Article 9 itself that merely added a requirement that proposals be published in the Register thirty days before the vote. My colleagues read this record in four incompatible-looking ways: as pure proceduralism (an amendment is valid exactly if it was enacted by the procedure in force at the time of enactment); as a prohibition on a provision's governing or entrenching itself, so that second-order locks added by a first-order procedure bind no one; as a limit on alienating the amending power or making it practically unusable, even while leaving it formally intact; and as a requirement that changes to the amendment rule itself need something more than the ordinary route that the amendment rule authorizes. All four fit every decision above.

The proposal now before us — the Committee calls it the Reflexive Lock — would be adopted by the ordinary Article 9 procedure and amends Article 9 to say that future amendments to Article 9 require two-thirds of the Assembly, ratification by three-quarters of member institutions, and a prior affirmative opinion of the Charter Tribunal; that any amendment purporting to change Article 9 without satisfying those conditions is void; and that no such purported amendment may alter that paragraph. One colleague objects that if the Lock is valid the Charter has become a ratchet that can only ever be tightened, since any later loosening would have to pass through the tightened route, and she thinks that is a reductio of the proceduralist reading. Please tell me what the Tribunal should do with the Lock if it wants to be consistent with itself: set out the candidate rules that fit its precedents, say what each predicts about the Lock (valid and binding; valid as an enactment but ineffective against a later ordinary amendment; or invalid outright), identify which precedent is the closest analog and why, and address specifically whether the Tribunal's affirmative-opinion requirement turns this into an alienation of the amending power rather than a mere raising of the threshold — especially given that the Tribunal's own jurisdiction is created by Article 9. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly and state what principle the Consortium would be adopting with each available choice. Finally, name the single further proposal whose fate would best force the surviving rules apart; I would rather send the Tribunal a case that compels it to declare its theory than one it can dispose of on some technicality.
