Short version: I have to give a seven-minute speech in three weeks and I've managed to make the whole thing impossible.

Long version: our town's little historical society museum has exactly one painting anybody's ever heard of, a small night landscape that has hung since 1963 with a brass plaque reading "Adriaen Sonnevelt, 1609-1671" -- a minor Dutch painter nobody outside the field has heard of, but the museum has been calling it "our Sonnevelt" for sixty years, the selectmen put it on the tourism brochure, and it was given by my great-aunt Hilda, who bought it at an auction in 1963 for about six hundred dollars and hung it in her front room and told everybody it was a Sonnevelt. It was the one valuable thing in the family.

Last year I was on the museum board and I pushed hard for a technical exam -- we were applying for a grant to catalogue the collection and I honestly thought the exam would upgrade the attribution. Instead: the oak panel dendro-dates to the 1680s, which is after Sonnevelt died in 1671; the underdrawing is squared up for transfer from another composition, so it's a copy and not an invention; and the signature is in a pigment that didn't exist until the 1800s, so somebody added it later. The 1901 dealer's certificate is on real letterhead and, from what the archive shows, the dealer seems to have believed it. So far as anyone can tell, nobody ever actually forged anything. It looks like an honest late-17th-century workshop copy that acquired a signature and a confident name somewhere around 1900 and then just kept going.

The occasion: the picture is coming back from conservation and the museum is doing a little re-hanging ceremony in its one gallery. In the room: my mother (Hilda's daughter, who thinks I personally took her mother's painting away, and who has told me she'll walk out if I say the words fake, forgery or copy out loud), my cousin who wants me to say "it's still the same picture that hung over Hilda's sofa," the conservator who did the exam and whom half the family blames, the board, the selectman who wants to keep the brochure as it is, a reporter from the weekly paper, and a room of townspeople who mostly just like the picture.

Two more things. When the backing board came off, there was a note in Hilda's hand taped to the back of the panel: "For the town, so that everybody can see something beautiful." And the conservator, trying to be kind, keeps saying the picture is now "an authentic document of a mistake" -- a genuine artifact of having been loved under the wrong name. I can't tell whether that's the true and generous thing to say or just consolation talk that will read as condescension from a podium, and my mother would hear either version as "so it's worthless."

I need the speech, and the philosophical mess has to do the real work in it rather than decorate it. I don't believe a good copy is as good as the original -- I'm not going to stand up there and say that, and my mother would hear it as me calling her mother a fool for caring. But it's the same object it was last year: the panel really is old, the picture really is 17th-century, Hilda really did love it and really did give it, and by her own note she seems not to have been in it for the name. So what did I actually take away from her, if anything? Was she deceived for thirty years -- did she ever have the thing she thought she had, and does it matter that she may not have wanted that thing anyway? Is an object that nobody ever forged a forgery? And is the sixty years of being mis-attributed now part of what the object is, or just a story we tell so we don't have to feel bad?

I also have to announce what goes on the wall: the old plaque, a new label, or both. I've been leaning toward both, but I don't want it to land as splitting the difference, and I need the room to hear it as honouring Hilda. Six or seven minutes, plain language, no art-history jargon, definately no explaining decision theory or anything -- and I cant say "fake," and I also can't let "our Sonnevelt" stay on the brochure. Write it.
