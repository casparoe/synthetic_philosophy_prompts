I am assembling a problem set for a first-year seminar on induction and concept learning, and one of the exercises needs to be a Bongard problem of a rather particular kind. For readers who have not met them: a Bongard problem shows twelve small line drawings, six in a left panel and six in a right panel, where every figure on the left has some property and every figure on the right lacks it, and the solver has to induce the distinguishing rule from those twelve examples alone, with no hint about what a rule is allowed to look like. Since the surrounding readings are on the underdetermination of theory by evidence and on projectibility, I do not want a problem that is merely hard or cute; I want one that makes the philosophical point out of its own materials. A friend of mine claims that whenever two candidate rules fit a Bongard problem equally well, simplicity will always separate them, and I suspect that is only true if you rig the simplicity measure — so I want a case where it plainly is not true.

So here is the design task. Give me a Bongard problem specified entirely in text — each figure in one sentence or in a compact notation of your own devising, unambiguous enough that a student could redraw it — built from a deliberately narrow vocabulary: figures made only of circles, squares and triangles, each filled or outline, each small or large, with relations restricted to containment, touching, and disjointness, and no more than three elements in any figure. Your problem must satisfy all of the following simultaneously: there is an intended rule that a human solver would hit on quickly and feel confident about; there is also a second, "shadow" rule that classifies all twelve figures correctly yet has a genuinely different extension over the space of figures the vocabulary permits, where the shadow rule is not a mere rewording of the intended one and is not allowed to cheat by tacking on anything indexical such as "and not in the right-hand panel", by enumerating the figures, or by disjoining over an arbitrary catalogue; and by some measure of simplicity you state explicitly — number of feature-predicates cited, length of canonical statement, depth of embedding, whatever you pick — the two rules come out equally simple. The shadow rule should also not be one a solver would immediately reject as cooked up: within the sample it must look at least as well supported as the intended rule, and the place where it goes wrong must be invisible from the twelve figures alone.

Then show that the design works and be honest about what is holding it up. Run all twelve figures through each rule so I can see both classifications agree throughout; exhibit at least one discriminating figure — a figure permitted by the vocabulary on which the two rules disagree — and say which panel it belongs in under each, since this is what proves the rules diverge rather than coincide; identify the single feature of your construction that is doing the work, together with what happens to the case if that feature is removed, for instance if the shadow rule cited the same kind of property as the intended one instead of a different kind; and finally write the paragraph that a philosopher defending the shadow rule would write — on what view of induction, simplicity, or naturalness the intended rule deserves no privilege, and what would actually have to be added to the problem, a thirteenth figure, a restriction on admissible predicates, a prior over rules, to force the intended answer. I am not interested in a case that stipulates its verdicts: both rules have to earn their fit with the data, and I want to know which one, if either, is rationally preferable, and what that judgement rests on.
