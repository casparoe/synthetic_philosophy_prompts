I'm putting together a handout for a first-year ethics tutorial on hedonism, and the section I keep failing to write is the one on the heterogeneity objection: the worry that pleasures are too motley a collection to be what a theory of welfare is about, because there is nothing, phenomenologically speaking, that the taste of a mango, the satisfaction of finishing a proof, the relief of sitting down after a long walk, and the absorption of being lost in a piece of music all have in common. The examples that get wheeled out for this are always garbled, and they let the defender off far too easily, so I want a purpose-built case rather than a borrowed one. No citations or further reading are needed for this — I know the literature well enough to place whatever you give me; what I need is the example itself, engineered properly.

So: design me three short episodes from a single ordinary evening in one person's life, built to the following specifications. (a) Episodes A and B must both count as pleasures by any ordinary standard, with no exotica — no drugs, no electrodes or brain stimulation, no wireheading, no science fiction — and, most importantly, they should be drawn from the *same* activity and, as far as possible, the same sensory modality: two ways of listening to music, two ways of eating the same meal, two ways of walking home. That clause matters more than the others, because the standard examples pair a taste with a sound with an intellectual satisfaction, and then the defender simply says that of course they feel different — one is gustatory, one is auditory — while the shared hedonic tone sits underneath both. (b) They should be matched for intensity and duration: the same person has had both, judges them equally pleasant, and would not trade one for the other for a given stretch of the evening, so that nothing in the argument is really being driven by an intensity or length difference. (c) They should come apart along more than one phenomenal axis, and I don't want the contrast to be exhausted by the calm-versus-excited or arousal dimension, since "pleasantness is one dimension and arousal is another" is the cheap way to absorb the case and I want that route closed. (d) The intended result for the pair is that a common-felt-quality or hedonic-tone account of what pleasures share fails, while a conative or attitude account succeeds — the thought being that each experience is one the subject is disposed to sustain while it lasts, to return to, to count as making their life go better, roughly in the spirit of the old "apprehended as desirable" formula. But that success has to fall out of the detail you build into the episodes, not out of the narrator telling us that the subject's dispositions are the same; if you find yourself having to stipulate the verdict, the case is a bad one. (e) Then episode C, from the same evening, must not be a pleasure, or at least not be pleasant, while sharing with A and B exactly the conative or attitudinal profile that carried stage two. I first tried to build C out of compulsive late-night phone-scrolling, and it felt weak to me, because there the subject's negative attitude seems to be a reflective judgement about the habit rather than anything about how the scrolling feels; tell me whether that diagnosis is right and either repair that case or replace it with a better one.

After the scenario — which should stay short, no more than a page in total and no more than a paragraph per episode — I want the check run properly. For each of the candidate unifiers, state the verdict on A and B, the verdict once C is added, and why: the common phenomenal quality or hedonic tone view; the conative or attitude view; and a purely neurofunctional common-currency view, which is what someone in the seminar always raises and which I suspect changes the subject rather than answering the question, though say what it would buy the hedonist if it worked. Then the diagnostics I actually need for the handout: which single feature of the design is doing the work, and what happens to the objection if that feature is removed — specifically, what is left of the argument if A and B are allowed to be drawn from different senses, and what is left if C is dropped altogether. I'd also like the strongest available attacks from the side that comes out badly: I can think of three, namely denying that one of A or B is genuinely a pleasure at all (absorbing rather than pleasant, pleasant only in retrospect), positing a hedonic ingredient that is real but not isolable by introspection, and treating pleasantness as a determinable with several determinates, so that no shared specific quality is required; say which of these is the best response, what each costs, and whether the last one is a real answer or just a relabelling of the problem. Finally, if nothing survives all three episodes, tell me what you think the hedonist should actually do — retreat to a disjunctive or family-resemblance treatment of pleasure, treat pleasantness as unanalyzable, or hand the theory over to a desire-based view — and, in a line or two, whether what remains still deserves to be called hedonism.
