I came to philosophy late. I spent a working life in marine ecology and, since retiring, I have been sitting in on a reading group in the philosophy department of the university where I used to teach. This term we worked through David Lewis's 1969 book *Convention*, with some Schelling alongside it, and at our final session two other members of the group — one a retired solicitor, the other a statistician, neither a philosopher — got into an argument that the rest of us could not settle. I am reproducing it as I heard it, and I have genuinely not formed a view as to who has the better of it.

As our group understood it, Lewis's analysis runs roughly as follows. A regularity in the behaviour of members of a population, in a recurrent situation, is a convention when it is true, and is common knowledge in that population, that nearly everyone conforms to it, that nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform, that they have roughly the same preferences over the possible combinations of actions, that each prefers that any one further person conform given that nearly everyone else conforms, and that there is some alternative regularity, incompatible with the first, that would have served their common interest just as well had the others followed it. Conventions are solutions to coordination problems; a solution often becomes available because one possibility is salient, and what makes it salient is, most often, precedent; once in place, a convention sustains itself because each instance gives everyone a reason to expect and to repeat it. I was struck, coming from a field where one asks how a trait originates and how it is maintained, that the account says much more about maintenance than about origin.

The case they argued about is one I can vouch for personally, because I spent many summers at the station where it occurs. The station lies at the end of a single-track gravel road running two miles across a salt marsh, with passing bays at intervals. There is no signage, no written rule, and no one in authority has ever pronounced on the matter. The year-round staff, driving the station's own vehicles, keep to the left. Visiting researchers, most of them from continental Europe and most of them in hired cars, keep to the right. Within each group the practice is stable; the members of each group expect their own colleagues to keep to their side and are expected to do the same. All of the near misses occur where the two populations meet, which after one bad afternoon involving a Dutch postdoc is now the subject of a nervous joke rather than of any regulation.

(1) Miriam opened by saying that there are simply two conventions here, one for each population, and that this is exactly what Lewis's analysis predicts, since the definition is explicitly relative to a population: a regularity in the behaviour of the members of P in situations of kind S. Take P to be the staff and you have a convention of keeping left; take P to be the visitors and you have a convention of keeping right. She added that the case is a good advertisement for Lewis precisely because the two conventions are arbitrary in opposite directions: either would have done, so what fixed each one was not reason but precedent.

(2) Douglas replied that this is a defect rather than a prediction. Whether the practice is a convention at all now depends on how one draws the boundary of the population. Take P to be everyone who uses the road and the first clause fails outright, because not nearly everyone conforms; so on that description there is no convention on the marsh road at all, even though nothing whatever has changed about the drivers, their habits or their expectations. If convention-hood can be turned on and off by re-describing who counts as the population, then either the notion is gerrymandered or Lewis has left out something important: what the drivers care about is coordinating with whoever happens to be coming the other way, not with an antecedently circumscribed group.

(3) Miriam answered that the boundary is not arbitrary in the way he suggests: one draws it where the expectations actually reach, and Lewis hedges his clauses with nearly everyone and almost any instance precisely because real conventions are imperfect and leaky. She pointed out that the near misses at the interface are themselves evidence for the account: each group's regularity reproduces itself by its own precedent, in the way Schelling described, since the staff keep left because they expect the staff to keep left and every uneventful pass makes that expectation a little more secure, and similarly mutatis mutandis for the visitors.

(4) Douglas then raised two objections. First, common knowledge: Lewis requires it to be common knowledge in the population that people conform, expect conformity and prefer it, and nothing remotely like that obtains on the marsh road. Nobody has ever considered the matter, nobody has beliefs about anyone else's preferences, and what actually sustains the regularity is not a preference about other people's conformity but collision avoidance, backed at the margin by fear and by the cost of a dented vehicle. Second, and more generally, Lewis models conventions as solutions to a problem that agents reason about, whereas the norms people actually have are picked up by imitation, are never noticed, and would lose their grip if they were noticed; so either the station practice is not a convention, or Lewis's analysis is too intellectualised to cover most of the norms there are.

(5) Miriam said that Lewis's clauses require expectations and preferences, not deliberation or articulate belief, and that an expectation can be perfectly real while being entirely inarticulate. She also insisted that Lewis's own story about origins is not negotiation but precedent: one instance becomes salient, expectations converge on it, and it becomes self-fulfilling. And common knowledge, she said, is less brittle than Douglas makes out, because the swerving is public: the conformity, the expectation and the preference are visible to anyone on the road, and each public instance makes them better known, and known to be known, by everyone involved.

(6) Douglas replied that precedent plus salience is very close to circular, since it needs a first precedent and Lewis has no account of what makes something salient other than that it has been followed before; the story explains persistence and not origin. Worse, in this particular case neither regularity is arbitrary in the sense Lewis needs. The visitors keep right because they have driven several hundred miles on right-hand mainland roads to reach the ferry; the staff keep left because the bays are cut on the landward side in such a way that pulling in leftward is simply easier in a long vehicle. So each regularity has a non-conventional explanation — one imported from an external convention, one dictated by the physical layout — and Lewis's arbitrariness condition is therefore not met. At most one of the two practices is a convention, and quite possibly neither is: what looked like a textbook case of salience resolving an arbitrary coordination problem turns out to be a collision between an imported habit and a piece of civil engineering.

(7) Miriam said this conflates two things: showing that a regularity had a cause, or a motive, is not the same as showing that the alternative would not have served. Lewis's condition, as she read it, does not demand that the alternatives be equally good, only that there be some alternative regularity that everyone would have preferred anyone else to follow had nearly everyone else followed it — and that is plainly satisfied here, since if the staff all kept right, keeping right would serve perfectly well, bays or no bays. She added that an inherited convention is still a convention; the visitors' right-hand habit is a convention on the mainland, and a convention does not cease to be one because it travels.

(8) Douglas closed by saying that even if all that is granted, the analysis is too permissive. A run on a bank, or a rush on the station's fuel store before a storm, satisfies every clause: everyone conforms given that the others conform, everyone would prefer the alternative given that the others choose it, and the preferences are common. Yet nobody would call a panic a convention, and it is an equilibrium that everyone would like to escape. So the clauses do not distinguish conventions from equilibria that nobody chose, nobody wants, and nobody would describe as conventional. And for anyone interested in how norms evolve, he said, there is a deeper gap: Lewis's account needs agents with expectations about other agents, so it cannot reach the phenomena a natural scientist would recognise as proto-norms in creatures that have no expectations at all — trail-following in ants, for instance, where a regularity is genuinely self-reinforcing and genuinely arbitrary, and where there is nothing corresponding to common knowledge of anything. Miriam's answer was that the bank-run case shows at most that Lewis needs his common-preference clause read more strictly, which is a repair rather than a refutation, and that the ants are simply a different phenomenon: Lewis was analysing convention, not every self-sustaining regularity in nature.

Would you please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves, say whether it is right, partly right or mistaken, and why, rather than merely announcing a winner. I would be especially grateful if you could do four further things. First, tell me where the two of them are talking past each other — where a move is correct about one thing and being used to establish another. Second, say whether the marsh road practice really is a convention on Lewis's account, and, if the answer depends on how the population is drawn, whether that dependence is a genuine defect in the analysis or a feature Lewis should welcome. Third, mark explicitly which moves turn on a reading of Lewis's clauses rather than on the facts of the case, since I have not yet made up my mind about the text and it would help me to know which disputes are textual. Fourth, say what, if anything, both parties are missing — in particular whether the origin-and-maintenance worry I mentioned at the outset, and the animal cases Douglas raises at the end, are external to Lewis's project or fatal to it. I have no reliable way of checking the secondary literature, so please argue from the analysis as I have stated it above rather than from what you recall of the commentaries on it.
