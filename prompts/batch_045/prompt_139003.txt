I want the specification before I look at the theories. The dispute I have in mind is the one about whether what someone knows can depend on what is practically at stake: the family of views on which the standards for a knowledge attribution shift with the attributor's context; the family on which practical stakes for the subject herself enter the knowledge relation; the variant on which it is the attributor's stakes that matter; the variant on which the truth of an attribution is settled only relative to a later assessor's perspective; and the variant on which knowledge is a three-place relation between a subject, a proposition and a contrast proposition. Before I score them against each other I want to know what I am entitled to ask of any of them. So: write the desiderata.

Fix the purpose first, because adequacy is relative to it, and I want the theory to earn its keep twice over. It must state the condition under which a subject may treat a proposition as a reason for acting, in the way a knowledge norm on practical reasoning says she may; and it must state what a speaker says and can be held to when she attributes, withholds, denies or retracts knowledge across contexts. Tell me whether those are two jobs for one theory or two theories in a shared vocabulary. If you think the requirements divide into a semantic list and an epistemic list, say where the division falls, whether the same cases pull in both directions, and whether the division is itself a finding rather than a convenience.

Here is the raw material your list has to be answerable to. These are the phenomena, not my proposed requirements; I want you to convert them into requirements, say what each is really asking for, and drop or merge any that turn out not to be independent.

1. The baseline stakes pairs: same evidence, same truth, same subject, no cognitive difference, but a trivial decision in one version of the case and a serious one in the other, with speakers inclined to attribute knowledge in the first and withhold it in the second.
2. Skeptical contexts: an error hypothesis made salient, and whether whatever happens there is the same mechanism as the stakes effect or a different one that merely looks like it.
3. Statistical and lottery-type evidence: very strong support of the wrong modal sort, where knowledge seems to fail even though nothing of consequence hangs on the matter.
4. Hidden stakes: the subject does not know how much hangs on the question, or is wrong about it — a routine report that will in fact be acted on catastrophically, a passenger unaware that the instrument is faulty. Say whether the relevant stakes are the objective ones, the believed ones, or the ones it would be reasonable to believe, and say what happens to the action-guiding role of knowledge if they are the objective ones.
5. Asymmetric error costs: acting as if p when p is false is catastrophic while failing to act on a true p costs almost nothing, and the mirror image of that. Does the standard respond to both directions of error, and must a theory track them separately?
6. The floor: when literally nothing at all hinges on p, does every true belief become knowledge, or is there an evidential minimum the standard cannot fall below however idle the context?
7. Deferred and third-party stakes: a long-dead scientist, a non-human animal, a committee whose members face different losses, a stored output consulted later by someone with everything riding on it. Whose practical situation sets the standard when the subject has none of her own, or when the interested parties diverge?
8. Diachronic cases and knowledge retained from memory: acquired in an idle context, needed later when much is at stake, and the question of what the agent may then treat as a reason.
9. Leakage: heavy stakes attached to some other proposition, or to some other decision the subject faces. Does a large bet on q raise the bar for knowing that p?
10. Moral encroachment: cases where acting on a well-supported true belief would be morally objectionable, as with a statistical generalisation about a person's group. One phenomenon with prudential stakes, or two?
11. Second-order cases: attributing ignorance, or knowledge of ignorance, in a high-stakes context — whether the theory's own deliverances are hostage to the mechanism it posits.
12. Embedding: "knows" inside the antecedents of conditionals, in questions about whether someone knows whether, in reports of other people's attitudes, and under negation. Whether the operative context is introduced by the embedding environment, and what follows for the theory if it is.

Alongside the cases I want the distinctions a theory is not allowed to collapse, with the consideration behind each: knowing versus being reasonable to treat p as a reason, on pain of the theory having merely redescribed a decision-theoretic notion in epistemic vocabulary; knowing versus being in a position to assert; the standard's moving versus the subject's epistemic position's moving; whether someone knows versus how well positioned she is; genuine disagreement between a low-stakes attribution and a high-stakes denial versus a mere difference in what is said; and whether the theory can declare the low-stakes attributor to be in error without convicting ordinary epistemic speech of systematic falsehood.

Two things I want most. First, sort the requirements by status: mark which are load-bearing for any theory with the purposes I have fixed, and which are preferences of one camp that have been smuggled in as constraints — I have in mind the demands that knowledge cannot be lost without a change in the subject's evidence or mental life, that knowledge cannot depend on factors the subject could not know about, that the expression must behave identically in every embedding, that ordinary speakers' verdicts are data rather than the output to be explained, that a norm must be usable by the agent who is guided by it, and that a semantic account must not be revisionary. Adjudicate those: for each, is it a desideratum or a methodological commitment in disguise? Second, identify any subset of the surviving requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect there is a three-way trade among the truth of low-stakes attributions, the truth of high-stakes denials, and the claim that both are about one and the same relation between the subject and the proposition; if that is right, say what each candidate view buys by giving up which corner, and whether the trade is really a trilemma or an artefact of how I have set the purpose.

Finish with two short things. Nominate the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work, and say why it is that one rather than the more frequently cited ones. Then, in a paragraph or less per family, note where each of the five approaches I opened with fails the list — as a check on the list, not as a ranking: I want to know what each failure shows about the requirements, not which theory wins. If nothing on offer satisfies the whole list, sketch in a sentence or two what a view that did would have to look like.
