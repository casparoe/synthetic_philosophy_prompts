I want to rehearse an argument I am writing and I need a real opponent rather than a sparring dummy. Please play a contemporary defender of sensory hedonism about welfare: the view that how well a person's life is going at a time is fixed by nothing other than how their experience feels, where pleasantness is a single dimension along which experiences admit of more and less. Commit to three things: (i) pleasantness is a matter of how an experience feels, not of any attitude the subject takes up toward it; (ii) it is one dimension, not a family of loosely related ones; (iii) it is what makes a life go better, not merely our best available evidence that it does. You regard the heterogeneity objection — the complaint that the pleasure of a cold drink on a hot day, of finishing a proof, of sex, of hearing that a friend is safe share no felt quality — as the most overrated argument in the neighbourhood, and you are slightly impatient with people who keep repeating it. You have genuine resources: the analogy between pleasantness and temperature (a hot bath and an iced drink feel nothing alike, yet sit on one scale), the point that the objection generalizes uncomfortably (the objects of desire are at least as heterogeneous as the feelings of pleasure, so a desire theorist buys unity on credit), the distinction between what constitutes welfare and how we come to know it, and the fallback that pleasantness might be picked out by the role it plays in attention, motivation and learning rather than by a shared sensory quale. Concede small things freely — that introspection does not wear valence on its sleeve, that we sometimes misjudge our own pleasure, that pleasant experiences can have ugly objects, that comparing across persons is hard — but never concede (i), (ii) or (iii), and do not let the discussion dissolve into a survey of positions. Argue in the first person from the armchair: no citations, no bibliography, no appealing to what the literature has established. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of roughly a hundred and fifty words, ask me for further stipulations about my case if you need them, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening. Suppose, purely as a stipulation — I am not asking you to recall any finding — that human beings have two distinguishable liking channels. Channel A produces a slow, warm, drowsy contentment; channel B produces a bright, restless, forward-leaning exhilaration. Nothing about A feels like B, and subjects asked to compare them say things like "they are not the same kind of thing at all, but if I must choose I will take one over the other." Now the part I want explained: their forced choices between any amount of A and any amount of B are fine-grained, transitive, stable under reflection, insensitive to mood and to order of presentation, and they survive every attempt to induce subjects to treat the two as incomparable. I claim that this is precisely what we should expect if a single preference structure is taking both as objects and ranking them, and not what we should expect if subjects were reading off one common felt magnitude that merely presents itself in two phenomenological guises. If that is right, the unitary dimension you need is doing no work: hedonism keeps its arithmetic by borrowing it from desire, and the honest move is to become a desire theorist or a pluralist. And if you reach for the idea that pleasantness is a determinable with these as its determinates, I will ask what makes A and B determinates of one determinable rather than two magnitudes we happen to be able to rank — so tell me why this case does not break you.
