I work on inference in archaeology — specifically, on when patterned material remains license a claim about what people believed, or about the institutions they lived under. I have a tentative principle and a set of verdicts that I don't think it handles cleanly, and I would like the principle stated properly, or replaced.

My tentative principle has two parts. Call the first the double-difference condition: a pattern P licenses an inference to a belief or institution B only if P would be improbable both given the absence of B and given the presence of each of B's plausible nearby alternatives. Call the second the friction condition: for institutions specifically, as opposed to beliefs, P must additionally show signs of being maintained against what some participants would otherwise have preferred — evidence of enforcement, sanction, or coordination cost — since an institution is a rule that holds when it is costly. I do not think this second clause is right, but I don't know what else separates the cases below.

Here are my verdicts. The sites are stylized composites, so please treat them as such rather than checking them against real excavations.

1. Sixty houses in a single-phase village each contain the same grinding installation, and the adjacent storage rooms cluster tightly around one volume. Verdict: I infer that processing of a staple was organized at the household level rather than centrally. I do not infer anything about who owned the staple or how it was divided.

2. Jar stoppers impressed with seal motifs occur in dedicated, otherwise empty rooms; there are four recurring motifs, and motif frequency tracks the four spatial quarters of the settlement almost perfectly. Verdict: I infer four corporate groups with recognized authority over stored goods. This is the case I am most confident about.

3. In each of those four quarters, small platforms hold burned deposits of seeds — four species, in very nearly the same proportions, in nearly every house. Verdict: I decline to infer a shared calendrical festival or a shared belief about the seeds. Conformity, imitation, or a single widely copied practice would produce the same regularity, and my principle's first clause is not satisfied.

4. The same architectural signature — thick walls, single narrow entrance, a restricted approach — appears at two sites. At one, the buildings contain feasting debris and formalised seating and no storage; at the other, they contain grain and dense rodent gnawing. Verdict: I infer institutional authority at the first and only pest-management storage at the second. Architecture on its own licenses nothing; the contrast licenses the difference. Note that this sits awkwardly with my friction clause: at the first site I have no evidence of enforcement at all, only of feasting.

5. In every house foundation across a two-hundred-year sequence, an identical set of four vessel types is deposited before the floor is laid; the deposits are sealed and never visible or accessible afterwards. Verdict: I infer a shared normative rule about what a house needs — a belief, in the broad sense I care about.

6. In every grave across the same region and period, the same four vessel types appear in the same arrangement. Verdict: I refuse to infer a belief about an afterlife. The uniformity is equally explained by a potters' standard funerary kit, or by display on the part of the living.

7. A settlement in a region where its neighbours have fortifications and plentiful weapons has neither, for three centuries. Verdict: I decline to infer pacifism or an institutionalised norm against violence. I am, however, willing to infer the weaker claim that raiding was not a routine constraint on how people laid out this settlement, and I am unsure why the weaker claim is fine and the stronger one is not.

8. Ten independent lines of evidence — faunal, isotopic, spatial, use-wear, textile impressions, and others — are each individually ambiguous but all point to the conclusion that a particular building was where a particular subset of people worked textile. Verdict: I am confident, more confident than any single line would make me, but I cannot say why convergence of individually weak lines helps, and I worry that the lines share a hidden source of error.

9. The motifs in case 2 match a phrase in a text written a millennium later, in a related but different language, in a documentary genre, referring to a storehouse of a quarter of a town. Verdict: I count this as a small boost to case 2, smaller than I can justify, and I would count it even if the gap were larger.

10. Forty pits at building corners, over several phases, contain only the right forelimbs of cattle, with no butchery marks and no other refuse; no functional explanation has survived scrutiny. Verdict: I infer something symbolic or ritual. I am aware this is inference to the residual, and I dislike it, but I hold it.

Cases 5 and 6 are the pair I keep returning to: they are structurally parallel, and I give opposite verdicts. Cases 3 and 10 may be a worse pair, since in both the regularity outruns every functional explanation I can think of and I nevertheless split them.

Please do the following. State a principle — or a small set of rival candidates — precisely enough that I could apply it to a new site, and tell me which case motivates each clause. Say explicitly whether beliefs and institutions need different conditions, and if so, whether my friction clause or something else does the work. Then give the verdicts your principle yields on three cases I have not listed: deliberate breakage, where hundreds of metal objects are broken in the same way and deposited in a river over centuries with nothing else present; discontinuity, where a pattern holds for the first two phases of a site and disappears exactly when isotopic evidence shows an influx of non-local people; and a reflexive case, where much later investigators infer a two-party electoral institution from our own seasonal lawn signs, recurring colours, and door-knocking paraphernalia. Finally, state plainly which of my ten verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn first.
