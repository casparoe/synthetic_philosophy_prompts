The position I want stress-tested is a package of three claims about the levelling-down objection. I don't need a survey of the debate; I want a map of what this package risks, and what would have to be true for it to be false.

First claim: the levelling-down objection reaches only views on which some comparative, non-separable feature of a distribution has intrinsic value — paradigmatically the telic egalitarian's equality — and it does not reach the priority view, which scores outcomes as a sum of an increasing, concave transformation of individual well-being and is therefore separable and Pareto-satisfying. Levelling down strictly lowers such a sum; and because the view is monistic about the good, there is no separate respect in which the levelled-down outcome could come out ahead. So there is no distinctively prioritarian version of the problem.

Second claim, about why the objection nonetheless feels forceful even to people who don't accept egalitarianism: the pull is explained by three things that are not reasons — a slide from better-in-one-respect to better, a tacit person-affecting constraint smuggled into the judgement, and the fact that in realistic cases levelling down is evidence of something independently objectionable, such as resentment or a refusal to help the worse off when one could. Strip those away and the intuition, I claim, thins out.

Third claim, the one I'm least confident of and most want attacked: the immunity result is normally stated for same-people, fixed-number, risk-free comparisons, and that is arguably the only setting in which better for no one is well defined at all. With different numbers of people there is no one across the two outcomes for the change to be better or worse for; under risk, whether ex ante or ex post evaluation is correct determines whether a Pareto-style constraint is even the right thing to impose. So: wherever the objection can be formulated, prioritarianism answers it, and wherever it cannot be formulated, it cannot generate a counterexample. I realise this third claim looks like it might be a jurisdictional dodge dressed up as a result, and I want that possibility taken seriously rather than charitably interpreted away.

Two cases I take to be the real pressure, and which I'd like you to classify — genuine levelling-down problems, sibling objections that merely inherit the name, or something neither: (i) one person at well-being 100 versus three people at 20 each, where a square-root priority function prefers the second outcome despite its smaller total and despite the original person being much worse off; (ii) under risk, a certainty of (10, 10) versus an even chance of (30, 0) or (0, 30), where an ex post priority view prefers the certainty even though each person's expected well-being is higher under the gamble, because the concavity that does the priority work also penalises spreads in final outcomes.

Against that: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which of them would a committed prioritarian concede as counting against them, as opposed to dismissing as a confusion? Which depend on assumptions I haven't stated — about the shape and boundedness of the priority function, about whether the view is total or average or critical-level, about whether it is ex ante or ex post? And is my second claim, the debunking explanation, the kind of thing that could be falsified at all, or has the package been qualified into a position that can absorb any counterexample by relocating it to a case where the objection cannot be stated? If it has, say whether that is a defect here or whether a claim of this kind is entitled to that insulation.

For what it's worth, the two things that would move me most are: an argument that better-in-one-respect is a genuine axiological datum that no monistic, separable view can accommodate, combined with a reason to think that datum is not itself just the person-affecting constraint in disguise; and a case in which a view that is both separable and Pareto-satisfying still ranks a levelled-down outcome higher, via its handling of numbers or of risk. I'd take either as close to decisive, and I'd take a mere reassertion of the intuition as close to worthless.
