I am a retired professor of evolutionary biology who has lately been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and I keep tripping over one slogan. At an animal-welfare conference last month a speaker moved, in about a minute, from the claim that the way we treat pigs in industrial agriculture is indefensible to the claim that one must give near-equal consideration to every vertebrate and in all likelihood to insects as well; the bridge was the line that any boundary you draw between species is arbitrary, since nature has no sharp edges, so you cannot stop at pigs, or at vertebrates, or anywhere at all.

I would like that sentence taken apart. Please lay out, as a menu rather than as a single analysis, the distinct precise claims it could be making — ordered roughly from the thinnest reading to the strongest — and give a verdict on each: trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or false as a matter of biology. The readings I suspect are hiding in it, and which I want separated if they are genuinely distinct, are these. That species membership as such cannot be a ground of moral status. That the properties usually thought to ground moral status — sentience, or nociception, or something richer — are themselves matters of degree rather than all-or-nothing. That even where a property is sharp, there is no non-arbitrary threshold at which it begins to matter. That we simply do not know whether the relevant property is instantiated in, say, an insect or a nematode with 302 neurons, so that any cut-off registers ignorance rather than judgement. That "arbitrary" means unmotivated by anything at all, as opposed to merely underdetermined by the evidence, so that a boundary could be principled without being uniquely picked out. That evolutionary continuity, or the continuity of a trait's distribution, rules out differences large enough to matter morally, as distinct from ruling out a unique cut-point. That the demand for a line is misplaced because moral status is a ranking and not a threshold. And that, given uncertainty about another creature's capacities, the expected cost of wrongly excluding a sentient being so greatly exceeds the cost of wrongly including a non-sentient one that the precautionary answer is maximal inclusion.

For each reading, say in a sentence or two whether it is true, what it would take to establish it, and — this is what I most want — whether it would actually carry the speaker from the premise about pigs to a conclusion about vertebrates generally and insects in particular. Where a reading supports the premise about pigs but nothing beyond it, say so plainly. Where a reading is one a working zoologist should reject on empirical grounds, say why. And if the distinctions I have drawn collapse into one another, or if some reading belongs on the menu that I have not listed, correct me.

Please write in British English, and keep the menu to what the argument actually needs.
