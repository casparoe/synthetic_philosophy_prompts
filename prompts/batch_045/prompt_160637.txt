I've been going through Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* with a small reading group, and I keep getting stuck on the way the book sets up its central case: the ethical is described as the universal, something that applies to everyone and can in principle be stated and argued about in public, whereas Abraham's relation to God is a private relation of the single individual to the absolute, one that cannot be translated into universal terms at all, so that from the ethical point of view he is simply a man trying to murder his son, and he cannot explain himself without ceasing to be who he is. I want to press that framing against something concrete rather than just exegetical, so I want to ask about a family of legal practices.

As I understand it, most liberal legal orders accommodate conscientious objection in a very specific shape. A person can be released *in advance* from a legal duty she has not yet performed — conscription, performing a particular medical procedure, taking an oath — but only if a tribunal is satisfied that her objection is sincere and that it is religious or conscience-based in character; the tribunal is generally forbidden, however, from assessing whether the belief is true, reasonable, or well-founded. The accommodation is almost always limited to refusals: no jurisdiction lets someone off for a religiously mandated *action* that the criminal law forbids, and as far as I can tell nobody has seriously proposed it. Even successful objectors are usually required to do something else in place of the refused duty, alternative civilian service or a transfer of the patient, as though the debt were owed to the community's aims rather than discharged by conscience. And then, past the point of accommodation, the person who has already broken the law for conscience's sake gets no defence at all: she is convicted, and whatever recognition there is comes later, from sentencing leniency or executive clemency, as an act of mercy outside the rules rather than as a right within them.

So I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed. What does this package — prospective exemption, sincerity-without-truth, refusal-only, substitute service, conviction-then-mercy — have to assume about the relation between conscience, the universal or ethical order, and whatever it is that stands above it? In particular: does the sincerity-but-not-truth rule quietly presuppose that the state can classify a claim as religious while being barred from evaluating its content, and is that coherent? Does requiring substitute service, or convicting while mitigating, presuppose that conscience generates an excuse rather than a justification, and would that be a way of conceding the Kierkegaardian point while refusing to give it legal form? Where is the package underdetermined — could both a Kierkegaardian view, on which the claim of the single individual is inexpressible and can only appear to the ethical order as a crime, and a broadly Hegelian or Millian view, on which conscience is internalised social normativity with no independent authority, license the very same institutional arrangement? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what merely fits it comfortably, whether the refusal-only asymmetry is a genuine moral commitment or just an evidentiary hedge, and what would actually have to change — in the direction of more accommodation, or of none — if a legal system took seriously the claim that there is a relation to the absolute that the universal cannot adjudicate and that the person in it cannot speak.
