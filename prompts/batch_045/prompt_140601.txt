Here's the question (25 marks):

Buridan's ass stands exactly midway between two bales of hay that are alike in every respect the ass has any concern with. If it does not walk to one of them it starves. For this question, use the following labels:

- the CAPACITY claim: an agent with no all-things-considered preference between A and B cannot bring itself to intend, or to do, either one.
- the REASON claim: an agent with no all-things-considered preference between A and B has no reason to do either one.
- the NO-PERMISSION claim: if A and B are exactly equally good, then neither is the option the agent has most reason to do, so a requirement to always do what you have most reason to do leaves the agent with nothing it may do.

Two readings are on offer.

Rankin: the example is a reductio of treating reasons as essentially discriminating. Rationality requires a reason for acting, and a reason that merely tells you to eat some bale or other is not the kind of thing that can justify an action, because it does not single out the action. Rankin grants that the ass has a reason to eat some bale or other, since it will otherwise starve, but denies that this is enough. So an arbitrary pick is rationally defective, and the agent must generate a further reason, for instance by adopting a tie-breaking procedure it can itself justify.

Ostrow: the example shows that permissibility is not always a matter of being singled out by reasons. Where options are exactly equally good, each is fully permissible, and neither requires support from a reason that favours it over the other. Nothing more is needed, and no tie-breaker is rationally mandatory, though using one is fine.

(a) 8 marks. Identify which of the three claims each of Rankin and Ostrow is committed to rejecting, and say which claim the example is standardly used to target. Be careful to keep the CAPACITY claim separate from the others.

(b) 9 marks. The Rotten Core variant: the two bales are identical in every way the ass can detect, but the ass knows that exactly one of them has a rotten core that will make it ill, and knows it will only find out which after it has eaten. So whichever bale it walks to, it will turn out that the other was the one worth having. Does the fact that the ass will certainly end up having taken the worse bale give it a reason to prefer one bale to the other? Does it make either choice less than fully rational? Is this the same problem as the original?

(c) 8 marks. In the original case the ass considers flipping a mental coin. A classmate objects that this only relocates the difficulty: the ass has no more reason to consult a coin than to walk to the left, so the coin cannot help. Is the objection sound, and does it have the same force against both readings?

And here is the answer, verbatim apart from me retyping it:

---

I think Ostrow has the better of this, and the ass's real mistake is that it is treating having a reason as the same thing as having a reason to prefer this one over that one. Reasons don't have to pick out a unique option, so the claim under attack is NO-PERMISSION: the ass assumes that unless something singles out a bale there is nothing it may do, and that assumption is what generates the standoff. Ostrow is right that both bales are permissible and that the ass can simply walk to one. The CAPACITY claim is a different, psychological claim, and both readings set it aside — the ass is not paralysed, it is just confused about what rationality demands of it. Rankin, by contrast, is committed to rejecting the REASON claim: he thinks the ass does have a reason to go to each bale, namely that it will otherwise starve, and that is why he thinks an arbitrary pick is defective.

In the Rotten Core variant I don't think anything important changes. The ass knows one bale is bad, so it now has a reason to prefer the sound bale, the reason being that eating the rotten one will make it ill. But it can't tell which is which, so the reason can't guide it, and ex ante the two bales are still symmetric and both choices are equally rational. So the variant collapses back into the original case: it makes no difference which one it picks, and it should just pick arbitrarily. Rationality is assessed from the agent's position at the time of acting, not with hindsight, so the guaranteed regret doesn't count against the choice.

The coin objection is unsound. It does have some bite against Rankin specifically, since if you need a reason to pick a procedure you also need a reason to pick the procedure that picks the procedure, and Rankin owes us an account of why that stops. But against Ostrow it fails, because the coin genuinely does supply something the ass lacked: after the coin comes up left, the ass has a new reason to go left, namely that the coin came up left, and that is why the resulting intention is no longer arbitrary. Indeed on Ostrow's own view the tie-breaker is rationally required, because without one the ass has nothing to go on, and if it has nothing to go on it will stand there and starve, which is the outcome both readings are trying to avoid.
