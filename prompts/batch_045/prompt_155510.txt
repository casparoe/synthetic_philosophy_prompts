I am writing the take-home examination for my third-year seminar on the philosophy of emotion, and I need a new case. The cases currently in circulation have lost their usefulness: students recognise them and recite the verdicts rather than deriving them. I want something ordinary enough to be answerable in fifteen minutes, but engineered so that the standard dialectical assumptions do not do the work.

The four views the students have been working with are these. State, in one sentence each, the version of each that you will use; where a view admits of stronger and weaker formulations, take the formulation least friendly to the case you want to build, and flag where you have done so.

(i) Judgment theory: emotions are, or essentially involve, evaluative judgments that the subject endorses and would defend if challenged.
(ii) Perceptual or attitudinal theory: emotions are states in which an evaluative property is perceptually or quasi-perceptually presented to the subject — the situation appears to her as insulting, or dangerous, or admirable — and this appearance is not merely inferred, remembered, or accepted on testimony. Consider separately a somatic variant, on which what is presented is a bodily change carrying evaluative content.
(iii) Feeling theory in the Jamesian vein: emotions are, or are constituted by, feelings of a characteristic pattern of bodily change.
(iv) Affordance-priority theory, a position I have been developing and which the students have seen only in outline: an emotion is a state that re-ranks the subject's field of available actions — what now solicits approach, avoidance, repair, retaliation, withdrawal — where this re-ranking need not involve an evaluative judgment, need not involve an evaluative seeming, and need not have any distinctive bodily phenomenology of its own.

The specification. Construct a single episode, no more than 250 words, set in an ordinary workplace, school, or family setting, in which (i), (iii), and (iv) all return the verdict that an emotion is present and (ii) returns the verdict that no emotion is present — where the views that say yes all converge on the same emotion, which should be resentment. So the perceptual theory must come out as the restrictive view. That is the point of the exercise: I want to know whether perceptual theories are genuinely the permissive option in this debate, or whether their permissiveness is an artifact of the small set of cases we always discuss.

Constraints on the scenario. No clinical, neurological, or pharmacological exotica; no phobias; nothing turning on the subject's being self-deceived, akratic, exhausted to the point of unreliability, or otherwise impaired. The bodily state you use must be one an ordinary person could simply be in on an ordinary day. Write the scenario in plain, non-technical vocabulary — circumstances, what is said and done, what the subject notices about herself in ordinary terms — so that a competent student derives each verdict from the facts rather than reading it off the description. In particular the scenario may not stipulate the outcome: it cannot say that she is resentful, or that she is not really resentful, or that nothing seemed insulting to her, or that she judged herself wronged. It may only give the facts from which those claims would follow.

After the scenario, give me the following.

First, the verdict for each of the four views with the reasoning that gets you there from the facts, two or three sentences each, including the verdict for the somatic variant of (ii) if it differs from the non-somatic one.

Second, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and show what happens when it is removed: tell me which verdicts flip, and name the stock case in the literature that the remainder collapses into. I expect the case to be the mirror image of the recalcitrance cases — same ingredients, opposite arrangement — so tell me which ingredient would have to be subtracted or rearranged to turn it back into one of those.

Third, give me the strongest objection available to a defender of (ii), stated as she would actually press it, and show me which feature of the scenario blocks it. If the objection in fact succeeds against the case as you have written it, say so plainly rather than protecting the case; I would rather know now than after the papers are in.

Finally, a marking note: what a fully correct student answer must include, and one specific way a strong student could go wrong that I should be prepared to partial-credit.
