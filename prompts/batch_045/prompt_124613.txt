i volunteer on the grants committee at a tiny free clinic and we've been having the same argument for over a year: two of us want the money to go to a very expensive program for the very worst-off patients, two want the broader program that helps way more people who are only moderately badly off. I started reading the philosophy to settle it and reading made it worse. The literature looks just as stuck: prioritarians say a benefit matters more the worse off the person is, so they're never moved to level down for equality's sake and they take that to mean the levelling-down objection simply doesn't touch them; egalitarians either say equality can make an outcome better in one respect even when it's worse overall, or treat the objection as a cost they'll absorb — and then everyone repeats themselves.

Here's the case I keep bringing to meetings and nobody has an answer to. Compare two people at (1, 1000) with the same two at (2, 3). Plug in a thoroughly standard concave weighting — the kind welfare economists use constantly, say weight = -1/u — and prioritarianism prefers the second: it wipes out 997 units of well-being for the better-off person in order to give one unit to the worst off. My gut says absolutely not, and it's the same gut that's supposed to sink levelling down. But in levelling down nobody gains anything, whereas here somebody does gain. So which is it really: is the levelling-down intuition about nobody benefiting, or about losses being wildly out of proportion to gains?

My hypothesis is that the fight is upstream of equality altogether: it's about whether 'better' has to be traceable to somebody's being better off, and about whether 'better in one respect' is a claim with any decision-guiding force or just a restatement of your favourite. Diagnose it using what each side actually argues — no survey please. Verbal, substantive, two different questions under one heading, or a hidden question about the shape of the weighting function doing all the work? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, some formal result about which desiderata can coexist, a change of question — or should I expect none and why? If it falls out of the diagnosis, tell me what we should do with our little budget too.
