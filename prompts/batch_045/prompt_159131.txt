I got talked into being on the commitee our town set up to figure out what to do about a couple of names and one statue, and I've been going in circles for weeks. I'm not an academic — I do scheduling for a plumbing company — and I have no library access and no way to read whatever books people keep saying are the serious ones on this, so please don't send me to a reading list. I just want to know whether there's a principle behind the gut calls I keep making, because right now it feels like I'm making them up one at a time.

Here's where I land.

1. A high school named after a man who died in 1919 and left the money for it in his will. He was also one of the state's loudest campaigners for forced sterilization. Take the name off. And note the bad thing has nothing to do with what he was honored for — he was honored for the bequest.

2. A street named after a doctor who worked out a surgical technique that's still used and has saved a huge number of lives, and who developed it by operating on enslaved women without anaesthesia. Take the name off the street. But I dont think we should rename the technique — the surgeons say it would cause mistakes in hospitals, and I grudgingly accept that. That bugs me though. If the problem is that we shouldn't be handing this guy honors, why does convenience get to decide? I don't want my principle to collapse into do whatever is cheapest.

3. A statue of the same doctor outside the hospital: take it down. The same statue moved into the county museum with a wall of text about what he did: I'm fine with that, actually I think it's better than fine.

4. A big honest plaque next to the statue explaining exactly what he did: that mostly fixes it for me. The identical plaque in the lobby of the school explaining who the school is named after: does not fix it at all. Why the asymmetry? That's the one that bothers me most.

5. An elementary school named after a woman who campaigned hard for women to get the vote and who also wrote some genuinely vile stuff about immigrants. Nobody at that school has any idea who she was — the name is just the name of the school now, the way Washington Elementary is just a name. I say leave it. But that seems to flatly contradict 1, where I also said nobody knows who the guy was and I still want it changed.

6. Same man, two objects. A statue put up in 1921 by an organization that said out loud they were putting it there to show who runs this town. And a headstone from 1882 paid for by people who were sad he died. Take down the 1921 statue, leave the grave alone. But then what am I actually judging — the man, or the reason the thing is there?

7. A library reading room named after a living donor who gave the money on the condition the name stays up for fifty years, and who has since gone to prison for something with no connection to libraries. I keep changing my mind. Part of me says a deal is a deal and we already spent the money on the room. Part of me says the room is ours and we don't have to keep thanking him in it. I also noticed that taking the name off and handing the money back feels cleaner to me than taking the name off and keeping the money, which makes me think I'm not only thinking about honors here.

8. A statue of a firefighter with no name on it, just the firefighter. Nobody is upset about it. And a statue of a mayor who was mediocre and did nothing especially bad: nobody is upset about that one either. So my rule can't be only honor people who deserve it.

9. When we do rename things, I keep wanting us to name them after something that isn't a person — the creek, the street's old name, a poem, whatever — since whoever we pick will turn out to have done something awful in ninety years. And I'm oddly drawn to two half-measures: chiseling the name off the statue's base and leaving the thing standing, and renaming the street but putting up a small marker saying what it used to be called and why it changed. I can't explain why those feel better to me than just deleting the thing.

The rough principle I've been trying out is: keep the honor only if what we're doing with it — saying the name out loud, keeping the statue standing, paying to maintain it — is something we'd be willing to do on purpose today, knowing what we know now. But that principle seems to give the wrong answer on the grave (we do maintain that cemetery) and maybe on the museum too (we're choosing to keep the statue there, on purpose).

So: is there one principle, or two that have to be traded off against each other, that gets most of these? Which of my calls does it force me to give up? And what does it say about the two I'm stuck on — the living donor's reading room, and whether the 1921 statue belongs in a museum or in a scrapyard?
