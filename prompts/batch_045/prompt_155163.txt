I'm a journalist preparing a piece on whether what we know can depend on what we have at stake, and before I quote or paraphrase a source I want to know how much of it I can trust. The characterization below comes from a forthcoming popular book on epistemology — I've only seen a galley, without the notes — and it is written with real confidence:

"Until the 1990s, epistemologists agreed that knowledge was simply true belief supported by good enough evidence: a single fixed threshold, the same for everyone in every situation. Then the philosophers who introduced the much-discussed 'bank cases' pointed out that whether you know the bank will be open on Saturday seems to change depending on whether a missed mortgage payment would ruin you, and they concluded that knowledge is context-sensitive: whether a sentence of the form 'S knows that p' comes out true depends on the practical stakes of whoever is assessing S. This position — pragmatic encroachment, of which epistemic contextualism is the leading version — says that what you know shifts with what you have riding on being right, so that knowledge is not, at bottom, a matter of truth and evidence at all, but of whether it is worthwhile to act as if p. Critics objected that the view makes knowledge depend on stakes the subject can hardly be said to have evidence about, and even sympathetic epistemologists now concede that the bank cases show nothing more than that the English word 'know' gets used loosely under pressure, so the research program has largely been abandoned."

My own instinct is that knowledge shouldn't fluctuate with my mortgage balance, but the book's tone makes me wonder whether I'm just defending common sense against a view I haven't really understood. So I'd like a sentence-by-sentence audit: which claims a defender of pragmatic encroachment or contextualism would accept as stated, which are fair simplifications, which conflate two positions the literature keeps apart or quietly invert how the stakes are supposed to work, and which are live objections presented as settled refutations. I especially want to know whether the book's closing move follows from anything defensible in the passage — its claim that the view vindicates tiered verification in newsrooms, so that a reporter staking her career on a story needs more evidence in order to know it than a colleague filing a routine brief, and escalating checks are requirements of knowledge rather than just risk management.

Finally, give me two or three sentences I could safely print for a general reader: what the dispute is actually about, and what its status is now.
