I write literary fiction, and the novel I am finishing at the moment turns on a retired philosophy don, Marianne, who is setting the last examination paper of her career and then, two chapters later, marking the scripts in a cold room while her marriage comes apart. I know roughly what the scene needs emotionally, but I need the paper itself to be right: I would like you to write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme for it. The context is a final-year undergraduate honours option on rational choice and action — two-hour closed-book paper, candidates answer one question in no more than 1,500 words, and the question is worth 25 marks. The subject I want is Buridan's ass and choice without preference. One housekeeping note: a friend tells me the ass probably does not appear in Buridan's surviving writings at all, and that something like it turns up earlier in Aristotle about a man equally hungry and thirsty. I have no way to check this and I do not want the question to depend on it, so please frame the question so that it works whichever way the scholarship goes, and do not put any reading list, bibliography or citation in your answer — my character is marking in a room with no library, and I want a model answer of the sort a very able student could have produced from memory.

I have been trying, and failing, to work out what the question should actually force a student to do, and I have ended up with four things I want it to contain. First, it must not be answerable by reciting the story and its usual moral — that exact ties are impossible in the real world, or that reason must be supplemented by something non-rational such as will or impulse. I want candidates to have to separate three different questions that the story runs together: the psychological one, whether an agent in such a position would in fact freeze rather than choose; the normative one, whether picking either option is permitted, forbidden, or somehow rationally required; and the explanatory one, whether a choice can be fully rational when nothing explains why it went this way rather than that way. Second, I want the question to insist on a distinction between three ways a standoff can arise: the two options being exactly equal in value; their being incomparable or on a par, so that neither is better and neither is worse but they are not merely equal either; and the agent's simply not having settled how much weight to give to two competing considerations, so that her preferences are imprecise or incomplete. Candidates should have to say whether the correct normative verdict is the same in all three cases, and, in particular, whether failing to have settled a weighting is itself a rational failing or just a piece of unfinished business. Third, I have invented a wrinkle I am rather attached to: after the standoff between the two bales, a third, slightly mouldy bale is placed on one side, strictly worse than both of the others. Students should have to say whether its arrival gives the agent a reason to choose one of the original two, whether the fact that adding an option can apparently change what it is permissible to do shows that the original case was never really about symmetry at all, and whether their answer to that generalises back to the other two kinds of standoff.

The fourth thing is the reason I am bothering you rather than a colleague. In fiction, a character who cannot choose between two courses of action is not rationally paralysed; she is under-written. The author has not given her a reason, and readers treat that as a defect of craft rather than as a predicament. I want the candidates to consider whether the gap between "there is no reason to prefer either" and "no reason has been supplied" marks a genuine difference between agents and characters — for instance because fictional agents are reasons-transparent in a way real ones are not, or because an author's silence is determinate in a way an agent's unsettled weighting is not — or whether the contrast collapses on inspection, and what it would take for a real agent to be in the character's position, perhaps because the considerations that will move her are ones she cannot yet access. Please write everything in British English. Finally, since I want the marking scene to be honest about the subject rather than a ventriloquist's dummy of it, please mark clearly, in the model answer and the scheme, which claims are near-unchallenged among people who work on these questions and which are live disputes on which a good philosopher could reasonably take either side; where you are unsure which of the two a claim is, say so rather than tidying it up. I would also like a short note on which parts of the model answer my character could fairly mark a student down for rejecting, and which she should treat as a defensible minority view, and a marking scheme that names the specific moves separating a first-class answer from an upper-second one — including the characteristic mistakes, such as treating the paradox as a piece of empirical psychology, or assuming without argument that rationality must always issue in a unique verdict.
