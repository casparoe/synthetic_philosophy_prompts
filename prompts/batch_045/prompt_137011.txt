I'm a high school senior writing an entry for a philosophy-of-physics essay competition, on Gödel's rotating-universe solution and his claim that it tells against the objectivity of time. My physics teacher read a draft and said my central move looks "suspiciously tidy," which I can't tell is a compliment or not. Below are the essay's working notes. I'd like your comments on them before I cut this into a final draft.

THE ARGUMENT AS I READ IT. Gödel exhibited a solution of Einstein's field equations describing a homogeneous universe of slowly rotating matter with a negative cosmological constant, not expanding, in which closed timelike curves pass through every point: a traveller who sets out, accelerates suitably, and comes home arrives at an event lying in her own past along the worldline she left, having aged normally throughout — say forty years of proper time. From this he drew an idealist conclusion: if such a universe is physically possible, then the lapse of time — the coming-into-being of successive nows — cannot be an objective feature of reality, and what we call temporal passage is something we contribute. As I reconstruct the reasoning, it has two steps: (i) if time objectively lapsed, reality would consist of a stack of global nows coming into existence one after another; (ii) in a universe with closed timelike curves there is no such stack, no way to slice all events into a single ordered sequence of total world-stages, so there is nothing for the lapse to be a lapse of.

WHAT I THINK IT ACTUALLY ESTABLISHES. I want to separate two claims that I think get run together. Call the first UNSLICEABILITY: there is no observer-independent partition of the universe's events into a single series of total states. Call the second ASEQUENTIALITY: there is no objective succession, no accumulation of moment upon moment. Gödel's solution gives him the first. I do not think it gives him the second, because along any single worldline everything we associate with passage is intact: proper time elapses, memory accumulates, records pile up, entropy increases. What disappears is not succession but the bookkeeping device that would let us say how far the universe as a whole has got.

THE DIARY TEST. Suppose the traveller keeps a diary. On arrival she has forty years of entries, while the world around her is at the earlier stage. But nothing in the contents of the world at that event distinguishes her first visit from her second, since both visits are the same event. So she cannot tell, by inspecting the world, which pass she is on. I claim this shows that the failure is in the notion of the state of the universe at a time, not in the notion of passage: the diarist's own elapsed time is as robust as anything in physics, and her ignorance is ignorance of where she is in the world's history, not of whether time passes.

WHY A MERELY POSSIBLE WORLD SHOULD MATTER. Our universe expands and is not observably rotating in Gödel's sense, so his model is not ours. Why then should a cosmology we do not inhabit tell us anything about our time? My answer: objectivity is a modal notion. If the lapse of time were objective, it would have to be objective in every world the laws permit, since objectivity is not the sort of property that can hold here and fail in a nomically possible case. So one counterexample world suffices.

CLOSING DILEMMA. Either becoming attaches to worldlines or to the universe as a whole. If to worldlines, then the question how fast time passes has only the trivial answer one second per second, the lapse is not a quantity at all, and it drops out of physics. If to the universe as a whole, Gödel's solution shows the laws permit worlds in which there is no such quantity. Either way the lapse is not part of the physical inventory, though succession along worldlines is — and that, I want to say, is all the idealist conclusion is entitled to.
