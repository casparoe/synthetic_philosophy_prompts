Write an essay, in British English, of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, on the following controversy: whether the Malthusian hypothesis about population and subsistence should be filed as a theory that was falsified, or as one that was right but early.

I do not want a survey of the demographic and economic history, and I do not want a verdict that depends on what Malthus is best read as having really meant. The essay should treat the episode as a test case for how we reason about long-run predictions that fail during the window in which they were supposed to bite, and it must end by defending a determinate verdict rather than a balanced shrug. A disjunctive verdict is acceptable — for instance, one that pronounces the geometric-versus-arithmetic framing dead as stated while granting that some separable mechanism survives as a conditional claim, or vice versa — provided each half is argued for and the combination is stable.

Three constraints on substance.

First, disambiguate the defences that get bundled together under the right-but-early heading, and say which of them, if any, is legitimate. I suggest at least these four, though you may refine the taxonomy: defence by deferral, on which the mechanism is intact and only the date was wrong; defence by screening, on which the mechanism is intact but has been masked for two centuries by a countervailing force of unusual magnitude, and would reassert itself were that force withdrawn; defence by re-scaling, on which the mechanism never bound at the level of whole economies over long horizons but does bind locally or over short ones, which is a change of domain rather than a delay; and defence by re-description, on which the theory is quietly rewritten so that its central quantity is no longer income per head pinned near subsistence but something weaker, such as an occasionally binding ceiling.

Second, confront the cheapness objection. If a failed long-run prediction can always be rescued by positing a temporary countervailing force, then right-but-early is not an epistemic achievement but a rhetorical licence, and the interesting question becomes what would have to be true for the rescue to be earned. So state, explicitly and in general form, a criterion for when a right-but-early defence is admissible, set it out before applying it to Malthus, and make sure it is not simply a description of the Malthus case dressed up as a rule. A criterion I would particularly like you to address is whether the defence requires the theory to have independently predicted something it was not designed to explain, rather than merely to be consistent with what later happened.

Third, test the criterion for over- and under-fitting by applying it to one further long-run prediction of your own choosing that has so far failed, from any field. Choose a case where the honest answer is not obvious. If your criterion acquits everything or convicts everything, that is a defect in the criterion, and the essay should say so and repair it.

Two house rules. Argue from the structure of the problem, not from authorities: do not cite, quote, or allude to specific scholarly works, and do not gesture at a literature the reader would have to look up. And where you invoke a number, a date, or an empirical regularity, use only ones you are reasonably confident of, and mark any you are not.
