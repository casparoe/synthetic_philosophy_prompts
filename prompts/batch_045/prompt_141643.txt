I'm three years into a philosophy PhD (mind and cognition, with a side of philosophy of biology) and I'm trying to turn a seminar paper on Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary — the ant colony that talks back to you — into something I could actually defend in front of a committee. My reading group and I keep circling a pattern that we can all describe but none of us can name, and it's driving me slightly mad. Here it is. Whenever we attribute some mental-ish state to the colony, two questions come apart that, for an ordinary believer, would just be two ways of asking the same thing. One question is: what is this state about, what would make it correct? That answer always comes out at colony scale — that there is food to the north, that the old nest site has become unusable, that the colony is short of foragers. The other question is: what is the state, what is its causal role actually made of? That answer always comes out at ant scale — which trails are carrying traffic, how many ants passed a fork in the last hour, which workers switched tasks. And they don't line up in a way I can put my finger on. Hold the ant-level story fixed and you can apparently change what the colony's state is about just by changing what's out there in the world; hold what it's about fixed and two different colonies will realize it with ant-level arrangements that share no description except one that already mentions the colony-level fact. My supervisor has started writing role-content decoupling in the margins of my draft, which feels too tidy for whatever is actually bothering me.

The case that really gets me is the self-directed one. Sometimes the thing the colony seems to be registering is a fact about the colony itself — that it has too few foragers relative to nurses, or that it just lost a chunk of its workforce. No individual ant, and no subgroup I can pick out, is in a position to represent that fact; from any ant's point of view the fact isn't even there to be noticed. And yet the colony reliably reallocates workers in the right direction, the way you'd expect something that had noticed to do. So the content isn't merely partly outside the system, the way I take externalist stories to have it — it's partly inside, but parked at a level where nothing local carries it. Then there's the flip case, which is maybe the same thing or maybe different: the colony's verdict changes, north to south, while every ant alive has exactly the dispositions and local information it had before the change. What changed is only which ants are alive and where they happen to be standing. The collective state flips with no individual state flipping.

So: does this have a name? Is there an established concept for it, either in the collective-agency and superorganism stuff or in the distributed-cognition end of things, under a label I just don't happen to know? I don't need papers or reading lists or who-said-it-first — I want the carving. What exactly is the mismatch, what are the nearest concepts to it, and how does what I described differ from each of them. I'm fairly confident it is not just ordinary content externalism, because the content here isn't merely wide, it's self-directed and unlocalized; I don't think it's plain multiple realization either, because the realizers share no neutral description; and I don't think appealing to the intentional stance settles it, since my question is whether the stance is picking out one thing here or two. If nothing established fits, coin a term for me, define it properly, and tell me what would count as a borderline case — and whether the same mismatch shows up anywhere else, in brains or in institutions, or whether its special to colonies. I'll also take suggestions about whether the flip case and the self-directed case are really one phenomenon, since my group is about evenly split on that and we've been going in circles about it for weeks.
