Something I keep going back and forth on, and I want help reconstructing the theory that's implicit in it rather than a verdict on who's right.

Look at how basically every deployed LLM system is actually set up. The system prompt is written in the second person and hands the system a first-person role: a name, a temperament, a style. Training uses preference rankings or thumbs up/down, and everyone involved describes this as rewarding or penalizing what the assistant did. When output is false, the lab's own writeup and the press say the model claimed such-and-such, got it wrong, confabulated, refused, tried to mislead. Safety teams build tests for whether the system can tell it's being evaluated and adjust accordingly, or whether it's quietly pursuing something other than what it says. And when an assistant-role output is awful, there's a whole incident apparatus: it did a bad thing, we patch it, we retrain it.

But in the same documents, the same people say it isn't an agent, it has no beliefs or desires, the word I in the output is just what a text like that looks like, and there's nobody home. And there's a second wrinkle: if a user gets awful text out by asking for a fictional scene or a villain character, nobody treats that as the system doing something bad — obviously that's just the character talking, and it's fine.

So: what view of agency, or of what kind of thing these systems are, does this package of practices presuppose? I dont just mean they're secretly realists about agency; I want the reconstruction. Does the practice strictly commit to the default assistant being an agent in a way the roleplay character isn't, or can the pure simulator picture — the model isn't anybody, it's completing a text, and the I belongs to whichever character that text makes most likely — generate the whole pattern including the blame asymmetry without saying the assistant is anybody? What is the feedback step even doing on that picture, if there's no one being rewarded? And what do the tests for evaluation-awareness or hidden goals presuppose that the rest of the practice doesn't?

Two more things I'd like pinned down. First, whether the inconsistency dissolves if agency is just a role we assign inside a practice of holding things answerable, so that the practice itself decides who counts and there's no deeper fact being inconsistent. Second: suppose we took the simulator story seriously as what the practice is committed to. What concretely would have to change in how system prompts are written, how feedback training is done, and how incidents get described? My guess is that the honest version says we aren't training an agent to be good, we're reshaping which characters show up in which contexts, and that blame talk is a useful shorthand that goes incoherent the moment you ask who exactly is being blamed — but I can't tell if that's really a different view or just the agent view with extra steps.
