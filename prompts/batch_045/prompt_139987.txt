Write a satirical internal document: Guidance Note 12-B (Rev. 7), "Application of the Doctrine of Double Effect to Unplanned Routing Events," issued by the Office of Ethical Assurance of a fictional autonomous freight-rail consortium to its onboard routing systems, operations staff, and post-incident auditors. Length: 900–1000 words. Target: the practice of treating the doctrine of double effect as a decision procedure that can be codified, audited, and certified, and the register of compliance drafting that would do the codifying.

Format the piece as a numbered guidance note with the following sections: (1) Purpose and Scope; (2) Definitions; (3) The Four Conditions and Their Operational Tests; (4) Worked Determinations; (5) Form 12-B: Declaration of Intended Effects. Use the tics of a real administrative document — revision history, "shall" and "may," cross-references to superseded annexes, a sunset date, a footnote conceding a difficulty and disposing of it by stipulation.

Constraints on content:

The doctrine must be stated correctly. Give the four standard conditions — the act itself must not be wrong independently of its consequences; the agent must intend the good effect and not the bad, the bad being foreseen but not aimed at; the bad effect must not be the means by which the good effect is achieved; and the good must be proportionate to the bad — and use the right contrasts throughout: intended versus merely foreseen, means versus side-effect, diverting a threat that already exists versus enlisting a new victim, doing harm versus failing to prevent it. A moral philosopher should be unable to find a substantive error in the doctrine as the note presents it.

The operational tests must be real ones rather than strawmen. Use, for example, the counterfactual test for means: if the foreseen death had somehow failed to occur, would the plan still have saved the five? If not, the death was doing causal work and the routing fails Condition 3. Use something like the aiming/acceptance distinction for Condition 2, and a comparative proportionality calculation for Condition 4. The comedy should arise from what these tests do when applied carefully to the cases below, not from the tests being silly or from the drafters being stupid.

The Worked Determinations must include: (a) the standard pair — a runaway vehicle that can be diverted from a track with five workers to one with a single worker, versus a vehicle whose only way of stopping is to rout it at a bystander whose body will jam the braking mechanism; the note should record, in an offhand clause, that most readers independently accept both verdicts, and the drafters should be slightly proud of this; (b) a variant in which the vehicle's sensor needs only to register a worker's reflective vest in order to brake, so that the worker's death is not physically load-bearing but is still what triggers the braking — is that a means or a side-effect?; (c) a variant in which the control model establishes with certainty that the worker on the side track will be killed ninety seconds later by an unrelated failure, so that rerouting sets back none of his interests; (d) a variant in which that worker has signed a waiver; (e) a variant in which the routing is chosen partly because the operations supervisor wants the worker dead; (f) a proportionality case, one life against five, in which the five are elderly and the one is young, where the note must confront the fact that proportionality demands the very kind of comparison the rest of the document forbids. Resolve (b) by a stipulation that later has to be quietly revised in the annex, and let (f) produce the one passage where the drafters' composure visibly costs them something.

Do not let the narrator notice that anything is wrong. The doctrine must never be called gerrymandered, post hoc, or incoherent, and no character should gesture at the reader. Unease may appear only in institutional form: audit exposure, litigation risk, staff attrition in the Ethics Assurance unit, the proliferation of revision numbers, an annex listing superseded tests. At least one determination should show the doctrine doing work that even a critic would grant it — the note's strictness blocking a maneuver that is, on its own terms, genuinely appalling — so that the satire stays fair.

The final form must contain at least one checkbox whose honest completion would incriminate the person completing it, and the note should address, deadpan, the operator who ticks the declaration of non-intention while privately hoping the foreseen death occurs.

Avoid puns on trolleys, tracks, switches, junctions, or derailment, and no jokes about lawyers. All humor must come from the structure of the doctrine and from what compliance culture does to it. Do not name or quote any real person, living or dead, and do not quote or cite any real philosophical text; every official quoted in the note is invented. The result should be funny to someone who has taught these cases and comprehensible to someone who has not.
