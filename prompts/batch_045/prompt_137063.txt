ok so I had this massive argument with a friend last night and I'd rather settle it with an exercise than with more shouting. The fight was about the gap between explaining consciousness itself and explaining why people say the things they say about it — why we report, judge, insist that there's a "what it's like", and get stubborn when someone tries to explain it away. His view: if you can explain all the reports, judgements and stubbornness, you've explained everything there is to explain, and the leftover sense that something is missing is just one more report. My view: that's a bait and switch, explaining the reports isn't explaining the thing. He goes: fine, then tell me what observation would tell those two apart. And I couldnt.

I'm running a 90-minute session at a consciousness summer school in two weeks, about 24 people, half undergrad philosophy, half CS grad students (so half the room will try to build the mechanism instead of arguing about it). Design an activity where they run into that gap themselves — that's all I want them to come away seeing. Two structural things I want built in: (1) during the main phase nobody may say "conscious", "experience", "qualia" or "what it's like" — they have to invent their own word for whatever they're talking about, and we only map it onto the real vocabulary in the last 15 minutes; (2) there should be a second round where the mechanism gets upgraded so that it now also produces the conviction that something was left out, and the room has to argue about whether that closed the gap or just moved it up a level.

Give me the setup, materials, rules, timeline and the debrief (including where exactly to name the two problems and let them use the real words). And be honest about three things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; which part of the distinction the activity literally cannot stage, so I just have to say it out loud; and what would make you scrap the design — i.e. what you'd see happening in the room that would convince you it's teaching my friend's "it's all reports all the way down" conclusion rather than the difference between the two explanations, and what you'd change instead.
