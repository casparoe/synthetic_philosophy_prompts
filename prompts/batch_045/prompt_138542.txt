hey — we run a fortnightly reading group that's mostly value theory people and epistemology people shouting at each other, and I've been asked to write up my philosophical profile on value and reasons so everyone else can take shots at it. Before I send it round I'd like to know whether it actually hangs together. Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) I'm a buck-passer about value — for something to be good, admirable, worth wanting just is for there to be reasons to favour it, with no extra reason-providing property of goodness sitting on top of the reasons. (2) The reasons in the analysans have to be the *right kind* of reason, and I want to say which ones those are without helping myself to value or to fittingness — because if the right kind just means "the ones that bear on whether the attitude fits," and fittingness is itself fixed by the good, then value has come back in through the window and the account isn't reductive anymore. (3) My own attempt, which I've started calling the scrub test: a consideration R is a right-kind reason to favour x iff R would still be a reason to favour x once you scrub away every causal upshot of your having the attitude — what it causes, what you'd get paid for having it, what it would feel like from the inside, all of it. The demon's threat gets scrubbed away; the painting's delicacy doesn't. (4) I'm an evidentialist about belief: only evidence is a reason for belief, pragmatic considerations can be reasons to get yourself to believe but never reasons to believe, and I take this to be the very same phenomenon as in the desire and admiration cases — one question about which reasons bear on whether an attitude is correct. (5) I also find pragmatic encroachment attractive: whether you know that p depends in part on what's at stake for you. (6) Wrong-kind reasons can be perfectly good reasons, even decisive ones — with the demon you have most reason to admire him and it would be a kind of irrationality not to end up admiring him, while it's still true that admiration isn't fitting and that there's nothing admirable about him. (7) And I think desires have correctness conditions fixed by their constitutive aim, the good, the way belief's correctness condition is fixed by truth.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture, and please dont just hand me a reading list — I can find the literature myself, what I want to know is whether *this package* works. Go through the pairs and tell me which are really in tension and which only look like it, ordered by how severe each tension is rather than flagging everything as equally bad, and for each real one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. One friend says (4) and (5) can't both be true and another says (6) contradicts (1); I suspect the second of those is fine but I can't say cleanly why. I'm also separately unsure about the scrub test itself: it tells me that the gratitude case is right-kind — my life was saved by someone who's actually kind of mediocre, and I have a reason to admire out of gratitude, and it survives scrubbing because nothing caused by my admiring her is doing the work — and that feels wrong to me, but I can't tell whether my test is broken or my intuition is. Promise and loyalty-ish cases bother me the same way. So: is (3) repairable, or should I drop it, and if I drop it am I stuck with a fittingness account, and would that actually be fatal to (1)?

Last thing: be explicit about where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. If you tell me two of these are compatible, tell me what argument or case would convince you they aren't, and if you tell me one has to go, say which piece of the story you're least confident you've understood.
