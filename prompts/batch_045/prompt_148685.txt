My partner is seven months pregnant and I've fallen down a rabbit hole I cant get out of. Shes been nauseous for most of the pregnancy and everyone — the midwife, her mum, half the internet — keeps telling us the body doesn't do anything without a purpose, so it must be protecting the baby from something, usually toxins in food, and we should trust it and ride it out. A friend of mine with a biology degree rolled her eyes at this and said it's exactly the Panglossian habit Gould and Lewontin were taking the piss out of with their spandrels — the triangular spaces you get for free when you set a dome on arches and then decorate — and that most features of an organism are like that, with the story about what they're for bolted on afterwards.

What I keep noticing is that both of them seem to be running on the same picture and just disagreeing about how good the builder was. The picture is: the organism is a machine built to a specification, a trait is a tool, and biology is reverse engineering — look at the part, work out what problem it solves, and you've got what it's for. My friend uses the same picture negatively: no competent engineer would have turned out this mess, so there's nothing it's for.

I'm a software engineer and I do this kind of reverse engineering every day on a codebase nobody alive understands, and in my world the picture is transparently unreliable: half the fields in our structs are padding or backwards-compatibility cruft, there are modules built for a feature that got cancelled in 2013 and then quietly reused for something completely different, and there's a function everyone still calls by the name of what it used to do. So my instinct is that the analogy is doing damage on both sides, but I cant say exactly where it breaks or whether it can be patched.

Could you audit it for me. I want the mapping spelled out — what plays the designer, what plays the specification, the client, the build process, the test suite, and the engineer standing over the finished machine trying to work out what a part is for — and then the places the mapping fails. I especially want the features of the engineered-machine case that the analogy quietly drags across into biology: that somebody had the function in mind before the part existed, that parts are modular and cleanly separable, that the design is near optimal given the constraints, that the raw material and the previous model don't constrain the designer the way ancestry and development constrain an organism, that you can scrap it and start again, that there's a single correct answer to what a component is for. And I want the reverse question too: what is each side smuggling in? Is the adaptationist version helping itself to a designer's intention, and is the spandrels version helping itself to a standard of what a decent engineer would produce, so that anything messy or suboptimal gets classed as a byproduct by definition rather than by argument?

Then, concretely: does either conclusion survive? On one side, that this nausea has a function and we should leave it alone; on the other, that it's a byproduct and current usefulness tells us nothing about how it got there. If the analogy is carrying either conclusion, say so and say what's left once it's taken away. And does the thing I reach for instead — a trait as cruft in a legacy codebase, something that arrived as a side-effect of a change made for a different reason, got reused later for something else, or was never cleaned up — distort things in its own ways? Code has authors and evolution doesn't, for one thing, and you can refactor, and nobody has to keep running the old build. Is that analogy better for some jobs and worse for others, and is there a better one?

One more thing if you can: what kind of evidence would actually tell you whether a given feature is an adaptation, an exaptation, or a spandrel, because the engineering picture makes it sound like you can just tell by looking at it.

Please write in British English, and in continuous prose — no headings, bullets, bold text or tables — just something I can read on my phone.
