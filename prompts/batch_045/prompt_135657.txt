Last night I got into a long argument with a friend — a historian by training, Goldhagen-leaning — about whether the ordinary-men picture and the willing-executioners picture of the German reserve police battalions are really competing explanations, and I came away unable to answer her best point. My own paper, which I present in three weeks, argues that they are not rivals: participation in the killing was motivationally overdetermined, and the apparent conflict between the two accounts comes from an unstated assumption that explanations must compete for a single causal role. I distinguish enabling background conditions from difference-making causes, and I propose to test difference-making not on the average member of a battalion but on the man at the participation threshold — the one who joined the shooting detail last, hung back, or asked to be excused — asking which factor's removal would have moved *him*. I also lean on a bit of apparatus I've been calling participation elasticity: how much the rate of participation moves per unit of situational pressure (visibility of peers, whether a way to opt out is publicly available, physical proximity to the killing) as against per unit of cultural saturation in the unit's region of origin. My friend's point, which I still can't answer cleanly, is that overdetermination is supposed to make both factors necessary in the sense that removing either changes nothing, yet the cultural account seems to predict something the situational account does not — the gratuitous, inventive cruelty, the torture and humiliation that exceeded what the orders required — and if it predicts that while the situational account doesn't, then they aren't symmetrical contributors at all.

The talk is a two-hour seminar slot, about forty minutes of presentation and a long, genuinely adversarial question period, and the room is mixed in a way I find harder than a specialist audience. There is a senior historian of the shooting war who thinks the archival record settles the matter in favour of the cultural-eliminationist reading and who treats situation-talk as a polite form of excuse. There are two historically-minded social scientists who do causal identification — natural experiments, counterfactual identification, treatment effects — and who will want to know exactly what my threshold counterfactual is identified *off* and what my units and variation are. There is a moral philosopher who works on moral luck and collective responsibility and who will ask what any of this does to blame, and whether a finding of overdetermination quietly excuses. And there are a handful of graduate students who mostly want to know what would falsify the view and how it would apply outside this one case.
