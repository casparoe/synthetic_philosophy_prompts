I recently joined the committee that signs off on every probabilistic statement my hospital publishes — in the decision-support product, in patient-facing trial material, and in our own papers — and I have to draft the minute on a new submission this week, so I want to know what we have actually been committed to.

Our rulings so far: we approved "this patient has a 0.5 chance of being allocated to arm B" where allocation used a certified 1:1 randomizer, and said 0.5 was correct rather than merely permissible; we struck the same sentence where allocation ran through a vendor randomizer we could not audit, on the grounds that the number would report our ignorance rather than the procedure and that no single number was ours to publish; we approved "0.3 probability that the emergency department exceeds capacity tomorrow," from our ensemble model, on condition that it be revised as the day's data arrive; we approved "0.2 probability that this patient's chest pain is coronary in origin, given the workup so far," on condition that it be revised after the troponin; and we refused to let any number appear as "the probability that this patient would have avoided the complication had we operated a day earlier," on the grounds that nothing we could ever observe settles it.

The new submission: last night our pipeline ran the current model version on Ms K's data. The run is deterministic — fixed version, fixed seed — and the recommendation is already sitting in the log, unreviewed. We want to show the reviewing clinician "0.7 probability that the stored recommendation is unsafe," where 0.7 is the output of a monitoring classifier whose calibration we validated on last quarter's cases. Nothing random happened, the seed-and-input combination occurred once, and the monitor's score falls in a region where we have only a handful of comparable past cases, so there is no well-populated class whose frequency we could quote instead; but the claim has a determinate truth value, and we could check it by opening the log.

Should we approve it, if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Please state the rules our precedents are jointly compatible with, what each predicts here, which precedent is the closest analog, what we would be committing to under each choice, and which single further case would best separate the rules that survive. I suspect we have been running some sort of chance-or-frequency rule without ever saying so, but I cannot see how that rule alone makes sense of the coronary case.
