I have about one postdoc-year of conceptual-research funding to spend on the cluster of problems around expected value fanaticism, and I want to put it where the answer does the most downstream work. I'm less interested in re-litigating whether the fanatical verdict in tiny-probability/huge-stakes cases is correct than in why the debate looks stuck: my sense is that both sides are defending different theses under one label. The decomposition I'd start from: (i) whether value is unbounded at all, or whether some finite cap on how good an outcome can be is independently defensible; (ii) whether, granting unboundedness, small chances of astronomically large goods must be allowed to swamp moderate certain ones, or whether a principled temperance or discounting move survives; (iii) whether the estimation noise and model uncertainty that come with any real-world estimate of such probabilities and stakes are enough to defuse the fanatical verdict — or whether treating that uncertainty correctly just reinstates fanaticism one level up; and (iv) the self-application worry: every argument for or against fanaticism is itself held with less than certainty, and if you are a fanatic you seem required to weight heavily the small chance that the true decision theory or the true axiology makes the fanatical verdict catastrophically wrong, which looks as if it either supports fanaticism trivially or collapses into paralysis. Where (iv) sits in the dependency structure is my main open question.

Give me a defended ordering for one researcher-year: which question is most upstream, which is genuinely tractable in a year by someone working alone, which matters but needs more than I have, and which I should drop because settling it wouldn't change what any funder, policy shop, or individual donor actually does. Be explicit about the criteria you're trading off, and about what evidence over the next year or two would move items up or down the list.
