Okay so I'm a philosophy PhD student and on Thursday I'm presenting a paper at the joint meeting of a perception-and-action lab and our philosophy of mind reading group — so the room is mostly vision scientists who do psychophysics and eye tracking, maybe ten of them, plus two philosophers (one Fodorian, one who thinks the whole penetration debate is sterile), a guy who does predictive-coding / Bayesian ideal-observer modeling, and one skeptic who was part of a multi-lab registered replication of the classic "backpack makes the hill look steeper" style studies and thinks all of it is demand characteristics and response bias. My thesis is narrow and I'd rather you target it as stated: I claim perception IS cognitively penetrated, but only by states that set the frame of reference or the action-structure of the task — task-set, intention to act, motor preparation — and never by beliefs, desires, emotions, or stereotypes; so the famous desire-and-value cases (the ones the big review a few years back mocked as judgment effects) all fail, while the action-specific ones (reachability, tool-use, the looming-versus-receding timing effects) survive. To make that testable I'm proposing an "adaptation signature": a genuine penetration effect should produce an aftereffect under prolonged exposure, transfer across eyes or effectors, and show up as a shift in sensitivity in an objective discrimination task rather than only in criterion or in verbal report. My punchline is that the surviving cases aren't really cognition reaching down into perception at all — they're perception being partly constituted by what the agent is ready to do — so the old modularity debate was asking the wrong question, because a module could be informationally encapsulated and still be motorically unencapsulated.
