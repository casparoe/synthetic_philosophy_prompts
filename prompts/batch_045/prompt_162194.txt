I'm a high school senior, and for four months next term I'll be living with a host family in Verenna, a city whose everyday conventions I've been briefed on in some detail. I'm also writing about them for my ethics independent study, and I'd be grateful for your help with the conceptual part.

Here is the situation as it has been described to me. First, restaurants add a mandatory fifteen percent charge labeled "service," but that money goes to the house rather than to the server; the local custom is to leave a further cash tip of roughly eight percent, and the figure is not written down anywhere. Second, at bakeries and ticket counters there is no line: people stand wherever they like near the counter, and a place in the queue is claimed by catching the eye of whoever is already waiting, so arrival order is tracked by mutual acknowledgment rather than by position in space. Third, pedestrians cross against the red figure as a matter of course, drivers anticipate this and treat a pedestrian who waits as an obstruction, and the ordinance is enforced perhaps once a year, usually against tourists.

Two ideas I've been working with, which I haven't come across in anything I've read and would like to see taken seriously. Call the first legibility: the degree to which an outsider could work out the convention by watching, without being told. Eye-contact queueing scores very low, the extra cash tip somewhere in the middle (you can see people putting bills down, but not the percentage), crossing on red very high. Call the second ratchet-sensitivity: whether one person's overcompliance raises what everyone else is then expected to do. That seems true of the tip, since a visitor leaving twenty-five percent makes locals who leave eight look stingy and invites resentment toward visitors generally, and false of the jaywalking. My tentative principle is that a convention is a genuine moral obligation for a visitor only if it is high in legibility, and that where a convention is ratchet-sensitive the visitor's obligation is not merely to comply but to comply at exactly the local level, neither more nor less.

Please describe the most important implications of applying that two-part test to the three Verennan conventions, for the broader question of whether everyday conventions are moral obligations at all and whether visitors must follow local ones. I especially want to know where the test yields conclusions I probably won't like: for example, whether it makes obligation depend on facts a visitor could not be blamed for failing to know, whether it handles the case where compliance is itself mildly dangerous, and whether ratchet-sensitivity could ever oblige someone to do less than they would otherwise freely choose to do.

Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Please write in American English.
