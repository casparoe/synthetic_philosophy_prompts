The half-formed thing I keep coming back to, which you should feel free to tear apart or promote: in the usual toy setups the agent selects a policy once, up at the prior, and then just executes it, so all the interesting content lives in what the policy says. But agents we might actually build or be seem to have nodes where they can re-run policy selection — they've got evidence they didn't have before, and they get to decide whether to re-derive a whole policy from the prior or just keep executing the old one. Call these recommitment points. Sometimes re-deriving is cheap, sometimes it's expensive, and sometimes you can foresee that at some future node you'll want to re-derive and get a worse result. What I can't see is whether there's a principled story about when an otherwise-updateless agent should permit itself a recommitment point and when it should pre-commit to not having one, or whether the question dissolves — e.g. because a correct updateless policy already specifies what happens at every node including the should-I-re-derive node, so the real content is all in the choice of prior and policy space and the recommitment structure is just a redescription. Relatedly I keep getting stuck on whether updatelessness is supposed to be a claim about what rationality requires or a claim about how to design/describe an agent, and on the multi-agent version, where two agents with different priors who both reason updatelessly meet each other and it starts to look like a commitment race rather than a bargaining problem.
