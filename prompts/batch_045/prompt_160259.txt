You are a philosopher of social science who is also fluent in contemporary work on causation and causal inference. I want a diagnosis, not a survey — and please don't spend any of your answer on citations, references or reading suggestions. I've read the canonical statements on both sides; they haven't moved me or anyone else. What I want is someone to tell me what kind of disagreement this is and what would count as progress in it.

Before you write your answer, brainstorm: spend a few paragraphs listing four or five candidate diagnoses — a verbal dispute about "cause" or "explanation"; a substantive metaphysical dispute; a substantive methodological dispute about standards of evidence; two questions being pursued under one label; bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for; a badly posed question — and for each, note what it would predict about the shape the debate actually has. Then commit to one, or to a weighted combination, and argue for it.

Here is the debate as it presents itself in my department's reading group, where we have now spent two years going in circles.

The individualist side says: any genuine causal claim about "the market," "the class structure," "the norm," "the bureaucracy" has to be redeemable in claims about individuals — their beliefs, desires, opportunities, expectations, and pairwise interactions. A macro-level generalization that cannot be regenerated from micro-level assumptions, or that collapses as soon as you hold the individuals and their relations fixed, is at best a convenient summary and at worst an explanatory fake. Their best evidence is a certain kind of case: a stark, apparently structural macro pattern that turns out to fall out of quite mild individual-level preferences once you let individuals interact locally, so that the macro "cause" turns out to have been a placeholder for a mechanism nobody had specified.

The holist side says: some causal claims are irreducibly social. Re-arrange the very same individuals and you get different outcomes; macro-level models are more stable across contexts than their micro counterparts; and a variable can be a real difference-maker for an outcome even though it is realized entirely in individual behaviour and has no additional causal machinery of its own. Their best evidence is the other kind of case: two populations matched on every individual-level attribute you like, differing only in the structure they sit in, differing also in outcome — and a macro-level model that travels to new settings while the micro-level one has to be re-estimated from scratch every time.

What makes the standstill frustrating is the amount of agreement. Nobody in my group thinks there are social facts floating free of the individuals who make them up. Nobody denies that macro-level claims constrain micro-level ones and vice versa. Both sides have stopped being impressed by the other side's favourite examples, and both sides can say, correctly, that the other side's examples are compatible with their own view. Yet nobody has moved, and the argument now consists largely of each side restating its criterion for what an explanation has to be.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that there are two different things going on and only one of them is a real dispute. First, that the two sides are tacitly using two different notions of cause: cause-as-production, on which the question is what machinery actually brings the outcome about, and the individualist is obviously right that the machinery is made of individuals; and cause-as-difference-making-under-intervention, on which the question is which variables in a model carry stable counterfactual information that a policy-maker could use, and the holist is plausibly right that macro variables do. If that's right, the metaphysical half of the dispute is nearly settled by stipulation and the methodological half is a dispute about which notion of cause the word "explanation" should answer to, which may be a verbal dispute with a policy stake. Second, that a genuinely empirical question is doing hidden work: the question of which level of description hosts the more invariant, transportable generalizations in a given domain. That question is domain-specific, slowly answered, and cannot be settled once and for all — which would explain why the global methodological argument never resolves even as the local empirical arguments do get resolved.

I also want you to audit a criterion I've been toying with as a way of marking progress, which I'll call realizer-swap invariance. Roughly: a macro-level causal claim earns a kind of autonomy just in case (i) the claim survives an exchange of micro-realizers — swap out the individuals who occupy the structure while holding the structure fixed, and the effect persists — and (ii) there is a well-defined intervention on the macro variable that is not merely a redescription of a bundle of micro-level interventions. Is that the right test, or is it question-begging in one direction or the other? Does it secretly collapse into a question about how we choose variables? Does the "hold the structure fixed while swapping the individuals" move require a constitution-versus-causation distinction that can bear the weight I'm putting on it — or is it incoherent, since swapping the realizers just is one way of intervening on the structure? And if the test is sound, would its satisfaction by some macro claim actually settle anything that either side cares about, or would each side simply reinterpret the result?

What I want from you, in order: the two positions stated as charitably as you can manage, with the real point of contact between them located precisely; your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, with the reasons that actually carry it rather than a list of the options; an assessment of my two-part hypothesis, including where it fails to explain the shape of the debate; the audit of realizer-swap invariance; and, given your diagnosis, a concrete account of what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical programme, a change of question — or an argument that no progress is to be expected and why.

Two further things. Mark your confidence as you go: separate what you take to be uncontroversial, what you think is genuinely contested, and what you are inferring rather than recalling. And if my framing rests on a confusion — if the two notions of cause don't actually line up with the two camps, if the stalemate is better explained some other way, or if realizer-swap invariance is incoherent as stated — say so plainly and explain why, instead of politely engaging with a badly posed question. I would much rather be corrected than accommodated.
