I've been going back and forth on the duty to remember after reading around the memory literature — Margalit on memory as an ethical relation, and the Rieff-ish complaint that therapeutic culture has turned letting go into a kind of health — and the big question everyone argues about (is remembering a duty at all, or just sometimes nice?) is too coarse to help me, because my verdicts on actual cases are all over the place. So here they are, gut only.

My grandmother raised me and died six years ago; I think about her maybe twice a year and feel zero guilt. Fine. My grandfather informed on neigbours to the occupying authorities and never admitted it; my dad knew and kept it quiet. I think passing that down is right and the silence was wrong. A friend broke a confidence fifteen years ago, apologized properly, and has been reliable since — I still factor it in when deciding what to trust him with, and I think that's fine, not a grudge. Different friend, same shape: apologized, I said I forgave him, and I'm still keeping a private ledger I'd be ashamed to say out loud. That one is wrong, and I can't tell you why it differs from the last one. My own worst action, years ago: I'm required not to let it fade, even though nobody asks it of me. My own stupid adolescent cruelty, which I've rehashed for twenty years without any change in how I act: I'm required to let that go — its vanity, not memory. Some guy on a train insulted me twelve years ago and I still remember his face; I owe nobody that, and I think keeping it is a small vice. But I do think I owe something to the dead of atrocities I have no connection to — the names, the record — even though no one alive is answerable and no relation of care ever existed, and I can't defend how selective this is, since there are atrocities whose victims I've never learned one name from and don't feel guilty about.

My tentative principle: memory is owed only where something is still owed — a live relation of care, or an accountability that hasn't been discharged — and it lapses the moment the debt is discharged. That handles the grandmother, both friends, and the train guy, and I think it breaks on my own wrongdoing, my own rumination, and the atrocity case, or at least needs extra clauses that end up doing all the work.

So: is there one principle that gets the pattern, or do some of my verdicts have to go? And what does yours say about the case I actually lie awake on — I'm the only person alive who knows a dead friend did something genuinely bad that nobody suffered from afterwards and nobody has any claim on. Do I owe it to anyone to keep remembering it, or is letting it die with him the right thing?
