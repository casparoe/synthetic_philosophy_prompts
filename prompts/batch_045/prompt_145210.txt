I've agreed to give the short lay address at a naturalization ceremony in three weeks: four minutes, delivered after the clerk's remarks and before the judge administers the oath, to a room of about seventy people from some thirty countries and their families. In the front rows will be a federal judge who has presided over these ceremonies for years and treats them as civic sacraments; behind them, several families with small children; near the back, a friend of mine who runs a legal-aid clinic for asylum seekers and is coming mainly to see whether I can get through the thing without what she calls nation-worship; and my mother, who has already told me that if I fail to say something about how fortunate they are to be here she will be disappointed in me. I have read the scripted parts of the ceremony, including the oath's requirement that they renounce allegiance to any other state, and I don't want to contradict it from the podium, but I don't want to ratify its rhetoric either.

The reason this is harder than a welcome speech should be is that I've been reading about whether compatriots really do have special claims on one another, and I've become convinced by a version of the view my old college roommate, who now works on this, sets out as follows. There are two candidate grounds for the special claims. On the relationship account, shared national membership is itself a valuable relationship, kin to friendship or family, and generates partial duties with the same shape as the ones I owe my sister. On the subjection account, the claims arise because we are jointly subject to, and jointly authors of, a single coercive legal order, so that what we owe each other is really what we owe anyone bound by rules we impose in each other's name: that the coercion be justifiable to them. He adds that on the second account the priority of compatriots is not a general permission to prefer them; it is a priority that holds only above a sufficiency floor that also covers non-citizens whom the order reaches, including people abroad who are affected by policies they had no say in. I find this convincing, and it is nearly useless for my purposes. It offers me no version of welcome home. It implies that most of the people in the room never chose this country in the morally loaded sense the ceremony presupposes, and that they have not thereby ceased to have demanding obligations to the people and places they came from — which is just what the wording of the oath appears to deny, and just what I cannot say in front of that judge. What it leaves me with is something like: you now have a say in an argument you were already bound by.

So write me the address: 550 to 600 words, plain enough to be followed by people listening in a second or third language, short-sentenced enough to read aloud and to be parsed without the scaffolding of a page. Concretely, I need it to avoid claiming that they chose us or that we chose them, to avoid national-family and melting-pot language of every kind, to avoid implying that their obligations to the people they left behind have lapsed, and to avoid both conceding and denying that the judge's framing is nation-worship. What I want in their place is a speech that genuinely welcomes, that I can deliver with conviction rather than with irony, and that someone holding the subjection account could endorse without wincing — one that makes the room feel addressed rather than instructed, and that doesn't ask the new citizens to feel grateful for anything. Then add a note of under 250 words flagging the two or three sentences where you came closest to saying something the account rules out, naming which of my four constituencies each of those sentences exists to placate, and what it would cost me to cut it.
