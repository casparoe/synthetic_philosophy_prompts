I sit on the Residential Standards Committee of a small liberal-arts college, which hears appeals under our housing agreement. Two clauses of that agreement have collided in a case we must decide next week, and I need a decision memo I can circulate to the other five members.

Clause 11 gives each resident final authority over their own conduct in their half of the room — including whether they play music there — and says the Committee may not override it. Clause 3 says that where all affected residents agree that one arrangement is better than another, the Committee shall give effect to that agreement.

The appeal: two roommates in a shared double. Maya is a light sleeper who studies in the room. She would most prefer enforced silence; but if music is going to be played, she would rather play her own records, whose volume and hours she controls, than listen to Tomas's, which she finds unlistenable. Tomas likes hearing Maya's records, is happy enough playing his own, and dislikes a silent room most of all. Note the consequence: both of them prefer that Maya be the one who plays. So Clause 3 seems to require Maya-plays over Tomas-plays; Clause 11 gives Maya silence over Maya-plays and gives Tomas Tomas-plays over silence; and we go round in a circle.

One member, a political-theory PhD student, says our two clauses are provably incompatible for some preference profiles and that this is one of them, so no amount of deliberation will yield a consistent ranking. Others think the difficulty is only that we are choosing among three coarse options, and that enriching the menu — headphones only, scheduled hours, listening in the common room, both play — would dissolve it. Two amendments are already on the table: restricting Clause 3 to residents' preferences about their own experience rather than about what their roommate does, and re-reading Clause 11 as a veto over what the Committee may order rather than as a constraint on how it ranks the options.

Draft the memo, addressed to the committee, with a concrete recommendation: how we dispose of this appeal, whether and how the agreement should be amended, and by when. Give reasons Maya and Tomas could each actually respond to. Engage the strongest argument against your recommendation, including the worry that a resident's authority over her own conduct was supposed to protect her against exactly the outcome you may be recommending, and the worry that a rule which only ever blocks certain pairwise comparisons does not deliver what residents think Clause 11 promised them. If you think the honest answer is that the Committee should decline to impose a room-wide arrangement at all and route the residents into some other process, say so and specify that process.
