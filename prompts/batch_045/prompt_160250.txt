I am a PhD student in computer science working on multi-agent systems, and I am writing a short paper for our reading group on cooperation between autonomous agents. I want to defend what I take to be a strong and practically useful claim: that the one-shot prisoner's dilemma stops being a dilemma once players can inspect each other's decision procedures, and that this yields a design recipe for getting competing AI systems to cooperate rather than defect.

The setting is the one where each player submits, not an action, but a program that takes the other player's program as input and outputs Cooperate or Defect. My proposed program, call it MirrorBot, is: read the opponent's source, run it on my own source, and return whatever it returns; if that computation has not halted within a fixed step budget, return Cooperate as a default. It seems to me this dissolves the dilemma. Reading the opponent's source tells me what it is going to do, so I never gain by defecting first; an unconditional defector gets only the mutual-defection payoff against MirrorBot, while MirrorBot against itself gets the mutual-cooperation payoff; and since no program can do better against a cooperator than cooperate, or worse against a defector than defect, cooperative-but-unexploitable programs look weakly dominant over every alternative, so any learning or evolutionary dynamics should converge on them. My advisor's response was that mutual defection is still an equilibrium and that I have changed the subject rather than solved anything. That objection seems to me to take the base game as normative when the whole point is that real agents choose procedures rather than actions. The application I care about is AI: if labs published their agents' decision procedures in a shared registry and agents read each other before interacting, competition between them would turn into cooperation.

So my questions. Is the step-budget-with-default-to-Cooperate clause the right way to make the mutual simulation well-defined, or is there a cleaner way of handling the recursion that does not depend on an arbitrary cutoff? Against an opponent that randomizes, cooperating most of the time but defecting with small probability, is my dominance argument still the correct way to show that MirrorBot is the rational submission, or should I switch to a proof-based variant that cooperates only when it can find a short proof that the opponent cooperates with it? And how should I answer the you-changed-the-game objection so that the result reads as a solution to the prisoner's dilemma rather than as a description of a different game?
