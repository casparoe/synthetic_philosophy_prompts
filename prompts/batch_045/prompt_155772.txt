I'm putting together a short position paper for a reading group I run with some people from the policy school — mostly economists, who keep asking me to "write down my axioms" before they'll engage at all — and in doing it I noticed my longtermist committments are probably less tidy than the way I present them in seminars. So here they are, roughly in the order I came to them:

(1) Strong longtermism: in expectation, the best things I can do with my time and money are the ones that make the very long-run future go better, and that stays true even when they're clearly worse for identifiable people alive now. (2) No discounting for distance: I don't discount future well-being because it's far off in time, and I don't discount the well-being of people whose existence depends on what I do — "merely possible" people — relative to people who'll exist either way. (3) Anti-fanaticism: I don't think you should maximize expected value when that means letting yourself be pushed around by tiny probabilities of astronomically large payoffs; I'm drawn to something like a probability threshold or a bounded/tempered value function, and my refusal to pay Pascal's mugger is one of the intuitions I trust most. (4) Lock-in over survival: I think the biggest determinant of how good the long run is will be which values and institutions get locked in — whatever gets built into the systems that persist — rather than whether we or our descendants survive at all, and I'm skeptical of the framing that puts existential-risk reduction at the top of the list by default. I also accept the "hinge of history" thought, that we're at a moment of unusual leverage, which is part of why (4) matters. (5) Moral uncertainty: the right response to disagreement between moral theories is to hedge — give the theories something like votes in a parliament — rather than acting on whichever one I find most plausible. (6) Near-term work: I'm happy to support global-health-type stuff, but I've been arguing it should be funded for its flow-through effects on the long run (relationships, institutions, information, whatever) and not for its direct benefits, which I take to be negligible by comparison.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and — ranked by severity — what the cheapest fix is for each real tension, plus which single revision would clean up the most. Please be opinionated: take a definite position on what has to go and why, don't just lay out the options for me to choose between, and mark clearly where you think you're on firm ground versus where a reasonable person could go the other way. For what it's worth, I'm mildly worried about two pairs — whether (3) quietly guts (1), and whether (5) quietly guts (1) and (2) — but I honestly don't know if those are the load-bearing problems or if I'm worried about the wrong thing, so don't take my anxieties as a map of where the trouble is.
