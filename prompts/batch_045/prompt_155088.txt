I am a novelist, and I have been asked to write the introductory essay for a reissue of Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*. Rather than the customary piece about cultural loss under colonialism, I want to attempt something narrower and, I hope, less forgiving: a reading of the novel through Chesterton's fence and the Burkean presumption in favour of inherited arrangements — the reformer who comes upon a fence erected across a road, cannot see what it is for, and proposes to clear it away at once; the reply that he should go away and think, and not be allowed to remove it until he can say what it was doing there. I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions, and I would much rather be told where the instrument fails than be handed a tidy application of it.

My sense is that the principle is not one principle, and I want the variants separated before any of them is brought to the novel. I have been using four labels of my own: a *dormant-purpose* fence, whose original reason has lapsed but which has quietly come to do some other work nobody designed; an *unstateable-purpose* fence, whose reason is real but tacit, carried in the practice itself and not available on demand to the people who keep it; a *ratchet* fence, whose value is precedential — it is worth keeping not because of what it does but because allowing it to be reopened licenses the reopening of everything; and an *identity* fence, whose function is to mark a boundary, so that no archaeology will ever yield a reason the reformer recognises as one, because the reformer's criteria (efficiency, health, consistency) are not the currency the fence trades in. Which of these does Chesterton's parable actually need in order to yield its injunction? Does the parable entitle the careful reformer to a verdict in favour of the fence, or only to a procedure — an enforced delay and an investigation — whose outcome might as easily be demolition? And is there a risk that my fourth category quietly immunises any practice from criticism?

The novel, as I read it, puts pressure on the parable in a way I have not seen discussed. Chesterton's scene presupposes a single going concern: the reformer and the fence-keeper are members of the same polity, which is roughly what Burke means by describing society as a partnership across generations. But in Umuofia the reformers — Mr. Brown, Reverend Smith, the court messengers, the District Commissioner — arrive from outside the practice's constituency, and the fence-keepers cannot always answer when their own people ask. Obierika is the figure I keep returning to: he asks why Ikemefuna had to die, and later broods over why a man should be driven into exile for a killing he did not intend, and the answers he receives are answers of authority rather than of reason. Does the principle then impose a duty on the fence-keepers to articulate reasons they have no vocabulary for, or a duty on the stranger to do the archaeology before he acts? Can the parable even be stated when the question "why?" is itself, inside the practice, a kind of impiety — when asking is what Okonkwo cannot do and Obierika can only do at the cost of standing slightly outside his own world?

There is a harder objection I want tested rather than smoothed over. Chesterton's fence is a remedy for one specific vice: reform that proceeds from ignorance. Much of what Umuofia suffers is not that. The colonial officers do not fail to understand what the egwugwu are for; they understand it well enough to know that unmasking an ancestral spirit will break the clan's machinery of judgment, and the destruction that follows — the church, the unmasking, the seizure and humiliation of the elders and the extraction of the fine, the meeting broken up, the body left hanging because custom forbids touching it — is carried out by people whose difficulty is not comprehension. What is the force of a principle about ignorance when the agent is a knowing destroyer? And does invoking it in this setting covertly flatter the practice — asking us to admire the ingenuity of twins left in the evil forest, or of the outcaste's exclusion — in a way the novel itself does not invite? I want the essay to say plainly which fences in the book the Chestertonian reading would defend and the novel would not let us defend.

Finally, the part I care about as a novelist. Achebe spends his early chapters doing patiently what the parable demands: showing a custom from inside, so that the Week of Peace, the masked judgments, the hostage from Mbaino, the argumentative patience of Mr. Brown beside the brittleness of his successor, all become legible as things people have reasons for. Against that stands the District Commissioner, who has already decided that Okonkwo's life will occupy a short passage in a book about the pacification of the tribes of the Lower Niger — description in the service of administration, arriving after and on top of the destruction. Is the novel itself an act of Chestertonian reason-giving, and does performing that archaeology for an outside reader concede the outsider's premise that reasons were owed? Is the form — sustained, inhabited, particular — doing work here that argument about the principle cannot do, or is it merely doing the same work more seductively, which for my purposes would be the more damaging verdict?

What I want from you is a reading: the scenes you would put at the centre of each claim, the verdicts on my four variants, the places where the Chestertonian lens distorts the novel or the novel outruns the lens, and, if there is one, the thing this book adds to the debate about inherited institutions that the philosophers arguing about it have not managed to say.
