I am a final-year undergraduate reading Philosophy and Economics, and I am currently taking a module on the history of political economy in which we have spent the last few weeks on Hume's *Political Discourses* (1752) — *Of Commerce*, *Of Money*, *Of Interest*, *Of the Balance of Trade*, *Of the Jealousy of Trade* and *Of Public Credit*. My essay question asks me to do something the seminars have not prepared me for: to say what Hume would have made of monetary and trading arrangements that came into being long after his death in 1776, and to say how far his own apparatus survives the change of institutions. I would be grateful for a reconstruction I can then argue against, rather than a summary of what modern economists say, which I already have from my other modules.

The cases I have been set are:

1. Pure fiat money managed by an independent central bank with an inflation target, with the bank expanding its balance sheet to purchase government debt in a slump — the policy that goes under the name of quantitative easing. Hume wrote at a time when the money stock was anchored to metal, when there was no institution setting a policy rate, and when he had in living memory seen John Law's paper-money system collapse in France in 1720, an experience that seems to colour his hostility to funded public debt and to paper credit.
2. A large economy running current-account deficits year after year without any drain of specie, because its currency is fiat, its exchange rate floats and capital moves freely. Hume's argument that a trading deficit is self-correcting — that a country losing money becomes cheap, sells more, and thereby recovers its money, so that no state can permanently drain or permanently hoard beyond its natural proportion — looks as though it was built for a world of commodity money.
3. A hard-capped, issuerless cryptocurrency with no use-value: a money whose supply is fixed by a protocol, whose value seems to rest on nothing but convention and scarcity, and which is now proposed as legal tender and as a reserve asset.

I also want to add a fourth case of my own, which I would like taken seriously rather than waved away: a state currency that is pure fiat, but whose supply is fixed by rule and which cannot be lent against twice over — a full-reserve, hard-capped government money, without a discretionary central bank and without a commodity behind it. Does Hume's framework contain any reason to prefer this to either discretionary management or a return to metal, or does his insistence that the absolute quantity of money is of no consequence entail that the whole question is a pseudo-question?

What I need from the answer, in roughly 1,200 words:

- Reconstruct the load-bearing commitments Hume actually argues for in the 1752 essays: the self-correcting character of the balance of trade; the claim that a gradual increase in money animates industry before it raises prices while the long-run level of money is of no consequence; the claim that interest is set by the proportion between borrowers and lenders and the extent of commerce, not by the quantity of money; the rejection of the zero-sum picture of trade; and the suspicion of public credit.
- For each, say which of its premises is an institutional fact of the 1750s rather than a general economic claim, and what happens to the argument when that fact is removed — and, where a mechanism disappears, say what if anything replaces it.
- Give Hume's likely verdict on each of the four cases, reasoning from those commitments rather than from the gesture that he was a proto-classical liberal who would have liked anything that looked like a free market.
- Deal explicitly with two tensions I cannot resolve. First: how can the quantity of money be of no consequence and yet its gradual increase be beneficial to industry — is that a real inconsistency, or does it turn on the gap between the short run and the long run, and does that gap survive when prices are sticky and expectations are formed about policy? Second: Hume treats the value of money as in some sense conventional rather than intrinsic, which ought to make him friendly to an asset with no use-value behind it; but he also seems to think that a money anchored to nothing invites either instability or the fiscal abuse he feared. Which way does the weight of his commitments fall?
- Mark clearly where the text underdetermines the answer, and where answering at all requires attributing to Hume a concern he never formulated: a target for employment as opposed to a price level, monetary sovereignty in an open economy, the distributional effects of asset-price inflation, or the idea that a government might deliberately run a deficit for demand management. I would rather have those flagged as inventions on my part than smoothed over.
