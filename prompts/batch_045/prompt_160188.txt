I've been going round in circles on something and I'd like an essay on it. Bit of background: I've got no academic background at all, I just got into this stuff through things I read online, so keep it in plain language and don't send me off to read someone's paper. I want your own reasoning argued out, not a summary of what's been written about it, and definitely no reading list.

Two puzzles set it up. In the first, there's a predictor everyone calls Omega, which is basically never wrong about what you'll do. It flips a coin out of your sight. If it comes up heads, it puts £10,000 in your account, but only if it predicted that you'd hand over £100 in the tails case. If it comes up tails, it turns up at your door and asks for the £100. So when it's standing there you know the coin came up tails and the £10,000 isn't coming — handing over the £100 doesn't cause anything except you being £100 worse off. As far as I can make out, the popular answer is that you should still pay, because the version of you Omega simulated is "you" in the way that matters, so being the paying sort of person is what gets you the money.

Second one: a blackmailer posts you a letter saying pay me £100 or I'll put something embarrassing about you online. They're a good enough judge of character that the letter only gets sent when exactly one of two things is true: either you'd pay, or your floorboards have woodworm. Never both, never neither. The answer I keep seeing is don't pay — and, weirdly, that getting the letter is good news, because for someone who never pays it means woodworm, so it works like a free pest inspection.

Here's the bit I can't settle, and it's my own addition rather than something I read. Say that in the first puzzle you find out about the whole setup a day early, and Omega lets you put yourself on a "do not ask" list for a tenner: if the coin comes up tails it simply won't come round. My first thought was great, I get the upside without the cost, since the money depends on what I'd do if asked, and I'd pay. Then I thought: hold on, Omega's prediction is a simulation of me, and the real me is now the sort of person who dodges, so the simulated me dodges too and never gets asked, which means no £10,000 either. So is dodging just refusing by another name, or is it a real free lunch? And does the answer depend on exactly what Omega said it was predicting — "what you'd do if asked" versus "whether you'll actually end up paying"?

Then I noticed the same question turns up the other way round in the blackmail case. There, it looks like you'd want to make sure you do recieve the letter, because it tells you something you can act on. So avoiding the situation looks clever in one puzzle and stupid in the other, and I can't tell whether that's a real difference or just me confusing myself.

So: write me an essay that sorts this out. Should you pay the £100 in the ordinary version? Should you take the do-not-ask option, and does it matter how Omega's prediction is described? What does your answer mean for whether the blackmail letter is worth getting? If you think my dodging setup is rigged or confused, say so and fix it. Weigh the arguments properly on both sides, then commit to a verdict and defend it — don't end on "it depends which decision theory you like".
