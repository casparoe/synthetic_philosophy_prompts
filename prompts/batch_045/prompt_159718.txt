A colleague of mine has firm, carefully hedged views about when the intentional stance is warranted — about when it is right to explain and predict a system's behaviour by attributing beliefs and desires to it. Her verdicts so far: yes for a crow, an octopus, a laboratory rat, a human infant, a nematode whose connectome has been mapped in full, and a current RLHF-tuned language-model assistant; no for a thermostat, a hand-tuned chess engine (she grants that saying what it wants and what it expects the knight fork to achieve predicts it perfectly), an old pattern-matching chatbot, and a hand-written tax-advice expert system grown to millions of individually readable rules that nobody can trace through in real time; and she treats an ant colony and a market as not candidates for the question at all. She insists none of this is about consciousness or moral status: she takes the stance toward systems she is confident are not conscious, and withholds it from some she is agnostic about.

The case I want to put to her is a small transformer trained by self-supervised prediction on a narrow corpus, which an interpretability team has since fully reverse-engineered: for any input they can name the circuit involved and predict the output mechanically, more accurately than any belief-description would manage — but which, within a session, does change what it does in response to what you tell it, so that mentioning that a function has been deprecated is enough to stop it recommending that function.

Predict her verdict, but put most of your answer into the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, what each of them predicts for this case, which one you would bet on and why, and the single further case that would best tell those rules apart.
