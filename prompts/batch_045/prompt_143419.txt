I've just joined a regional species list committee — we maintain the official checklist and the conservation listings — and I'm trying to work out what we actually think a species is before the next vote, because I suspect we've been running two different rules without noticing.

Here's our track record, as far as I can reconstruct it from the minutes:

1. Two forms of brush-finch that differ in song and plumage and don't interbreed across a 200 km overlap, though there's a narrow hybrid zone elsewhere producing fertile hybrids — voted two species.
2. Two oaks that hybridize freely (a lot of introgression, some individuals are clearly intermediate) but stay distinct in form and in the soils they grow on — two species.
3. Three all-female, asexual whiptail lineages of hybrid origin, each genetically distinct, each in its own habitat — three species.
4. Two populations of a minnow separated by a waterfall for maybe 8000 years: deep mitochondrial split, small morphological difference, never meet in the wild, but in the lab they interbreed and produce fully fertile young — one species.
5. Two look-identical lineages of a parasitic wasp on different host plants, no gene flow where they co-occur — two species, after a long fight.
6. A gaudy mainland colour form of a widespread bird, interbreeding freely across a broad contact zone — one species, subspecies at most.
7. A lichen: we ruled that the name belongs to the fungus and that we don't name the partnership.

A colleague says all this just tracks whatever question happened to be in front of us — Red List unit versus checklist name — and that there's no single rule to find. I'm not sure she's wrong.

Next month's case: two forms of a planthopper in the same valley, one on a sedge in the wet hollows and one on a grass on the dry ridge, ranges fully overlapping, each occasionally found on the other's host. Genome-wide markers show essentially one gene pool, except for two or three regions associated with host use. Mitochondrial DNA shows two deeply divergent clusters, perfectly associated with host plant. There's also a maternally transmitted bacterium, likewise perfectly associated with host plant: crosses between the forms in the lab fail in one direction, but once the insects are cured of the bacterium with antibiotics every cross succeeds and the offspring are fertile and do fine on both hosts. And when the bacterium occasionally moves between forms in the wild, the host association reshuffles over a few generations.

So: how should we vote if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Give me one call, not a menu — one species or two — and the rule you think we're running that delivers it. Tell me which of the above is the closest analog and which one it's really in tension with, what principle we'd be committing to with each choice (including the pluralist option that we've just been answering different questions), and then name the further case that would tell the surviving rules apart. Be defiantely committal: if you think our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say so, but still make the call and tell me how confident you are in it.
