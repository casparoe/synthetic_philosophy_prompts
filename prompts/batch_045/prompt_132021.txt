I'm drafting a paper on accounts that treat phenomenal consciousness as a user illusion — an interface a cognitive system presents to itself rather than a property things literally have — and I want the specification before I start comparing the accounts. Write the desiderata any such account must meet in order to be usable as a working hypothesis in an empirical research program: one with which a researcher could return a verdict, however hedged, on whether a given system has experiences (a patient emerging from general anesthesia, a split-brain subject, a non-human animal, a large language model), and which says what kind of evidence could bear on that question at all.

For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. At minimum: what the account owes the illusion-for-whom regress, given that an illusion normally needs someone it appears to; the difference between denying the property and denying the judgment, and the regress that opens if the judgment is demoted to illusion as well; the obligation to explain the specific structure of the appearance — ineffability, immediacy, apparent intrinsicness — rather than gesturing at cognitive simplification; the asymmetry between a graphical interface, which simplifies something real, and the claim that nothing of the relevant kind is there to be simplified; the degraded and partial cases (blindsight, dreams, the apparent richness of the visual field, a system whose reports are produced by a confabulating reporter with no access to the processes it reports on); and the apparent causal role of experience in learning and deliberation.

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement excludes the most candidate views. Close with a one-line scorecard, at most, for illusionism, realist representationalism, and one further family of your choosing.
