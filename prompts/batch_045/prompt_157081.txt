I build distributed systems for a living; my only philosophy is a couple of undergraduate electives long ago, so I'd much rather have this framed in terms of data structures, budgets, and computation than in modal-logic notation. I've been reading about formal models of belief of the kind where an agent's belief state is represented as a set of possible worlds, or a probability distribution over one, and about the standard objection that such models make agents logically omniscient. I get the headline version: the model can't distinguish someone who believes P from someone who believes P but hasn't gotten around to deriving that P entails Q. What I don't get is why this is treated as a deep problem rather than an implementation detail. If I were building it, I'd store beliefs explicitly and derive consequences lazily under a compute budget, so that what the agent believes at time t is what it has actually computed plus whatever it has cached.

When I've said this out loud, the objections I get back seem to me to be two different complaints, and I suspect they get conflated. Call the first one derivational closure failure: the agent fails to believe consequences that are cheap and sitting right there. Call the second reformulation insensitivity: the agent believes a claim in one form and not in another that is trivially, mechanically equivalent to it — it holds the factorization of N in a table, and so believes that N equals a times b, while having no opinion whatsoever on whether N is composite. My sense is that the first is a benign idealization, of a piece with the fact that no belief report anyone gives is complete, while the second is the real embarrassment, because there it stops being clear that the model can say what the agent believes at all: belief looks like it attaches to sentences rather than to whatever the sentences are about.

There's a third thing I haven't seen anyone name, and I want to know whether it's the same animal. Any system like this has to reason about its own reliability: decide how much to trust a result memoized by an earlier version of itself, or produced by a module whose source it can audit but whose runtime it can't predict. Call this self-trust under boundedness. My worry is that the moment I give a clean story about why the agent should trust its own derivations, I've smuggled omniscience back in one level up, since being confident that its own checker is sound looks like it requires something very close to knowledge of all the consequences of its own rules.

So, three things. First, is my two-way split the right way to carve up what philosophers call the problem of logical omniscience, or does the real fault line run somewhere else entirely? Second, why, at a gut level, does modeling belief with propositions rather than sentences make the second failure mode look incoherent rather than merely unrealistic — and is that incoherence a genuine cost, or just an artifact of how representational my own intuitions are? Third, is the self-trust worry a third instance of the same problem, or a different problem wearing similar clothes, and if it's different, what would a satisfying answer to it even look like? Argue it out concretely; I don't need references or reading suggestions.
