OK so I think I've talked myself into something and I need you to find the seam.

Setup, no citations needed: three judges, agenda {P, Q, R} where R holds iff P and Q both do. Judge 1 accepts all three. Judge 2 accepts P, rejects Q and R. Judge 3 rejects P, accepts Q, rejects R. Propositionwise majority gives P, Q, not-R — inconsistent. That's the discursive dilemma, nothing new.

What I cooked up instead is a rule I've been calling MAXCON: don't decide propositions one at a time, look at every complete consistent judgment set, score each one by how many (judge, proposition) pairs it agrees with, and take the highest scorer, ties broken by a fixed ordering. I already hit the obvious problem: in my three-judge case it's a three-way tie — {P,Q,R}, {P,not-Q,not-R} and {not-P,Q,not-R} all score 5 — so the tie-break decides everything and the "anonymous" rule isn't really anonymous. Set that aside; assume a bigger group or a cleverer tie-break.

Here's the argument I keep failing to puncture:

(1) A group's collective judgment set should be consistent and deductively closed; otherwise the group can't act on it, can't be held to reasons, and in the belief case can be Dutch-booked. Call that collective rationality.
(2) The discursive dilemma is a problem for propositionwise majority voting in particular, not for group decision as such.
(3) MAXCON outputs a consistent, closed set on every profile of consistent individual inputs — that's just how it's built — it's anonymous up to tie-breaking, and it doesn't override a unanimous verdict (I think).
(4) So there's a democratic procedure that always satisfies collective rationality, which means there's no in-principle tension between democracy and collective rationality.
(5) The impossibility results I've seen are all shaped like this: no procedure has universal domain + anonymity + unanimity + independence + collective rationality. So independence is the only thing MAXCON is paying, and independence — whether the group accepts P depends only on who accepts P — is a technical axiom nobody outside the formal literature has ever cared about.
(6) Generalizing: any impossibility result of that shape is dissolved by dropping whichever axiom does the work and picking outcomes globally instead of proposition by proposition. So the discursive dilemma, Arrow-style cycling, the strategic-voting results — all of them are artifacts of bundling axioms, not real tensions.
(7) Therefore a group can be fully democratic and fully collectively rational, and the paradoxes are bookkeeping artifacts.

The thing that makes me suspicious: step (6) would also "dissolve" the whole mess by dropping anonymity instead and handing the agenda to one well-informed expert, and nobody thinks that's a solution. But I can't say what's different about (5).

If you had to keep one thing, I'd keep (1) and the conclusion in (7); I'd sooner give up my strong claims about MAXCON in (3) than those. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me — do I lose the conclusion, or just the cheap route to it?
