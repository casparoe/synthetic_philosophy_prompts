You are a political philosopher who also works on tax policy. I sit on a commission that is redrafting our inheritance tax, and I don't want to vote on instincts I can't state as a principle, so please work from my cases rather than from the standard pro-and-con list. Brainstorm several candidate principles before committing to any of them, and be explicit about where each one breaks. No citations or reading lists.

Here are my verdicts, as I actually have them.

1. A distant acquaintance leaves me eight million in index funds: tax it hard, seventy per cent or more, and I feel no complaint.
2. My cousin inherits the dairy farm she has milked since she was fifteen, worth about as much on paper but illiquid and unsellable without ending the farm: tax it lightly or not at all.
3. A founder hands her company to a son who has run the sales side for ten years: light tax. The same company to a son who will flip it to a competitor the week after probate: heavier tax. I know this is odd, since the assets and the recipient's resulting wealth are the same in both halves; what it seems to track is whether the firm survives.
4. A grandparent pays private-school fees directly, during life: I am uneasy about taxing that, even though it is the same transfer of advantage as a bequest and shapes the child's competitive position even more directly.
5. Wealth traceable to a legally authorised but now-condemned wrong, held by a grandchild who had nothing to do with it: I want this taxed harder than ordinary inherited wealth, and the thought "she did not earn it" does not explain why, since that is equally true of ordinary inherited wealth.
6. An heir with severe disabilities whose entire inheritance buys care and never converts into any advantage over anyone: my objection to inheritance largely evaporates, even at twenty million.
7. A perpetual dynasty trust paying great-grandchildren unearned income indefinitely: this strikes me as worse than the same sum handed over outright at death, and worse than I can justify by size alone.
8. The inheritances I think matter most for equal opportunity — a good school district, a parent's professional network, a stable household — I do not want taxed at all, and I cannot say why they fall outside the base while the farm falls inside it.

Two cases I go back and forth on: two siblings split ten million, one a surgeon and one a care worker, and some days I want the tax to depend on what each already has, other days I think the recipient's own earnings are irrelevant. And I keep changing my mind about whether money that funds a life of no competition but real social value — endowing a young mathematician to do research instead of consulting — is the best or the worst case for taxing.

The principle I keep returning to is: tax transfers at death to the extent they hand the recipient unearned competitive advantage, and exempt them to the extent they preserve a form of life the recipient's plans are already built around. It seems to handle 1, 2, 3 and 6 and to have nothing to say about 5 and 8.

What I want from you: state that principle, or the two or three best candidates, precisely and clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; say what each predicts for the cases I have not settled — the unequal siblings, the endowed mathematician, and inheritance received at seventy rather than twenty-five; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best available principle cannot accommodate and which ones I should give up.
