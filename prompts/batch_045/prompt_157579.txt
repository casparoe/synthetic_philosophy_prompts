i'm putting together the final for my upper-level aesthetics seminar and i want one essay question on aesthetic testimony that a student can't just coast through by reciting the acquaintance principle vs. credentialism. 90 minutes, about 1000 words, 20 marks, no citations needed — everything they need should be in the question itself.

Background they'll already have: the acquaintance principle says you get knowledge (or justified belief) about a work's aesthetic properties only from first-hand experience of that work. Two live explanations of why: the epistemic/perceptual one (aesthetic properties are response-dependent, so a second-hand belief lacks the right kind of basis or even the right content) and the value one (deferring forfeits something worth having — autonomy, making the judgment your own — even if the resulting belief is perfectly justified).

The case i want to hang it on: a one-night-only light installation in a dome, tickets assigned by lottery, you don't get one, and afterward the piece is destroyed by design with no recording permitted. A critic who has reliably tracked your own reactions across dozens of works you've both seen tells you it was sublime but edged into sentimentality. So the standard "aesthetic testifiers are unreliable / taste is too idiosyncratic" escape route is closed off.

Two things the question should force students to do. First, the accessibility test: should an adequate account say the same thing about testimony concerning works you can never experience as about works you could experience but haven't? Which of the two explanations passes that test, and does failing it actually refute the other one, or just relocate it? Second, the downstream use: you go on to use that belief (a) to decide whether to fly to the artist's retrospective in another city and (b) as a premise in an essay arguing that her late period turns sentimental. Should the acquaintance principle treat those two uses the same way, and whatever they answer, what does it show about what the principle is supposed to be for?

Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points with the marks for each (total 20) plus a one-line note on how students typically get each wrong. Name the A/B split specifically: I want the B answer to be the one that notices the case is doing diagnostic work but doesn't follow through on what it diagnoses.
