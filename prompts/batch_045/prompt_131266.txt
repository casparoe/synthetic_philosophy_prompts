I'm 71 and retired three years ago from a career in experimental low-temperature physics — most of it was thermometry and calorimetry, which in practice meant most of it was arguing about error bars, calibration chains, and whether anybody's thermometer could actually be traced back to a standard. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy seminar at the local university (the department lets auditers in if they promise not to talk too much, a promise I find hard to keep), and I've gotten stuck on the business of interpersonal utility comparisons. The question in the background is an ordinary policy one: does this tax-and-transfer help the people it helps more than it hurts the people it hurts? Everyone seems to agree the answer depends on whether well-being is interpersonally comparable, and if so whether merely ordinally, or only "unit comparable" (same units, different zeroes, as I understand it), or fully comparable including levels — and then on whether a given moral theory, utilitarianism being the obvious customer, is entitled to help itself to that much comparability. My problem is that I can't tell whether any of this is a claim about measurement of the kind I'd recognize from a lab meeting, or something else wearing measurement language. I've read a little Harsanyi and a little Sen and I do not want a literature survey or a reading list — I can't look anything up and I don't much care who said what first.

So here's what I'd like. Restate the comparability menu — intrapersonal von Neumann–Morgenstern cardinality, ordinal and non-comparable, unit comparability, full level comparability — in the vocabulary I actually think in: quantity and dimension, scale type (nominal through ratio), unit and zero point, gauge freedom and invariance groups, calibration against a standard, traceability, extensive versus intensive quantities, latent variables behind a monotonic instrument response, identifiability, error propagation, and what you can and can't conclude when your measurement is only defined up to a transformation group. Take them one at a time and be honest about where you have to fudge. Two things I especially want you to grapple with: first, whether there's any operation in the welfare case that plays the role that thermal contact plays for temperature — an equivalence relation like "in equilibrium with" that lets you compare across systems before you have a unit at all, or whether the absence of any interpersonal analogue of heat flow is exactly the point; and second, my own pet idea, which I half suspect is a false friend: that each person's utility function is a fiber with its own affine gauge freedom, so that comparing two people is like comparing vectors living in different tangent spaces, which requires a connection to parallel-transport them together and a metric if you want to compare magnitudes — extra structure, not something the manifold hands you. If that translation smuggles in assumptions (smoothness, a common underlying space, a natural zero) that welfare economics doesn't have, say so plainly.

Then give me the residue report, since that's the part I'm really after. Which claims translate cleanly, which only with distortion, and which have no expression at all in metrological vocabulary? My guesses, which you should feel free to demolish: that the invariance talk is already just measurement theory and translates perfectly, possibly because the philosophers borrowed it from there; that "calibration" translates only badly, because there is no welfare kilogram in a vault in Paris and no chain of comparisons leading back to one; and that three things have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — the fact that the demand for comparability seems to come from the moral theory rather than from the data (a metrologist would just say: then you don't get to have it), the idea that there might be a fact of the matter about how much A gains versus how much B loses even though nothing could ever measure it, and the odd normative notion that some information can exist and yet be inadmissible — that the social decision rule may not use it, for reasons that don't look epistemic. Is that last one perhaps just a prior, or a robustness requirement under model uncertainty? I don't think so, because in my experience priors get washed out by data and here no data is ever arriving. Finish by telling me what you think the residue means: is it a defect of my vocabulary, which was built for quantities you can get at with instruments and has no slot for prescriptive constraints or non-empirical facts; or is it a sign that the philosophers are running three different questions under one heading — an empirical psychological one about whether there's a common currency in human beings, a formal one about invariance classes, and a normative one about what a social decision rule is permitted to presuppose — and that the fight is really about the third one while everybody talks as if it were the first?
