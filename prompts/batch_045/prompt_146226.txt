So my friend Dana has really firm opinions about who is allowed to call someone else out for something, and I've started keeping a list of her verdicts because the pattern is kind of bizzare once you line them up. She calls it "standing," which I think she picked up from somewhere online.

All of these are about the same thing — people who borrow money from friends and then quietly never pay it back:

- Dave, who still owes money to two people, tells our roommate "you're a jerk for not paying me back." Dana: no standing.
- A total stranger with no connection to anyone involved, who has never borrowed a cent, publicly calls out a guy who stiffed her. Dana: standing, fine.
- Priya stiffed people for years, then paid everyone back with interest, apologized to each of them, and hasn't done it since. Same sentence to the roommate. Dana: standing, not even a close call.
- Someone who has never done it but says out loud "I'd have done exactly the same thing if I'd been that broke." Dana: standing, and she says what you *would* have done is irrelevant.
- A woman who runs one of those pay-in-four apps and makes her living getting people into debt they can't clear, complaining that a friend never paid her back. Dana: no standing — this was the one she got most worked up about, and the app woman has never borrowed anything herself.
- A guy who shoplifts compulsively, which is a totally different kind of thing, complaining about the same roommate. Dana: standing, obviously.

So it's not about being close to the person, not permanent, not about what you'd have done, and not general moral cleanliness. But it's also not just "did you do the thing," because of the app woman.

New case, which came up for real last week: Marcus currently owes money to three people including me. He told our roommate that not paying me back was out of line — and in the same breath said he's just as bad, that he hates this about himself, that he's a hypocrite for even saying it, and he's actually started paying me back. Nothing he said claimed he was better than the roommate; if anything he ranked himself lower.

I'm less interested in yes/no than in the machinery. What rule is Dana actually running, what other rules fit all six of her old verdicts just as well, and which of those come apart on Marcus? Give me your best guess on her answer and say why you prefer it. And if her old verdicts genuinely leave it open, tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to with each answer.

Last thing: what single extra case should I bring up next time to tell the rules apart? I was thinking either a guy who does it, blames someone, and only admits he's just as bad later in private — or someone who never did it himself but paid someone else to do it on his behalf. Which of those is more diagnostic, and for which pair of rules?
