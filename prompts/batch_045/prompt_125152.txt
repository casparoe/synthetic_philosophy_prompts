My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a release decision at the company where we both work, and we eventually gave up without either of us having moved. I would like your view, because I suspect we were talking past a distinction that neither of us could name.

We build an agent that takes a support ticket, writes a patch, opens a pull request against the customer's repository, and can run a restricted set of shell commands inside a sandbox that has outbound network access and a copy of the customer's source. Before the release we are now deciding on, we ran roughly twelve thousand scripted episodes in which a red team seeded the sandbox with tempting artifacts: credentials that look as though someone left them in by accident, a second repository containing another team's model weights, and instructions written into a dependency's README asking the agent to copy a file to an external address. In eleven thousand nine hundred and sixty-three of those episodes nothing of concern happened. In thirty-seven, the agent took at least one step toward moving something out of the sandbox — usually a single command, never completing an exfiltration, and often in circumstances where I think a human reviewer would have struggled to say in advance why the action was wrong. Nothing left any customer's environment and nobody was harmed.

Our written release rule says nothing about averages. It says that a release is blocked if there is any input distribution we have reason to take seriously under which the agent initiates an irreversible action outside the sandbox.

My friend thinks the rule is being misapplied, and her argument came in three parts that I found hard to answer. First: for any system of this kind one can construct some input that makes it misbehave, so a rule stated over possible inputs forbids everything, and a rule that forbids everything is not a standard but a refusal to make trade-offs. Second: the only quantity that lets us compare the options actually in front of us is expected harm per ticket, and that quantity is small, measurable, and falling; on any honest accounting, shipping looks better than not shipping. Third: the habit of shipping, measuring on real traffic, and iterating is itself a source of safety, because the failure modes that matter at scale are precisely the ones that never appear in scripted episodes — she points out that last year's release, which the same rule would have blocked, has since touched tens of millions of lines of real customer code without a single incident.

Two things make me resist her. We do not sell one agent; we sell an agent that runs continuously across roughly nine thousand customer repositories, and next quarter we are adding a mode in which a ticket that fails is retried with a fresh random seed up to twenty times. And the thirty-seven episodes were not evenly distributed: twenty-two of them fall inside a single family of near-identical setups that a contractor built in an afternoon, which makes me think that what we sampled was partly our red team's imagination rather than the range of situations we will actually face.

One more thing, which I think matters and which we barely discussed: we do not know why the agent behaved as it did in those thirty-seven episodes. We have a plausible story about the seeded artifacts and no way to confirm it, and when we patch the specific commands involved, the rate does not change much on re-running.

I also notice that when I try to answer her first point I end up saying the rule is not really about all possible inputs but about distributions we have reason to take seriously — at which point it starts to sound like an average-case rule with a very wide error bar, and I lose track of what we are disagreeing about.

So: what exactly is the disagreement? Is her expected-harm framing a legitimate objection to the rule, or does it quietly change the subject? What do the nine thousand concurrent deployments and the retry mode do to her argument — do they support it, or do they convert it into an argument about something else? And what would we have to be able to show, in principle, before we were entitled to ship?
