"Here's the rule. Take a partition E1, ..., En and suppose some experience moves your credence in the cells from P(Ei) to q(Ei) without making any of them certain. Then your new credence in any hypothesis H is just the weighted average P_new(H) = Σ_i q(Ei)·P_old(H | Ei), and everything else falls out of that. First, if you do become certain of one cell — q(Ek) = 1 — the sum collapses to P_old(H | Ek), which is ordinary conditionalization; so strict conditionalization is just the special case where the evidence arrives with certainty. Second, the rigidity condition built into the formula, that P_new(H | Ei) = P_old(H | Ei), isn't really an extra assumption you have to go out and check: it's just the law of total probability applied to your new credence function. You're holding the conditional distribution inside each cell fixed and only redistributing weight between cells, so there's nothing normative going on here — it's a theorem, not a principle. And if rigidity ever looks violated, you can always repair it by choosing a finer partition until it holds. Third, because the rule is just a weighted average, it's fully general: the order in which you take your uncertain evidence doesn't matter, you land on the same credence function either way. Fourth, it's also the minimal-change rule — of all the ways of accommodating your new marginal on the partition, Jeffrey's rule is the one that moves your overall credence function as little as possible. And here's a nice payoff: it dissolves the problem of old evidence. If E is something you've known for years, conditionalizing on E does nothing because P(E) = 1, but with Jeffrey's rule you can just dial P(E) down to, say, 0.99 and let E confirm the theory after all. Bottom line: real experience never delivers certainty, so strict conditionalization is a limiting case that basically never applies, and probability kinematics is what finally turns Bayesianism into an actual theory of learning from experience."
