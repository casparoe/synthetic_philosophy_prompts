My old moral philosophy tutor — retired now, posts case notes on a blog maybe six people read — has handed down verdicts on a big pile of cases over the years, and I'm trying to work out what he's actually running, because I want to know what he'll say about a case I made up to annoy him.

His verdicts so far (permissible, or not):

- Standard switch case: divert the runaway trolley off the track with five onto a spur with one, where a buffer stops it anyway: permissible.
- Footbridge: shove the heavy guy off the bridge so his body stops the trolley: not permissible.
- Loop version: divert onto a spur where the guy's body is what stops the trolley before it can loop back around to the five: not permissible.
- Transplant surgeon killing the healthy patient to harvest organs for five: not permissible.
- The two obstetric cases: crushing the skull of a baby that can't be delivered and will otherwise kill the mother: not permissible. Removing a cancerous pregnant womb, where the fetus dies as a side effect: permissible.
- Bombing a munitions plant, where the civilian deaths in the neighborhood do none of the work of winning the war: permissible. Bombing civilians to break morale: not permissible.
- One I added to test something: same as the switch case, except the one person on the spur is anonymous — you know there's exactly one track worker somewhere on a long spur, you don't know who or where, and the trolley would be stopped by the buffer at the end regardless, so the death isn't what stops it. He said permissible, and added that anonymity doesn't change anything in that case.
- Last ventilator, five patients who all need it, no medically relevant difference between them: he okays allocating it by lottery: permissible. Killing one patient to free it up for another: not permissible.

He keeps saying that the condition doing all the real work in this area is the one about the good effect not being brought about by way of the bad one, but I'm not sure that's what he's actually doing.

Here's my case. Runaway freight train, five people on the main line, dead for sure if nothing happens. The only way to stop it in time is for it to hit something heavy on one of five spurs, and there's one maintenance worker standing beside a heavy cart on each spur: whichever spur gets picked, the collision with that worker and his cart is what stops the train, and that worker certainly dies. The switch is thrown by an automatic system that selects one of the five spurs with a random number generator — the yard was built that way on purpose, so no worker is ever singled out in advance. At the moment the controller authorizes the switch nobody knows which spur will be selected, the controller has no preference, and he'd be delighted if all five workers had wandered off to lunch. One expected death instead of five certain ones.

What I want: your prediction of his verdict, the two or three rules that fit his whole track record about equally well, which one you think he's really running and why, what each of those rules says about my case (especially the ones that come apart here), and the one further case I should put to him that would actually seperate the rules that agree on mine. Also flag if there's some detail I haven't thought about that flips the answer — the workers knowing about the setup, the randomizer being installed specifically for this occasion, the death being necessary versus the worker being necessary, whatever. My worry is that any rule that turns on what's in the agent's head can be gamed with a randomizer, and I can't tell whether that's a bug in the rule or a bug in my case.
