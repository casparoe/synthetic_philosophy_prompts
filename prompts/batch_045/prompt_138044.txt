I'm a high school senior working on an independent project about tie-breaking in rational choice, and I want you to compare two positions on Buridan's ass rather than summarize either one. By "Buridan's ass" I mean the story as it is usually told: a hungry donkey standing exactly midway between two identical bales of hay, equally attracted to each, with no consideration whatever favoring one over the other. (Later retellings often put the example to work in arguments about the will; I don't need the historical details, so please don't build the answer on them.)

The two views I want compared:

(1) The permission view. When an agent is genuinely indifferent, either option is rationally permissible. Rationality narrows the field of admissible options; it does not also require a further reason to cut that field down to one. On this view the donkey has no real problem: it eats. Starving between the bales would be a failure of nerve, not of reason. Going left, flipping a coin, and picking the bale with the slightly funnier shape are all equally acceptable, because a tie-breaker need not be a reason.

(2) The reason view. Rational action is action for which the agent has a favoring reason, so a tie is not something to be dissolved by fiat; it is a sign that the options are incomparable, or that deliberation is unfinished. On this view the donkey's predicament is genuine: whichever way it moves, it cannot act for a reason, so it cannot act rationally. Coin flips don't rescue it, since the coin landing heads is not a reason to prefer the left bale.

To make the disagreement bite I've been using two devices of my own, and I'd like you to run both views through them. First, the serial ass: the very same two bales, every morning, for a hundred mornings, with the donkey remembering what it did yesterday. Second, a distinction between first-order and second-order indifference: an agent can be indifferent between the outcomes and yet not indifferent about how it selects — caring about being the kind of agent who flips rather than the kind who always goes left, or the reverse — and equally can be indifferent about the method while caring about the outcome.

For each variation, say what each view implies and whether what it implies is plausible. Then give a verdict: which view handles the two variations better overall, and whether the two views are genuinely disagreeing or partly talking past each other. Please also tell me if one of my two devices is doing less work than I think it is.

Use these sections, in this order: "Shared ground" (about 100 words: what both views concede), "The one-shot case" (about 150), "The serial ass" (about 200), "First- and second-order indifference" (about 200), "Which view does better" (about 200), "How confident I am and where I'm least sure" (about 150). In that last section, state an explicit confidence level for your verdict, name the single point you are least confident about, and flag anything in your answer that professional philosophers would contest, anything you suspect you have oversimplified, and anything in my two devices that rests on a confusion. Please argue from the structure of the problem rather than by naming authors, papers, or further reading.
