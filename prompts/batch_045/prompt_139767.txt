I'm in an upper-level seminar on collective responsibility this term, and our final assignment is to write a short satirical piece on one of the arguments we've covered. I want to write a modest proposal in the strict sense: a straight-faced policy document that follows a real argument to a conclusion the reader recoils from, without the narrator ever winking. My target is the inefficacy objection — the move from "my individual contribution makes no difference" to "so I have no obligation to stop contributing" — in the cases we discussed: a household's meat purchases, one driver's emissions, a single vote.

The piece should take the form of an internal white paper, about 700 words, from an invented government body (something like a Bureau of Causal Superfluity) announcing a scheme for implementing that argument as policy. Households that can demonstrate that their consumption falls below the threshold at which any outcome changes — no extra animal bred, no extra lorry dispatched, no election decided — are issued a Superfluity Certificate entitling them to continue, since their acts are causally inert. The remainder, the difference-making residue that would otherwise be scattered invisibly across millions of people, is concentrated by lottery in one designated citizen each year, the Margin, who performs all of it on everyone else's behalf, so that no one else's action ever crosses a threshold again. Voice: deadpan civil-service prose, numbered clauses, closing with a short FAQ for regional offices.

The jokes have to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from caricaturing it: the gap between making no difference and making no expected difference, threshold or step-function harms, the difference between a causal and a participation-based or complicity-based reason, and the awkward fact that the argument generalises to voting and to the Bureau's own staff. The reductio should arrive without being announced: the scheme leaves the harm entirely undiminished while laundering every conscience in it, and it concentrates so much causal power in the Margin that on the argument's own terms the Margin is now the only obligated person — who of course declines, whereupon everyone certifies that one refusal makes no difference, and the Bureau papers over the collapse in a sub-clause. Please don't let the document state the standard replies (universalisation, collective obligation, complicity) anywhere; a reader who knows the literature should recognise them as the thing being ironised, and should feel that the satire is fair to the argument rather than a straw version of it.
