My friend Theo and I got into it a couple of nights ago, two drinks in, about the classic utility-monster objection, and I think between us we built an argument that proves way too much. I can't find the bad step and it's been bugging me since.

Setup so this is self-contained. Cardinal welfare scales -- the numbers you plug into a utilitarian sum -- are normally said to be unique only up to positive affine transformation: for a single person, replacing w with a·w + b (a > 0) preserves everything about that person's welfare levels and differences. That's supposed to be why it makes no difference whether you measure Bea's welfare in utils or micro-utils; the second just multiplies all her numbers by a million and changes nothing about Bea.

Here's the arguement:

(1) A change of units represents no difference in the facts. If two assignments of numbers to people's welfare differ only by per-person positive affine transformations, they represent the same situation, so a correct moral theory cannot give different verdicts depending on which assignment you happen to use.

(2) What total utilitarianism recommends is determined by the sum of the numbers.

(3) So total utilitarianism's verdicts depend on the units. Concretely: three people, a fixed stock of 100 units of some resource, each with the same diminishing-returns welfare function (say welfare = square root of resources). Measure everyone in the same units and the sum-maximizing split is 33/33/33. Now measure only Amara in micro-utils, multiplying her numbers by a million, and the sum-maximizing split hands her essentially all 100 units and leaves the other two with basically nothing.

(4) Which means a utility monster can be manufactured out of anybody. Take a completely ordinary person, grade their welfare on a fine enough ruler, and the utilitarian sum says to sacrifice everyone else to them. You never had to suppose a creature with a bizarrely efficient psychology; you just needed a smaller unit for that one person.

(5) But being a utility monster is supposed to be a substantive possibility about a creature's psychology -- that there could be someone who gets vastly more welfare out of any sacrifice than everyone else loses. That can't depend on which ruler we picked.

(6) So either the utility monster objection is empty -- a complaint about bookkeeping rather than about what utilitarianism would have us do -- or utilitarianism's recommendations about distribution are indeterminate, and the decades of fighting between utilitarians and their critics about distribution was never about anything.

(7) Both of those are absurd. The objection obviously has teeth, people on both sides feel its force, and it isn't a units quibble. And utilitarians plainly do say substantive, determinate things about distribution.

So which step do I reject? I'd give up almost anything before I accept (6), but I also really don't want to say that the fine-grainedness of your ruler can change what morality requires of you. Name the step, say why it looked fine, and then price the alternatives: for each candidate thing to give up, what does it commit me to downstream -- in population ethics, in how I think about interpersonal tradeoffs generally, wherever the cost shows up -- and which of those bills would I regret later?
