so i got sucked into experimental philosophy this semester — the whole move where you hand somebody a one-paragraph made-up story with a guy in it called "the chairman" and then ask them a yes/no question, and then treat the pattern of answers as evidence about what intentional action really is.

the case everyone starts with: a chairman is told that a new program will raise profits but also harm the environment. he says he doesn't care about the environment at all, he just wants profit. the program runs, the environment gets trashed. did he intentionally harm the environment? most people say yes — somewhere around 80%. now rerun the exact same story with "help" instead of "harm": same indifference, same outcome, and only about a quarter of people say he intentionally helped the environment. the conclusion people draw is that our concept of intentional action is shot through with moral judgment, that the line between the descriptive and the normative doesn't sit where everyone assumed.

here's what i cant figure out, and it's part method and part leap. on the method side: the field is extremely rigorous about getting the answers — random assignment, big samples, preregistration, replications, the works. but then there's this one extra step nobody seems to argue for, the step from "people answered this way" to "that's what the concept is." and there's a weird asymmetry in how disagreements get handled: when the folk answers clash with what philosophers say, sometimes that's treated as a big discovery about the folk concept, and sometimes it's treated as the folk being confused and philosophers being the experts — and i can't tell what's supposed to decide which. then i tried writing my own version and got completely stuck: an engineer tunes a recommendation model to maximize watch time, a coworker warns her it'll also get teenagers addicted to the app, she says she doesn't care about addiction at all, she just wants watch time, and it happens. did she intentionally get teenagers addicted? i genuinely don't know whether that's even the same experiment anymore — whether the thing being measured is moral valence, or blame, or whether she could have done otherwise, or what.

so what i want: what does this practice as a whole presuppose — about concepts, about what a survey answer is evidence of, about agency — and which of those does it strictly need versus just sit comfortably with? where is it underdetermined, i.e. what rival theories would license the exact same practice? and if you swapped in the view that the answers are really tracking blame, or conversational pragmatics, rather than intentional action, what would have to change in how the studies get run and reported? and please say how confident you are in each part of what you claim, and single out the bit you're least sure of.
