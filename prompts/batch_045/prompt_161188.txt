I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am finishing a column on long-run forecasts of catastrophe — the kind that name a mechanism, a quantity and a date, and then outlive their own deadline. Over two years of collecting these I have hit a pattern I cannot label properly. I need a concept rather than a slogan, because I intend to use it in print and the forecasters I write about will read it and will tell me I have got it wrong.

Here is the shape of it as I keep meeting it. Malthus's old essay argued that population, left unchecked, grows far faster than the food supply, and that famine would therefore check it before very long. In the country where he wrote, that did not happen; food production rose faster than population for a long stretch afterwards. When I put this to people who still take the essay seriously, the reply is always that the mechanism was right and only the date was wrong: the reprieve was bought by a one-off burst of agricultural innovation, the pressure is still there, deferred. Then, when a famine or a food-price spike occurs anywhere in the world, the same people book it as confirmation, and the missed date drops out of the account entirely.

I see the same configuration in a widely publicised 1970s computer-model exercise that projected industrial collapse from resource exhaustion before the end of the century and was defended afterwards as directionally right; in warnings about antibiotic resistance made decades before the resistance became routine, which do now look genuinely vindicated; and in forecasts of an imminent peak in oil production whose dates passed and were quietly moved. What interests me is not that forecasts fail — I take that for granted. It is the bookkeeping. Both outcomes, failure and vindication, get entered on the same side of the ledger, and the theory is never revised in either direction, even though it did name a date and could in principle have been tested against it. Nor does anyone in this line of work ever say the symmetrical thing: that they were early, that the mechanism they named has since been shown not to operate, and that the forecast should be withdrawn. And the move seems available only for unwelcome forecasts. Someone who predicted abundant fusion power by 1990 is simply wrong; nobody says he was right but early.

I can get part of the way with notions I already have: moving the goalposts, claims that cannot be tested, true warnings ignored, and the loss of credibility that comes from repeated false alarms. But all of those seem to concern either the audience's reaction or the internal structure of the theory, whereas what I am describing is something a forecaster does after the fact, in both directions at once, and can do while sticking scrupulously to a stated date.

I also think there are two different moves that get called by the same name. In one, the mechanism is retained and only the date is pushed, usually with a stated reason for the delay that could be checked independently. In the other, the mechanism quietly changes: the forecast was that a resource would physically run out, and what is later pointed to as vindication is a price spike with political causes, or a scarcity in a different part of the same sector. My working labels are date-shifting and mechanism drift, and I want to know whether that is where the joints are.

So: does this have an established name? If there are several near neighbours, which one fits best, and how does each differ from what I have described — particularly the two-move distinction I am trying to make? If there is nothing established, say so plainly and give me a definition I can defend, with the boundary cases settled. Is a forecast that really was vindicated after a long lag — a nineteenth-century calculation linking atmospheric carbon to surface temperature, for instance — an instance of the same phenomenon or a different one? Does the asymmetry between unwelcome and welcome forecasts belong inside the definition, or is it incidental to it? And at what point does delay stop being a defence and start being a withdrawal? Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I need the concept and its edges, not sources to chase.
