Last spring I joined my school's sustainability club, and most of my weekends since have gone into the vegetable plot we share with a neighbouring elementary school. It has turned out to be a philosophy problem rather than a gardening one. Our county extension office runs its pest advice on what it calls integrated pest management: walk the rows, count pests on a sample of leaves, and spray only once the population crosses a threshold at which the crop damage you expect would cost more than the control would; when you do spray, pick the least toxic thing that will work, avoid spraying while bees are foraging, and rotate modes of action so the survivors don't evolve resistance. So we are told to treat killing as a last resort and to minimise it — and at the same time the threshold is purely economic, nothing in the guidance counts the insects themselves as mattering at all, and the labels are written on the assumption that we will be doing this every season forever against pests we will never get rid of. Meanwhile, the vertebrate eating the same plants gets handled completely differently: the groundhog is trapped and driven out to a park, and the neighbour's cat, which kills far more animals in a year than our entire pest-control effort does, is somebody's property and we aren't allowed to touch it. The same animal switches category depending on where it is — rabbits and cats are culled as invasive pests in Australia while being pets or livestock here — and where there are rules about killing at all, they govern the method: some places restrict glue traps for rodents because the death is slow, and there are accepted and unacceptable ways to euthanise a trapped animal, but there is no rule whatsoever about how an insect may be killed, even though common insecticides work by overstimulating the nervous system and can take hours or days. Even the organic rules seem to be tracking something other than harm: they bar a long list of synthetic compounds but allow naturally derived ones such as Bt, as if the source of the chemical rather than what it does to the animal settled the question.

I've been arguing about this with two friends and we each keep producing a different principle, which makes me think we're all just describing the practice back to ourselves. One says the line is sentience and insects fall below it. One says it is numbers: no single insect death matters enough to regulate, but the aggregate matters enormously, so we govern the aggregate through thresholds. I keep coming back to something else, because the same animal has a different status in a garden bed than in a living room, which makes moral status look like it is being assigned by the animal's relation to us rather than by anything about the animal. I also notice that the people who say intelligence is what confers status have trouble explaining why we count the interests of a human being with severe cognitive impairments fully, and that fact seems to me to push against any criterion that insects fail and every human passes.

So here is what I would like from you. Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral status — or of harm, death, or animal welfare — that this whole cluster of practices only really makes sense under, and be careful about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits well with; I suspect some parts of it are compatible with several quite different theories and I want to know which parts those are. Then tell me where the parts pull against each other, because that is what I can't settle: the last-resort, least-toxic, don't-spray-during-bloom language sounds like it takes pest death to be a harm needing justification, while the threshold that actually triggers the spray counts only money and not the insects, and neither sits comfortably with there being no constraint at all on how an insect dies. Say whether the practice is in fact inconsistent, or whether some single view can hold all of it together. Tell me which rival theories the practice quietly rules out — I assume it rules out anything like equal consideration for every sentient being, and it may also rule out treating status as a fixed property of the organism rather than a relational one — and finally, describe concretely what would have to change in the way a garden, a farm, or a regulator operates if we took insect welfare seriously, given that the pesticides are what the thresholds are for. I'd rather you didn't give me a reading list or name-drop authors I'd have to look up; I want the structure of the argument and the shape of the practice.
