Historians and philosophers of history have long divided over whether history has a shape that repeats — the cycle of constitutions in Polybius, the generational dissipation of group solidarity in Ibn Khaldun, the organic life-course of high Cultures in Spengler, challenge and response in Toynbee — or whether it is a one-way accumulation, in which every generation inherits more than the last and apparent declines are local accidents on a rising curve. Write an essay of roughly 1,200 words settling this dispute. Do not spend the essay expounding the four theorists; their schemata are to be used as instruments, not reported as content.

Begin by separating three claims that the dispute habitually runs together, and say which of them a cyclical theorist is genuinely committed to and which a linear theorist can concede without loss:

1. Recurrence: the same political forms appear in the same order again and again, because of something in the human material, not because of imitation, diffusion or accident.
2. Boundedness: any given polity or culture has a finite span, and once it is over it is not reconstituted as the same thing.
3. Non-transmission: nothing durable crosses a breakdown; a later society does not stand on the remains of an earlier one as on a foundation.

Then adjudicate the dispute by applying it to a case I will stipulate. Suppose historians of the year 2200, working from an unbroken four-thousand-year written record of a single isolated civilization — call it the Kavari — report the following. Central political order has dissolved and been reconstituted twelve times, at intervals ranging from ninety to five hundred years. After every dissolution, within two centuries, there has arisen a polity at least as extensive and as administratively articulated as the one that failed. The quantity of energy the Kavari can mobilize, and the number of distinct techniques in their possession, have increased monotonically across the whole four thousand years, with no episode of net regression lasting more than a century. The Kavari had no contact with any other civilization, and no other civilization is known.

Three things must be settled about this record.

First: does it confirm the accumulation view? Consider the strongest cyclical reply available — that four thousand years of monotonic technical and extensive growth is exactly what a Spenglerian expects of the terminal phase, the passage from Culture into *civilization*, in which expansion, technical facility and self-satisfaction replace creation, so that the rising curve is evidence of exhaustion rather than of progress. Decide whether this reply is a genuine prediction entailed by the theory or a redescription manufactured after the fact, and say what would distinguish the two.

Second: consider the two remaining cyclical resources — treating the twelve dissolutions as internal pulsations within one continuing civilization rather than as deaths, and shifting the unit of analysis from polity to culture so that the four thousand years count as a single culture not yet arrived at its end. Say what evidence in the record would discriminate between these manoeuvres and the admission that the cyclical model has failed here.

Third, a test of symmetry: if the Kavari record had instead shown monotonic decline in population, energy mobilized and administrative articulation across four thousand years, with no recoveries, would that confirm the cyclical view or refute the linear one? If you treat the rising record as counting for the linear side but decline to treat the falling record as counting for the cyclical side, explain the asymmetry or give it up.

Conclude by stating whether cyclical versus linear is a determinate disagreement with a possible arbiter, or a pseudo-dispute generated by the choice of unit — dynasty, polity, culture, species-wide technique — together with stage-lengths that can be stretched to fit any record whatsoever. Take a side and justify it. It is not sufficient to conclude that each view is right about a different unit, unless you also name one measurable quantity whose observed value would settle the matter and state what value you expect it to take. Close with a single sentence saying what observation would change your mind.
