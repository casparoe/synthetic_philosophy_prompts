I have recently ended up knee-deep in nuclear strategy more or less by accident. My department asked me to help run a two-week summer school on crisis decision-making, which finished with a tabletop exercise: two teams, a disputed border territory, a nuclear-armed patron behind each side, and umpires injecting intelligence shocks at unpredictable intervals. My team played the status-quo patron — the larger arsenal, the better second-strike survivability, the more careful legal and political oversight — and we lost all three runs. The same shape emerged every time. The more carefully we calibrated our threats to what we were actually prepared to carry out, the less our opponent believed them; the team that got its way was the one whose side had visibly lost its grip on its own launch procedures, a junta whose regional commanders held pre-delegated authority and whose chain of command was visibly fraying. In the debrief, the umpire who designed the scenario gave the pattern a name, the delegation trap: the moves that make a nuclear threat credible — pre-delegation, automaticity, burning the boats, arranging things so that you could not back down — are the same moves that make it possible for the threat to be executed without anyone deciding to execute it, so you cannot buy credibility without simultaneously buying more of the risk of the very catastrophe the threat was issued to prevent. He claimed this was not an artefact of his scenario but a structural feature that the classic brinkmanship literature never properly absorbed, since it treats loss of control as a tool rather than as a hazard.

Since the summer school I have been reading around the subject in the evenings, and I keep hitting the same frustration I know from other literatures: the deterrence theorists and their critics seem to have been at each other for decades, and neither side appears to have moved anybody. On one side there is the rational-deterrence picture — credibility as a product of capability and demonstrated resolve, the labour of making threats proportionate and survivable, the distinction between counterforce and countervalue targeting, escalation dominance, the stability-instability paradox, and the thought that the long peace between the great powers needs an explanation and that deterrence is the best candidate. On the other side there is the battery of objections I now half-know by heart: the non-occurrence problem, that peace is not a datum from which you can read off a cause; the organisational and psychological critique, that real leaders in real crises do not reason in expected-utility terms but under time pressure, misperception and bureaucratic routine; the catalogue of accidents and near-misses, where the danger came from nobody's decision at all; the observations that nuclear superiority has repeatedly failed to deter conventional probing; and the sharper charge that the theory is unfalsifiable and functions mainly as a rationale for keeping the weapons. There is also a minimalist strand which holds that the bare existence of survivable arsenals does all the real work, so that everything else — war-fighting doctrine, counterforce modernisation, extended deterrence guarantees to allies — is at best redundant and at worst destabilising.

My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that this is not really an empirical dispute. Everyone seems to agree on the observable record; what divides them is what we should infer from a counterfactual causal claim we cannot test, given that being wrong in one direction is catastrophic — a disagreement about evidential standards and precautionary burden wearing an empirical costume. But I can see at least three competitor diagnoses and I cannot rank them: that it is partly verbal, in that one side means by deterrence the threat changed the adversary's decision while the other means no great-power war has occurred since 1945 and nuclear weapons existed; that it is two different enterprises sharing a name, one predictive and one advisory to policymakers; and that it is an unresolved modelling question in disguise, about whether signalling games with imperfect control over one's own actions have equilibria at all, or whether the credibility/control trade-off in my umpire's delegation trap makes the whole apparatus unstable in a way that no amount of case study work could reveal. So: diagnose the structure of this disagreement for me, using what each side actually argues rather than a survey of positions, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction, a formal result, a body of declassified evidence, a change of question — or why I should expect no resolution. Two things I care about in particular. First, without looking anything up, tell me whether what my umpire called the delegation trap is already a named and discussed problem in the literature — my suspicion is that it is a repackaging of something Schelling-adjacent about the rationality of irrationality, but I would rather be told it is old hat than go around repeating it as my own insight. Second, separate clearly what is settled among people who genuinely work on this from what is contested, and do not let a contested reading pose as the consensus: flag your own confidence, say where the debate is genuinely open, and say plainly if you suspect my hypothesis is wrong.
