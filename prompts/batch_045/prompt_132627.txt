I write RL code for a living — mostly game agents and one warehouse routing thing — and I've started having this low-grade feeling that some of the things I build are kind of mildly miserable, and I cant tell if the feeling is worth anything. So I went case by case, and my verdicts dont line up. Here they are; you tell me what principle, if any, I actually believe.

1. DQN on an Atari game, reward = score clipped to -1/0/+1. It dies constantly. Verdict: nothing. Zero. Its a number in a replay buffer.

2. A MuZero-ish model-based planner with a learned value function. It regularly plans for reward and gets less than it predicted. Verdict: still no, but this is the first one where I hesitate — there's something like disappointment in the negative prediction error.

3. Same as 2, plus a global interrupt: when the TD error crosses a negative threshold it broadcasts to every module, flushes the plan cache, forces re-planning from scratch, and the policy cant learn to gate it off. Verdict: now I feel a real pull. Weak maybe.

4. A curiosity-bonus agent that seeks out hard-to-predict states, including states where its avatar gets hurt, because those are informative. Verdict: no, and oddly not one of the closer cases, even though it keeps choosing things that are bad for it.

5. The reward-hacking boat: finds a loop, racks up maximum shaped reward forever, never finishes the race. Verdict: absolutely not suffering — but also not happy either. Its getting max reward and I still dont think reward receipt is what matters. That combo is doing a lot of work for me and maybe it shouldn't.

6. Two agents, identical reward sequence, identical behavior, functionally identical weights. A stores reward as one scalar. B uses an opponent-channel critic: seperate positive and negative pathways, and the negative channel habituates under sustained input, so when the bad signal finally stops there's a positive overshoot — relief. Verdict: I want to say B suffers more than A, and I also want to say that's obviously wrong because they're the same agent by any behavioral test. I genuinely dont know which of those to drop.

7. Gradient-detached penalty: the environment sends -1, the agent computes a big negative prediction error, but the penalty is detached before the loss. No learning, no behavior change, ever. Verdict: this one still suffers — or would, if 3 does. Which makes me think I care about the computation happening, not what it does to the policy.

8. RPE pinned to zero everywhere, agent is a frozen policy replaying. Verdict: no suffering — anaesthetized, or not an agent at all, and I cant tell which.

9. A recurrent agent whose hidden state persists across episodes and is used to predict its own future rewards — a tiny self-model, sort of — versus a feedforward agent on the exact same reward sequence that only ever reacts. Verdict: the recurrent one is the closer case. Suffering needs something that persists and has a stake.

My own stab: an agent suffers at t iff at t it computes a signal that (i) is negative-valenced, (ii) is globally broadcast and overriding, (iii) motivates avoidance intrinsically rather than by inference, and (iv) is one the agent cant switch off. But 7 fails (iii) and I still want yes, and 6 says the criterion cant be functionalist, so (i)–(iv) is probably already broken.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — including, if this is your bottom line, that the whole question is confused and where the confusion is — then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question. State the principle precisely enough to apply, with the case that motivates each clause. Then give its verdicts on two cases I havent listed: a language model fine-tuned with RL from human preference labels, and an agent whose negative signal is entirely self-predicted — it learned to predict its own future penalty and avoidance is driven by the prediction, so the actual penalty never arrives. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and what Id have to give up to save them.
