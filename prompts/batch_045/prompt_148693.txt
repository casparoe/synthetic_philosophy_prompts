I'm on the exhibitions committee at the regional history museum where I volunteer — I'm the token non-historian (I do risk auditing for a living, which I think is why they keep me around) — and we have to put something to a vote on the 14th. I need a draft memo I can circulate beforehand.

Here's the case. A local collector died last year and left us a restricted bequest: enough to fund a gallery for three years, staff and all, on condition that we mount an exhibition called "Lost Chapters: Rethinking the Ancient World." Three of the planned rooms: (1) Atlantis as a real Bronze Age state whose location was lost; (2) the claim that the pyramids at Giza were built using a technique that has since been lost and that we cannot now replicate; (3) a compressed chronology, i.e. the view that large stretches of documented ancient history are a later fabrication and the real timeline is much shorter than scholars say. The donor's clause, drafted by his lawyer and binding if we take the money, says: no label anywhere in the gallery may describe any interpretation as false, mistaken or debunked; every interpretation must be presented as a "competing hypothesis"; the word "pseudo" may not appear anywhere in the gallery; and at the exit there is to be a touchscreen where visitors vote on which account they find most plausible, with the running tally shown inside the gallery itself.

The committee is split four ways. A: decline the bequest and say why publicly. B: take it and run the gallery as written. C: take it and run it as a critical exhibit about how we know anything about the past at all, with the Atlantis and chronology material kept as case studies in evidence and error. D: take it, keep the subject matter, but relabel everything according to a written inclusion rule we adopt ourselves — the obvious risk being that the estate's lawyers call that a breach and pull the money.

Two things make this harder than it sounds, and I want the memo to deal with both rather than around them. First, about ten years ago our own "Pioneer Room" repeated a state commission's account of an 1863 massacre in the county; a tribal historic preservation officer showed us the state account was wrong and that the community's own oral history had been right all along. We fixed it, but two trustees now say: you told us then that the official experts were wrong and the outsiders were right, so why is this different? Second, our curator of collections points out that some of the compressed-chronology material leans on a premise that is technically true — the oldest surviving copies of lots of classical texts are medieval, so textual transmission really is a live scholarly problem — and she doesn't want us standing on the claim that "our oldest manuscripts are late copies" is by itself a crazy thing to say. She's right, and it means we can't just rule room 3 out because one of its premises is true; we have to say why the inference from that premise fails.

What I want: a memo, in my name, 700–900 words, recommending one of the four options, that (a) gives the committee a decision rule we can apply to each individual placard rather than to the exhibition as a whole, since I have no interest in a verdict based on vibes; (b) deals with the exit-vote device head on — my worry is that a plebiscite among people who have spent forty minutes in a room is being quietly treated as if it were evidence about the past, and I want that inference explained rather than the visitors sneered at; (c) spends a real paragraph on the trustees' Pioneer Room comparison as the strongest thing on the other side, including where it does bite and where it stops biting; and (d) doesn't sneer at the donor either, since half the board knew him. Also — and I'm definately going to insist on this — no citations, no suggested reading, no names of books. I'm writing this on a train with no signal and a memo with footnotes in it will just irritate everyone.
