I'm in the second year of a PhD in comparative politics, writing a chapter on how outside observers decide whether a country has stopped being a democracy, and I keep losing the same argument with my supervisor because I don't have a word for the thing I am gesturing at. The argument runs like this: take almost any single measure that gets counted as backsliding somewhere in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or the United States — expanding the bench of a court that has been ruling against you, moving the retirement age for judges, redrawing district lines, tightening who may observe a count, requiring organisations that take money from abroad to register — and you can nearly always find a democracy nobody doubts that has something close to the same rule on its books. So what, she asks, is the object of the judgement? Not the rule, since the rule has respectable company. Not the whole institutional package either, since nobody ever votes on a package and she doubts anyone could rank two countries' entire constitutional setups against each other. What is left is whether the observer likes the direction of travel, which is her way of saying the concept is a partisan label with a methodology stapled to it.

What I want a name for is the move that produces this, because I don't think it is any of the things it looks like. The defender is not pointing at the critic's own misdeeds, so it isn't whataboutism or tu quoque. The defender usually isn't denying that the step is small and reversible, and the salami-slicing or slippery-slope worry is the critic's accusation, not the defence. And the defender is often right on the narrow point: the borrowed sentence really is close to the sentence in the respected democracy's statute book. The problem is that what a rule like this actually does depends on what surrounds it — who appoints the replacements, whether there is a second chamber or a supermajority that would have to agree before the rest of the package could pass, whether the change is a one-off or repeatable at will, whether the courts that would review it are the same courts being repopulated. Those surrounding facts are exactly what is in dispute, so the comparison quietly helps itself to a ceteris-paribus clause it isn't entitled to. My own candidate name is rule laundering, by analogy with laundering a provenance, and my candidate test is a swap test: imagine transplanting the lender's whole surrounding arrangement along with the sentence, and ask whether the borrower would then behave like the lender. But I can see two ways this collapses: either the test is unfalsifiable because a defender can always say the context differs, or it is circular because we only notice the difference in context once we have already decided the country is sliding.

So: does this configuration have an established name in the comparative-institutions or democratisation literature, or a name borrowed from somewhere else — I have a vague sense that lawyers dealing with antitrust conduct, conspiracy, or cumulative environmental harm have had to work out how to judge a course of conduct rather than a single act, and I would like to know whether that machinery transfers? If there is no settled term, please coin and define one, and do more than hand me a label: I want to know what the phenomenon is not as well as what it is, how it differs from whataboutism, from ordinary salami tactics, and from a good-faith comparative argument that a borrowed rule really is innocent here, and what its boundary cases are. I also want to know what you make of my supervisor's deeper point: if the honest object of judgement is a trajectory rather than a rule or a package, what kind of thing is a trajectory, who gets to say which one a country is on, and does answering that either rescue the concept of backsliding or show that it really is just a partisan label? Please argue in concepts rather than sources — I am writing this from a field site with no library access, so a reading list would be useless to me even if you could produce one.
