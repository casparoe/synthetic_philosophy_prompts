Here's the thesis I'm defending. I'm going after extrapolated-volition accounts: the idea, familiar both from idealized-preference theories in ethics and from the version the AI-alignment crowd calls coherent extrapolated volition, that the proper target for a law isn't what people actually want but what they would want if they were more informed, less akratic, more coherent, and so on. My claim is that "extrapolation" isn't a single operation but a bundle of at least five — (1) add factual information, (2) scrub out irrationality and weakness of will, (3) render each person's preference set internally coherent, (4) undo preferences deformed by injustice or by narrowed option sets, (5) merge the resulting individual wills into one — and that these do not commute, because each one takes the output of the previous ones as its input, so in general there is no order-independent endpoint and no fixed point to converge on. Two toy cases I use: a woman in a strongly patriarchal village who says she doesn't want to work outside the home — do (1) first and she learns what other lives are like, do (4) first and you declare the preference deformed and set it aside, and the two routes come apart badly; and a case where two people each have intransitive preferences and the order of (3)-then-(5) versus (5)-then-(3) hands the decision to different people. My fix, which is the part I'm shakiest on, is to bootstrap rather than eliminate: stop trying to define the endpoint at all, and instead let the citizens themselves choose the *path* of idealization, under a deliberately thin idealization (sober, informed that the choice matters, given a plain-language explanation of what each of the five operations does and why the order matters), and treat whatever path they pick as the legitimate one. I want to say this rescues extrapolation from my own objection without collapsing into ordinary deliberative democracy, but I'm definately not sure I can.
