You are a decision theorist and a philosopher of rational choice, equally at home with evidential, causal, and functional or updateless approaches, and with the standard stock of examples: Newcomb's problem, transparent Newcomb, the smoking lesion, counterfactual mugging, Death in Damascus, XOR blackmail. Please think carefully and slowly. Before writing your answer, brainstorm for yourself: state the mapping the analogy proposes, then generate a generous list of candidate points where it might break, and only afterwards decide which of those points actually carry weight for the work the analogy is being asked to do. I would rather have a short, exact audit than a general survey of decision theory. Do not give me references, further reading, or citations; argue from the cases as I set them out, and tell me explicitly where you think a case is underspecified and what you would need in order to settle it.

The setting: I am part of a small group writing a short explainer on updateless decision theory for philosophers who work in other areas. We keep falling back on the following framing, and a draft my colleague is circulating leans on it heavily:

Updateless decision theory tells you to choose as if you were behind a veil of ignorance about which of your own instantiations you will turn out to be — the real, flesh-and-blood you in the room; the simulation the predictor is running; the copy on the other side of the building; the version of you facing an entirely different decision problem tomorrow. Since you do not know which one you are, pick the policy that is best from that ex ante standpoint, and once you find out which instantiation you are, do not update out of it.

The framing is then made to do three things: it is supposed to show why you should take only one box in Newcomb's problem, why you should pay in counterfactual mugging even after the coin has landed badly, and — in my colleague's draft — why you should refuse to pay in XOR blackmail. My worry is that the veil of ignorance is doing something in that argument that the mathematics of the theory does not do, and that the three conclusions are not equally supported.

Two cases we cannot get the group to agree about, which I would like you to use as stress tests rather than as settled examples. First, the Patchwork Twin: Omega does not run a fine-grained simulation of you. It runs three cheaper surrogates, and I want all three considered separately. One is a fine-grained computational model of your decision algorithm fed a stylized description of the situation and a stock life history with the last month missing. One is a coarse behavioral twin trained on a decade of your past choices, which tracks your policy on familiar inputs but generalizes badly to strange ones like Newcomb's problem. One is a genuine snapshot of your decision algorithm taken ten years ago, run on the inputs it actually had ten years ago. Each has been about equally accurate in the past. Omega fills the boxes on the basis of whichever surrogate it is using. Second, the Diverged Copy: whatever you choose, Omega will tomorrow create a copy of you running the same decision algorithm but with a modified utility function — this copy cares only about something you are indifferent to, and it will face its own Newcomb problem with its own Omega. Should your policy be chosen to serve its ends as well as yours, and if so, how are the two weighted?

I would like an audit of the analogy as an argument. Concretely:

1. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the parties behind the veil, of the positions in society they might occupy, of the ignorance of one's position, of mutual disinterest, of the equal weighting or maximin rule, of the publicity and stability conditions, of the fact that in the original every party must be able to endorse the result. Mark for each row whether the target of the analogy really has that feature.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for this use from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular: behind the veil the chooser genuinely lacks information, whereas the agent in Newcomb's problem knows perfectly well that it is the unsimulated one and can see the boxes — so what the analogy calls ignorance is really a normative instruction to ignore evidence, which is a different thing. Is that difference load-bearing or not?

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports that the theory itself does not assert. I suspect at least four things: a criterion for when a physical or computational system counts as another instantiation of you; a rule for aggregating over instantiations; a weighting of instantiations that morally favors treating them alike; and the legitimacy of evaluating policies from the ex ante standpoint at all. Say whether each import is harmless, needed, or question-begging.

4. Apply the audit to the Patchwork Twin and the Diverged Copy. Does the veil framing give a principled verdict about whether a coarse-grained twin, a deceived twin, or a ten-year-old snapshot is you for decision-theoretic purposes, or does the framing presuppose an answer to that question and then look like it is deriving one? Does it have anything to say about the Diverged Copy, or does its aggregation machinery go silent once the instantiations stop sharing a utility function?

5. Give a verdict on each of the three licensed conclusions: one-boxing, paying the counterfactual mugger, refusing in XOR blackmail. Which survive once the disanalogies are on the table, which were being carried by the analogy rather than by the decision theory, and which depend on assumptions the analogy smuggled in?

6. Finally, tell us what to write instead. If there is a better analogy available — something like a programmer choosing source code whose compiled instances do the running, or standing orders given to soldiers who will face different battles, or a choreographer and the dancers — say which, show that it survives the two stress cases above, and say where it too fails. If you think the honest thing is to drop the veil of ignorance and state the theory in the mathematical register, say so, and give us one or two sentences of plain prose that carry the same content without the misleading picture.
