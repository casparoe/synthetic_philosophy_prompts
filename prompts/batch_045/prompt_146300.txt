I work in infrastructure and reliability engineering — about twelve years, mostly platform and developer tooling: interface contracts, backward compatibility, deprecations, service level objectives, incident response, root-cause analysis. I have no philosophy training. Through a friend I've ended up in a recurring argument with a group of people in medicine and bioethics about human enhancement, and it keeps turning on a distinction that I think I already have good words for.

The distinction is therapy versus enhancement: therapy is said to restore or preserve normal functioning, or to treat disease, while enhancement is said to improve a person beyond what is normal or healthy for a human being, or to add a capacity that was never there. The distinction is also supposed to carry moral weight — roughly, that restoring someone to the baseline is something we owe them, while improving them past it is something we may permit but are not required to provide. Alongside it there are three accounts of where the baseline itself comes from, and I want all three translated:

- The baseline is statistical: normal means typical for the reference population, so a trait counts as a defect when it falls far enough down the distribution.
- The baseline is historical: normal means what the trait was shaped to do over our evolutionary history, so a defect is a failure of a system to do what it was selected for, whatever the current statistics look like.
- The baseline is evaluative: normal means whatever conduces to a life the person in question has reason to want, so the relevant question is not what is typical or what is ancient but what benefits this person.

There is also a fourth move my friends make that I find easiest to understand: sometimes the deficit is not in the person at all but in the arrangement around them, so that changing the environment and changing the person are competing fixes for the same complaint.

I'm reconstructing all of this from dinner arguments, so please work from my descriptions and tell me if one of them is not a position anyone actually holds.

Here is what I want. Restate the distinction, the moral asymmetry attached to it, and each of the four accounts above as precisely as you can using only my working vocabulary: spec versus implementation, intended behavior versus observed behavior, bug versus feature, root-cause fix versus workaround or shim at a different layer, regression versus new behavior, reference platform and configuration, acceptance tests and golden files, alert thresholds set on continuous metrics, SLOs and error budgets, the reason a line of code was originally written as recovered from commit history, maintenance work versus feature work, severity-one incident versus feature request, breaking changes and migrations, and the observation that once a system has enough users, every observable behavior of it ends up depended on by somebody (I've seen this called Hyrum's Law; the name doesn't matter, the phenomenon does).

Then run that translation against these cases, which are the ones the argument actually stalls on:

1. A condition so common in a population that it is statistically typical — dental decay is the example they use.
2. An intervention that restores the user-visible function by an entirely different mechanism, bypassing the broken part rather than repairing it — a cochlear implant is their example.
3. An intervention that restores to a sixty-year-old a capacity that person had at twenty and lost through ordinary ageing.
4. An intervention that adds a resistance or immunity no human has ever had.
5. A trait on a continuous scale, where the cut between healthy and pathological is set by a committee, has been moved more than once, and would place a large share of the population on the treated side.
6. A trait that some people do not experience as a defect at all, and around which they have built a language, a community, and institutions, so that removing it would break things other people rely on.
7. An intervention that improves one measured capacity and degrades another — less need for sleep, shorter lifespan — so that the trade is not a strict improvement on every metric.

For each case, tell me what the translation carries cleanly, what it carries only by distorting something, and what it cannot say at all. Then give me the residue report as a whole. I specifically want you to flag every place where the translation has to smuggle in an assumption my vocabulary does not actually contain — I suspect the biggest one is that my vocabulary always has someone who wrote the spec, and I want that marked rather than smoothed over. I also want your view on whether the residue points to a limitation of my vocabulary or to the original distinction, or some account of the baseline, being less clear than it looks; and if some of my seven cases collapse into the same case under your translation, say so, since that would itself be a finding.

Please don't give me a survey of the literature, and don't suggest anything to read or cite — I want only the translation and what it fails to carry. At the end, state how confident you are in the overall verdict and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of.
