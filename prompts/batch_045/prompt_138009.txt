I am an MA student in philosophy. This term I am taking a seminar on higher-order evidence, and I am also grading for the third-year epistemology course, whose midterm included the question below. I would like an independent read on one script before I finalise marks: I would rather not say what I make of it myself, since I don't want to anchor you.

For context, the lectures for that course characterised things as follows. First-order evidence is evidence bearing on whether some proposition is true; higher-order evidence is evidence about the epistemic situation of the thinker — about the reliability of the process that produced a verdict, or about whether the evidence was properly assessed. Epistemic akrasia was characterised as holding a first-order attitude together with a higher-order attitude that condemns it (for instance, continuing to believe that p while judging that one's evidence does not support p, or that one ought not to believe it), and was contrasted with practical akrasia, where an agent judges that she ought to do something and then intentionally does otherwise. Conciliationism about higher-order evidence was presented as the thesis that acquiring higher-order evidence rationally requires some revision of the first-order attitude it bears on; steadfastness as the denial that there is any such general requirement. The peer-disagreement literature was taught as one route into the same set of issues.

The question on the paper was:

Question 4 (20 marks). Answer all parts.

(a) [5 marks] Draw the distinction between first-order and higher-order evidence, and construct an example of your own (not one from the lectures) in which a thinker has strong first-order evidence for a proposition and then acquires higher-order evidence bearing on that evidence.

(b) [5 marks] Explain what epistemic akrasia is, and say how it differs from practical akrasia. A definition plus an example will not by itself earn full marks; you should also say why the state is held to be problematic.

(c) [10 marks] Assess the following objection: "Conciliationism about higher-order evidence requires too much. If the point of higher-order evidence is to tell a thinker something about her own reliability rather than about the world, then no amount of it can make it rational for her to give up a belief that her first-order evidence strongly supports; and in any case confidence is not something we set at will, so a view that requires us to revise it is requiring the impossible." Your answer should engage with at least one reply available to the conciliationist and at least one consideration in the objector's favour.

The script I am marking reads as follows.

(a) First-order evidence is evidence that bears on whether the proposition under consideration is true. If I want to know whether it will rain, the barometer reading and the colour of the sky are first-order evidence. Higher-order evidence is evidence about my evidential situation: about whether the process that produced my verdict is reliable, or about whether I assessed the evidence properly. Crucially, higher-order evidence is not evidence about the matter at hand at all — that is what makes it higher-order rather than simply more first-order evidence. If I learn that the barometer has been sticking, what I learn concerns the trustworthiness of my evidence, not the weather.

My own case. I am a junior doctor reading a chest film late in a shift. There is a faint shadow in the left upper lobe, and given my training that shadow is strong first-order evidence that there is a tumour. Before I write up the note, a senior colleague tells me that the films developed on that machine that morning were under-exposed, and that under-exposure manufactures exactly this kind of shadow. That is higher-order evidence: it concerns the reliability of the reading, not whether the patient has a tumour. The same structure is at work in cases of peer disagreement, which are really just a special case of the same phenomenon: learning that an epistemic peer has reached the opposite verdict tells me something about whether my own handling of the evidence is likely to have gone wrong, and nothing about how things stand with the matter we disagree about.

(b) Epistemic akrasia, as it was characterised in the lectures, is holding a first-order attitude together with a higher-order attitude that condemns it: continuing to believe that there is a tumour while at the same time believing that my evidence does not support that belief, or that I ought not to hold it. It differs from practical akrasia, where an agent judges that she ought to φ and then intentionally does something else. Belief is not an action, so the epistemic case is not a case of acting against one's better judgement; it is a combination of attitudes held at the same time. The parallel is nonetheless real, since in both cases the agent's own assessment of her reasons comes apart from, and fails to govern, the state she is actually in.

The state is held to be problematic because the incoherence is not merely psychological. If a thinker can grant that her evidence does not support p and go on believing p anyway, then it is hard to see what work the higher-order assessment is supposed to be doing; the higher-order attitude has, as it were, no authority over the first-order one, and the apparent rationality of the first-order belief is insulated from anything we can say about it from above. Note that the state is perfectly possible even though belief is not under direct voluntary control: whether one is in an akratic combination is a matter of which attitudes one has, not of what one can do at will.

(c) The objection is, I think, largely successful. Its first premise is right, and is really a point of definition: higher-order evidence is evidence about one's own reliability, and so it is not evidence about whether p. If I acquire higher-order evidence, then, I acquire no reason to think that p is false; and if I have no reason to think p false, while retaining a strong reason to think it true, it cannot be rational to give up the belief that p. The conciliationist will reply that higher-order evidence can nonetheless require one to suspend judgement or to lower one's confidence. But suspension is itself a first-order attitude, and it too needs a first-order reason, and the conciliationist has not said where that reason is supposed to come from.

A variant of my own case makes the point from the other direction. Suppose instead that the senior colleague says that under-exposure on that machine washes out faint shadows, so that films from it systematically conceal tumours rather than manufacture them. Here the news does bear on whether the patient has a tumour: it is evidence that a tumour may have been missed. But then it is not really higher-order evidence at all. It is straightforward first-order evidence for the proposition, dressed up as information about the machine. Conciliationism has no purchase on such a case, since nothing in it requires me to lower my confidence. This confirms that the category of higher-order evidence is by its nature the category of evidence that does not bear on the first-order question, and that is why the conciliationist demand cannot get going.

The second limb of the objection is also correct, and it is independent of the first. Conciliationism requires that on receiving higher-order evidence I lower my confidence. But confidence is not under voluntary control: I cannot simply decide to be less confident, any more than I can decide to find a joke funny. Since "ought" implies "can", and I cannot do what conciliationism demands, there is no such requirement. The conciliationist will say that I can do it indirectly, by re-reading the film in better light or by asking another colleague. But that is a different thing altogether: what I would then be responding to is new first-order evidence, not the higher-order evidence itself. The demand is therefore unsatisfiable, and the appearance that reason requires akrasia of us is only the appearance of a demand that we could never meet.

I conclude that the steadfast position is correct. A thinker who acquires higher-order evidence may reasonably retain her original attitude, and any view that requires her to revise it is committed either to a confusion about what sort of evidence higher-order evidence is or to a requirement that cannot be met.
