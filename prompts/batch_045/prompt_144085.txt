I'm drafting a position paper on the conditions for valid consent for a clinical-ethics reading group that I run with a couple of people from the philosophy department — half of them work on research ethics, the other half on coercion and exploitation, and they keep pulling me in different directions. Before I commit to anything in print I want an audit of my own package of commitments, because my supervisor has twice now said that "three or four of these cannot all be true" and then refused to tell me which she'd drop. I'm not looking for a general essay on consent, and please don't give me a reading list: I want you to work on the views as I've stated them below and trace what commits me to what.

Here is where I think I've landed. First, I hold an attitudinal view: consent itself is a mental state — an attitude of willingness or intending that something happen — and not an utterance or an act; communication matters only for other people's knowledge of it and for coordination. I'm willing to bite the bullet that a sincerely willing person who never says anything does genuinely consent, even though nobody may act on it. Second, I want to explain consent's peculiar normative force by saying that it is the exercise of a normative power: to consent is to waive a claim and thereby change what others are permitted to do, which is why it has the force it does rather than merely being evidence that an action was fine anyway. Third, on deception: I think a misrepresentation invalidates consent only when the misrepresented feature was one the person was actually relying on, or would have decided differently about had they known — deliberate deception that makes no difference to the person's actual decision leaves consent intact. I've been calling conditions of this sort will-tracking. Fourth, I think there is a separate class of conditions that are not will-tracking but will-constituting — they determine whether the will in question is really the agent's own in the first place — and competence belongs there; moreover I hold that competence is a threshold rather than a scalar matter, so that once someone clears the bar, their residual failures of understanding or appreciation don't diminish the validity of what they consent to. Fifth, I read coercion in a moralized way: a proposal can be coercive in virtue of the injustice of the background situation even when the person's will is intact and their alternative isn't catastrophic — poverty-wage overtime, or sleep with me or I'll exercise my undoubted legal right to tell your employer something true about you — and I want to say that consent extracted by such proposals is invalid even when the person, on reflection, wholeheartedly and resentlessly chooses to comply. Sixth, I also want to say that manipulation through choice architecture — the consent form whose layout was A/B-tested so that agreeing is the path of least resistance — can wrong a patient without invalidating their consent, provided the risks were described accurately and they would have agreed anyway; invalidity and wrongfulness are separate axes for me. And seventh, here is a case verdict I'm attached to: in a study where participants are told the research is about memory when it is really about obedience to authority, if a participant later says they would not have cared about the true purpose and I believe them, then their consent was valid, and whatever the researchers did wrong is a separate matter.

What I'd like from you: go through this set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how severe each tension is, and separately which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection and why — I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything as problematic. My suspicion is that some of these are fine individually, and fine pairwise, but generate something I can't accept when three or four are combined; the case I have in mind involves the merely borderline-competent patient, and if that intuition is right I want it spelled out. For each real tension, say what specifically would have to be given up, what that would cost me elsewhere in the set, and how much of the set survives; then rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what the residual cost of that revision would be. Don't tell me which commitment I'd personally prefer to keep — I genuinely don't know — but do tell me if one of my commitments is doing more work than I've noticed.
