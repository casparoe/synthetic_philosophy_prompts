I work on model evaluation, and in internal discussions 'it's just a stochastic parrot' functions as a trump card. It gets used for two very different jobs: to end arguments about whether a model is reasoning, and to argue against deploying a given system in a high-stakes setting. I want to be able to say precisely what is being claimed, and which version of the claim, if true, would actually do either job.

Here is why I think the question is harder than it looks. Suppose two systems are behaviorally indistinguishable: no input I can construct separates them. M was trained by next-token prediction on a static corpus of scraped text. E was trained by the same kind of objective, but on a stream interleaved with the perceptual inputs of an embodied agent, so that its predictions were continuously corrected by whether the world cooperated; its weights were shaped by that feedback rather than only by a frozen archive. Nearly everyone I ask says M is a parrot and E is not — while also saying that whether a system understands is a matter of what it can do, not of how its weights came to be as they are. I don't know how to hold both thoughts at once.

So please give me a menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing: about where a system's competence comes from; about statistical association versus causal or referential contact with what its words are about; about the absence of any check of outputs against the world; about error never having mattered to the system; about recombination of memorized fragments versus genuine generalization; or anything else you think is worth separating out. For each, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false or ill-defined, and whether it is a claim about behavior or about provenance. Then say which readings would actually support each of the two moves the slogan is used to make, and which leave the move unsupported. Attach a confidence level to each verdict, and flag the one you are least sure of.
