I'm putting together the closing session of a graduate reading group on social epistemology and would rather spend it on one case than on another pass over the survey literature. The case is Watergate, and I'd like it worked through the epistemology of conspiracy theories in both directions at once: what the theory says the participants in the episode were facing and should have done, and then what the episode does to the theory.

Here is the shape of the episode as I understand it, so we don't stall on facts. In June 1972 five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee offices in the Watergate complex; the White House press office characterised it as a minor burglary unconnected to the President's re-election campaign. Over the following two years the hypothesis that the break-in had been authorised or at least covered up from the top of the administration moved from something the press and much of the public treated as speculative embellishment to the finding of a Senate select committee, and then to the President's resignation in August 1974. Along the way: a small circle of senior officials paid hush money and destroyed evidence; a foreign-intelligence agency was asked to tell the FBI to stay away on national-security grounds; two newspaper reporters built much of their account on a single anonymous senior source whose identity was not publicly confirmed for more than thirty years; the President ordered the special prosecutor fired and the Attorney General and his deputy both left rather than carry the order out; and it then emerged that the President had been tape-recording himself, which is what finally settled the matter. The point I care about is this: at the moment the hypothesis was most contested, its evidence base was anonymous testimony, witnesses who changed or contradicted themselves, documents whose significance was disputed, and a large number of people who would have had to be lying. Note also that the vindication was uneven — the cover-up charge stuck, while some of the more ambitious claims about prior knowledge were never established — and I want that unevenness handled rather than flattened into "it turned out true."

So the mapping I want is this. Reconstruct the Watergate hypothesis as it stood in, say, late 1972 to mid-1973 and run it against the standard apparatus: the definition of a conspiracy theory as an explanation that invokes a conspiracy and conflicts with an official account; the particularist view that such explanations must be assessed one at a time on their evidential merits; the generalist or vice-epistemological view that the class carries a presumption of defect because of how such explanations are typically produced and held; the leakage argument, that conspiracies involving many people tend to surface and so large ones are improbable; and the empirical finding that accepting one such explanation predicts accepting others that are mutually inconsistent. For each, say what it would have told a scrupulous inquirer in 1972–73 to do, and then say which of the warning signs it registered were genuinely diagnostic of falsehood and which were artifacts of the definition or of hindsight. Two features seem to me to do special work and I want them taken head-on. First, here the official account was itself being manufactured by the conspirators, and the institutions that would normally adjudicate — the Bureau, the Justice Department, elements of the intelligence community — were either implicated in the matter or being used to obstruct it, so any account that keys the category to "the official story" is keying it, in this case, to the story told by the people with the strongest interest in the lie. Second, what I've started calling the credibility ratchet: when such an explanation is vindicated, the vindication appears to convert it from a conspiracy theory into a plain historical fact without refunding the reputational cost borne by those who held it early, and without any visible revision of the heuristics that produced the dismissal. If vindication restores nothing, I want to know whether the generalist's presumption is doing epistemic work at all, or is a social sanction wearing epistemic clothing.

Then turn it back on the theory, which is the part I'm most interested in. Is Watergate simply a confirming instance for particularism and an anomaly for generalism, or is that too quick — is it better read as a case the literature was never built for, since philosophical treatments tend to cast the officials as epistemic authorities and the theorist as an outsider with no access? Where exactly is the theory silent: about what an inquirer should do when the adjudicating institutions are themselves compromised; about whether later vindication is admissible as evidence about the rationality of the original belief; about what the right reference class is for applying base-rate style considerations to a claimant with privileged but unverifiable access; about how to score a hypothesis whose core is vindicated while its periphery is not? And is there anything in this literature — or in the epistemology of testimony, or of institutions — that would have let a 1973 inquirer separate the Watergate hypothesis from the run of false ones by the standards available at the time, rather than only by its later being proved true? If the honest answer is that there isn't, and that the case is decided by luck and by the tapes, I'd rather hear that than a tidy verdict.
