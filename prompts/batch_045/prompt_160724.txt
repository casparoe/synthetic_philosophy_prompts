I convene a monthly discussion group — half philosophy graduate students, half people from a local animal-advocacy organization — and next month's session is on what we owe, if anything, to insects and other very small invertebrates. We keep getting stuck in the same loop: someone points out that insects, mites and other arthropods outnumber vertebrates by several orders of magnitude, so that even a modest per-animal probability of valenced experience looks as though it should swamp every competing cause in expected terms; someone else replies that the probability is simply too low to build anything on; and the argument stalls there without anyone finding out what they actually disagree about. I want to replace the stalemate with a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree that members can walk through and land on a named position at the end, so that we can see whether our differences are about the size of the probability, about how probabilities combine with numbers, about what counts as a unit of welfare, or about what to do under practical uncertainty. I am deliberately not asking for any citations, reading list, or survey of the insect-sentience literature: the handout should work for someone reasoning from scratch, and invented labels are fine where the literature has none.

Please build the following: a printable one-page decision tree for readers who have basic decision theory and basic moral philosophy but no background in this debate, with eight to ten branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves. The root should be a single entry commitment — the reader grants that there is some non-zero, non-trivial chance that an insect has valenced states, and that if it does those states carry at least some moral weight — and every node after it must be phrased as a claim or small case the reader can answer without knowing where it leads. I want the nodes drawn from the logical space rather than from a list of names, but please make sure most of the following distinctions do the splitting: whether the uncertainty about insect sentience is resolvable-in-principle empirical uncertainty or is instead indeterminacy arising from sentience being a vague, multiply-realizable cluster property, and in the latter case whether an expected value can be aggregated at all; whether a ten percent chance that an insect is a full subject of experience is equivalent, morally, to ten percent of the moral weight, or whether risk about status must be distinguished from degree of status the way risk is distinguished from ambiguity; whether the unit of concern scales with the number of organisms, with the number of valenced moments, with total neural or functional machinery, or with the richness and temporal integration of each subject's experience; and whether the reader accepts expected-value maximization across very small probabilities of astronomically large stakes or instead applies a probability threshold, a variance penalty, or some compromise among competing moral theories. Two cases are required and must be placed where they discriminate best. First, a trade-off: a program that certainly averts a fixed amount of suffering in a small number of chickens versus one that with some single-digit probability averts the same per-animal amount across millions of insects and otherwise does nothing — ask at what probability the reader is indifferent, and let that number do the sorting. Second, a population case: if insect lives are on balance bad, does that count in favour of reducing insect abundance, and how does the reader handle the ecological and epistemic risk of trying, or is there a constraint against intervening that way even granting the expected welfare arithmetic? I also want one node that extends the reader's own principle downward, from insects to mites, nematodes or soil microfauna, where the numbers are larger again and the probability lower, so as to separate principled stopping points from the mere feeling that the extension is absurd; one node on whether our inability to predict the effects of intervening defeats the priority claim or merely converts it into a case for investigating first; and one node on whether the scale argument, if it wins, generates an obligation or only a permission to reallocate.

Each leaf should carry a label, or the word unoccupied, together with one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment and one sentence on what it says about the two required cases. Among the leaves you must include: the straight expected-value insect prioritarian; a research-first postponer; a capacity-threshold or lexical-threshold view that stops short of insects; a vagueness or indeterminacy skeptic; an anti-fanatic or probability-threshold view; a tractability-based deprioritizer; and at least one leaf for someone who accepts the probability and the numbers but rejects the aggregation, because moral concern requires a definite identifiable bearer rather than a merely probable one. Please also name one or two combinations of answers that your tree permits but that nobody seems to hold, and say briefly why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate — two genuinely different positions that return identical verdicts at every node — and say what further case would pry them apart, or whether nothing would; then close with a short note on which single node is doing most of the work in the disagreement my group keeps having.
