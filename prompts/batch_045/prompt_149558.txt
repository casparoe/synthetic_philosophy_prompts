Right, I'm running a first-year ethics seminar next term — 18 students, one 80-minute slot, mostly people who've never done any philosophy — and I want them to walk out having *felt* the gap between doing the right thing and doing it for the right reason, rather than having been told about it. So: no lecture up front, and nobody should hear the phrases "moral worth", "from duty" or "in accordance with duty" until the debrief. Ideally they'd be arguing about the cases for forty minutes before they notice there's a doctrine sitting there waiting for them.

The stuff I want them to run into:

- Two people can do the exact same helpful act, at the same cost, with the same result, and we credit them completely differently — and we're extremely confident that the difference is real.
- The test we instinctively reach for to tell the two apart is a counterfactual one: would she still have done it if the bit that made it pleasant or easy or flattering were taken away? I'd like them to *live* that counterfactual rather than imagine it — run the same choice twice with one feature of the situation quietly removed between rounds, so they're comparing two actual decisions they made instead of speculating.
- Then break the test for them. I want a case where somebody clearly would still have done the right thing with every selfish incentive stripped out, but who also obviously wanted to do it, enjoyed it, or was partly doing it to be seen doing it — the overdetermined case. Some of the room will say the worth survives, some will say it's cancelled, and I want that to be a genuine disagreement between them, not a tidy one.
- And a case where the good outcome gets produced anyway: one participant can quietly hand the job to someone else — or to a rota, a system, a machine — and the beneficiary is helped either way. Ask them to locate what, exactly, has gone missing there.

Constraints: seminar room, paper, envelopes, and about £15 for real stakes. I'd rather the stakes be real and tiny (tea, chocolate, an actual boring bit of admin labour, a genuine thank-you card someone has to hand over in front of people) than hypothetical. The activity should do the work: roles, private information, small incentives, forced choices, and at least one round where students allocate praise or thanks rather than money. But nobody should end up feeling exposed about their own character in front of the group — I can't send anyone home stinging.

What I'd like from you: the setup and materials, the roles and any private cards, a timeline with minutes, what I actually say at each stage (keep it short), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity back onto the idea — including the point where we finally name the in-accordance-with / from distinction and check it against what they've already said themselves. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm expecting someone to spot the manipulation and start playing strategist, someone to refuse the premise altogether, and someone to give me the answer they think I want) and how to handle each. And be honest about which feature of the view the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to state in plain words myself: my worry is that any activity built out of payoffs, observers and repeated rounds secretly rigs the case toward "worth is about what you'd have done otherwise", and quietly begs the question against someone who thinks it's all about what the agent's will was actually up to at the moment.

British English throughout please, and keep it practical — I have to run this thing, definately not read an essay about it.
