Here is the argument.

(1) A miracle is, by definition, an event that violates a genuine law of nature.

(2) On a Humean regularity view of laws, a law of nature is nothing over and above the most comprehensive description of the uniform course of our past experience; our evidence for any law is therefore just the uniformity of that experience.

(3) Consequently, the evidential force of uniform experience against any miracle is as strong as the evidence for the law itself; on this view, the prior probability of a miracle (that is, its probability before we consider any testimony for it) is not merely very low but zero, because the law that it would violate is constituted by the very uniformity that the miracle would contradict.

(4) The credibility of testimony is always finite; even the best ordinary testimony supplies only a finite likelihood ratio in favor of the event testified to, by which I mean that it multiplies the prior odds by some finite factor, however large.

(5) In Bayesian terms, if the prior probability of a hypothesis is zero, then no finite likelihood ratio can raise its posterior probability above zero; a nonzero posterior requires a nonzero prior.

(6) Therefore, given (1) through (5), no possible testimony could ever establish a miracle; this reproduces Hume's conclusion that no testimony can establish a miracle unless it would be even more contrary to uniform experience for the testimony to be false than for the miracle to occur, which in practice never happens.

(7) The very same reasoning applies to any putative violation of a well-confirmed scientific regularity on the basis of testimony or recorded experiment: if a trustworthy colleague reports an experimental outcome that contradicts a well-confirmed law, the uniformity of our past experience of that law makes the prior probability of the anomaly zero on the regularity view, so no report could ever establish the anomaly either.

(8) Therefore, no scientific anomaly could ever be rationally accepted on the basis of testimony or experiment, and we could never have rational grounds to revise a law of nature; on this reconstruction, the acceptance of any scientific revolution would be, by the argument's own standard, irrational.
