Last night a friend and I spent the better part of two hours arguing about whether a critic can hand you an aesthetic judgment. Her side was the ordinary one: we take recommendations from people whose taste we have learned to trust, we act on them, we are rarely let down, and it is perverse to say that all of that amounts to ignorance. Mine was the stubborn side: that a verdict about beauty, grace, power or clumsiness is the one kind of judgment you cannot inherit, that a report from someone who has not met the work is at best evidence about how the work tends to land on people. We got as far as agreeing that the real disagreement is about whether aesthetic judgment has a first-personal authority that other judgments lack, and then, unsatisfyingly, we went to bed.

I want to write a piece of satire against the position I was defending, and I would like your help with it.

Please write a Swiftian modest proposal in the form of a public consultation paper: Draft Statutory Guidance on the Formation of Aesthetic Judgments, issued by a fictional Office for Aesthetic Integrity and open for comment. Roughly 900 to 1,000 words, set out in numbered clauses, with a short definitions section near the front and one worked example. The voice is deadpan civil-service prose: scrupulously reasonable, allergic to drama, entirely sincere. The Office is not evil; it is simply administering a principle.

The principle it administers is mine: that no one should form or express an aesthetic judgment about a work except on the basis of first-hand acquaintance with that work. I want you to exaggerate that real feature rather than invent a straw man. The paper should get right why someone holds it — that appreciation is something you do rather than something you can be told, that there is something genuinely off about asserting beauty while conceding you have never encountered the thing, that the value of art seems bound up with the experience of it in a way the value of, say, a scientific result is not. A reader who actually holds this view should recognize it, laugh, and not wince.

What I want the piece to work through, in roughly this order:

The definitions, where the Office has to say what counts as acquaintance, what counts as a verified encounter, and — the part that should be funniest — which predicates count as aesthetic and which are merely descriptive. Running time, key, materials, plot: plainly transmissible, no problem. But then the hard cases in the middle: well-made, funny, moving, profound, boring, a slog, overrated. The line keeps sliding and the Office keeps having to issue rulings.

The permitted loophole. Critics may not issue verdicts, but they may issue recommendations — predictions about how the reader will react, which are about the reader and not the work. The Office should notice, without the slightest change of tone, that the entire substance of criticism has migrated into this loophole while remaining fully compliant, and should propose some escalating administrative response that makes it clear the loophole cannot in fact be closed.

The hard cases. Works that nobody now living can be acquainted with: a fresco lost in a flood, a score never performed, a single performance scheduled to conclude in the twenty-seventh century, a piece held in a private collection. The Office must not simply exempt them. It has to produce an absurd but internally consistent policy — licensed surrogates, acquaintance by proxy, provisional judgments, a deemed encounter — and the reductio should be that every fix either abandons the principle or is straightforwardly a matter of taking someone's word for it.

The closing turn. The Office cannot verify anything itself; its inspectors cannot have encountered every work in the jurisdiction. The paper should end by quietly resting on the reports of other people — an inspector's summary, a panel's consensus, a form someone signed to say they were there — without ever registering that it has done so. No winking, no final paragraph that explains the joke.

Two constraints. First, fairness: the paper should contain, somewhere in its own bureaucratic vocabulary, one genuinely strong line for my friend's side — a risk register entry or dissenting annex noting that ordinary critical practice gets along perfectly well and that the guidance would render most of what anyone says about art either unlicensed or false — which the Office dismisses a little too briskly. Second, self-consistency: the paper must never itself make an aesthetic judgment about a work it has not encountered, so it may only refer to real or invented works through neutral descriptors. Let that constraint visibly tighten the screw as it goes.

By the end, a reader should have felt the full pull of the thought that aesthetic judgment is essentially first-personal, and also the full absurdity of what follows if you really mean it — and should be unsure, briefly, which of us won the argument.
