Me and a friend got into a two-hour arguement about the absent-minded driver last night and neither of us moved an inch, so please settle it. The case we were using: two exits, the driver can't tell them apart and has no memory of having passed the first one; leaving at the first exit is worth 0, leaving at the second is worth 4, missing both is worth 1. Im not going to tell you which of us said what, I just want a fair verdict.

(1) One of us: it's just a mixed strategy. Continue at an intersection with probability p, expected payoff per trip is 4p − 3p², which peaks at p = 2/3 for 4/3. Nothing paradoxical, done.

(2) The other: that can't be the end of it, because conditional on finding yourself at an intersection you're at the first one with probability 1/(1+p), which at p = 2/3 is 3/5 — so taking the exit right there is worth (2/5) × 4 = 1.6, which beats 4/3. So the plan tells you to abandon itself the moment you arrive at an intersection, and a "solution" you'd walk away from isn't a solution.

(3) First one: that 1.6 is fake. You only get it by quietly assuming you'll behave differently at the next intersection; priced consistently at the same p, continuing is worth 1.6 as well — you're exactly indifferent, so there's nothing to deviate to. And there's only one decision token here anyway: what you do at the intersection just is what you decided at the start, so deviating isn't even available.

(4) Second one: fine, different angle. Say he makes this drive every morning for a year and keeps score. Per trip he averages 4/3. But the number of decisions per trip is 1 + p, and p is up to him, so his average payoff per decision is (4p − 3p²)/(1+p), which peaks at about 0.53, not 2/3. And per-decision is the only thing he can care about from the driver's seat, since he really doesn't know which leg of the trip he's on.

(5) First one: that's just swapping in a new objective. His payoff is defined over which exit he ends up leaving by, per trip, and on your measure he does better by making it less likely he ever reaches the good exit at all, which is mad for someone trying to get home. Divide by miles driven or minutes awake instead and you'd get a third answer, so the denominator is doing all the work.

Can you go through the five moves and say who's right about what in each one — not just who wins overall — plus whatever both of them are missing? Reply as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no summing-up at the end.
