I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm designing the scoring and aggregation layer of an internal forecasting tool. I'd like you to help me think it through by writing a dialogue, and I want the thinking rather than the literature: please don't cite papers, attribute views to named authors, or hand me a reading list. I have no way to look anything up, and a name would not tell me whether an argument is any good. Everything I state below is something I worked out on a whiteboard or in a spreadsheet, so I'd like the dialogue to argue at that level of concreteness, and to define any technical term (strict propriety, calibration, partition, pooling) in plain words the first time it comes up.

The tool: roughly forty people log probabilities on binary or multi-outcome questions about our own operations — will the database migration finish by Friday, will on-call pages exceed 200 this month, which of three vendors will we pick. Two outputs matter: a per-person accuracy score that goes on a leaderboard, and one aggregated probability per question that leadership acts on. Each forecaster answers only about 10–25 questions a quarter, and resolutions are sometimes noisy, since a question gets resolved by whoever owns it. A philosopher friend told me the framework this belongs to is something like accuracy-first epistemology: the claim that the fundamental epistemic good is having accurate credences, so that norms like coherence and Bayesian updating can be vindicated by dominance arguments about accuracy alone, without appealing to practical consequences. That is the framework I want used.

Two of my colleagues disagree about the whole design. Dana wants us to score with the log rule (log probability of the outcome that occurred), report in bits, never clip the score, aggregate by averaging in log-odds space with some extremization, and make people forecast a canonical fine-grained partition of every question. Milo wants squared error (Brier), no clipping needed because it is bounded already, aggregate by a plain arithmetic mean of probabilities with only mild extremization, and let people answer questions at whatever granularity they find natural. Both of them are good at this, so please don't make either one a foil: every reply should engage the specific claim it answers, and I want at least two points where one of them catches a genuine error or overstatement in the other's reasoning and the other concedes it or rebuts it on the merits. Let them converge where they honestly can and leave the rest flagged as unsettled.

Four things I worked out that I want them to actually grapple with rather than restate:

1. Tail risk and the temptation to clip. I floored the log score at −3 so one freak event cannot destroy someone's standing. I then checked a case: with a true credence of 0.01, reporting about 0.000001 beats reporting 0.01 (roughly −0.030 versus −0.040 expected), because the floor removes the downside of being confidently wrong while leaving the upside of a near-certain correct call intact. Flooring the report space at, say, [0.01, 0.99] looks like it has the same effect. I take this to mean any such cap destroys strict propriety and pushes toward overconfidence rather than away from it. Milo should say why boundedness is still the right way to get robustness, and Dana should say what Brier's boundedness costs us — in particular whether it can distinguish a forecast of 0.001 from one of 0.000001, which matters because some of our questions are tail risks where leadership cares exactly about that difference.

2. Framing. For a perfectly calibrated forecaster I get expected log score equal to minus the entropy of the resolution distribution, and expected squared error equal to 1 minus the sum of squared probabilities, over the finest partition they answered. It follows that splitting an outcome of probability q into sub-outcomes distributed as r costs q·H(r) bits under the log rule and q²·(1 − Σ r_k²) under squared error: both rules mechanically penalize answering a finer question, so part of our leaderboard measures how people chose to frame things, and the two rules penalize that differently (Brier's penalty scales with q², so it punishes splitting a likely outcome far more than splitting an unlikely one). Have them argue about the fix: forcing a canonical fine partition, letting people choose but scoring against a house baseline on the same framing, normalizing per question, or something better — including what each option costs in forecaster time and in false precision.

3. Aggregation. If our forecasters' evidence is conditionally independent, I think the Bayes-correct combination adds log-likelihood ratios, i.e. averages log-odds, and that a plain average of calibrated forecasts is therefore systematically underconfident and should be extremized; but if their evidence overlaps heavily, I think the same reasoning says the average should instead be pulled back toward 0.5. Have them work out which objective each pooling rule actually optimizes (minimizing whose expected inaccuracy, measured under which distribution), how the choice interacts with which scoring rule we picked, and what that implies for how much extremization is safe when only ~15 people answer a given question and resolutions are noisy.

4. How rich the scored question set has to be. I have been told that the accuracy-based argument showing incoherent probabilities are self-defeating only goes through if you are scored on a rich enough set of propositions — a full partition, closed under the Boolean operations — so that someone who only ever answers coarse yes/no questions can be incoherent in ways no scoring rule will ever catch. Both of them should say what that implies about what we force people to answer: whether we should score derived questions (given someone's P(A) and P(B), score them on the implied P(A or B) filled in by coherence), and whether that rewards us for coherence the platform supplied rather than the forecaster.
