long time lurker of the rationalist blogs here (sequences, the old comment wars, all of it) and I have a very practical itch. I want a spec, not a survey: what would a rule for appraising research programmes actually have to get right before I'd trust it? The job I want it good enough for is narrow — me deciding whether to keep putting years of my own career into a field whose programme looks like it might be stalling, and a grant panel deciding between funding the incumbent programme and funding a scrappy rival. So it has to work for someone who is allocating real resources, not for someone writing history with the ending already known.

Give me the requirements list with the case behind each one, and be explicit about which are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp (I suspect "must cash out in novel predictions" is one of those, and I suspect "must be statable as a hard core plus a protective belt" is close to tautological). In particular I want it to say something defensible about: a zombie programme whose own practitioners think the core is false but keep using it because it keeps landing confirmed predictions; a black-box programme where the positive heuristic is a machine learning system, nobody can articulate the core, and the hit rate on novel predictions is well above chance; a shotgun programme that emits thousands of predictions and reports the ones that came off; an absorber programme that keeps explaining its rival's successes as limiting cases, which looks like triumph and like degeneracy depending on the mood I'm in; a programme that hasn't been refuted so much as priced out, where the next decisive test costs a billion dollars and takes thirty years; and the case that actually matters for my decision, where the programme looks degenerating and there is no rival to defect to. I also want the level distinction kept intact — "this programme is in trouble" is not the same claim as "I should leave it" — and I want the rule to be usable in real time by someone who can't see the future and who knows that the scientists being evaluated will start gaming whatever metric you hand them.

The part I most want is where the list fights itself. Tell me which pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied, and whether the whole thing is self-defeating: it looks to me like "give a verdict now" and "don't declare a programme dead prematurely, since programmes revive" are in genuine tension, and like anti-gaming and applicability pull in opposite directions, and like any criterion that scores methodologies by how much of the history of science they recieve as rational gets two opposite verdicts on the same episode whenever two reconstructions both cover a lot of ground. Then say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. After that, a couple of sentences each on how Lakatos's own version, Popper's falsificationism, and a straight expected-value-of-information Bayesian rule do against the list, and we're done.

No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write it in an email to a friend who asked.
