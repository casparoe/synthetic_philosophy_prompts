I want to pressure-test a position I hold before I commit to defending it in print.

Call it Q. Qualia, as philosophers standardly conceive them — intrinsic, ineffable, private, and directly apprehensible in consciousness — are an incoherent posit: nothing could instantiate all four of those marks together, and the stubborn conviction that one's own experience has such properties is best explained away as an artifact of introspection, which misrepresents its own activity in a stable and systematic way. I take myself to be holding the quining-qualia line, the one Dennett made notorious and Chase and Sanborn have argued more recently, but I don't want to shelter behind any particular formulation of it.

Two stipulations first, which I'd ask you to grant rather than adjudicate.

The first is a neurological dissociation I'll call phenomenal muteness. Its subjects have full access-consciousness: they discriminate stimuli as finely as anyone, reason fluently with what they take in, report accurately and with well-calibrated confidence, and pass every forced-choice, metacognitive, and postdictive test we can devise. What they deny is that there is anything it is like for them. The denial is spontaneous, stable across years, and unaccompanied by any other deficit in introspection or self-knowledge: asked to describe a perceptual state, they say without puzzlement that nothing is like anything. Suppose the condition is common, reliably diagnosed, and that these subjects are otherwise introspectively unimpeachable.

The second is a distinction inside Q. The hard version says the four-fold characterization is unsatisfiable in principle, so the concept is empty and necessarily uninstanted. The soft version concedes the concept may be coherent but denies we have any reason to think it instantiated, since every datum offered for qualia is equally well explained by access plus introspective misrepresentation.

So: is phenomenal muteness evidence for Q, or the one thing Q cannot describe? Does the hard version quietly become the soft one the moment its target characterization is allowed to be negotiable? And is Q forced to explain away the very datum it begins from — that experience seems some way to the subject — in a way that smuggles back the notion it rejects?

What I want from you is an honest map of Q's exposure, and I want you to commit: rank the defeaters, say plainly which of them actually succeeds and which the view can absorb, and give your reason, rather than laying out a menu and leaving me to choose. Distinguish what a defender of Q should himself concede counts against him from what he would rightly dismiss as begging the question in the intuition's favor. For what it's worth, the line I personally find hardest to answer is that Q's own starting datum is either a phenomenal seeming or it isn't, and both horns seem to cost me something.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, ordered most to least decisive, with no introduction and no concluding summary.
