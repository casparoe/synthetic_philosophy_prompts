Two people in my book club stayed an hour after everyone else left arguing about Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, and I told them I'd get a third opinion because I honestly cant tell who was winning.

The setup: Hofstadter's idea that the "I" is a pattern, not a thing, so bits of one person's pattern can live inside other people's heads -- he makes a lot of his wife Carol, who died in 1993. He put it in a 2007 Wired interview like this: a soul is an abstract pattern, and we can therefore internalize in our brain the souls of other people. Rosa thinks that's literally true. Eli thinks it's a poetic way of saying people remember you. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Rosa: if the self is a pattern and not some soul-stuff, then a large chunk of that pattern instantiated in someone else's brain is a chunk of that person, not a picture of them. It's just not binary -- pattern identity comes in degrees, so "part of him is still here" is a literal claim with a fuzzy truth value, not a metaphor.

(2) Eli: what's in another head is a model of you. A biography of Napoleon, or an actor playing him, reproduces a lot of the pattern too, and nobody thinks Napoleon is partly alive in the library. Survival needs the right kind of causal continuity, not just structural similarity.

(3) Rosa: model vs. instance is itself a difference of degree, and on Hofstadter's own picture there is no "original" to compare a copy to -- your self at 50 overlaps your self at 20 only partially and we happily call that survival, so overlap across skulls shouldn't be different in principle. Also your causal-continuity clause is doing all the work: Carol's version of him was built by decades of actual coupling with him, so it has the right history in a way a biography doesn't.

(4) Eli: coupling can't be the test either, because a language model trained on someone's complete letters and diaries would continue his sentences better than any widow and nobody thinks he partly survives in it. So the test either lets that in or you bolt on ad hoc conditions. More basic: there's no Hofstadter-shaped subject of experience in Carol's brain. What's there is only ever conscious as part of *her* stream. Persons are the things that have experiences; patterns aren't.

(5) Rosa: the unity objection begs the question -- the unity of a self is itself manufactured, and selves already aren't spatially neat (split-brain cases, memory spread all over the cortex, people who think with their notebooks and phones). On the model: the difference is whether the copy is live. Carol's version sat inside an agent that acted on it and got updated by the world; the trained model is a frozen snapshot.

(6) Eli: "live vs. snapshot" makes survival something that switches on and off, and even something other people can create by paying attention to you. And it leaves the amounts unrankable. Hofstadter wants to say there's more of him in some people than others. Name a metric -- fraction of dispositions preserved, accuracy of counterfactual prediction, causal weight in their decisions -- because I think each one either collapses into "they remember him well" or forces a sharp cut into something you yourself admit is fuzzy.

(7) Rosa: here's a case that forces the issue, and it's not a memory case at all. Take two people, A and B, coupled for decades so tightly that some of their loops only close through the other: A can finish thoughts only when B answers back, B's self-model only runs certain structures while A is watching, they complete each other's sentences as a matter of course. The feedback loop that makes each "I" literally runs across two skulls. That's one self spread over two brains, or at least a real case of distribution -- and if you say it's nothing new, explain how it differs from a merely tight causal coupling like a conductor and an orchestra.

(8) Eli: that's exactly the problem: coupling-to-constitutive-loop is a continuum, and what's missing at the end of it is still a single stream of consciousness. Two streams that reliably track each other aren't one stream.
