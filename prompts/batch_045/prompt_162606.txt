I'm a novelist, and I've accidentally given one of my characters an argument I can't write my way out of. She's a neurosurgeon who insists her own decisions do something; the other character is a philosopher who runs the exclusion argument at her (every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, the mental isn't identical to the physical, so the mental either systematically overdetermines everything or does no causal work at all). I've been reading around the replies — grounding and causal inheritance, proportionality, the interventionist/difference-making stuff — and I can't beleive how little movement there's been in forty years. Same moves, same counter-moves.

My working theory, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter: it isn't a disagreement about mind at all. It's a prior disagreement about what causation is — whether it's some productive transfer of oomph (one slot per event, and physics has filled it) or whether it's counterfactual dependence under interventions (lots of true causal claims about one event, at different grains). If that's right, the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, and the original question may be ill-posed rather than hard.

But check that against what each side actually says, because it might be a real disagreement in disguise: about whether the completeness of physics is a causal claim or only a claim about laws, about whether systematic overdetermination is genuinely impossible, or about whether there is causal slack at the physical level that's an empirical matter.

Here's the case from my draft that I think might matter, and I want to know whether it does. My surgeon has a neural implant: it monitors for the decision-related firing pattern and, when the biology fails to produce it, stimulates a different pattern that realizes the very same decision. So across occasions the decision's physical realizer varies — biological here, stimulated there — while the decision-level state is the invariant one, and the thing you'd have to intervene on to reliably change what she does. Does that do any work, or does each side have a canned answer ready (the exclusionist: the cause on that occasion is whichever realizer actually occurred; the other side: a disjunctive, non-proportional physical description isn't a cause at all)? If everyone has a canned answer, that's evidence for my diagnosis, and I'd like you to say so.

Give me the diagnosis, and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or why I should expect none. Also: keep clear what people who know this literature actually agree on versus what's genuinely contested, don't hand me a contested view as the consensus, and mark the places where your diagnosis is your own bet rather than something the field accepts.
