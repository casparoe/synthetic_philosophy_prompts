After a career in field biology and museum taxonomy, I have been using my retirement to work slowly through some philosophy of science, and I have arrived at the raven paradox. The book I am reading sets it out briskly: take the hypothesis that all ravens are black; grant the rule it gives (Nicod's criterion, I believe it is called) that an observation of a black raven confirms the hypothesis, an observation of a non-black non-raven confirms it, and an observation of a non-black raven refutes it; note that the hypothesis is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens; conclude that my noticing a white shoe in the hallway is, ever so slightly, evidence that all ravens are black. My gut rejects that conclusion flatly. I would like to know whether my gut can be turned into a defensible principle, so let me report my verdicts and ask what captures them.

1. An adult common raven, unmistakably black, in good light, in the valley where I have watched these birds for thirty years. Confirms, but only just: one more bird.
2. The same observation, except that the bird was in fact a crow and I corrected the identification that evening from photographs. Confirms nothing at all; it is a datum about my own error rates.
3. A raven seen at dusk and entered in the notebook as dark, probably black. Confirms a little, distinctly less than case 1.
4. A leucistic raven, roughly a third of the plumage white, otherwise healthy. Refutes outright, and I stop collecting.
5. A raven that is black because a rehabilitation centre dyed it before release. Confirms nothing; it makes me more careful, not more confident.
6. Two hundred black ravens, all from one flock feeding in one field. Confirms markedly less than two hundred birds drawn one apiece from two hundred separate watersheds, and I want a principle that says why.
7. A white shoe in my hallway. Confirms nothing.
8. A white shoe photographed by a trail camera in the raven's nesting territory, during a survey whose aim was to find ravens. Confirms a trace, I think, though I cannot say why.
9. A white shoe during a deliberate sweep whose stated aim was to find non-black ravens. Confirms rather more than case 8, and this difference feels real to me even though the object observed is the same.
10. A white shoe in a shoe shop in a city with no ravens. Confirms nothing.
11. A grey granite pebble, a red bus, a green apple. Confirms nothing, and increasingly emphatically nothing the further the object sits from anything corvid.
12. A museum drawer of forty specimens labelled raven, three of which turn out on re-examination to be other corvids. Disconfirms my earlier confidence, and retrospectively unconfirms whatever I thought those three had contributed.
13. A black raven logged by a phone app whose classifier is known to mistake crows for ravens about five per cent of the time. Confirms weakly; I discount it.
14. The same app flagging a white raven, which on inspection turns out to be a plastic bag. Leaves the hypothesis exactly where it was.
15. And the case that troubles me: the hallway shoe considered as evidence for the contrapositive, that all non-black things are non-ravens. Here I do feel a faint confirmation, even while I grant that the two sentences are equivalent and even while I feel nothing at all for the original.

The principle I have been trying out says that an observation confirms a generalization to the extent that it constitutes a genuine trial of it: the observed thing must be drawn from a population in which a counterexample could have turned up, the observation must be made under conditions in which a counterexample would have been detected, and the report must be accurate enough that we would know a counterexample if we had one. Anything else is not a failed test but no test, and a non-test confirms nothing. This handles cases 1 through 14 rather comfortably, including the survey-context contrast between 8 and 9. But it seems to convict me on case 15: a non-black thing drawn from the world's stock of non-black things is exactly a trial of the contrapositive, the shoe passes the trial, so by my own principle the contrapositive is confirmed, and therefore so is the equivalent original sentence — which is precisely the verdict my gut refuses.

So I have three questions. First, can my principle be repaired so that it respects the equivalence while delivering verdict 7, or does it have to be abandoned in favour of something else — a likelihood-ratio treatment, a relevance or sampling account, a projectibility constraint — and if a Bayesian treatment is what fits, please show me how the numbers actually go for cases 7, 8 and 9, since the difference between them is the one I care about. Second, and bluntly: which of my verdicts must I give up? I would rather surrender one verdict than adopt a principle with an ad hoc clause for each case, so tell me where the cheapest concession lies and what it costs me. Third, what does the surviving principle predict for cases I have not listed: a white plastic bag in a photograph returned by a Mars rover; an exhaustive sweep of a valley in which every non-black object encountered was catalogued and none was a raven; a raven whose genome predicts black plumage but which was photographed pale grey; a drawer of pale objects catalogued in 1890, long before anyone framed the hypothesis. I would also be glad to know whether the principle should change when the hypothesis is restricted, say to all ravens in this valley, rather than universal. Please give me the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each of its clauses.
