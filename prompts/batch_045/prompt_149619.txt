I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm midway through a long piece on how states decide who may cross a border and who then has to take responsibility for the person who crosses it. I've sat through two closed-door workshops on this in the past year — migration lawyers, a couple of political theorists, several former interior-ministry officials — and I keep returning to the same puzzle. As far as I can tell, the regime contains no right of entry at all: no one, however desperate, has a claim to be admitted to any particular country. But once a person is physically within a state's control, that state may not return her to a place where she would face persecution, and that prohibition is treated as absolute, not as something to be weighed against numbers, costs, or politics. Much of the machinery of the last thirty years looks designed to keep the second duty from ever being triggered: interception at sea, visa regimes enforced against airlines, carrier sanctions, safe-third-country rules that send an applicant back to the first country she set foot in, and offshore processing paid for by the state that does not want her. Yet the same governments resettle people out of camps they never tried to reach, and they describe that as generosity rather than as discharging an obligation — which suggests that at some level they think the duty tracks need rather than presence. The workshops also kept using the phrase "durable solutions," with a strong sense that the best outcome is eventually going home, and that protection is in principle temporary.

At the second workshop, a former official circulated a proposal I have not been able to stop thinking about. Call it the clearinghouse. Every state is assigned a notional share of the world's protection burden, computed from population and GDP. A person who would otherwise be a given state's responsibility can be transferred, with funding attached, to any state willing to host her for as long as the danger lasts; her status, work rights, and eventual path to citizenship travel with her; she must consent to the particular receiving state. If and when it becomes safe to return, the funding follows her home, on the theory that repatriation is the outcome everyone should be aiming at. The author insisted this is not a way of buying your way out: the receiving state must genuinely consent, the person must end up no worse off than she would have been in the first state, and the total number of people actually protected goes up. There is no enforcement beyond a public ledger of who has met their share. One critic in the room said that a duty you can discharge by paying someone else to perform it was never a duty owed to the person in the first place, and I haven't been able to work out whether that is a devastating objection or a confusion.

So here is what I want, in terms I can translate for readers who have never thought about any of this. Reconstruct the theory of the duty that this whole architecture implicitly relies on, and say what it strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits comfortably with. In particular: is the duty one that a particular state owes to a particular person because of some relation — that it has her in its power, that it caused the danger she fled, that it is best placed to help — or is it a duty to see to it that someone protects her, with the allocation to any given state being mere administrative bookkeeping? Is the clearinghouse different in principle from the machinery states already use, or does it only make explicit a commitment that safe-third-country transfers and offshore processing already contain? Does the answer change if the ground of protection is not persecution at all — which requires someone doing something to you — but something like "no state will secure your basic rights," which is what would cover people displaced by drought or sea-level rise, and which seems to sit badly with the assumption that protection is temporary and ends when home becomes habitable? And I'd especially like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined: which of these questions the existing regime has not actually answered, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the policies we have, and which parts of my piece would therefore be reporting a philosophical choice rather than describing a settled one.
