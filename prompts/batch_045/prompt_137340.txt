I've drifted into believing that Occam's razor doesn't need a justification of its own, because Solomonoff induction hands it to you: put the universal prior — weight each candidate predictor by 2 to the minus its description length — over the computable environments, and your total prediction error is bounded by the description length of whatever's actually generating your data. The razor isn't assumed, it's derived. And the old "but it depends which universal machine you pick" complaint I take to be answered by the invariance theorem: two machines differ by a constant, and constants stop mattering once enough data is in.

Tell me what would count against that. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me the ones the view's own defenders would concede, not just the ones its critics shout. I've got one I half-invented and can't tell whether it's standard or whether I'm messing something up: take a universal machine and hard-wire into it a huge finite codebook — a trillion digits of pi, a big slab of human commonsense, whatever. It's still universal, so the theorem still applies, but now the constant pointing the *other* way is enormous: the clean machine needs billions of bits just to simulate the bloated one, so the bloated machine gets a head start that doesn't wash out over any horizon I'll ever see. Is that right? Is there an answer to it besides "in the limit"?

Two more things I want: how much does it matter that the guarantee only ranges over computable sources — what would the world have to be like for that to actually bite, as opposed to the usual hand-wave about hypercomputation? And what if the bound holds but the number on the right-hand side is itself astronomic — if the true generator is laws plus a billion bits of frozen accident, "your error is bounded by the complexity of the source" sounds like a theorem and buys you nothing.

Also take an honest run at whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: in the limit, up to a constant, over computable sources. For a claim of this kind is that a defect, or just what a theorem is? What would definately move me is a case where the longer hypothesis beats the shorter one across every amount of data we could realistically get, with nothing telling us when that stops being true. Commit to a verdict on how exposed the position really is, and write it in British English.
