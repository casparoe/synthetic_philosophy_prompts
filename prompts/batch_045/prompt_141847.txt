so I've been chewing on this on my own since christmas — no philosophy background at all, I got into an argument with my sister about whether I still owed our dad anything after he died, given I'd promised him something on his deathbed and then quietly didn't do it — and I've written down roughly where I've landed. I'd like to know if it actually hangs together or if I'm helping myself to something somewhere. Here it is:

(1) When I promise, the bare fact that I said the words is itself part of my reason for doing the thing. It isn't just that keeping promises has good effects and breaking them has bad ones. Even in a made-up case where breaking it would go better in every consequential respect, there's something left over that counts against breaking it.

(2) But I also think that only works because promising is a real social practice, with rules people mostly follow and can be held to. If I said "I promise" to someone who had never come across the idea, or to a dog, or out loud to myself on a desert island, I don't think anything happens at all — no reason, no obligation, nothing.

(3) What a promise does, I think, is hand the other person a small temporary bit of authority over a bit of my life — that bit is theirs to direct for a while. That's why it's them specifically I've wronged if I break it, and them specifically who can let me off, not "society" or whoever happened to get hurt.

(4) A promise I was bullied into, or that I only made because someone lied to me about what I was agreeing to, isn't a weakened promise. It's void. There's nothing there to weigh up.

(5) Same for promising something that was already wrong to do — helping a mate hide something from the police, say. Doesn't bind, and I don't think it leaves any residue either: no apology owed to anybody afterwards, nothing I have to explain away.

(6) But if I promise something and it later turns out that keeping it would be a bit bad for a third party — not wrong exactly, just worse overall — I think it still binds. I either keep it or I go back to the promisee and get released. I don't get to run the numbers myself and quietly drop it.

(7) And I think the promise to my dad still bound after he died, even though he was never going to find out either way, and even though I'm fairly sure the whole point of having promises is to make it safe for the living to rely on each other. Same for a promise to someone who's completely forgotten it and would be mildly irritated if I turned up years later insisting on keeping it.

(8) I think it matters that the person actually heard me and took me to be promising. If they thought I was joking, or they didn't really understand what I was saying, I don't think it binds however sincerely I said the words.

My own suspicions, which might be wrong: (2) and (7) feel like they're pulling opposite ways. (1) and (2) feel like it too, because the only story I can tell about why we'd have the practice in (2) is that it's useful, which sounds like exactly the consequentialism I was rejecting in (1). And (4) bugs me — if the practice's rules are what make promises bind, then whether coercion voids them looks like a local detail of our rules rather than the principled thing I take it to be. On the other hand (5) and (3) might only look like they clash: if nobody is wronged when I break an immoral promise, isn't (3) hollow? Or does that dissolve once you're careful?

So: tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, and rank the real ones by how bad they are rather than listing them all as equally serious. For each real one, say what would have to give and what dropping it would cost me — in terms of what else I'd have to stop saying, not in terms of what some theory would prefer. If there's one single revision that would tidy up several at once, say what it is. And if any two of my eight are secretly the same belief wearing different clothes, tell me that too. Also say which one you think I'm least likely to actually abandon given how the rest of the set is shaped.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write someone a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. British English please.
