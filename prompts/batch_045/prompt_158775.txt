I keep going back and forth with a friend from my ML reading group about the "strange inversion" pair — the Darwin one (you see the exquisite fit between an organism and its environment, and instead of explaining it by a mind that had the design in mind first, you explain it by a mindless sorting process, so the design-looking thing turns out to be an effect with no designing cause behind it) and the Turing one (you run into something that performs as if it understood — good moves, good answers, sensible-looking explanations — and instead of explaining that by a comprehender, you explain it by competence without comprehension, built up mechanically). Everyone talks as if these are the same argument wearing two different costumes, and I get the family resemblance, but they don't seem to land the same way, and I want to know whether that difference is in the logic of the arguments or just in who happens to find them irresistable.

Here's my guess about where the asymmetry actually sits, tell me if I've got it wrong. In Darwin's case what gets taken away is a claim about origins: the adaptations stay real, the wing still flies, only the claim that a purpose came first gets dropped, and nobody has to deny that the eye does what it does. In Turing's case the disputed property isn't a fact about how the thing got built — the skeptic says the comprehension would have to be there in the doing, so a story about how the competence was produced by mindless search can be completely true and still leave it wide open whether any comprehension is present. So the inversion dissolves designedness because designedness always was a claim about where something came from, but it apparently can't dissolve comprehension, because comprehension was never a claim about where anything came from. Is that the real structural difference, or is it just an artifact of how I've set the terms up?

Two more things I'd like folded in. First, self-application: if Turing's inversion is right about me as well — if my own judging "that system doesn't really get it" is itself competence produced by machinery — does that undercut my authority to make the judgment, or is its authority untouched, the way a reliable thermometer doesn't need to understand heat? I've started labeling the two cases exportable versus self-consuming inversions, but I can't tell whether that distinction does any real work. Second, what kind of property should we even take comprehension to be? If it's dispositional — the thing can handle follow-ups, notice when it's out of its depth, redeploy what it did in a new setting — then the whole fight starts to look like a partly empirical question about which dispositions the system has. If it's closer to a what-it's-like property, in the neighborhood of the hard problem of consciousness, then no amount of competence evidence settles anything and the inversion is quietly answering a different question than the one being asked. Can you lay out how the Darwin/Turing analogy looks under each of those two readings, and which reading makes the analogy look strongest? Please answer in American English, and please don't hand me a reading list or a pile of citations — I want your own explanation, worked through on the concepts.
