i'm taking a seminar on AI alignment this term and this week's topic is side-effect avoidance and low-impact agents. In Friday's discussion one of the PhD students kept leaning on a medical analogy: a low-impact agent is just the medical do-no-harm slogan for robots — the agent should go after the goal we hand it the way a surgeon goes after a cure, and otherwise leave the world as close as it found it. He used it to argue something stronger, that we don't need to solve value specification to get systems safe enough to switch on, because we can just make them conservative, penalise deviation from the do-nothing baseline, and get most of the safety without the impossible part.

I definately half believe him and half think the analogy is doing all the work. Can you audit it properly?

Start with the correspondence: what is the patient, the disease, the treatment, the side effect, the indication, the consent form, the baseline of normal functioning, the specialist's training? I want it as an actual table. In particular I want to know what plays the role of the indication, because in medicine something counts as a side effect only relative to what the treatment is for, and I can't tell what the analogue of that is supposed to be for a free-standing agent that just gets handed an instruction.

Then the places it breaks — and this is the part I actually care about — separated into the breaks that matter for the conclusion he's drawing and the ones that are cosmetic. The two I keep circling: (a) in medicine the no-harm baseline is homeostatic, there's some discoverable fact about what normal functioning for a human organism is, and it doesn't look like the world at large has anything like that, so deviation-from-inaction ends up measuring something arbitary; and (b) medicine trades harm against benefit inside a single patient's interests and with that patient's consent, and neither has an obvious counterpart when the effects spread across everyone.

I also sketched my own fix and want to know if it's a dodge. Instead of comparing the agent's world to the world where it was never switched on, compare it to the world as it would have been if a competent, well-briefed human assistant had been given the same instruction — call it the human-substitute baseline. My claim is that the medical analogy, done right, pushes toward this rather than toward inaction, since in medicine the baseline isn't nobody-does-anything, it's what a competent clinician would have changed. His reply is that this just hides the entire value specification inside the word competent and I've gained nothing. Which of us is closer to right, and can the analogy settle it either way?

And if the medical one can't carry the weight, is there a better comparison — a houseguest, a contractor working in someone else's house, an invasive species, something else — that isolates what's actually hard here without quietly smuggling in the answer?
