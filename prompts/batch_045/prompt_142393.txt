I've been reading a short popular book about Wittgenstein's late notebooks on certainty, published after his death, and I want to know how much of its account of "hinge" propositions to trust. The book's summary of the view, as nearly as I can reconstruct it from my notes, runs like this:

Moore thought he could refute the skeptic by producing certain empirical knowledge: holding up his hands and saying that here is a hand, and that he knows it, was supposed to be a proof of an external world. Wittgenstein's mature view is more radical but in the same spirit. He agrees that Moorean truisms — that here is a hand, that the earth existed long before I was born, that every human being has parents — really are certain, and he makes this the basis of everything else: these are the foundations of knowledge. Call them hinges. A hinge is an empirical proposition we are maximally confident of; everything else we believe rests on it, in the sense that our other beliefs are justified by being supported by these basic ones; and the hinges themselves are self-justifying, since any attempt to justify them would be circular. The point of the name is that our questions and our doubts turn on them: doubt only makes sense inside a system, and the hinges are what hold the system still, exempt from doubt not because we have checked them but because calling them into question would destroy the practice in which doubting has a use.

The consequences the book draws are more sweeping than this sounds. First, skepticism is not so much refuted as defused: the skeptic's doubts are not real doubts at all, only noise, because they violate the conditions under which a doubt could have a point. Second, and the book says this is the part readers usually miss, hinge epistemology is a form of relativism. Hinges are not supported by evidence, and any justification of them would be circular, so a community that hinges on astrology rather than on astronomy is not making a mistake: within its own system there is nothing for it to be wrong about. The same holds locally. If someone's hinge-commitments include that a famous landing on the moon was faked, and that no photograph or testimony could ever bear on the matter, then there is nothing we can rationally say to them; we can only record that their hinges differ from ours. Third, this is why arguments with conspiracy theorists break down so reliably. Rational criticism consists in giving reasons, reasons are internal to a system of hinges, and there is no neutral ground outside every system from which the system itself could be criticized.

Two things in this make me suspicious. I had the impression that Wittgenstein thought Moore's use of the words "I know" was the mistake rather than the solution, and that his talk of certainty is about how these words work rather than about how confident anyone feels — but I don't trust that impression, and beyond it I can't tell where the real errors are. So please audit the passage.

Go through it clause by clause and sort it into what a sympathetic specialist would accept as fair, what is a simplification or a tendentious formulation that a specialist would wince at but concede as roughly right, and what a defender of the reading in those notebooks would reject outright. Pay particular attention to whether hinges are best described as propositions we are certain of, as the foundations or grounds of our other beliefs, and as self-justifying.

Then take the three consequences in turn and tell me whether they follow from the view once it has been stated carefully. I especially want to know whether the relativist consequence is one a defender has to swallow, or whether the argument for it depends on the misreadings you have already identified; and if it matters, distinguish the local case, where one person's hinge-commitments differ from mine on some contested matter of fact, from the global case, where a whole community's practices are built on quite different ones.
