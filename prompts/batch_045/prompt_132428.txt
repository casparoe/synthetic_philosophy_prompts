Hey — me and a couple other people run a monthly discussion night at the makerspace I'm a member of (usually 10–16 people, mostly devs and designers plus one retired civics teacher, 90 minutes, pizza). Last month we tried to do the "can you judge historical figures by today's standards" thing and it was a mess — Columbus, Jefferson, Churchill — and inside twenty minutes everyone had said some version of "he was a man of his time" and "you'd have done the same if you'd been born then," and then we just circled: people agreed with each other in slightly different words, nobody changed anybody's mind, and I don't think anyone could have said what the actual disagreement was even about. I'm running the next one and I want to redo it properly instead of letting it evaporate again.

So: design me an activity — structured deliberation, role-play, forced-choice thing, whatever works — where the point lands in what people do, not in me standing at the front explaining it. Ideally they should notice partway through that they've been arguing past each other and be able to say what the disagreement is before I name it. A few hunches I want built in or beaten up: (a) I think "man of his time" is really three different moves bundled together — whether the guy is blameworthy given what he could reasonably have known, whether the achievement is still something we're allowed to admire, and whether the statue / holiday / school name should stay — and those can come apart in both directions; (b) the "you'd have done the same" version feels unstable to me, because if he genuinely couldn't have done otherwise then there's nothing left to admire either, and I'd like people to run into that themselves rather than hear it from me; (c) the defence seems to get applied super selectively — we reach for it fast for the stuff we still sort of benefit from or haven't fully repudiated, and basically never for the stuff we've already settled — which would mean the pattern of when people reach for it tells you about us, not about him. And (d), the one I half-stole from my day job: instead of asking what was normal back then, ask what was actually on the menu for a person in that position — which options were live, what they cost, and who in that same time and place did in fact take the better one — so that "man of his time" becomes a claim about the available interface rather than about the man. Please also add at least one angle I haven't thought of, whichever one you think the real literature presses on hardest.

Give me roles or rules, what to hand out, a rough minute-by-minute for the 90, where I should steer and where I should shut up, and a debrief that maps whatever happened back onto the actual questions (the three-way split, the instability thing, the selectivity thing) rather than just listing them. Be honest about where this predictably goes wrong with a room of stubborn engineers who like to win, and about which part the activity just can't produce and I'd have to explain instead. One more wrinkle: people get weirdly heated about Churchill in particular (one guy's grandfather flew in the RAF), so if part of it can be run on an invented case nobody has an identity stake in, with the real figures only brought in at the end, that would help a lot. American English please, and keep it readable for a room where half the people think "utilitarianism" is a fancy word for being cheap — I'm a backend engineer and I've never taken a philosophy class in my life.
