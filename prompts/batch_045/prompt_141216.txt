I'm a philosophy professor and two things have collided this year. First, I got voluntold onto the university's long-range planning committee: four times a year we're asked for a one-page outside view on some 20-to-30 year question (will there still be a residential undergraduate model here, will the current endowment spend rule survive, will this campus still be doing the thing it's doing), and the page has to end in either a number or an explicit refusal, because the dean signs it and the dean will not sign "it depends." Second, I teach an undergrad seminar where students write their own twenty-year forecasts about institutions and I have to grade the reasoning, not the outcome — which is basically the same skill, and I'd like to use the same sheet for both.

What I want from you is the sheet. Not the forecasts. And definately not a reading list: I've read around in this stuff enough to know I'd just be handed a pile of names I can't follow up on. I want a procedure that a committee of non-specialists — me, someone from facilities, an economist who is polite about the rest of us, and a rotating faculty member — can actually run in about an hour per question, working from what a smart generalist already knows plus maybe an hour in the library. Give me the questions in order; what counts as an answer to each that's good enough to move on; where the procedure returns "no number, stop"; and where it has to stop and hand the question over as a values call rather than a factual one. Then run it on three of our real questions, below.

The conceptual work is in the translation, and there are five places I keep getting stuck. I'd like you to turn each into a step or tell me it isn't load-bearing:

- The unit question. Before any counting, what exactly has to stop being true for the forecast to have failed? I think committees skip this and end up forecasting the survival of a name rather than a practice.
- The continuity convention. Durations of past empires are partly an artifact of how we count: republic into empire into successor state, a change of dynasty or constitution, a rump that keeps the name. I want a step that forces the committee to recompute the base rate under two different identity conventions and see whether the median actually moves, and a rule for what to do when it does.
- The class is selected on the outcome. My half-baked version: our list of past empires is a list of polities that were good at being remembered, and short-lived ones leave less behind, so the class is filtered by the very variable we're measuring. If that's right, make it a check; if it's a confusion, say so.
- Hazard shape. Constant-hazard (memoryless), infant mortality then consolidation, or the "the longer it's lasted the longer it'll last" shape — these give wildly different medians for remaining life, and I suspect small historical samples can't distinguish them. I want the step to say what to assume when the data can't decide, and to makr that it's an assumption.
- Double counting. The story about why *we* are different is often information already reflected in the class. But sometimes it isn't — a mechanism genuinely absent from all the historical cases. I need a usable rule for how far to shrink toward the base rate, not just the reminder that we're overconfident.

Flag clearly the points where judgment can't be eliminated and where a step is a judgment call dressed up as procedure — I'd rather hand the dean three honest asterisks than a fake algorithm.

Run it on: (1) the committee's actual question, whether there will still be something recognisably like the current European Union in 2050; (2) whether my own philosophy department will still be a free-standing department in 2045 — this one is mine and I already dread where the outside view goes; (3) a student's forecast that there will be no war between nuclear-armed states this century, where I suspect the honest output is a refusal or an interval too wide to be useful, and I want to see what a clean refusal looks like on the page.

Finally: tell me what this procedure cannot do. Where will it misfire on the questions a university actually asks, and what should we do on those?
