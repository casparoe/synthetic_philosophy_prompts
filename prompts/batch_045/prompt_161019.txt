I write novels, and I've got a chapter that won't move, so I'm hoping a short story will shake it loose. It's a side-quarrel with something I keep circling: the boundary problem, who gets to count as the people who get to decide.

What I want, in practical terms: a reader who's never thought about democratic theory should finish it feeling sick about a specific machine, and a reader who has thought about it should be able to name exactly what the machine does. Nobody in the story says demos, or franchise, or representation, or gives any version of the little speech. No narrator explaining either.

The setup I've half built, steal it or change it: a walled salt-marsh city. Its charter fixes the voting roll once a year — whoever is asleep inside the sea-wall on the night of the new year counts as a citizen for the year following. Out on the tidal flats beyond the wall there are stilt hamlets, a couple thousand people. The city's sluices and the wall itself decide when the flats flood, so those people are pushed around by the city constantly and can't vote on any of it. Then a measure goes to the city vote: extend the wall outward, enclose the flats, drain them. It would stop the stilt houses washing away and it would destroy the tidal fishery they live on, and it would also put them inside the wall — i.e., on the roll.

Heres the part I can't get right, and probably the reason the chapter is stuck. It's a measure that can only pass under the franchise that passing it destroys. The city votes yes partly because the reclaimed land is worth money and partly because everyone can already see what happens next: the new voters immediately vote to breach the wall and let the tides back, which puts them outside the city again, which takes them off the roll, which lets the city vote the enclosure back in. A pump, swinging every couple of years. And the thing I think is the cruel part, which should be felt and not stated: those two thousand only ever get a vote on undoing what was just done to them, never on what's about to be done.

Don't resolve it. End at or just before the second vote, with whoever keeps the tide-register holding the pen — and make her neither hero nor villain. Both of the obvious tests for who should count, everyone affected and everyone subject to the law, should look genuinely attractive here and both should break on this case, as consequence rather than as argument. Please don't let it turn into a Brexit story or a voter-ID story or a gerrymandering story; I've read all of those and they flatten it into something I already know. Around 1500 words, and it has to work for someone who hasn't read the novel, so no info-dumping about how the city runs — get it in through the cracks. Just the story, no notes after, no explanation of what it means. I've been chewing on this for like a week and its driving me nuts.
