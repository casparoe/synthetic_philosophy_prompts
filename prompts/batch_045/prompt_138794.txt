I am one of seven trustees of the Marchmont Trust, a small English charitable foundation that disburses roughly £12m a year to organisations working with care leavers in the North East and the Midlands. Ahead of our away day on 14 March I have to circulate a paper recommending what we should do about one part of our grant-making, and I would be grateful if you would draft it for me.

The part in question is the small-grants stream: about two hundred awards a year of under £50,000, decided jointly by one programme officer and one trustee on a fortnightly call, with no capacity for individual monitoring. (The large-grants stream, £250,000 and above, always goes to the full board with a site visit, and is not up for discussion.) For six years the small stream has been allocated through an outcomes framework: applicants commit to a cost-per-outcome figure on a common metric — sustained engagement at twelve months, weighted for the vulnerability of the cohort — and the officer ranks applications by that figure. Two failures have become impossible to ignore. The first is gaming: last year a provider met its numbers by redefining engagement so that a text message counted, and we only learned of it because a whistleblower wrote to us. The second is quieter and worse. The organisations working with the most chaotic young people cannot promise twelve-month outcomes at any price, so they have stopped applying. The stream is now funding the sort of work that is easy to measure rather than the work we would actually choose.

Our director of programmes has proposed abolishing the ranking metric for this stream and replacing it with what she calls an endorsement procedure. Each application would be stripped of the applicant's name, charity number and track record with us — region, amount requested and description of activity retained — and put to a language model trained on the Trust's own twelve years of decisions and written reasons, asked only whether this is the sort of thing the trustees would endorse. That score would do the ranking; the officer and trustee would then read the top band in full and decide. She has also floated a further wrinkle: that the material put to the model should describe each application as it would appear in next year's annual report rather than as it arrives, on the grounds that we judge our own grants most honestly after the fact.

The board is split roughly down the middle, and I need the paper to do more than split it again. Three worries have been voiced and I think they are serious. First, a model trained on our past decisions will learn to predict our approval, not the reasons we take ourselves to have — and our past approvals were themselves partly shaped by a metric we now disown. Second, we have already had two applications this year containing passages that read as if they were written for a machine rather than for us; once applicants know a model ranks them, the gaming does not go away, it moves. Third, and to my mind hardest: because we cannot monitor small grants individually, we have no ground truth. If endorsement replaces the metric, how would we ever find out whether it is doing better, rather than replacing a failed proxy with an unmeasurable one we cannot audit?

What I want is a short paper — say 900 to 1,000 words — addressed to my fellow trustees, with a bottom line they can vote on at the away day: reject the proposal, adopt it, or adopt it subject to conditions, and if conditions, then named, dated and checkable ones. It should state plainly what we should do about the monitoring problem: if we adopt, what would we put in place so that in two years we have some evidence rather than a feeling? It should give the losing side its strongest case, not a version of it that is easy to knock over. And it should be usable by trustees who have never read a word of AI safety — one of our number has started describing this whole argument as a choice between approval-directed and goal-directed systems, and I would like the paper to say whether that framing actually earns its keep here or merely relabels a problem we already had.
