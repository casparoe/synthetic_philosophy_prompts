**Question (25 marks; 8 / 7 / 10).** Two rather different arguments go by the name *the doomsday argument*.

(a) Set out the Carter–Leslie version, in which you treat your birth rank among humans as a random draw, and state exactly what it entitles us to conclude about the total number of humans who will ever live.
(b) Set out Gott's version, including its delta-t form, and say what it assumes in place of what (a) assumes.
(c) The reference class problem asks which class of observers you should treat yourself as a random sample from. Explain how it bears on these arguments, and say whether it undercuts them.

**Student's answer:**

The Carter–Leslie argument asks me to treat my birth rank r among humans as a random draw from the integers 1 to N, where N is the total number of humans who will ever live. If I have rank r ≈ 60 billion, then for any N ≥ r the probability of finding myself at exactly that rank is 1/N, so hypotheses predicting a much larger N are penalised in proportion to their size: there is a 95% chance that N is less than 20r, i.e. that fewer than about 1.2 trillion humans will ever exist. The argument requires the Self-Indication Assumption — the principle that, other things equal, you should count yourself more likely to be in a world containing more observers, since such a world contains more slots you might have occupied — because without it there is no route from the fact that I find myself at rank r to any conclusion about N.

It is important to see that this is a Bayesian shift rather than a proof of imminent extinction. It updates whatever prior over N one began with, and a prior that strongly favours a large future — say, because a long future gives civilisation more opportunity to spread and produce people — can outweigh the shift entirely.

Gott's version reaches the same 95% figure from a different direction. He imposes a scale-invariant prior on the total, P(N) ∝ 1/N, and in the more general delta-t form treats the present as a random moment in the lifetime of any process: at 95% confidence, a process of present age t has a remaining duration between t/39 and 39t. Applied to the human species this yields a future of a few thousand to several million years. So where (a) needs a sampling assumption about persons, (b) needs only a choice of prior.

The reference class problem bears on both, since in each case something has to be sampled — observers in one case, moments in the other — and different choices return different answers. And that is what makes it fatal. Widen the class to include future posthuman or machine minds and our rank becomes typical, so the argument dissolves; and since nothing in the argument tells you which class is the right one, the shift it recommends carries no real evidential weight.
