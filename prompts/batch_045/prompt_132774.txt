ok so I just had a three-hour arguement with my friend about whether two reasonable people can agree to disagree. she's read Aumann and says the theorem basically settles it; I said it settles nothing because every assumption is fake — common prior, common knowledge of each other's exact posterior, unbounded computation — and that real disagreement is mostly about who has the better evidence. we went nowhere because we kept proposing norms instead of saying what a norm has to do.

so: write me the desiderata for a norm of disagreement. not a theory of rationality in general — a rule that takes as input (i) your credence in some proposition, (ii) something you learn about another agent's credence in it, and outputs your new credence. what does such a rule have to deliver to count as adequate, and what's the case or consideration behind each demand?

what I want on the list at minimum:

- the ideal limit: does it have to reproduce Aumann-style agreement when the assumptions all hold, or is that the wrong test to impose on a rule meant for bounded agents?
- the coarse case: you don't learn their number, you only learn that they disagree with you at all, or that they'd take the other side of an even-odds bet.
- the expert case: you have positive evidence they're worse informed, biased, or drunk. the rule has to let you hold your ground while blocking the move where you always conclude you're the one who's the expert.
- order-independence: learning about A's view then B's shouldn't leave you somewhere different from B then A, unless the rule can say why the order should matter.
- self-consistency: what does it say when the other person is running the same rule and you both know it — including what you should announce afterward, since that's what feeds their next input?
- exploitability: two agents both following the rule shouldn't be reliably money-pumped by a third party who just watches them disagree.

purpose, so you know what counts as cheating: I want something I could actually implement in a software agent, not a characterization of what an ideal agent does. so tractability is a real requirement, not a nice-to-have.

then: mark each requirement negotiable or non-negotiable, flag any subset you think is jointly unsatisfiable (I strongly suspect the ideal-limit one and the tractability one fight), and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate rules — conciliationism, steadfastness, averaging in log-odds, whatever else you'd put in the running. keep that last part short, I can fill in the scorecard myself.
