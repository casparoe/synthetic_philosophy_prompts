I am putting together a case study on model failure for a graduate seminar on uncertainty in economics, and I want the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998 worked through using Keynes's theory of probability, not the usual popular apparatus. I already have the pop-Knightian version — they treated uncertainty as risk — and it is of no use to me; what I want is a disciplined mapping that runs in both directions: what Keynes's framework says the fund's principals faced and should have done, and what the episode in turn shows about the framework.

Take the facts as given, so that nothing turns on looking anything up. The fund was founded in 1994 by John Meriwether, with Myron Scholes and Robert Merton among its principals; Scholes and Merton shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in 1997 for work on option pricing. The core strategy was convergence trading: identify pairs or clusters of securities whose price relationship had been stable, take large positions when the relationship widened, and wait for it to narrow again. Positions were financed at very high leverage, by most accounts upwards of twenty times equity, and were held across many markets and asset classes at once. In 1998 the fund lost something in the region of $4.6 billion in under four months, in the wake of the Asian crisis of 1997 and the Russian default and devaluation of August 1998; in late September a consortium of fourteen banks put together a recapitalisation of about $3.65 billion in a deal brokered by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and the fund was wound down and dissolved by 2000.

The lens I want applied is Keynes's book on probability of 1921: probability as a logical relation between evidence and conclusion rather than a frequency or a degree of belief, the claim that not all probabilities are numerically measurable and that some are not even comparable with one another, and above all the distinction he draws between the probability of an argument and what he calls its weight — the amount of relevant evidence behind it, which increases with new evidence whether or not the probability itself goes up. I also want the later material on convention, confidence and liquidity preference brought in where the early framework runs out, and I want it stated plainly where that is happening.

Concretely, please do the following.

Separate three diagnoses that get run together. Call them calibration thinness: the historical sample does not pin down a distribution tightly enough to support the number used. Structural novelty: the process generating the sample is not the process in force during the episode, so no amount of extra data of that kind would have helped. Non-comparability: there is no determinate probability, and not even a determinate ordering, for the prospect in question. Say which of these Keynes's apparatus is even about, which of them the weight notion can express, and whether his view is best read as holding that some prospects have no probability at all or only that their probabilities are not measurable or comparable. Then say which diagnosis actually fits LTCM, and on what grounds, rather than asserting that all three were present.

Do the mapping properly. Identify who the decision-makers are, what their real options were at the point when the positions were built and again as losses mounted, what counted as evidence for them, and what the payoff structure looked like once leverage, collateral terms and the possibility of forced liquidation are included. I suspect the interesting feature is that the loss mechanism was institutional — margin calls, illiquidity, everyone wanting out through the same door — rather than a matter of the spreads never converging. Say whether the Keynesian story of convention and liquidity preference predicts that mechanism or only its mood, and where the early framework falls silent because it is about propositions and evidence and has nothing to say about leverage, feedback or the endogenous liquidity of a position.

Construct the decision rule that is missing. Keynes gives no account of how weight is to be used in acting; he distinguishes it from probability and then largely leaves it there. Build the rule a Keynesian principal would need — one that takes both the probability and the weight as inputs — and then say honestly whether it is still recognisably Keynesian or whether it collapses into something familiar, such as maximising expected utility with a cautious prior, or a worst-case rule, or a two-parameter rule that penalises thin evidence. Then apply it to LTCM: would it have changed any actual decision, and would the change have been visible before August 1998 or only in retrospect?

Finally, say what the episode does to the theory. My worry is that the Keynesian diagnosis is unfalsifiable in hindsight: almost any model-driven blow-up can be relabelled as a case of low weight after the fact, and I want to know what would count as evidence against the diagnosis, whether weight can be assessed ex ante at all, and whether a fund whose entire business is leveraged convergence betting could have adopted the framework without closing itself down. Close with a verdict — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the framework never anticipated — and with the single question about Keynes on probability that this case cannot settle.
