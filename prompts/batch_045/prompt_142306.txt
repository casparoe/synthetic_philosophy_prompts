I've ended up on my hospital's clinical ethics committee (don't ask — the department needed a body and I was the only one who didn't leave the room fast enough), and we keep a running internal log where every consideration that comes up in a consult gets tagged either "accomodate as a value" or "correct as a bias." I need help with Thursday's case, because the log really doesn't settle it and I can't tell whether that's because the case is genuinely hard or because our past rulings were just a mess. A friend who does moral psychology told me something like "you can't separate a preference from a bias without a theory of what the response is even tracking," which sounded deep and helped me decide exactly nothing. What we've ruled so far: parents of a four-year-old with a devastating prognosis demanding full intensive care and saying flat out that they don't care what it costs the hospital or who else doesn't get the bed — value. A staff surgeon asking that his neighbour's mother's elective hip be moved ahead of patients who have waited longer — bias. A bereaved family asking that the kidneys go only to a recipient from their own ethnic community — bias. A patient refusing a life-saving transfusion on religious grounds, even though we think the belief behind it is false and he can't give a reason a non-believer would accept — value. A nurse who wants to be reassigned away from a patient with a severe facial deformity because she finds him hard to be around, while fully granting that it isn't his fault — bias, and we told her to work on it rather than swapping her off the case. A family insisting on aggressive treatment while conceding the outcome is no better than comfort care, because stopping would be giving up on her — bias; the team re-described the options a few times and the insistence basically evaporated. A heart failure patient who'd rather have six good months at home than two years on a device, where the whole team thinks she's badly underestimating the two years — value. And a forty-year patient asking that the nurse who has looked after her for years be the one to tell her the bad news instead of the attending — value.

The new one: a man in his seventies with ALS who talks through an eye-tracker has been using, for about two years, a language model trained on his late wife's emails and voicemails. He says it's the closest thing to her he has, he uses it daily, and he wants us to treat what it says as a real input when we decide whether to do a procedure with maybe a 15–20% shot at buying him a few more months. He's fully competent, he knows perfectly well it's software, and when we re-described it to him as "a prediction of what a model trained on her texts would output," he said yes, that's exactly what it is, and it still carries weight for him; he's told his kids, who are fine with it. The thing that's actually keeping me up: one of our residents ran a version where the outputs were swapped for ones from a model trained on a stranger's texts, and he rated those as just as helpful and just as meaningful — although he also says that if he'd known they came from a stranger he'd have discounted them entirely, which I think is a different claim from the one the resident thinks she tested. Our chair says obviously a value: it's his decision, it costs us nothing, and we accommodate Jehovah's-Witness-style refusals all the time. That resident says obviously a bias: we'd be humoring a delusion and laundering it as respect.

So: if we want to be consistent with our own log, how should we tag it, and which precedent is the closest analog? More useful to me though — what are the two or three different rules that fit everything we've ruled so far, what does each of them predict here, and which would you actually bet on and why? And this is the part I keep failing at: what single further fact, or further case, would genuinely tell those rules apart, rather than just giving everyone one more thing to have a bare intuition about? I've tried glossing the distinction as "doesn't survive reflection," "doesn't track the reasons the person gives," and "wouldn't be sayable to the person it disadvantages," and all three seem to me to split on this case, which is definately a sign I've got the wrong three.
