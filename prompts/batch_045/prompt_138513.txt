I have been defending, in a setting where it has recently started to matter practically, a specific account of what left and right are, and I want to know how exposed it is.

The account has two parts. First: neither side is a doctrine or a policy package, since almost any concrete policy has been held by both at some point; what is stable across that churn is a second-order fact about which state of affairs each side treats as standing in need of a reason. On the left, existing asymmetries of wealth, status and power are presumptively suspect and must be justified, while departures from them are not. On the right, established practice, earned deserts and the spontaneous outcomes of markets and civil society are presumptively acceptable, and it is their deliberate disruption that must be justified. Call this the asymmetry of the justificatory default. Second: what actually holds each side together day to day is not that default but an adjacency structure — two people at opposite ends of a coalition may share no principle at all, each link in the chain sharing something with its neighbour, so that realignment pressure shows up first in the links rather than at the ends — plus a shared object of distrust, a settled answer to the question of which authority is not to be believed.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be specific about which counterexamples a defender of the account should concede rather than explain away. I am most worried about three. One: the defaults look as if they have flipped in places — parts of the right now celebrate disruption and attack inherited institutions, while parts of the left defend them — and I cannot tell whether that refutes the account or whether it merely lets the default be re-attached to whatever each side currently has, in which case it predicts nothing. Two: the object of distrust may be doing all the real explanatory work, with the justificatory default as decoration; how would I test the two against each other with evidence I could actually gather? Three: it may collapse into a rival I have not named — plain conservatism-versus-reformism relabelled, or a view on which the real variable is which out-group you are willing to defend, or just the much-discussed sorting of Western electorates by educational attainment described in more flattering terms. Say also whether the account has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is fatal for something meant to do the kind of work I need of it. For what it is worth, in the group I work with the thing that best predicts whether someone walks out of a meeting is not which policies they hold but whose account of the housing crisis they find credible — which is exactly why I suspect the second part of my account is doing all the work and the first is ornament.
