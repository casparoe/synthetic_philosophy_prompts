After forty years in experimental physical chemistry — calorimetry, mixtures, a good deal of statistical mechanics — I have begun reading moral philosophy, and my instinct is to translate everything into quantities I can manipulate. What I want from you is not an explanation pitched at a beginner but a systematic translation of one debate into the vocabulary I actually think in, together with an honest account of what the translation loses.

The vocabulary you may use freely: ensembles and coarse-graining; extensive versus intensive quantities; Legendre transforms; Jensen's inequality; convexity and Schur-concavity (I know majorization under another name and will recognise it once defined); entropy-like functionals, large-deviation rate functions, and response or perturbation expansions about a stationary state. Assume no philosophy on my part beyond two survey articles, no familiarity with the literature's labels, and no wish for anything to be cited.

The claims I want restated, one at a time and as faithfully as possible:

(i) that a benefit to a person matters more, morally, the worse off that person is — stated, as I have met it, as a sum over persons of a strictly increasing and strictly concave function of their well-being;

(ii) that this is genuinely different from valuing equality, even though in practice it directs benefits toward the worse off;

(iii) the levelling-down objection as it is pressed against the view that equality is intrinsically good: that such a view must sometimes concede that a world in which the better off have been brought down to the level of the worst off, with no one made better off, is in one respect better;

(iv) the reply that the priority view escapes this objection, because its weighting function is strictly increasing, so that lowering anyone's well-being always tells against an outcome;

(v) the rider that the escape is partial or fragile — something about how the worse off are to be identified, whether by absolute level, by rank in the distribution, or by comparison with others; something about populations that are not fixed in size; and something about ranking uncertain prospects rather than settled outcomes.

My own reconstruction, which I would like tested rather than humoured: in thermodynamics a scale-invariant quantity — an entropy per particle, a coefficient of variation, a Gini coefficient — is not the sort of thing that can be an amount of anything at all, since it does not add across systems and does not change by a definite amount when a subsystem is removed. Levelling down looks to me like the price of admitting such a dispersion measure into an objective function alongside an extensive total; and prioritarianism looks like the trick of securing sensitivity to dispersion without paying it, not by appending a term that measures spread but by curving the transform, which leaves the total strictly increasing along every coordinate while making it Schur-concave. If that is right, (iii) is really a complaint about combining intensive and extensive quantities in one criterion, and (iv) is the observation that concavity gives you the inequality-sensitivity for free. I also suspect that the ex ante / ex post distinction in (v) is nothing but the distinction between an ensemble average and a single realisation — though I suspect that is too glib, since the expectation in question is not an average over anything that exists.

Where does this reconstruction break? In particular: is the analogue of exchangeability of identical particles exact or only approximate; is separability across persons really the moral counterpart of extensivity, and is it an independent assumption rather than something the egalitarian motivations give you; and does my framing quietly presuppose that the units being summed have no identity, which the debate may not grant?

Then the residue report: which of (i) to (v) translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all — I expect something about a benefit's being for someone, and something about the separateness of persons, to be inexpressible in a functional over a distribution. For each item of residue, say whether you take it to be a defect of my vocabulary or an indication that the original claim was less clear than it appeared. And if my reconstruction smuggles in a physical assumption the philosophical position does not make, say so plainly: that is precisely the error I cannot see from inside my own training.

Finally, since I mean to use your answer to decide what to read next: indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one about which you are least confident.
