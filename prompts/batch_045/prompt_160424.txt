I'm finishing a paper on the surprise exam paradox and need a variant case built to order rather than an illustration of one that already exists.

Setting: an ordinary school, a real teacher, a class of students I'll treat as sharing a single credence function and as fully trusting the teacher. No perfect predictors, no demons, no science fiction; the announcement is made aloud, once, to the whole class.

The case has to make all three of the following true at once:

1. The announcement is true, and true in a way that doesn't depend on any loose use of the word surprise. Spell surprise out operationally as follows: the quiz is a surprise just in case, on the evening before the meeting at which it is held, no student's credence that the quiz will be at the next meeting exceeds one half.
2. The students are entitled to full belief in the announcement: they know it is true, and their credence that a quiz of the announced kind will occur at all is 1. This is meant to block the easy move of making the teacher unreliable or vague.
3. The backward-induction argument that starts from the announcement and concludes that there can be no surprise quiz, and hence no quiz, fails. Tell me exactly which step fails and why. The failure has to be located in the structure of the case, not in the students' rationality, their logical omniscience, or the announcement's unknowability.

One constraint on how you do it: it is not enough that the students be unsure how many meetings remain while still knowing an upper bound. If they can ever identify some upcoming meeting as the last possible one, the last step of the induction is back in business and your case will quietly collapse, so show that they cannot.

Deliverables: the announcement, worded as a teacher could actually word it; the schedule and credence arithmetic showing that the announcement comes out true on the day the quiz is held and that the elimination cannot get started; one paragraph naming the single feature doing the work, together with a demonstration that deleting it — say by stipulating that everyone knows the quiz falls on one of next week's five meetings and that all five will take place — restores the standard paradox with the base step of the induction intact; and one paragraph on how someone who explains the original paradox by saying the announcement is self-referential and therefore cannot be known to be true even if it is true would object to your case, plus whether you think your case shows the paradox is at bottom a paradox about known finite deadlines. If more than one feature could do the work, say which is the minimal one and why the alternatives are less clean.
