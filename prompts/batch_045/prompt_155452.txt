I've been going back and forth on the Taurek-ish thought that the numbers don't count, and I'd rather start from my own gut on cases than from anything I've read. So here's where I actually land:

- one stranger drowning vs five strangers drowning, same distance, nothing else to distinguish them: I feel no real reason to go for the five. I do feel fine tossing a coin that gives each of the six an equal shot, which happens to rescue the five most of the time — but that reads to me as fairness to individuals, not counting.
- one stranger vs a million strangers: now I do feel a pull, and I can't name the number where it kicks in. That inconsistency bugs me and I half suspect the million verdict is just panic.
- one stranger dying vs a million people each losing a finger: nothing. A million people each with a month of mild back pain: still nothing.
- one stranger dying vs a million people each facing an independent 1-in-1000 chance of death: I feel something, but noticeably less than when the million are certain to die. So my pull seems to track how sure each individual's loss is, not the expected body count — which I can't fully justify.
- one vs five where saving the five requires breaking a promise I made to the one: I keep the promise. one vs five where saving the five just means not saving the one: no pull toward the five. Those two sit oddly together.
- the pair I really can't reconcile: one person drowning right in front of me vs five drowning a bit further out — I feel nothing. But one person drowning in front of me vs five I could save by mailing a check — I feel I should mail the check. Same decision, same numbers, opposite pull.
- one terrified conscious person vs five unconscious people who'll never know they were in danger: I lean toward the conscious one, and I don't trust that lean.

My tentative principle: what matters is what each individual stands to lose, discounted by how likely it is they lose it, and the numbers enter only through giving each separate claim equal respect (hence the coin), never as a sum.

Can you test that against the list and repair it? I want: the principle stated precisely enough that I can apply it, the clause tied to the case that motivates it, its verdicts on a couple of cases I haven't listed (say, five people who can only be saved all-or-nothing as a group, and five people whose deaths would be one shared loss because they're a family), and an honest note about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If the honest answer is that it quietly collapses into adding up expected harm, say so and tell me which verdict I should drop.
