I'm finishing a chapter on Moore's paradox, and an argument I built for a reading group has run away from me. It was meant to be a reductio of the transparency account of first-person self-ascription, but it ends by taking away something I need.

(1) Take the commissive form: "It's raining, but I don't believe it" is absurd to assert, and the absurdity survives every stipulation that would make it empirically explicable — the speaker has impeccable evidence for rain, no wishful thinking, no confusion about the words, no oddity in the context. So the explanation has to be conceptual: something about belief, about self-ascription, or about assertion.

(2) It isn't about assertion in particular. The same incoherence attaches to first-personally judging the conjunction and to taking it as a premise in deliberation. So it cannot be dissolved as a norm governing speech.

(3) It isn't about the words either. Believing that p and believing that one does not believe that p cannot be coherently combined in a single mind — even though each is possible on its own, and the combination is entirely unremarkable in the third person ("she believes it's raining and she doesn't believe she does").

(4) The asymmetry between the first- and third-person forms points to the method of self-ascription: I settle whether I believe that p by settling whether p. That single claim — call it transparency — explains (1) through (3) in one stroke.

(5) But transparency as a claim about method cannot do that work. Consider a two-channel subject: a first-order system that fixes that it is raining on the basis of the evidence, and a metacognitive system that estimates the first-order system's commitments from noisy higher-level evidence, the two dissociable from one another, as they appear to be in anosognosia. Such a subject could judge "it's raining" and, on independent and equally good grounds, "I don't believe it," with neither judgment defective. So if the Moorean combination is genuinely impossible rather than merely unassertable, transparency must be stronger than a thesis about method: the verdict of the transparent method has to be constitutive of believing, leaving no gap between believing that p and being in a position to judge that one does.

(6) Yet if there is no such gap, then in the two-channel case there is no fact of the matter whether she believes it is raining — nothing deeper than the conflict settles it. And if there is no fact of the matter there, there is nothing for a psychological science of belief to be right or wrong about, and no distance between believing and avowing.

(7) So the argument doesn't merely refute transparency. It also strips belief of the substantive, investigable character my chapter needs it to have.

I take (1) and (4) to be the parts most worth keeping, and I am least willing to surrender what (6) denies — that in the two-channel case something or other is the belief. So I don't want the cheap exit: I don't want to be told the case isn't coherent, or that the subject's monitor report isn't really a belief about her own mind. Which step do you reject, why did that step look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost the transparency theorist — in particular, what is she obliged to say about a subject whose monitor is simply misinformed? Keep it to the diagnosis; I don't need a map of the literature.
