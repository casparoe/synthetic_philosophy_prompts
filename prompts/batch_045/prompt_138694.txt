For years the same move has been doing quiet work in the arguments I read about how AI systems ought to reason, and I would rather have it audited than repeated once more. The move is to treat deontological constraints as the moral equivalent of a traffic code: bright-line, compressible rules that mostly line up with what a fully informed consequentialist calculation would already deliver, and that we fall back on only because real agents are myopic, computationally bounded, biased in predictable directions, and prone to motivated reasoning. On that picture deontology is a workaround for hardware and character limitations, and the conclusion people extract is correspondingly confident: a system with enough compute, better calibrated uncertainty, no ego to protect, and no temptation to fudge the numbers should be built to reason like an act-utilitarian, with rules retained at most as an output filter or a public-relations layer. Insisting that the rules stay *inside* the deliberation starts to look like treating the medicine as if it were the health.

Before I ask whether that conclusion survives, I want the correspondence stated explicitly rather than gestured at. In the traffic-code picture, what plays the role of the code, the driver, the other drivers, the accident, the patrol car, the road conditions, and the point the code is for? A table is fine. I am especially interested in which elements are doing real argumentative work and which are decoration.

Then the places I suspect the mapping is load-bearing:

First, a level-of-description problem. It seems to me that the question has at least four separable parts: what decision procedure the system actually runs, what criterion of rightness its outputs are supposed to track, what it says to users and overseers about why it acted, and what gets reinforced over the course of training. The heuristic story looks like an argument about the first of these, and I do not see how it reaches the second. Tell me which of the four the analogy can even touch and where it would be begging the question.

Second, and I think this is where the analogy may invert on itself: the premise is about the agent doing the acting, but in deployment the bounded, less trustworthy optimizer is often the human in the loop rather than the system. Bright-line rules may be doing their work by making the system's behavior legible and auditable to us — by letting us decline to endorse a case-by-case expected-value calculation we cannot check, computed over a utility function we are not confident we specified correctly. If so, the rules are for us, not for it, and the smart-agent premise cuts the other way. Relatedly, is a deontological rule best understood as a hedge against bias, as a hedge against model error, or as a hedge against an optimizer that may itself be subtly misaligned? My guess is that those three call for different hedges, and that only the first is well modeled by a driver who cannot see around the bend.

Third, the code works because its users keep its point in view. A system that optimizes within bright lines without grasping the purpose will hunt the letter of the rule — reward hacking inside the constraint set rather than against it — and I do not know what in the analogy rules this out. If the fix is to make the rules purpose-sensitive, does the view quietly become rule-consequentialism?

Fourth, coordination. Part of a traffic code's value is independent of whether any given rule is correct: everyone follows the same one and everyone knows it. With many principals holding divergent moral views, and with systems increasingly interacting with other systems, does that function reappear at a level the single-driver picture has no room for?

Give me a verdict at the end: does the drop-the-rules-once-the-agent-is-smart-enough conclusion survive, or was it carried by the assumption that boundedness is the only defect the rules were ever treating? And if the traffic code is the wrong picture, say what would be better. I have seen deontology compared to a constitutional constraint, to a Ulysses-style precommitment, to the rights-as-trumps idea, and to an interface contract between a system and its users. Which of those does comparable work without importing the same failure?
