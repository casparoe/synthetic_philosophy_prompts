I am one of two process designers for a series of citizens' assemblies on regional water policy: twenty-four randomly selected residents, six weekends, and a standing scientific advisory panel that has already produced a short "factual baseline" covering current reservoir levels, projected demand under three growth scenarios, the legally binding contamination thresholds, and the capital costs of the main supply options. We are now drafting the ground-rules document that participants sign before the first weekend, and the live disagreement is about the epistemic rules rather than the etiquette ones.

My proposal is that the baseline be adopted as settled for the purposes of the assembly. Participants could argue about what should be done about the facts, but not about whether they are facts, and facilitators should rule out of order any claim that contradicts an item in the baseline. I have been defending this on Arendtian grounds. In *Lying in Politics: Reflections on The Pentagon Papers* and the earlier essay "Truth and Politics," her argument as I read it is that factual truth loses whenever it is exposed to open political contestation: the liar is a man of action who can produce a coherent alternative account on demand, while the truth-teller can only repeat a contingent fact that has no necessity behind it and no power, and that peremptorily demands agreement rather than inviting discussion. She also holds that the damage done by an organized falsehood is not repaired by later correction — the lie leaves a hole, and what remains is a cynicism in which people stop discriminating between truth and falsehood at all. If that is right, then leaving the baseline open to contestation inside the assembly is just re-running her experiment on a smaller scale, and the consistent response is to remove the factual questions from the agenda before deliberation begins and treat them as procedure instead.

A colleague on the project says this turns the advisory panel into a ministry of truth and that Arendt would have hated it. Her objection seems weak to me: Arendt herself insists that factual truth needs to be sheltered by institutions standing outside the political struggle — courts, archives, universities, an independent press — and I do not see why the distinction between sheltering facts outside the arena and foreclosing them inside it should carry any weight, since both are ways of keeping facts from being outvoted by power. My colleague also says that Arendt's worry is not really about people ending up with false beliefs, which I take to be a verbal quibble.

So I would like two things, and for the second please take my reading of Arendt as given.

First: help me answer my colleague. What is the strongest Arendtian version of her objection, and what should my reply be?

Second, and more useful to me day to day: how should the rule actually be drafted? Should the prohibition attach to individual statements that contradict an item in the baseline, or only to the systemic case — the construction of a whole alternative account that replaces the factual texture rather than denying this or that item, which is what she took to distinguish modern image-making from ordinary political deceit? I lean toward the individual-statement version because it is far easier for facilitators to apply, but my colleague says that version is at once too broad, since it catches the participant who is merely confused or working from a different source, and too narrow, since it does nothing about the participant who never contradicts the baseline directly but reorganizes everything around a rival story. I need a rule I can hand to facilitators with no philosophical training.
