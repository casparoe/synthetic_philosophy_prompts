In the reading group I run with the engineers on my team, there is one picture we reach for every time the conversation turns to whether a system trained only on text can mean anything by the words it emits. Symbols, we say, are like balloons. Unless a symbol is tethered to something non-symbolic — tied down to perception and action the way a balloon is staked to the ground, or the way a building rests on bedrock — it floats free, and a free-floating symbol is not about anything. The picture does real argumentative work for us: it is what we use to license three claims we otherwise assert without much support. First, that a text-only system cannot refer at all, so that its fluent answers are a species of systematic bluffing. Second, that whatever would fix this has to be installed at the input end — perception, action, sensorimotor contact — and that no amount of downstream inference, self-supervision or scale can substitute for it. Third, that the question whether a system means anything by its symbols has a clean yes-or-no answer, since either the chain of definitions terminates in something non-symbolic or it does not.

I have started to suspect the picture is carrying more than anyone has checked, and I would like an audit of it rather than a restatement of it.

Lay out explicitly what maps onto what: the balloon, the rope, the stake, the ground, the wind, the altitude, what it would be for a rope to snap or to be spliced onto another rope. Then give me the disanalogies, and sort them into the ones that actually threaten the three claims above and the ones we can cheerfully ignore.

Some of what I want concerns what the source domain quietly imports into the target. The picture gives us a ground that is single, external, and beneath; a tether that is a two-place relation between one symbol and one thing; a grounding that is a property of individual symbols rather than of a whole system working in concert; a direction of explanation that is strictly bottom-up; and an ungrounded symbol that is, by the geometry of the image, weightless, drifting and worthless. Which of these are features of meaning as such, and which are merely features of balloons?

I also want the picture pressed against cases we have never pressed it against. What is the stake for the word not, or if, or all, or therefore — is there a perceptible thing at the far end of that rope, and if not, is the picture wrong or is our use of those words ungrounded? What is it for boron, which almost none of us has ever encountered and which we would not recognise if we did: are most speakers ungrounded in it, or can a tether be inherited from specialists, and if it can, does the metaphor have any room for inherited grounding? What is it for Vulcan, where the rope presumably runs to nothing at all? And what is the ground itself made of, if the ground is said to be sensorimotor categories — a category already sorts and classifies, so is the bedrock itself symbolic, and does the picture then generate its own regress?

I should tell you what my colleagues now say in reply, because I want their proposals audited too rather than applauded. One of them has begun saying meaning is not a tether but a currency: nothing anchors a banknote to gold, what makes it worth something is its position inside a network of acceptance, redemption and enforcement, and what you can do with it. Someone else floats the image of a ship rebuilt plank by plank at sea. For each: what corresponds to what, what it explains that the balloon did, what it loses that the balloon had, and whether claims (1) to (3) are still supported, weakened, or simply relocated.

Finally, tell me which of the three claims survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what, if anything, we are entitled to say instead. I am not asking for citations, further reading or the history of who first made these arguments — I can look that up myself, and it would not settle anything for me here. I want the argument.
