I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and in a few weeks I'm running a 90-minute hands-on session at a conference for about 14 other science writers — mixed ages, a couple of them proudly innumerate, two or three who will try to turn everything into an argument about politics, and at least one who thinks probability talk is a conspiracy. The subject is disagreement, and I want them to *feel* the force of that Aumann agreement theorem business — the claim that two honest Bayesianswho start from the same priors and genuinely take in each other's posteriors cannot just sit there permanently disagreeing — and then feel how thin the ground under it actually is. Constraints: index cards, poker chips, a whiteboard, no laptops, no phones, and I dont want to hand out real money. And no lecture up front — I want the room to walk into the result themselves, and I'd rather they not hear the name until the last ten minutes.

Here's the rough shape I have in mind, but please make it real and fix whatever is broken in it. Some private-signal setup: everybody gets a noisy clue about a binary question (a jar that's mostly red or mostly blue, or whatever works better), writes down a probability, then trades with a partner for a few rounds. For payoffs I want a scoring rule where reporting your true probability is what maximizes your chips — I've heard such rules exist, so pick one and make it playable with chips and without arithmetic worse than simple fractions. Then two twists I specifically care about. First, run it twice: once where partners may only exchange their current *number* (their conclusion), and once where they may only describe the *clue they actually saw* (their evidence) — I want the difference between those two protocols to show up in whether pairs converge. Second, somewhere in the session break the common-prior assumption without telling anyone: give seperate subgroups different background information about how the world was set up, so that some pairs really shouldn't be able to converge, then reveal it. Also cap the number of message rounds, because I suspect the finite-dialogue version behaves differently from the idealized one and I want people to feel the clock.

Then the debrief, which is the part I'm least sure I can do well. Map particular moments in the activity onto particular parts of the idea — the common prior, the difference between "I know your number" and "I know that you know that I know", the difference between learning someone's conclusion and learning what they saw, and the fact that in real arguments we almost always trade conclusions. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing somebody anchors and never budges, somebody meta-games or flat-out lies, the loud guy dominates his partner, someone refuses to play at all, and my sabotaged subgroup catches on early — and how to handle each without killing the session. And be straight with me about which parts of the theorem a card-and-chip game simply cannot stage, the bits I'll have to say out loud at the end because no activity will produce them. If a version of this could work as a written exercise for newsletter readers who aren't in the room, say so in a line or two.
