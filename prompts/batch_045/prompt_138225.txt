Next month I've got a 30-minute slot in my company's lunch-and-learn thing — about twenty data scientists and engineers, no philosophy background, they'll want to see numbers on the board, and at least two of them have strong views about veganism that they will air whether or not I invite it. The topic I've been landed with is the scale argument about insect sentience: the number of insects is so vast that even a fairly low credence that they're sentient makes their welfare swamp every other cause. I dont want you to write the talk. I want a plan for it.

The bit I keep messing up — the thing my friends still don't get after an hour of arguing — is the move that makes this not Pascal's mugging. My own half-baked way of putting it: with the mugger, everything hangs on one wildly improbable claim, so the expected value is carried entirely by the tail; with insects, the uncertainty is spread across billions of seperate individuals, so even at 1% you're near-certain that a huge absolute number of them matter, and it doesn't matter which ones. I think that's the hinge of the whole topic, but I don't know how to make it visible without putting algebra on a slide, and I'm not even sure I've got it right.

So: what order do the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what — do I open with the population figure, or with a single insect and the question of what would have to be true of it for it to count, or with the uncertainty? My worry is that opening with ten-to-the-something-or-other makes people laugh and check out. Which one example should carry the whole thing, and what does it need to have and lack — I'd guess it needs a huge number of small units and a decision I can actually make, and it needs to not already smuggle in "insects matter" and to not be about anything charismatic, but tell me what else.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive: someone will say insects have no cortex so there's nothing it's like to be one; someone will say if this were true nature would be a nightmare so it can't be true; someone will do the multiplication, get an absurd number, and conclude the argument is a reductio; someone will say the colony is the real organism so counting individuals is wrong; and one of them will ask whether I'm committed to thinking we should reduce insect populations. Which of those are really the same objection, where in the sequence does each one get defused, and do I raise the last one myself or wait for it?

Also: what should I flatly refuse to explain in 30 minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing, and what one question at minute 28 would tell me they'd got the structure rather than just the conclusion?
