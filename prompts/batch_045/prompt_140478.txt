I'm a first-year PhD student in a group that straddles ML and philosophy, and my supervisor says 'no free lunch' roughly once a week — I've slowly realised it's doing at least four different jobs. On Tuesday someone in lab meeting showed our new architecture beats the baseline on 38 of 40 datasets, and he said well, no free lunch: the gain is just the inductive bias being paid for, its not evidence the architecture is better in general. Then a postdoc said that if theres no free lunch we might as well pick architectures by coin flip, and he didn't push back on that either.

I want to be precise about what could be meant. The readings I can half-see: (i) the formal NFL result — state it cleanly, something like: averaged over the uniform distribution on all functions from a finite input space to a finite output space, every algorithm has the same expected error off the training set, so anything you win on one subclass of problems is exactly balanced by losses on the complement (we've started calling that the 'luck budget' — you can shuffle luck between problems but the average over the class is pinned); (ii) the optimization version, that averaged over all cost functions every search algorithm does equally well; (iii) something Hume-ish, that induction needs a uniformity principle which is either circular or unfalsifiable, so past success gives no reason to expect the future to match; (iv) something about priors and representations, that a bias-free learner isn't so much as possible.

For each reading: is it actually true as stated, how strong is it, is the conservation exact or only in some limit, does it survive leaving the uniform distribution or restricting the class of functions, is it basically a symmetry/rearrangement point that's nearly trivial once you see it, and is any of it just false? Then say which reading would support the 'not evidence the architecture is better' move, which would support the coin flip, and whether those two uses are even compatible with each other.
