I'm drafting a briefing note for a policy audience on whether a wealthy, low-fertility democracy should adopt a mildly pronatalist package — cash bonuses, subsidized childcare, and tax treatment that together shift completed fertility from roughly 1.5 to roughly 2.0 children per woman. Assume the package is non-coercive, that it is financed out of general taxation, and that the counterfactual is genuinely fewer people rather than more immigration. I do not want an essay defending a verdict; I want a weighting scheme I can put in an appendix.

Below are nine considerations that have been proposed for the note. Before you rate them, note two things: the policy is a marginal one (it is not a step toward maximizing population), and its demographic effects arrive mostly twenty to sixty years out, so anything that depends on cohort size in the near term has small leverage. For each consideration:

(a) mark its direction as pro (raises the case for the policy), con (lowers it), or structural (it changes which framework is the right one rather than pushing the answer in one direction);
(b) rate it from 0 to 10 for how much it should move the assessment of this particular policy, where the rating means expected magnitude times evidential robustness rather than mere validity, and where 0 means it should be cut from the note;
(c) assign it to one of four tiers: Tier 1 = should drive the conclusion, Tier 2 = substantial, Tier 3 = real but modest, Tier 4 = speculative or negligible;
(d) give one sentence explaining why it lands where it does.

Then rank the nine from most to least weight, rescale your ratings into weights that sum to 100, and state in two or three sentences the overall verdict those weights support. If any two considerations are not independent — if one is a special case of another, or if counting both would double-count a single underlying effect — identify the pair and adjust the weights accordingly.

The considerations:

1. Identity scrambling. A policy that changes fertility timing and family size also changes who is conceived, so within about two generations the comparison is not one society plus some additional people but one population versus a largely non-overlapping alternative population. Considerations phrased as benefiting or harming particular future people therefore have no purchase; the evaluation must be impersonal — comparing the two populations as wholes — or it must treat the choice as morally inert.

2. The entry-threshold question. Whether adding a life is an improvement depends on the level of wellbeing above which an addition counts as good: total and average views give different answers, and intermediate views require choosing a threshold that is hard to justify non-arbitrarily. For this policy specifically, the marginal child in a rich country is very likely to have a life well worth living, but also likely to receive somewhat less parental investment and fewer resources than the average child would have under the smaller cohort.

3. The slope toward the repugnant conclusion. If the case for more people is that total wellbeing rises, the same reasoning appears to favor arbitrarily large populations of lives barely worth living. The question for the note is whether the argument for a 2.0 fertility rate carries that commitment, or whether it can be severed — for instance by appealing to thresholds, to the identity of future people, or to the fact that a marginal change need not endorse the endpoint of the path it lies on.

4. Idea production: standing on shoulders versus stepping on toes. Larger cohorts mean more potential innovators, more experimentation, and larger markets over which non-rival ideas can spread. Offsetting this are duplicated research effort, rent-seeking, congestion, and crowding of fixed factors — plus a timing problem, since the additional innovators arrive a generation later, by which point automation may substitute for headcount in the production of research itself.

5. Extinction buffering and the long-run multiplier. A larger and more geographically dispersed population may reduce the chance that a single catastrophe terminates the human lineage, and if the expected future is very large, then even small changes in survival probability can dominate every other term. Against this: greater population and density plausibly raise pandemic and ecological risk, and the effect of half a child per woman in a country of tens of millions is small relative to baseline catastrophe risk.

6. Value pluralism as lock-in insurance. A larger, more diverse population produces more moral and institutional experimentation and more independent sources of dissent, making it less likely that one coalition's values become locked in for centuries. Against this: larger populations may require more centralized coordination, which cuts the other way, and pluralism is not clearly good if the values that happen to spread are bad ones.

7. Fiscal arithmetic and demographic momentum. In pay-as-you-go pension and health systems, an additional worker is a transfer gain to current retirees, which makes pronatalism look cheaper than it is. But the gain is redistribution rather than value creation, and it survives only as long as fertility does not fall again — the same structure that makes the policy look like a free lunch makes repeated application fragile. Costs also arrive immediately while revenues arrive decades later.

8. Uncompensated burdens and the headcount objection. The costs of additional children fall unevenly and largely on women, and even purely financial incentives operate against a background of unequal bargaining within households, so voluntariness is partly illusory. Alongside this is a standing worry: whether the state has any legitimate interest in more people as such, as opposed to better-off people.

9. Environmental and crowding externalities. Additional people in a high-consumption country add emissions, land use, and congestion, and the marginal child there has a footprint far above the global average. The accounting matters: a total-emissions framing makes the externality look large, while a per-capita framing makes it look small, and the relevant comparison may be to a world in which the same consumption is spread across fewer people.
