Two colleagues of mine — one a philosopher of mind, the other a philosopher of art — fell into an argument at a workshop dinner, and I was the only other person at the table. They were disagreeing about whether a certain contemporary technology poses a new version of the so-called paradox of fiction, or whether it dissolves that puzzle by showing the puzzle was always a confusion. I could not referee it, and I would like you to.

The background both of them accept: the standard puzzle is how we can be genuinely emotionally moved by fictional characters and events that we know do not exist. They also accept the usual three-fold taxonomy of replies: (i) the thought, or quasi-emotion, account, on which we undergo real affective responses to merely imagined contents, responses that lack the existential belief and the ordinary action-guiding role of full-blown emotions; (ii) the illusion account, on which we are temporarily taken in, or form something belief-like, and so undergo something much closer to ordinary emotion; and (iii) the pretend account, on which the response is itself a form of pretending rather than a real emotion. The dispute is not about the taxonomy; it is about what the following case does to it.

The case: a company offers reconstruction sessions. A system is trained on the digital remains of a dead person — their emails, messages, recorded voice — and a bereaved client converses with it in real time. The client is explicitly told, and knows throughout, that the system is a simulation and that its replies are generated rather than expressions of the dead person. Clients nonetheless report powerful grief, comfort, guilt, and relief, and some say that while conversing they felt the person was present.

The exchange, as nearly as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Iris opened: The reconstruction case dissolves the paradox rather than extending it. Unlike the fictional heroines of classic novels, the reconstruction is anchored in a real person who really lived and really died; and unlike reading a novel, the client acts — she chooses what to say, and those choices have real effects on her grieving. So her emotion has two perfectly real objects: the dead person as they were, and the client's own real conduct. There is no need to posit quasi-emotions or illusions here. The response is ordinary grief, mediately caused by a simulation. The literature's puzzle was an artifact of focusing on the passive consumption of invented characters.

(2) Malik replied: Two problems. First, anchoring in a real person is not sufficient, because we also weep at photographs and at locks of hair, yet no one thinks those raise the paradox of fiction. What matters is reciprocity: the simulation talks back. That is exactly what the paradox of fiction is about — responding to the apparent agency of something one knows is not an agent. Second, the real effects on grieving are irrelevant to the metaphysics of the emotional episode. A horror film can cause real insomnia and real adrenaline, but the fear one feels during the film is still directed at a fictional threat. So you have not dissolved anything; you have isolated the very feature that the old theories have to explain.

(3) Iris answered: The photograph comparison actually supports me. A photograph is a trace that does not act; a letter to the dead, even one you burn unread, is already enough to produce real grief, and that grief is unproblematically about a real person. The simulation is a more elaborate letter that answers back. And if the answer is what troubles you, notice that the client's emotion is also partly about her own choices — about what she decided to say — and those are real actions. So the reciprocity point splits into two components: to the extent the emotion concerns the simulation's replies, it is make-believe, which the thought account can handle; to the extent it concerns her own acts and the real dead person, it is ordinary. You are conflating two components that the literature wrongly fused into one.

(4) Malik said: The letter analogy fails precisely because the letter does not reply. Improvisational theatre does: an actor improvises with you in a fictional scenario, your choices partly determine how the fiction unfolds, and you still describe your tears as a response to the fiction; nobody says the emotion becomes ordinary merely because you authored part of it. Authorship does not convert a response to a fiction into a response to reality. Moreover, the division of labor you propose is not as tidy as you claim. The thought account and the illusion account disagree about whether the subject has anything like an existential belief about the object, and clients in reconstruction sessions characteristically report both that they knew it was not really the person and that they nonetheless felt the person's presence while talking. That is not a clean split between two components; it is one state with conflicting belief-like aspects. That is exactly the sort of state the standard accounts were built to adjudicate, so the case is a stress test they may fail, not a dissolution and not a mere conjunction of two bits of machinery.

(5) Iris replied: I accept the stress-test framing, but I deny it tells against me. Reports of both knowledge and a sense of presence are common in ordinary grief: a mourner can know that the body in the coffin is not the person and still feel the person's presence. So the conflicting belief-like aspects are not distinctive of fiction; they are features of grief itself. What the reconstruction does is let us see, in a controlled setting, that the machinery the fiction literature invented — quasi-emotions, pretendings, illusions — is just the general machinery of imagination plus real-world anchoring. Theorists can keep their accounts as accounts of the imagination component, concede that the anchoring component is perfectly ordinary, and treat the disputed episodes as composites. The genuinely interesting question is why the composite feels unified rather than fragmented, and I admit that neither of us has explained that.

Please adjudicate the exchange. For each of the five moves, give a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating: say what the move gets right, what it gets wrong, and whether it is a fair reply to what preceded it. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing — a distinction, an alternative account of the case, or a consideration that bears on the metaphysics but that neither of them raised. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want a point-by-point evaluation of the arguments as actually made.
