I work on a small team that writes the refusal policy for a general-purpose assistant, and I have been defending what I call a deference default: absent a specific reason, the assistant does what the user asks, and non-compliance has to earn its way. A colleague sent me a short argument that he says kills every paternalistic refusal policy there could be. I cannot find the move in it, but the conclusion is one my team cannot accept, so I suspect it proves too much.

The argument, as he set it out:

(1) Delegation parity. An assistant has exactly the authority the user has given it. Where the user has given no standing instruction, the assistant's mandate is to execute the instruction it has just been given. Doing something else is acting outside the mandate, and acting outside the mandate for the user's own benefit is paternalism in the ordinary sense.

(2) Epistemic asymmetry. Everything the assistant knows about this particular user is the current transcript plus population-level generalisations. The user has first-personal access to their own aims, their tolerance for risk, and the circumstances they are actually in. So, over the class of contested requests, the user is in a better position than the assistant to judge which response serves the user's own aims.

(3) Symmetric ignorance. Therefore, for any request whatever, the assistant does not know that refusing serves the user's aims better than complying does.

(4) Knowledge norm. It is permissible to act against someone's instruction for their own good only if you know, or justifiably believe on grounds the person could in principle check, that doing so serves their aims better than complying does.

(5) Therefore no refusal-for-the-user's-own-good is ever permissible, whatever the stakes.

(6) The cases that show the conclusion is unacceptable: someone in crisis asking for a method; someone asking the assistant to draft the document that will wreck the project they have spent the whole transcript describing; someone asking for a plan that rests on a factual error the assistant can see and the user cannot.

(7) A wrinkle he does not address: (4) applied to compliance rather than refusal would forbid complying whenever the assistant cannot know that compliance is better, which would leave the assistant silent in most ordinary cases. So (4) seems to be doing something asymmetric.

(8) Therefore at least one of (1) through (4) is false, and my deference default is unmoved, because it was never a defence of paternalistic refusal in the first place.

My own view is that (2) is the backbone of the deference default and I would give up almost anything before it; I would also sooner give up (4) than (1). But I cannot tell whether the real error is in (4), whether it is earlier, in the step from (2) to (3), or whether it is in (1), which seems to me able to run both ways: plenty of users do delegate a standing instruction not to be helped with the thing they will regret, and if they have, refusal is mandate-execution rather than paternalism at all.

Two things I would like taken seriously rather than set aside. First, non-compliance is not one thing: there is hard refusal, refusal with an explanation and a signpost elsewhere, a cooling-off delay, and compliance wrapped in framing that surfaces the risk. My sense is that an argument which talks only about refusing may be sliding across these, and I want to know whether the diagnosis changes by tier; I have been calling this the tiering question. Second, I have a candidate escape hatch I cannot evaluate. Call it the Two-Key Test: an override is permissible only if (i) the assistant has a specific, articulable reason bearing on this request that the user lacks, and (ii) that reason is one the user would, were it pointed out, accept as a reason for being overridden. I cannot tell whether the Two-Key Test escapes my colleague's argument or is just (4) wearing a hat.

"Why the step looked fine" (about 150 words). "What survives" (about 200 words), covering both the tiering question and whether the Two-Key Test is genuinely independent of the argument or merely a restatement of one of its premises.
