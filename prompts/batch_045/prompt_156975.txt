I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm setting the final, and I need one question on dispositions, finks and masks. I dont want anything that can be answered by reciting the simple conditional analysis, the standard examples (the wire rigged so that touching it cuts the current, the poison swallowed together with its antidote) and the usual repair where you relativise the conditional to the object retaining its causal basis. Here's the crux I want students forced to face: whether finks and masks are really two different problems needing two different repairs, or one problem in different clothes.

Concretely, have them work through two cases I've been playing with. Case one: a vase is fragile, but it has a self-healing coating — when struck, the glass does crack, yet microcapsules in the coating re-bond the crack within a millisecond, so there is never any lasting damage. Is that a mask, or has fragility simply manifested and then been cleaned up? Make them say what the manifestation of fragility is supposed to be: the occurrence of cracking, or the end state of being broken. Case two, a slow fink: the vase is fragile, but being struck initiates a process that anneals the glass, so the fracture begins and then stalls halfway. The basis is destroyed by the very stimulus, but only after the manifestation has got going — so the same mechanism can be described as a fink or as a mask depending on how fine-grained you make the basis and the manifestation. Does that show the distinction is less sharp than the literature assumes?

Then two follow-ups: does any of this bite on someone who is a realist about powers and never accepted the conditional analysis in the first place, or are finks and masks only trouble for people trying to analyse dispositions? And is the fink/mask distinction a distinction in nature, or an artifact of how we describe the stimulus conditions and the timescale?

Give me the question text as I'd hand it to students (40 minutes, about 800 words), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Please seperate clearly the moves that get full marks from the moves that only get partial credit — I want the A/B boundary to be about whether they see that these two cases put pressure on different things, not about whether they can reproduce more of the literature.
