I am tutoring a second-year metaphysics tutorial next week: seventy minutes, one whiteboard, and fourteen students who have had a single lecture on identity over time — enough to feel the pull of the puzzle about a thing's apparently incompatible properties at different times, but no exposure to the idea of a temporal part. The topic is endurantism versus perdurantism. I am not asking you to explain the debate to me; I am asking for a plan for how to get them to see what is at issue, and I would like the plan to be specific about the dependencies rather than a list of talking points.

Please give me the following.

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency behind each ordering decision: what has to be in place before the next move can land, and what specifically goes wrong if it is not.

2. One concrete object or scenario to carry the whole session, together with an account of what a carrying example for this debate must have and must lack. Say explicitly whether you would reject the Ship of Theseus, a cat that loses a limb, an event such as a game of chess, and a human being, and give the reason in each case — including if you think one of them should be kept.

3. A map of the misconceptions they will arrive with or will form in the room, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Two I am confident about: someone will take perdurantism to say that the object is spread out in time the way a road is spread out in space, and someone will conclude that the whole dispute is merely verbal. I also intend to ban the expressions "four-dimensional" and "block universe" from the room for the first forty minutes; tell me whether that helps, what I should use in their place, and what I should do when a student who has read ahead reaches for them anyway.

4. A check for the last five minutes that tells me whether they have grasped the disagreement rather than merely acquired the vocabulary. I have three candidate questions; tell me which of them actually discriminates between the views, and why the others fail to.

 - If only the last ten seconds of a person's life had ever existed, would that person have existed at all?
 - A relay team wins a race; is the runner who crossed the line the same runner who started it?
 - Could there be a world in which everything that exists exists for exactly one instant, and in which things nevertheless change?

5. One thing the session should openly decline to explain, and a single sentence I can use to make the refusal feel like a decision rather than an evasion.

Finally: where in the sequence, if anywhere, should I introduce the third position — that the persisting thing is an instantaneous stage, and that persistence consists in standing in the right kind of series-relation to other stages? Give me your reason for placing it where you do, or for leaving it out entirely. My worry is that in their hands it will either collapse into perdurantism or make the whole debate look like a choice of notation.
