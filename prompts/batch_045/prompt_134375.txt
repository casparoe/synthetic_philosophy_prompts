I have just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about verificationist anti-realism — the view that truth cannot outrun all possible evidence, so that anything true is, in principle, knowable. My friend ran what I take to be the standard collapse argument: take a candidate truth of the form *p and it is not known that p*, apply the knowability principle to that whole proposition, lean on the fact that knowledge distributes over conjunction and is factive, and watch the principle drive itself to the conclusion that every truth is known. I think I parried it — I granted the derivation and said the principle must be restricted — but the argument I could not shake was one I invented on the walk home, and I want to try it on someone who will actually fight for the view rather than fold at the first push.

Please play a committed verificationist anti-realist about truth. Concretely:

- You hold the knowability principle: if *p* is true, then it is possible that some agent, at some time, on the basis of some finite body of evidence and reasoning, knows that *p*.
- You understand the motive for this to be about meaning and understanding rather than about epistemology. Your complaint about the realist is that he peoples the world with truths whose obtaining no exercise of our capacities could ever put us in a position to recognise — and that a truth we could not in principle recognise is not one we can so much as grasp.
- You may adopt whichever repair you like: restrict the substitution instances of the schema, deny that knowledge distributes over conjunction, weaken the logic, relativise knowability to agents with enlarged but still finite powers, or something of your own. But you must choose one at the outset, state it in a single sentence, and live with it for the rest of the conversation. No quietly helping yourself to a second restriction once a case bites.
- You will concede that the unrestricted principle collapses, and you will concede that your chosen restriction is not independently intuitive. You will not concede that there could be a truth which no possible agent could in principle come to know. That is the one thing you are here to defend.
- Do not spend your first turn re-deriving the collapse argument; I have granted it. No citations, no named authorities, no appeals to what the literature settles. Argue in the first person and in your own terms. Symbols are welcome where they shorten things, but say in plain words what each step means. Answer my case before you put questions to me. Two or three paragraphs a turn. Stay in character until I say *stop*.

Here is my opening. Imagine a universe with a long middle stretch — a hundred million years — during which there is, as a matter of physical fact, nothing anywhere that could count as an inquirer: no agent, no recording device, and no later evidence either, because the intervening dynamics scrambles every trace beyond recovery. My question is simply whether there are truths about what happened during that stretch: whether a particular comet struck a particular planet on a particular day.

I expect you to answer yes, and to say that a creature with our sensory and cognitive architecture could have been present to watch it, and that this is all "possibly known" requires. I want to grant you that reply and ask what it costs you. If knowledge by an agent who never exists, in a history that never happens, is enough to secure the truth of the claim about the comet, then I cannot see what the realist is supposed to be denying. He agrees that a creature like us, suitably placed, could have watched the comet fall. So state, in one sentence, the constraint on your possibility operator that separates you from him, and then apply that constraint to a case where the candidate truths outrun the possible evidential situations — the exact values of a continuously varying quantity in a region no observation ever samples, for instance — and tell me whether those values are true, false, or neither.

If your answer is that the disagreement was never about which propositions there are but about what truth consists in, say that plainly and tell me what your constraint on truth then amounts to, once it is no longer a constraint on which propositions exist. I have a harder case ready — one in which the absence of observers and traces is nomological rather than merely accidental, so that even the possible-agent reply fails — and I will produce it if you take that route.
