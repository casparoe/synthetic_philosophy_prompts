I'm one of seven trustees of the Wychford Commons Restitution Trust, a small endowed charity created in 1963 by the last of the Pell family, whose money came from woollen milling and had nothing whatsoever to do with the thing the Trust is named after. The deed directs us to apply the income for the relief of persons resident in the Borough of Wychford who are in necessitous circumstances by reason of the loss of common rights effected by the Wychford Inclosure Act 1807. For sixty years this was a sleepy fund — a few hundred pounds a year in fuel vouchers to elderly residents whose grandparents had been cottagers — but an unexpected bequest in 2019 has left us with a corpus large enough that the arguments have stopped being polite, and two applications have now forced the underlying question. The first is from a care worker in her fifties, renting, plainly necessitous, whom a commercial genealogy database plus the parish registers traces back to a cottager named in the 1807 award as holding rights of common — and, as the same research cheerfully revealed, also back to one of the three commissioners who signed that award, through an illegitimate line his legitimate grandchildren never acknowledged. The second is from a man whose parents came to Wychford from Jamaica in 1958, who has no ancestral connection to the commoners at all, who lives on disability benefits after a factory injury, and whose case (set out rather well by his daughter) is that no living person can be compensated for the enclosure: the people wronged are dead, and had the commons survived, the pattern of marriages and migrations would have been different enough that he, and almost certainly she, would never have existed. Our clerk has also commissioned a local historian, who reports that within two generations of the award the parish population had fallen by more than a quarter as families left for the mill towns, which leaves me unable to say what anyone's counterfactual baseline even is. I've been reading about the non-identity problem since a friend mentioned it in passing and I can't unsee it now, and my partner, who works in impact evaluation, keeps muttering about deadweight and baselines whenever I describe the fund to her. A third option is already on the table from one of our number, the so-called Common Purse: stop making individual grants, use the corpus to buy land in the borough and vest it in a community land trust with use-rights for residents, on the theory that what was taken was a form of property — a right held in common — rather than a sum of money, so the fitting repair would be in kind.

What I would like from you is the discussion paper I'll circulate before our March meeting, addressed to the other six trustees: roughly 1,200 words, structured so that it can actually be voted on. It should recommend a concrete decision on each of the two applications and a standing rule we can apply to future ones, and it should give the trustees who lose the vote reasons they can live with — I'd rather not have two of them resign. In reaching the recommendation I'd ask you to get clear, and keep clear, on what the Trust is doing when it pays out: making a claimant whole against a counterfactual, or taking back an unjust enrichment from whoever currently holds the benefit, or repairing a relationship and marking a wrong, or simply directing money at people who need it, because these come apart sharply on our facts and I think half our meetings have been people talking past each other on exactly this. Two constraints: our clerk advises that we cannot rewrite the class of beneficiaries ourselves, so anything that amounts to that has to be flagged as requiring outside approval rather than assumed; and please don't spend words on case law, statutory citations or reading lists — we have a clerk for the law, and what I want from you is the argument.

One last thing, and I mean it rather than saying it for form's sake: tell me how confident you are, and identify the single premise — factual or moral — that your recommendation would collapse without, so I can point the meeting at the thing we're really disagreeing about. And if the framing is simply confused, say so plainly instead of helping us do it more elegantly. In particular, if a fund endowed out of a mill-owner's fortune, whose beneficiaries are chosen by descent from cottagers two centuries gone, cannot honestly be described as making reparation at all — if we are only using that word because the alternative would be admitting we're a small poverty-relief charity with a sentimental history — I would rather hear that from you than from the one trustee who has been waiting six months to say it.
