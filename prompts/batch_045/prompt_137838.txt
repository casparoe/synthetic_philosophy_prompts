You are a political philosopher who specialises in contemporary theories of liberty — negative, positive and republican — and who has advised regulatory and oversight bodies. Work through the problem carefully: before committing yourself, brainstorm a list of candidate decision rules, test each against every precedent below, and only then settle on a verdict. Answer in British English.

An independent oversight board at a large social platform has the remit of ruling on whether a proposed or existing product change restricts users' freedom; where it rules that a change does, the change must be withdrawn or redesigned. Its five published precedents are these:

1. Content removed under published, appealable rules that track the interests users themselves avow, applied to a user who endorses those rules — no restriction of freedom.
2. An outside moderation vendor granted unappealable discretionary power over users' posts, which the vendor in fact never exercised — restriction of freedom.
3. A forced choice between accepting new terms and permanently losing access to everything the user has stored on the platform — restriction of freedom.
4. A paid tier that restores, for a modest monthly fee, a capability withdrawn from free accounts — no restriction of freedom.
5. An engagement-maximising feed built on variable rewards, which users report being unable to stop using despite wanting to — restriction of freedom.

The board is now reviewing a feature called Curator. At onboarding, new users are shown a screen — which auto-advances after a few seconds — offering a choice among three feed curators: the platform's own recommender, pre-selected as the default, and two third-party curators, each of which requires a separate paid subscription and a separate login. Switching later is possible, but the control sits four menus deep and is labelled in internal jargon; 97 per cent of users are on the platform's default. The recommender's criteria are published and individual recommendations can be appealed, but the board's own user research finds that most users do not recall being offered a choice and could not say what their curator does — although they report no dissatisfaction with what they see.

Does Curator restrict users' freedom, on the board's own lights? Identify two or three rules about freedom that fit all five precedents, and say which natural rule the precedents rule out. State what each surviving rule predicts about Curator and which way each precedent pulls. Give your best guess at the board's verdict, with the reasons for preferring the rule that generates it, and name the closest precedent on each side. If you think the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say what the board should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, and what principle it would be adopting either way. Finally, specify the single further case that would best discriminate between the rules still in play, and explain why it discriminates where the earlier ones do not.
