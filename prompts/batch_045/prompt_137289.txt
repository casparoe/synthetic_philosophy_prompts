I'm writing a term paper (6,000 words, one semester) for a graduate seminar in philosophy of language, and I need a two-page proposal to give my instructor: a precisely stated question, why the existing positions leave it open, the method month by month, the possible conclusions and what would decide between them, and an honest assessment of scope.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into that proposal.

The standard debate about slurs asks whether their derogatory element belongs to semantic content or to pragmatics, and both sides appeal to the same small set of data: derogation appears to survive negation, questioning, and conditional embedding, and it appears to resist explicit cancellation. My suspicion is that this shared evidential base is confounded. Every one of these tests features a speaker who has used a slur in a context where a neutral, non-derogating way of picking out the same group was available and was passed over. If offensiveness is partly a matter of that contrast — the offense of choosing the marked expression over an available unmarked one — then the tests may be registering the cost of the choice rather than the presence of a derogatory component in what is said, and the inference from the projection data to a semantic conclusion is weaker than it looks.

What makes this more than a suspicion is that neutral counterparts are not uniformly available. Sometimes there is a clean one. Sometimes the ostensibly neutral replacement is itself contested or rejected by the group in question, or has become the next term to be objected to. Sometimes the erstwhile neutral term has become derogated while the older term has been reclaimed. And sometimes there is no counterpart at all, only a paraphrase. If offense really is contrast-sensitive, these cases should diverge systematically along exactly the dimensions the standard diagnostics measure — which would mean that the data the literature relies on, drawn largely from the first and cleanest kind of case, is unrepresentative.

Please say explicitly whether a successful paper would have established a result about slurs or a result about the methodology of the debate over them, and what a reader who began neutral should think at the end.

Three constraints on the proposal. First, no citations: refer to positions by the claim they make, and I will supply the names myself. Second, scope: this is one semester, with no budget and no ethics clearance, so anything involving experimental subjects or a purpose-built corpus is out — if you think some other empirical method is genuinely required, say so and say what that means for whether the paper can be written at all. Third, the largest risk I can see is that any contrast effect I find generalizes to non-slurring taboo or register-sensitive vocabulary, in which case I will have discovered a fact about appropriateness rather than about slurs. Build that risk into the design instead of noting it at the end, and close with a short paragraph addressed to the most skeptical person in the seminar: which single finding would lead you to abandon the contrast hypothesis, and where do you think the reasoning is most likely to break?
