I run a small reading group — mostly physics and econ grad students plus a couple of philosophers — and we did a session on the doomsday argument last month that ended in an argument I haven't been able to settle. My friend Priya, who works on global priorities stuff, says the whole thing is intellectually fun but has no practical upshot: even if you buy the shift, she says, it never changes what you should actually fund, because the same update that makes you think the future is short also shrinks the stakes you're multiplying by. I think she's half right but that the reason is different from the one she gives, and I want a case that settles it. So: build me one.

What I need is a decision problem with these properties. There are two hypotheses about how many humans will ever live — call them the short branch and the long branch, with the long one bigger by a factor of at least a thousand — and one person or committee choosing between two or three interventions that a real funder or government could plausibly face in the next twenty years. No aliens, no ancestor simulations, no infinite populations, no perfect forecasters; rough numbers are fine but they should be the kind you could say out loud without wincing. The chooser applies the ordinary self-sampling move: treat your own position in the birth order as a random draw from everyone in the reference class, so the likelihood of finding yourself where you find yourself scales like one over the total. If it helps, contrast that with the self-indication move, where the bare fact that you exist is itself evidence for more people existing; I mainly care about the self-sampling calculation, but tell me what the other one does to your case. And the update has to be big — I want the posterior on the short branch to go from roughly even odds to more than 99 percent.

Then the part that actually matters. The recommendation has to come out the same before and after the update, and it has to *earn* that: I don't want a case where both branches already agree on which intervention is best, because then any reweighting at all would preserve the answer and I'd have learned nothing. So set it up so the short branch favours one option — something near-term, cash transfers or bednets or pandemic stockpiles, whatever — and the long branch favours a different one, something that only pays off if there really is a long future: a century-scale institution, a seed vault, a values lock-in, you pick. Give the long-run option a payoff at or near zero in the short branch. Then give me the payoff table with explicit numbers, the expected-value sums under the unshifted prior and again under the shifted posterior, and show which branch is carrying the argument in each of the two calculations. After that, derive the exact condition under which the recommendation survives — my guess is it'll come out as something like: the per-person value of the long-run option times the population of the short branch has to beat the near-term option's payoff — and then show me the interval of that one parameter over which the two calculations genuinely disagree, meaning the unshifted prior says long-run while the shifted posterior says near-term. That interval is where the doomsday argument actually bites, and I want to see how wide it is in your case and how close to it your numbers sit.

Finish by telling me honestly what's doing the work. My hunch, which I want you to confirm or kill, is that it isn't the anthropic machinery at all but the shape of the payoffs: the long-run option is truncated at zero in the short branch, so its value scales faster than the population does, while the near-term option's payoff is basically flat across branches — and if you made both payoffs strictly proportional to the number of people in the branch, the update would cancel exactly and Priya would turn out to be right for the boring reason. Check that algebra rather than taking my word for it, say what happens to the case if you remove the truncation, and give me the strongest thing someone who thinks the doomsday argument is sound would say about a case designed this way. I'm expecting something like: you've rigged it so the argument only matters when the payoffs are already strange, which is a complaint about the decision theory and not about the sampling assumption — but say it better than that, and say whether it lands. Two last things: no one in the group is going to look anything up, so don't name-drop papers or lean on anything I'd have to go read, and spell out every assumption you're using, including what's in the reference class and why you put it there.
