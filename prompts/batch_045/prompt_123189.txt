I am writing a paper on what a colleague has started calling ownerless reasons, by which we mean considerations that are reasons without being anyone's reason, and I have built an argument for them that now seems to me to establish considerably more than I want. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it breaks.

Three categories are doing the work, and I suspect part of the trouble is that the argument slides between them. Call a reason unheld when it is a reason for some agent who does not recognize, accept, or feel the pull of it: the reason a severely depressed patient has to get treatment, the reason a traveller has to take a prophylactic whose mechanism she cannot follow. Call it dangling when its possessor does not yet exist: whatever reasons there may now be for the child you might have next year, or for the members of a generation three centuries out whose very existence depends on choices being made now. Call it ownerless when there is no agent for whom it is a reason and none in the offing: the reason, if there is one, against a world that contains nothing but mindless agony and nothing that could ever register it. Everyone in this debate grants unheld reasons. I want ownerless ones, and my argument was supposed to get me there.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Unheld reasons are possible: whether a consideration is a reason for you does not depend on whether you recognize it, accept it, or would be moved by it. (2) So the fact that a consideration is a reason cannot consist in, or be grounded in, any relation it bears to the attitudes of the agent whose reason it is, or to that agent's capacity to recognize reasons. (3) If nothing about the attitudes or recognitional capacities of the agent makes a consideration a reason, then nothing about agents makes it a reason: its status as a reason is settled independently of whether any agents exist. (4) Hence a consideration can be a reason while no agent exists at all, and so ownerless reasons are possible; and by the same token dangling reasons are possible, since whether the possessor exists yet cannot matter either. (5) Step 3 is entirely general, so no fact about any agent constrains which reasons there are: there will be reasons for states of affairs that no possible agent could bring about, reasons for agents who will never exist to do things they will never do, reasons whose content no possible agent could represent, and reasons in favour of things that are already impossible, since ability is an agent-involving constraint too. (6) But once reasons are detached from agents in this way, the tie between having a reason and any deliberative question snaps: it becomes unexplained how a reason ever becomes mine, why the reasons that exist should be so well matched to what creatures like us can grasp and do, and why the fact that there is reason for something should matter to anyone's question of what to do. (7) That last consequence I cannot accept. I hold on to step 1, and I hold on to the intuition that there is something to be said against the world of mindless agony even with no one in it to whom it is said. But I do not want a realm of reasons I can never be answerable to.

So: which step should I reject, and why did that step look like it followed? I would like the step identified, an explanation of the plausibility that made it seem safe, and a price list for the alternatives, including the price paid by someone who insists that there are no reasons without reasoners and must then explain unheld reasons, dangling reasons, and the pull of the agony-world intuition without them. Please also say, separately for each of my three categories, whether the argument's verdict on it survives the repair, and whether any of my three categories turn out not to be a single thing. I am not asking for literature or citations; I want the reasoning, and I would rather you quoted nothing than guessed at a source.

Write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a careful email to a colleague, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Along the way, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and single out the one claim in it you are least sure of.
