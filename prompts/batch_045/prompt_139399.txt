I'm writing a dinner scene where two characters — one who hasn't eaten an animal in nine years, one who eats grass-fed beef on purpose, on wild-animal grounds — have the same argument they've had in every draft, and I havent been able to get either of them to say anything I haven't already read somewhere. They agree on the farmed-animal numbers (beef takes far more land per calorie than pulses; chickens are small, so you need more of them per meal), they agree that suffering is the thing that matters, and they still land in opposite places because of the animals on neither ledger: the mice, voles and insects killed or displaced by growing the crops, whether a pasture on land too dry and steep for lentils displaces fewer of them than a lentil field does, whether animals in nature are living lives worth preventing at all, and whether killing a field mouse does anything when that population just grows back to whatever the land supports anyway. A third character, who mainly wants everyone to stop, says they're all averaging over hectares when what matters is the marginal hectare, and I cant tell if that's a real point or a way of changing the subject. My own theory is that the whole thing is downstream of one accounting choice nobody says out loud: whether you count over individuals — this cow, this mouse, this beetle's last three days in a spiderweb — or over the steady-state population the land carries, in which case most deaths just reshuffle who suffers when. Is that the fault line, or is it really an empirical fight in costume, about sentience and densities per hectare, or a fight about whether non-existence can be a harm? Diagnose it from what each of them would actually say, and tell me what would move it — a measurement, a distinction, a different question — or why nothing would. And if you can give my beef-eater one line she hasn't said yet, I'd take it.
