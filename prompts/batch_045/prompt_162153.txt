I'm a graduate student pulling together the second half of a reading-group syllabus on evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and it has dawned on us that we have spent three weeks trading objections and replies without ever settling what such an argument is supposed to accomplish. So before we read one more paper I want the specification, and I want you to help me build it rather than hand me a taxonomy of positions. Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy has to be adequacy for something: I am not asking for an argument that merely shows moral realism has an epistemological problem, or that leaves its user with a large set of moral beliefs she is told she cannot justifiably hold but is given no instruction about. I want an argument that a reflective agent could actually use to sort her first-order moral convictions — which to keep at full confidence, which to retain while lowering confidence, which to stop asserting while continuing to act on, which to jettison — and to do that without collapsing into either global skepticism about reason or a quietism on which nothing was ever at stake. Whatever else lands on the list, it has to earn its place by serving that purpose.

The case I keep returning to, partly because I think it decides more than people notice: imagine we learn that in our lineage two sets of moral judgments were shaped in very different ways. Judgments about promise-keeping and reciprocity were shaped heavily by selection for reputation management in small bands, with all the parochialism that implies. Judgments about the moral significance of people who will live centuries from now were shaped by nothing in particular — no selection, no cultural selection, no ancestral occasion to form them; we now form them by taking a norm that was shaped by small-band reciprocity and extending it with generalizing reasoning, off-label. Which set, if either, does a debunking argument actually reach? The parochial one is the obvious target, but the future-people judgment is an extrapolation from a debunked norm, and I can't tell whether debunking propagates along the reasoning that produced it or stops at the beliefs selection actually touched. That puzzle should be a test case for the list, not a footnote. Here is my scratch set of candidate requirements, several of which I suspect are really just prejudices of one camp or another, and I want you to tell me which: (i) sensitivity/independence — the genealogy must show our moral beliefs would have been what they are had the moral facts been different; (ii) selectivity — a principled account of which beliefs are debunked, and why the argument doesn't take down logic, arithmetic, modality, or memory along the way; (iii) a clean distinction between removing justification and establishing falsity, i.e. between the agnostic and the error-theoretic conclusion; (iv) a usable upshot for the agent, given my purpose; (v) falsifiability — some finding in evolutionary biology, archaeology, or cross-cultural psychology that would defeat the argument; (vi) non-self-effacement — it must not rest on a premise about the causal inefficacy of normative facts that also undercuts the debunker's own reliance on epistemic norms; (vii) a worked response to third-factor replies, where the facts selection tracked are necessarily correlated with, or partly constitutive of, the moral facts; (viii) a rule for how debunking propagates through inference; (ix) neutrality on contested metasemantics; and as suspected camp prejudices, (x) that an adequate argument must leave moral knowledge possible, and (xi) that it must not lean on intuitive verdicts about cases at all. Add the ones I've missed, aiming for eight to twelve in total.

For each requirement I want: a one-or-two-sentence statement; the concrete case or consideration that motivates it (the case, not the slogan); a status of non-negotiable, negotiable, or camp-prejudice; what is lost or gained by relaxing it; and which candidates it excludes. Then flag any pair or triple of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — if some subset is unsatisfiable, that is a result about the list itself and I most want to see it — and name the single requirement that does the most work in ruling candidates out. Finally, grade four positions I'll define in my own words so we needn't argue about labels: A, the argument that selection operated on the contents of moral judgment in ways insensitive to independent moral facts, so those beliefs are unjustified and likely false; B, the reply that selection tracked fitness-relevant natural facts which are themselves morally significant or necessarily co-vary with the moral facts; C, the reply that an evolutionary explanation of why we hold a belief is evidentially inert, since a true causal story about belief-acquisition is compatible with the belief's being supported by independent reasons; D, the position that the debunking touches justification only, leaves truth untouched, and recommends retaining the beliefs in some non-doxastic practical attitude. Keep the weight on the criteria; the grading should be a few words per cell.

Return the whole thing as a single JSON object with exactly these keys: "purpose" (one sentence, your restatement of what the argument is for); "desiderata" (an array of objects, one per requirement, each with fields "id" as D1, D2 and so on, "name", "statement", "motivating_case", "status", "cost_of_relaxing", "excludes"); "conflicts" (an array of objects with fields "ids" as an array of the clashing ids, "tension", "verdict" — whether the clash is genuine and which requirement you would keep); "most_excluding" (an object with fields "id" and "rationale"); "added_beyond_my_list" (an array of strings, one per desideratum you contributed yourself); "case_verdict" (an object with fields "promise_keeping", "far_future_people", "reasoning", the last being your rule for how debunking propagates); and "scorecard" (an object keyed "A", "B", "C", "D", each an object with fields "passes" as an array of ids, "fails" as an array of ids, "one_line").
