I'm a junior in a philosophy reading group at my school, and our last session was on vagueness — on what to say about words like tall, or a heap of sand, or too fast to drive in a sixty-five zone, where we can point to clear cases on both sides and no line in between. I expected to leave with one view and left instead with a pile of verdicts I can't fit together. I'd like a principle that captures them, or the smallest set of principles that does, together with an honest statement of which of my verdicts I'd have to abandon.

My own tentative principle, made up on the walk home, has three parts. When a vague word tracks a single measurable scale, there is an exact cutoff and we simply cannot know where it is. When the word is a bundle of criteria that can come apart, there is no fact of the matter at all. And in both kinds of case the location of the cutoff — or the standard applied to the bundle — is fixed by the comparison class the conversation is using.

The verdicts I am trying to capture:

1. My brother is five eleven and I call him tall; my friend at five nine I do not. I find myself wanting to say there is a real boundary somewhere in between that nobody could ever locate, even in principle. At the same time I resist the consequence that raising someone by a hundredth of an inch can flip the matter, which makes the word feel absurdly fragile.
2. Among the boys on our basketball team my brother is not tall. My verdict flips with the comparison class, and I don't think I changed the subject or contradicted myself.
3. Twelve people in a room is not a party; forty is. Here I feel there is no exact number — not merely an unknown one. Music, food, duration, and who showed up can come apart, and I can't see what a sharp line would even be a line between.
4. My father was ticketed for seventy-six in a sixty-five zone and grumbled that seventy-four would have been waved through. I think the statute's cutoff is a decision rather than a discovery, while for the phrase too fast there is a cutoff we cannot find. I also think the ticket was fair. But I equally think we should only punish drivers well clear of the line, because punishing someone for crossing a boundary nobody can locate is unfair. These two verdicts pull against each other and I can't let go of either.
5. A used-car seller told me the car was in good condition; I later learned the transmission was borderline. I want to say there is a fact of the matter about whether he misled me, and I blame him — even though good condition is a bundle of criteria of exactly the sort in case 3.
6. My sister signed a lease at five minutes past midnight on her eighteenth birthday. Legal adulthood has a sharp, knowable line, and I'm comfortable with that even though maturity is vague: the law can stipulate a line without pretending to have found one. Her friend, seventeen and eleven months, has supported herself for a year. I'd call her mature and I still think it's fine that the law doesn't.
7. The case I won't settle: my grandmother's memory has been failing for years, and I can't say when she stopped being herself. Both answers — an exact moment no one could identify, and no fact of the matter at all — feel wrong, and I'd rather refuse the question than pick.

So: does one account of vague language deliver all seven, and if not, which verdicts does it force me to overturn? Is my three-part principle coherent, or does the comparison-class clause do all the real work and leave the other two idle? And what does the best account predict for cases I haven't listed: a dorm inspection that grades clean against a printed checklist; a pool at seventy-one degrees being called warm; a radar gun that reads seventy-six with a two-mile-an-hour margin of error; a film that sits on the line between two ratings?
