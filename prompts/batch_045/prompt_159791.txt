A popular introduction to aesthetics I've been reading — a trade book, not an academic one — summarizes Hume's essay *Of the Standard of Taste* roughly as follows:

"Hume's starting point is that beauty is not a property of objects at all: it exists only in the sentiment of the person doing the contemplating, so there is no fact of the matter about whether a given work is beautiful. To keep this from collapsing into anything goes, he added a standard of taste: the verdict of the true judges, that is, trained critics and connoisseurs, whose advantage over the rest of us is a delicacy of taste largely inborn, together with long exposure to a great many works. Where such experts agree, their agreement just is the object's beauty; where they disagree, neither verdict is correct and the dispute is idle. But the account is circular: we can only identify the true judges by assuming they judge correctly, so the standard has no footing independent of itself, and Hume's attempt to reconcile taste with objectivity fails. What survives is the negative point, that aesthetic judgments report our own responses rather than features of the world — which is why philosophy after Hume treats beauty as subjective."

I have twice heard people insist that Hume was not a flat relativist, so the opening move sounds off to me, but I can't tell where the summary stops being a fair simplification. Would you go through it claim by claim and say what Hume actually holds, what a defender would accept as compression, and what a Humean would reject as misreading — in particular whether the true judges are simply professional experts, whether delicacy is innate, whether disagreement among them really leaves "no verdict correct," and whether the circularity charge refutes the account or is a standing objection with a Humean reply? I also want to know whether the book's conclusion, that beauty turns out to be subjective, survives once the errors are corrected.

One test of whichever reading survives: suppose archaeologists recover a large corpus of artifacts from a culture with no historical continuity with ours. A body of scholars spends decades immersed in that corpus alone, develops exactly the discrimination Hume describes — fine detection of small differences, repeated practice, wide comparison, no local prejudice — and converges firmly on verdicts, while having very little experience of our tradition; and their verdicts on the objects we call masterpieces diverge sharply from ours. On the corrected reading, does their convergence constitute a genuine standard of taste for that corpus, and does it carry any authority over us? Does the demand that a judge have compared many works smuggle in a parochialism, or does Hume have the resources — good sense, freedom from prejudice, the uniformity of the principles of taste in human nature — to say which convergence is the real one? I'm asking because I want to know whether the standard does independent work or merely ratifies the people we already call experts.
