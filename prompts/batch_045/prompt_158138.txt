My brother has gone fully down the "consciousness can't be computational" rabbit hole over the last few months and keeps sending me podcast episodes, and because I write ML code for a living I've somehow become the person he argues with about it. So this has become weirdly personal: he keeps telling me my whole field is chasing something that was ruled out in 1931 and I never know what to say back.

One of the episodes had a philosopher on (I think he teaches somewhere in the UK) who summed up Gödel roughly like this:

"Gödel's first theorem says that any system of axioms rich enough to contain arithmetic is incomplete: there are sentences the system can neither prove nor disprove, and those sentences are true. The trick is that such a sentence can be constructed so that it effectively says 'I am not provable in this system'. So if the system proved it, the system would be contradictory; and since it doesn't prove it, what the sentence says is the case — which is why it's a truth the system can't reach. The second theorem is the same point turned on the system as a whole: a system can never demonstrate its own consistency, so nobody can ever really be sure arithmetic won't one day spit out a contradiction. And this isn't really about mathematics. It's the same result Turing got about programs that never halt, and it generalises: any formalised theory, any computer program, any set of rules you like, has truths it can't get to. That's what Lucas and Penrose argued — a machine is just a formal system, so there's some sentence it can't prove, whereas we can step outside the system and see that the sentence is true, so whatever we're doing, it isn't running on rules. Which is also why this matters for AI: a neural net is a formal system too, so there are facts about the world, and probably about consciousness, that no AI could ever reach, and minds must be doing something else. The only caveat people raise is that the undecidable sentences are artificial constructions, self-referential trickery with no real mathematical content, nothing like Goldbach's conjecture — which is why most working mathematicians ignore the whole business."

I don't know this stuff well enough to argue with him, but two things make me suspicious: the Penrose jump feels like it's doing way more work than the theorem itself, and the "and those sentences are true" bit feels like it's smuggling something in. I could be wrong about both.
