I'm the graduate-student representative on the Public Humanities Committee, which has to sign off before any project can use the Institute's name and its matching funds. I'm also taking a seminar this term on ethics and historical representation, so I'm nominally the person who knows the literature — but our meeting is Thursday and I need a document, not a bibliography, so please reason from the case itself rather than from anything published, and don't invent citations.

The project: a dramatized feature about a woman who died in 1998 and who, in the 1950s, spent four years as an involuntary patient in a state psychiatric hospital after being arrested during a strike. Everything we have about her comes from the hospital: admission forms, physicians' notes, a disciplinary log. The only material in her own voice is a short self-published memoir from the 1970s that the notes contradict at nearly every point and that her surviving family freely admits is largely invented. The director wants to keep two fabricated elements: a composite character standing in for the staff, and a closing scene in which she burns her own file — which never happened. The defense is that strict fidelity to the archive would produce a film in which the institution is the only speaker, so that accuracy here would finish the job the hospital started. Her niece has consented and given interviews; the historian on our committee objects on the audience's behalf rather than the family's — that within a decade the invented scene will be what people remember, and that the file destroyed on screen is the file historians would otherwise have used.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate: recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, specify what has to happen and by when, and give the losing side the strongest version of its argument rather than a version that is easy to dismiss.
