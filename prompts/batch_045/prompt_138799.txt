I write about physics for a broadsheet and am drafting a piece on how cosmologists and particle theorists argue about theories whose distinctive claims cannot, at present, be tested. As background I read a long magazine essay — I would rather not name it — and before I repeat any of its framing in print I would like to know how much of it a working physicist or a philosopher of science would endorse. Compressed but, I think, faithful, its argument runs as follows:

— Theorists set out to find a single set of laws that would unify the forces, and what they got instead was a theory that permits an enormous number of different low-energy worlds, on the order of ten to the power of five hundred.

— Rather than count this as a failure, many of them reinterpreted it: if the theory allows all of these possibilities, and a few of them are hospitable to life, then finding ourselves in a hospitable one is just what you would expect — provided that all of them are actually realized somewhere in an eternally inflating cosmos. So the multiverse is not an extra hypothesis bolted on to rescue a failed theory; it is what you get by taking your own best theory seriously.

— And it is not merely a way of dodging refutation, because the package makes a genuine prediction: that a typical observer will find itself in a universe whose parameters permit observers. Since we are such observers, the fact that our universe turns out to be biologically friendly is evidence in favour of the whole package.

— Critics object that a theory compatible with every possible observation predicts nothing, and call the enterprise unfalsifiable and therefore, in the sense made famous by Karl Popper, not science. But that objection is dated. The current argument is that a theory can earn warranted confidence without making novel predictions, from considerations such as the persistent absence of any alternative and the way the theory's internal parts hang together in ways nobody designed. I am told this line has a name in philosophy of science and is associated with a philosopher called Richard Dawid, though I could not confirm the details.

Three things worry me. First, a cosmologist friend says the essay runs together two separate claims — that the theory has very many solutions, and that those solutions are all physically realized somewhere — and that the second needs a cosmological mechanism the first does not supply. Second, the step that treats our own existence as evidence is where my journalistic instincts fire; I cannot tell whether it is a genuine inference or a selection effect dressed up as one, and I gather that it turns on something called the measure problem, which the essay never mentions. Third, the essay's way of stating the critics' position strikes me as convenient: when I read what the critics themselves write, the complaint seems to be less about Popper than about what would have to be true for any of this to be confirmable even in principle.

What I need is an audit I can work from: which of the essay's four moves you would accept, which are defensible popular simplifications, and which are distortions; what the strongest version of the sceptical case is, as opposed to the version the essay attributes to its opponents; and whether the essay's conclusion survives once the distortions are removed. Two further things would help. One of the physicists I spoke to pointed me to Steven Weinberg's 1987 letter "Anthropic Bound on the Cosmological Constant" and told me it placed an anthropic bound on a quantity whose nonzero value was measured about a decade later — I would like to know whether that is an accurate description of the paper, and if it is, whether it tells against the charge that anthropic reasoning predicts nothing. And I would be grateful for the single distinction I would need to get right for my own eight hundred words not to reproduce the essay's errors. I do not want to run a piece that merely reheats the not-even-wrong line, and I do not want to run one that takes the essay's confidence at face value either.
