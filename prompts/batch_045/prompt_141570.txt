I sit on a small grant-allocation committee that funds work aimed at improving the long-term future, and over the last two years I've drifted away from the version of strong longtermism I started out with — the one that says positively influencing the long-term future is *the* key moral priority of our time — and toward a variant I've started calling the revisability hedge. On my version, what dominates the expected value of anything we can do now is neither the probability that a very large future exists (the survival/ existential-risk bucket) nor the expected quality of that future conditional on its existing (the trajectory-change bucket), but something closer to an option value: the expected degree to which whoever inherits the future retains the capacity — cognitive, institutional, cultural, archival — to notice and correct moral error in whatever values they have inherited. Operationally this pushes money toward things like the durability of historical and scientific records, institutional designs that make value revision possible rather than costly, and the preservation of genuinely divergent moral frameworks, and it pushes money *away* from anything that looks like locking in a particular value system, even a value system I'm fairly confident is correct. I like it because it seems to buy me out of the two objections that bother me most: it doesn't require me to know which values are right in order to act (so the cluelessness worry has less bite), and it doesn't require me to multiply a tiny probability by an astronomical payoff in the way the astronomical-waste style arguments do.

Lately I've been pressed on this by a friend in the group, and I realize I can't give a good account of what would make me give it up. Before you start: I've read my way through the familiar objections to longtermism generally — worries about whether we can affect far-future people at all, about cluelessness regarding downstream effects, about fanaticism and infinitesimal probabilities of astronomical stakes, about whether the future is more likely to be good or bad — and I don't need those rehearsed. My question is narrower and, I think, less often discussed: what would count against *this variant*? The failure modes I can half-see are these. It may be that corrigibility is cheaply re-derivable — any civilization that gets as far as ours will rebuild the relevant epistemic and moral infrastructure on its own, in which case preserving it has near-zero marginal value and I've been buying an option nobody was going to lose. It may be that moral convergence is likely and fast, in which case locking in the approximately-right values dominates keeping the question open, and my position is not just weaker but actively harmful. It may be that the capacities I want to strengthen are the very ones that raise existential risk, so the hedge has a capability externality that swamps its benefits. And it may be that "revisability" is defined loosely enough that any outcome whatever can be described as preserving it or as destroying it, which would mean my view has been qualified into something that can't lose.

What I want from you is a map of that exposure, ranked: which of these (or which others) would be fatal to the revisability hedge, and which would merely be awkward — something a defender should absorb and adjust around rather than abandon ship over. Please be explicit about which defeaters a committed defender of the view should *concede* count against it, as opposed to the ones they'd reasonably dismiss as resting on a caricature; and say what the view predicts that ordinary strong longtermism doesn't, since if the two are extensionally equivalent in every case I care about, that itself looks like the most serious problem with it. Two further things. First, give me your honest read on the unfalsifiability worry at the end of the last paragraph: has the view been insulated by the vagueness of its central notion, and if it has, is that a defect here in a way it wouldn't be for, say, a claim in population ethics or decision theory? Second, I'll tell you what I'd personally find most moving, so you can weight your answer: I suspect the decisive questions are empirical rather than philosophical — whether durable value lock-in is genuinely possible given digital minds, space settlement and stable authoritarian structures, and whether moral error-correction is a fragile cultural achievement or something any technological civilization regenerates automatically. If you think I'm wrong to rest so much weight on those, say so and explain why.
