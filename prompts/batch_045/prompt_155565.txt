I've been reading around the value problem for knowledge — why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than mere true belief, the question Socrates raises near the end of the Meno, with the statues that run away unless they are tied down and the man who gets to Larissa on nothing but true opinion — and I've come to think the contemporary literature is overcomplicating it. I want to stress-test the account I've settled on before I write it up properly. Call it the roll-forward account.

Setup: a believer is a Bayesian agent who updates by conditioning and whose belief-states are scored for accuracy at each step. Suppose a belief's being knowledge consists, roughly, in its being true and counterfactually robust — it would have survived the updates you might have made from here. Then the value of its being knowledge rather than merely true is the expected accuracy of the belief-states you roll forward into from here: the difference between the accuracy you can expect if you keep updating on a true, stable belief and the accuracy you'd expect from a true belief that a nearby update would have knocked out. Add that belief revision is not free — recomputing a plan, rebuilding a model, unwinding downstream commitments — and knowledge comes out with a positive expected value over mere true belief, in principle a number.

Two things seem to follow. First, this escapes the swamping argument rather than falling to it. As I understand it, swamping says that once you have the true belief, the reliability of the process behind it adds nothing, since reliability is worth only as much as the true beliefs it produces. Roll-forward value is not exhausted by the truth of the present belief; it is the value of future true beliefs, a separate quantity, and it looks like exactly what the tethering metaphor was pointing at all along. So swamping only bites accounts that locate knowledge's value in the production of the present belief. The Larissa point — that a man with mere true opinion arrives just as well — shows only that knowledge is unnecessary for a single trip; my claim concerns an agent with a future. Second, this suggests the debate has been asking why knowledge is better than true belief simpliciter, when the answerable question is why it is more useful to an agent who will keep updating.

What am I missing? Is there a swamping-style argument against roll-forward value in particular, and if there is, does that mean my present-versus-future distinction is doing no work? Is there any reason the value problem cannot be answered in expected-accuracy terms at all — is the question being asked a question about usefulness, or about something else? And is there a reading of the Meno passage on which the tethering metaphor is not making the point I attribute to it? I'd also like to know whether anything survives if you drop the expectation over future accuracy and keep only the revision-cost part.
