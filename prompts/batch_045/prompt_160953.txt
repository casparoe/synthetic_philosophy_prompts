I run a fortnightly reading group on decision theory — about 12 people, half philosophy half CS, and one economist who keeps sighing — and we get 75 minutes a session. I've got a personal stake in this one: about nine months ago I quit drinking, and the part that's actually hard isn't the not-drinking, it's that every single evening I re-open a decision I supposedly already made. Someone told me there's a whole position in decision theory about exactly that — choosing a policy once and then refusing to condition on the new information, even when the new information is free and true — and I've been chewing on it ever since. So I want to build a session around it, but I don't want to lecture, and I don't want them walking out having learned a label.

What I'd like from you is a runnable activity: real choices, real small stakes, where they hit the problem before anyone names it. Specifics I care about:

- Two rounds with the same surface form — same envelope, same "do you pay?" question — where in one round looking at the evidence first and updating on it is obviously correct, and in the other it's the mistake. I want the contrast to be felt, not announced, and I want them to have to articulate what's different rather than being told. In the second round, use a biased coin (something like 4:1 toward the branch where they never get asked) so whoever lands in the rare branch is squeezed as hard as possible, and make the numbers such that they can find the break-even themselves instead of me telling them the right answer.
- They commit in writing before the flip. Then — this is the bit I keep going back and forth on — I want a mid-game moment where they're offered the chance to rewrite their answer after seeing the flip, at a cost, and I want the debrief to say whether that offer is even the kind of thing a policy-first view can evaluate, or whether it just pushes the problem back one step.
- Stakes have to be small and non-monetary (chocolate, raffle tickets for a book, whatever), and the game must stay genuinely one-shot: nothing that lets them win by playing it ten times and being consistent.

Then give me a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the distinction I actually care about — the gap between *refusing to condition* and just *failing to use free evidence*. The thing I'm scared of is them concluding the view says "ignore the evidence" or that it's precommitment plus willpower. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I can already hear the economist objecting that the whole setup rests on an impossible predictor, and I can already hear someone saying it's just about being the sort of person who). And be honest about which feature the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself — in the real version the fact about what you'd have done is a fact about your decision procedure that an idealised predictor can read, and here we've swapped in a coin and my say-so, so I suspect the activity gets the structure across but not the part where the counterfactual is logical rather than physical. Say what to do about that.
