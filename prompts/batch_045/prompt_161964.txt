I work as a life-cycle assessment practitioner in food systems — about nine years of attributional product studies for a food manufacturer, some exposure to consequential modelling, marginal energy and land mixes, and the standard allocation hierarchy — but no training in moral philosophy beyond a first-year survey course. I have been asked to adjudicate a claim from an animal-welfare advocacy group that our sustainability team is taking seriously, and before I sign off on anything I want to translate it into a vocabulary I can actually audit.

The claim, as it reaches me, compares beef and chicken and runs roughly like this. Both products cause deaths in two places: the farmed animals themselves, and the wild animals killed in growing and harvesting the crops that feed them. On the first count chicken looks far worse, because the animal unit is small — live weight at slaughter differs between a broiler and a steer by more than two orders of magnitude — so a gram of chicken protein is bought with very many more farmed lives than a gram of beef protein, and the life of a fast-growing broiler is argued, on veterinary and behavioural grounds, to be worse per day lived than the life of a grazing beef animal. On the second count beef looks far worse, because ruminants convert feed into edible protein far less efficiently than poultry, so beef mobilises many more crop hectares and more tillage and pesticide operations per unit of protein, and the animals killed on those hectares — rodents, birds, and above all insects — are argued to be numerous enough that even a very small weight per insect death makes the crop term dominate the ledger. Different people running this comparison with different weights reach opposite verdicts, and the version I am being asked to evaluate holds that the crop term wins, so that reformulating a product from beef to chicken increases total animal suffering, contrary to our team's working assumption.

I want that argument restated in LCA terms as faithfully as you can, and restated in a form I could defend to a reviewer: as a choice of functional unit and unit of account; as a system boundary and cut-off decision about which mortalities enter the inventory at all; as allocation and co-product treatment, including beef originating from the dairy herd, hides and renderings, and any credit from system expansion; as characterization, where a physical flow becomes a damage indicator, and as normalization and weighting, the step at which LCA practitioners conventionally stop. Please also state it in attributional versus consequential terms: average versus marginal supply, indirect effects, and in particular what happens to the pasture that beef demand no longer occupies and what kind of land that pasture is. Treat parameter uncertainty — the kind you propagate through a Monte Carlo — separately from uncertainty about which weighting scheme is correct, if the two really are different things.

Four points where I want you to take a position rather than paraphrase. First, the unit of account: my inventories are denominated in mass and area, while the debate counts individuals, and I suspect the dimension of the inventory is not neutral and may move the sign of the result. Say whether it does. Second, additivity: wildlife ecology distinguishes additive from compensatory mortality, since removing individuals that would have died anyway of density-dependent causes does not reduce the standing population, and crop-field deaths plausibly occur against a high baseline mortality near carrying capacity. Can LCA express this at all, or is the crop term partly an artefact of an attributional framing with no baseline? Third, prevented existence: the farmed animal not bred because demand fell. Avoided-burden logic applies to processes, not to beings; tell me whether a life not lived is a negative flow, an avoided burden, or neither, and what happens to the comparison if farmed lives are themselves net negative. Fourth, sentience as a characterization factor: ecotoxicity indicators are conventionally stated at the level of species and are indifferent to whether the affected organism can suffer. Say plainly what is lost in that translation.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translated cleanly, which translated only with distortion, which cannot be stated in my vocabulary at all; where LCA's own conventions quietly take sides instead of being neutral; and whether the residue indicts LCA or the debate. Concretely: is the disagreement between the two sides an empirical disagreement, a disagreement about the counterfactual baseline, or a disagreement about what is being measured — and if it is the last, can any inventory settle it? Finally, the practical question: if I write this study, is it legitimate to report a parallel inventory of affected sentient individuals as an unnormalized, unweighted indicator alongside the usual categories, or does even that concede more than the method allows?
