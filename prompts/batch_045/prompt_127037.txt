so i just spent like two hours arguing with my friend Marta about Scanlon-style contractualism and i definately lost the last stretch of it, but i can't tell whether she actually had an argument or just pulled a fast one.

the case we were stuck on: we can either let one person loose a finger, or let two million people each have an itchy rash for an afternoon. Contractualism compares how strong the strongest individual complaint is under each option, so sparing the finger wins — and it keeps winning when you add zeros. a billion rashes, a trillion, doesn't matter. i was on the aggregating side and i kept saying that this is just a reductio of the whole framework, and she kept saying no, it's a feature.

then she made a move i'd never heard and i couldn't answer it on the spot. she said contractualism judges principles, not one-off acts, so the right question is what a principle does to a given person across a lifetime of situations of that kind. suppose K such emergencies arise over a life, in a population of N, and on each occasion the "save the finger" option spreads a mild harm across m people. under always-spare-the-finger you can expect roughly K·(m/N) rashes; under always-spare-the-rashes you can expect roughly K/N lost fingers. let N get big and the second term collapses while the first doesn't. so numbers come back in — and in proportion to how many people are affected — without anybody summing complaints across persons.

i said that's utilitarianism wearing a veil, and that the person who actually loses the finger gets no consolation from having had a 1-in-N shot at it. she said something about reasonable rejection already being an ex ante notion and i lost the thread completely.
