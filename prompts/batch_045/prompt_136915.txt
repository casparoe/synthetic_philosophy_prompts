Some background so you can pitch it right. I'm recently retired, thirty-odd years as an experimental chemist (spectroscopy, mostly), and I've come to philosophy late, through philosophy of science. Our local university has very kindly offered me a desk and a library card as a visiting associate for three months, but they want a two-page proposal saying what I'd actually do with the time. So it needs to look like a real proposal, not a reading list or an essay.

The thing I can't let go of is the no-free-lunch theorems and the problem of induction. As I understand it, the result is that any two search or learning methods perform identically once you average uniformly over all possible problems, so no method is better except relative to some structure in the problems it's handed. Every discussion I've found falls into one of two camps: either this is just Hume (and Goodman's new riddle) wearing a lab coat, or it's a genuine sharpening, because it puts a quantity on how much of a method's success comes from the method and how much from the world it was pointed at. I think both camps are missing something, and here's my half-formed idea.

First: the averaging in the theorem is over a uniform, or permutation-closed, set of problems, but choosing that measure looks like an empirical claim about what the world is like — so is the sceptic's use of the theorem self-undermining, or does the regress stop somewhere harmless? I've been calling this second-order lunch. Second, and more interesting to me: the theorem imagines one problem drawn from many possible worlds, whereas a real inquirer gets many problems drawn from one world, and success on the early ones is supposed to licence transfer to the later ones. Does anything like the permutation argument survive in that serial case, or does the trick that makes the theorem work quietly assume the tasks are unrelated, which is the very thing in dispute? I've started calling that serial lunch, though its probably a bad name.

What I want from you: state the question precisely; say why the two camps above leave it open; say what I'd actually do over three months, given that I can follow a proof but am not going to produce a new theorem, so be realistic about the deliverable; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and be honest if the whole thing just collapses into you have to assume the world is roughly uniform. Please don't give me a bibliography or any citations — I'll chase references up myself, and a model guessing at them is no use to me. If you need to gesture at a position, describe it rather than naming names and dates.

Two last things: keep the maths light and gloss any philosophy terms of art, because I'm still learning the vocabulary, and don't flatter me in the proposal — the people reading it will spot it a mile off.
