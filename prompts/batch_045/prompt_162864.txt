Science journalist here, I write a lot about expertise and I keep hitting the same thing in interviews and I dont have a word for it, or at least not one I trust.

Three cases. A neonatal nurse who can intubate a preemie in seconds, and who can watch a resident and say "too deep, you're going in too deep" before anything has visibly gone wrong. But the account she gives of what she is doing is not just incomplete, it is false: the angle she describes is not the angle her hands use. I have seen this on video, slowed down. She watches it, says "huh, I guess I do do it that way," and then nothing changes. She keeps the old description, her hands keep doing the other thing, and she is still the best person in the room. Second: a guy who rebuilds vintage transmissions. Same shape. He taught me to find a gear whine by sound, and every rule he stated while doing it was contradicted by what he actually did; when I pointed that out he said "that's what the manual says, I'm telling you what I do." Third, the one I think is genuinely different: a chess player who can give a reason for every move, correctly, in a way that survives a stronger player's questions, and who is still only a middling player.

So what I am after is whatever the nurse and the mechanic have and the chess player does not — or the reverse, I'm not committed. It isn't just "skill" and it isn't just "can't put it into words," because she can be made to see it and seeing it does nothing: the correct description neither helps her nor, apparently, is what she was using. Is there an established concept for this, the thing that survives being told a false theory of itself? And whatever it's called I need the edges, not the label. Is it one thing or several? How do I tell, in an interview, whether Im looking at the nurse's case or the chess player's? And is the fact that the description does no work actually evidence about what the knowledge is, or is it just evidence that she's bad at introspecting?
