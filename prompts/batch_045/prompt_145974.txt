I'm a staff engineer on the autonomy platform for a mid-size robotaxi fleet. I have no philosophy training beyond a few argument-mapping blog posts, so please name the moves explicitly even when they are the standard ones in this literature.

Our stack is not a frozen artifact. The perception models are retrained weekly by an automated pipeline fed by incoming fleet data. A human approves the *process* — data filters, evaluation gates, staged rollout policy — and the gates themselves are evaluated offline, by replaying logged data that was produced by whatever model version was live last month, through a simulator. Nobody reviews v37's weights, and nobody could tell you in advance which intersection it will fail at. Last quarter a pedestrian was killed, and the internal review has dissolved into argument. My safety lead and I wrote down the argument we can't find a way out of, and I'd like you to find the move we're missing.

(1) Epistemic condition: an agent is blameworthy for a harm only if, at the time of acting, they could reasonably have foreseen that acting as they did risked a harm of the kind that occurred.

(2) Of the kind has to be read at some level of generality, and the level chosen does all the work.

(3) Read specifically — this failure mode, say the perception stack losing a pedestrian who uses a wheelchair at dusk against a low sun — nobody foresaw it and nobody could have. Our validation samples the failure space rather than enumerating it, and novel failure modes are precisely what a retraining pipeline manufactures.

(4) Read generically — someone will eventually be physically harmed by a fleet driving millions of miles — every team that has ever shipped anything at scale satisfies the condition, including the careful ones, so it no longer separates the careful from the reckless.

(5) The intermediate reading that ordinary engineering leans on — the risk classes enumerated in a hazard analysis, with a probability attached to each — is unavailable to us. A hazard list has to be reasonably complete to underwrite blame; ours is knowingly incomplete; and for a continuously retrained model it goes stale on the same weekly cadence as the model, because the pipeline can invent failure modes that were never on it.

(6) So blame is either unobtainable, since any reading specific enough to be informative is a reading no one can satisfy, or automatic, since any reading we can satisfy is one everyone satisfies.

(7) Both are unacceptable. The team that skipped the evaluation gates to hit a launch date is blameworthy in a way we are not, and we are not blameworthy for a death that was, on our best ex ante numbers, the statistically expected price of a fleet that prevents more deaths than it causes. If I have to choose, I keep (7) and give up (1).

What I want from you, in prose first:

- Name the step you reject, and the reading of that step the argument needs in order to run. Explain why the step looked compulsory to us when we wrote it down.
- Price the alternatives. For each of the two or three steps you took seriously, say what giving it up commits me to. In particular: if the repair is to restate the epistemic condition in terms of foreseen *risk* rather than foreseen *kind*, say what becomes of that restatement under (5), since a risk estimate computed on stale proxy data is not obviously a piece of foresight at all.
- Say what becomes of the responsibility gap once the step is rejected: closed, relocated, or real but narrower — and if relocated, where exactly it now sits. I have a suspicion that what actually paralyzes us in design review is not unpredictability of individual outputs but the absence of a settled standard of care for continuously retrained systems, i.e. no agreed list of practices one can fall short of, so that negligence has nothing to grip. Tell me whether that is the same problem in different clothes or a genuinely different one.
- Run your diagnosis on both halves of (7) and tell me whether it returns the two answers we take ourselves to know.
