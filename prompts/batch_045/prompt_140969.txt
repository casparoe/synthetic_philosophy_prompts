I am writing a short essay, for an educated but non-specialist readership, on Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the public: the account he developed in his mid-1840s review-essay on the present age, and the one he then lived out in practice during the quarrel he picked with a Copenhagen satirical weekly in December 1845, when he published in a serious daily an article that more or less invited the paper to make a fool of him. I have the primary texts to hand, so I do not need quotations, citations, or a reading list; what I need is for the argumentative structure to be straightened out.

Here is where I have become stuck. As I understand him: the press brings into being a public, which is not a collection of persons but an abstract, detached, irresponsible something-or-other to which individuals hand over their judgement and thereby shed their responsibility; leveling is the process by which this abstraction reduces each person to a numerical instance; and the counter-category is that single individual, to whom Kierkegaard dedicated his upbuilding writings. My difficulty comes in two parts.

First, a contemporary case I want to use as a framing device. Imagine a small polity in which every citizen now reads only a personalized, algorithmically compiled digest, so that no two citizens ever read the same sentence and no one knows what anyone else has been shown. A colleague says: no shared object, no common organ, therefore no public in Kierkegaard's sense — whatever else may be wrong with that polity. I think this is wrong, but I cannot say precisely why; and the more I press on it, the more it seems to me that Kierkegaard's own remedy suffers from the same defect.

Second, that worry in general form. If the disease is the relation to an abstraction, and the remedy is to write for that single individual, then the remedy looks like a distribution strategy: reach readers one at a time, each severed from the others, each in his privacy. But a million isolated readers, each addressed alone, is the disease under another name — a public with no press. And this makes Kierkegaard's own conduct look like a performative contradiction. A man who held that the press was manufacturing an irresponsible abstraction nevertheless went on writing in the newspapers, signed his own name to polemics, deliberately courted public mockery from a satirical paper, and later campaigned against the established church in pamphlets. If the conclusion of his analysis was that one must withdraw from public discourse and speak only to the individual, why did he not do it?

Where has my reading gone wrong? I would rather be told which of my premises is mistaken, and what the correct distinction is, than be given an answer to the question as I have posed it. Please name the confusion, or confusions, explicitly before reconstructing the view, and say what a properly Kierkegaardian treatment of the personalized-digest polity would have to maintain. Finish with a single line of the form \answer{...}, containing a phrase of at most ten words that names the central mistake in my question.
