I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about Rhodes Must Fall, and I would like the argument settled properly rather than repeated. Take the facts as I stipulate them: the campaign began in March 2015 at the University of Cape Town, where within a month the council had voted the statue of Cecil Rhodes off its plinth; the parallel campaign at Oriel College, Oxford produced the opposite result — a vote to remove, then years of delay, expense and regulatory obstruction, and in the end not removal but a promise to keep the statue and add explanatory context to it.

My friend's position is that the three kinds of history Nietzsche sets out in his essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life are all one needs here: the statue is monumental history in bronze, the college's attachment to it is antiquarian piety, the campaign is critical history breaking a past that burdens life, and the Oxford compromise is antiquarianism wearing critical clothes. I think the scheme fits less neatly than that. Nietzsche's monumental history runs on great deeds and exemplary achievers, on a chain of human greatness that gives the present something to emulate; what the campaigners were building, first with a defaced statue and then with an empty plinth, is a monument to the wronged rather than to the great. Take that as a working notion — monumental history of the injured — and take it seriously rather than nodding at it.

Analyze the episode through the threefold scheme and then tell me where the scheme fails. I want the mapping done carefully: which mode the statue itself belongs to, which mode the demand for removal instantiates, which mode contextualization is, and what Nietzsche's own test, service to life, would actually prescribe here, and for whom. Then run it in the other direction: does the case confirm the scheme, is it an anomaly inside it, or is it a kind of case the scheme never anticipated? In particular I want answers on three points — whether a criterion of life can adjudicate between two parties whose historical needs conflict, whether critical history can do its work at all without some antiquarian attachment to what it destroys, and whether monumental history of the injured rescues the framework or exposes a hole in it. Where the scheme is silent, say so instead of making it speak. No literature survey, and nothing I would have to look up: the facts are as I have given them, and the argument is what I am after.
