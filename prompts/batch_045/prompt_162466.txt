I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology and interpretation of machine learning systems, and I host a small podcast where each episode is a long conversation with one person about a single argued position. For the next episode my guest is Dr. Naomi Reinholt, a philosopher of mind and cognitive science whose recent monograph *Stance and Sufficiency* defends a thoroughgoing interpretivism about belief ascription to AI systems. Her package, as I understand it: a system has the beliefs it does in virtue of the fact that adopting the intentional stance toward it — predicting and explaining its behavior by treating it as a rational agent with contents — pays off for interpreters like us, and there is no further fact of the matter beyond that; where two incompatible interpretations tie in predictive payoff, the question of which contents the system really has is simply indeterminate rather than having a false answer; because content can be fixed by a state's role inside the training distribution rather than by causal contact with the world, she denies that large language models' lack of sensorimotor grounding blocks ascription; and she adds a "counterfactual export" condition meant to keep the view from over-generating, requiring that an ascription keep its predictive edge under interventions on the input distribution and in novel prompting contexts, not merely in next-token prediction over familiar text. She is also very good at three escape hatches, which I would like the question sequence to progressively close: the quietist shrug (no fact of the matter, so the question lapses), the "merely verbal" move (the dispute with realists about machine belief is a fight about the word "belief"), and the methodological demurral (she is offering a strategy for prediction, not a test for belief). I'm genuinely undecided between her view and a mildly realist alternative, so I want questions whose answers are informative either way rather than debating-club gotchas, and each question should be askable in under a minute and intelligible to a listener with some philosophy background who hasn't read the book.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions, ordered so the episode builds, together with — for each one — the reply she is most likely to make and the follow-up that reply makes available to me? The areas I want covered, at minimum, are these. First, over-generation: if the export condition is what separates an LLM from a thermostat or a chess-playing program, what exactly does it demand, and does it smuggle in a realist constraint about the system's causal structure or about what counts as a legitimate intervention? Second, interpreter-relativity: if a system that predicted the model's outputs perfectly without using content — a successor model, or a completed mechanistic account — were available, would the model thereby cease to have beliefs, and is "has beliefs" a property of a system or of a system-interpreter pair? Third, the subject or bearer of ascription: the prompted persona, the weights, the training process — what is supposed to be the thing that believes, and why doesn't that multiply bearers without limit? Fourth, consistency and closure: the same model will confidently assert p in one context and not-p in another, and will fail to draw consequences that are obvious to any interpreter; a real believer is not logically omniscient, but contradiction and scattered failure of closure feel different from mere non-omniscience — at what point does the attributed state stop being belief and become something else, and would that something else still do the predictive work she needs? Fifth, confabulation: when the system produces a confident falsehood, does she attribute a false belief or withhold ascription, and if she withholds, hasn't the stance become hostage to truth in a way her view was supposed to avoid? Sixth, notional content: if contents are fixed by internal role in the training distribution, can ordinary ascriptions like "the model believes that Paris is the capital of France" ever be straightforwardly true, or are most of our everyday ascriptions indeterminate on her own terms? Seventh, the rise of mechanistic interpretability: if the design stance becomes predictively competitive with the intentional stance, does her own criterion downgrade content ascription, and is there then no fact of the matter about whether such systems believe anything at all?

Finally, I want the sequence to end somewhere other than a shrug. Please include a closing question that forces her to say what empirical or engineering finding — about scaling, about interpretability, about the failure of ascriptions to survive export to new contexts — would lead her to stop ascribing beliefs to these systems, and a penultimate question that makes the "merely verbal" move unattractive by asking what substantive claim her interpretivism makes that a realist about machine belief actually denies, whether about the norms governing ascription, about what we owe systems we interpret, or about responsibility for what they do. If it helps to calibrate the tone, the kind of question I have in mind for the over-generation issue is something like: "Your export condition is doing a lot of work in your argument, so I want to know whether it's a constraint on belief or a constraint on us — could a system have a belief that no interpreter will ever be in a position to export, and if not, what happened to the claim that the stance is only a strategy?"
