I'm TAing for my supervisor's graduate seminar on truth and knowability, and I have to lead discussion on Fitch next week, so I'm trying to pre-empt getting ambushed by the one guy in the seminar who has read everything. She's an anti-realist of the verificationist sort, she returns our problem sets with comments in green pen, and she always insists we isolate *which premise* a proposed fix is touching before we say whether it works.

Quick recap so we're on the same page: the knowability principle says every truth is knowable, roughly for any true p it's possible that someone knows p. Fitch's argument picks a truth p that in fact nobody knows, and applies the principle to the conjunctive proposition p and it's not known that p. If that conjunction were knowable, then someone would know p and would know that p isn't known, and the second conjunct (knowledge being factive) makes the first impossible — so the conjunction is unknowable, so by the principle it isn't true, so there are no unknown truths and every truth is known. She makes us write the derivation with knowledge distributing over conjunction as its own step and the final step — the one that takes you from not-possibly-known to not-true, or in the intuitionistic version to the merely doubly-negated conclusion, everything true is not-unknown — as another.

Over the last few weeks she's ruled on a series of proposed fixes. Her verdicts, with roughly her reasons:

Restrict the principle to sentences containing no epistemic vocabulary at all — rejected. Her note: this excludes exactly the sentences that cause trouble and nothing else, and the anti-realist's thesis was supposed to govern all of our declarative discourse, including what we say about knowledge.
Restrict it to atomic sentences — rejected, same complaint only worse.
Give up the principle that knowledge distributes over conjunction — accepted. Independent reason: that closure principle commits you to logical omniscience and no finite agent knows all the consequences of what they know; and she says it costs the anti-realist nothing, since the route from manifestability to anti-realism doesn't need it.
Run the derivation in intuitionistic logic, so the last step only gets you the doubly-negated conclusion — accepted. Reason: the anti-realist already has independent grounds for rejecting excluded middle in that region of discourse, and it isn't gerrymandering because it changes the rules for every inference rather than only the embarrassing ones.
Read the principle de dicto, it's possible that every truth is known, instead of every truth is possibly known — rejected. Reason: the thesis has to attach to each statement individually or it can't do the semantic work anti-realists hire it for.

Next week's paper (I've only read the abstract, it's behind a login and I haven't had time) keeps classical logic and full closure and instead restricts the quantifier to contingent empirical propositions: out go necessary truths, mathematical truths, and propositions about anyone's epistemic state. The motivation offered is that verificationism was always a thesis about regions of discourse where talk of evidence is substantive — the ordinary anti-realist is a realist about the observable present and an anti-realist about the past or about mathematics — so the restriction isn't manufactured for the occasion. I genuinely can't tell whether she'll buy it. On one way of reading her track record she should: local anti-realism is her own position, and the motivation looks independent. On another she should hate it: it's still a restriction on which propositions the thesis ranges over, and she's rejected two of those already, just at a different grain.
