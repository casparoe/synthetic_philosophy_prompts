I'm finishing a PhD in conservation biology (the population-genetics side) and I've been drafted onto a working group revising our threatened-species listing rules, which means I keep having to say what, exactly, the current rules are protecting. I'd like the philosophical commitments straightened out before I write anything down.

The practice, as it stands, has four features that I can't fit together. First: protection attaches to taxa — species, and below that subspecies and separately named populations — and only derivatively to organisms. It is an offence to kill or seriously injure a single member of a listed taxon even when that removal has no measurable effect on the taxon's viability, and, as I understand it, the statutory purposes include conserving the ecosystems the listed taxa depend on. Second: the same regime issues permits authorising the killing of members of a listed taxon as an incidental consequence of habitat development, on the condition that the activity is managed so that the taxon persists. Third: captive breeding programmes for listed taxa are run as pedigree management — which pairs breed is decided by mean kinship, to equalise founder representation — and individuals whose genome is already well represented are contracepted, or, in several zoos, euthanased while healthy; the public justification is that this serves the population and sacrifices no one to the abstraction of a species. Fourth: the regime treats gene flow as a threat when it arrives from a related taxon or from captive stock — introgression, loss of local adaptation — but increasingly as a tool when a population is small and inbred, via translocation and deliberate managed gene flow.

What I want is the implicit theory of levels. Does the pedigree management in the third feature commit the practice to a gene's-eye picture, on which the real thing being managed is allele frequencies rather than animals or populations? Does the first feature commit it to something like species-as-individuals or species selection, on which the lineage itself is the unit with a history? Does the flip in the fourth commit it to treating the population as a functionally organised whole whose local adaptation can be degraded or repaired, which sounds like group-level talk? Or does none of it commit the practice to anything about the units of selection at all, because conservation is about what we value and want to persist, and selection levels enter only as causal background?

I'd like three things distinguished, please: what the practice strictly presupposes and could not survive without; what it merely fits comfortably, so that several rival views of the unit would license it equally well; and where it is genuinely underdetermined. Then the counterfactuals: under a gene's-eye view, would we have to stop treating taxon boundaries and purity as objects of protection and list heritable adaptive variation instead — a version of that has been floated in our group, protecting units of adaptive potential rather than named taxa — and under a multi-level or group-level view, would we have to protect demographic function and ecological role, so that a taxon whose members persist but no longer do anything would no longer qualify? And is the fourth feature's flip really inconsistent, or does one coherent view of the unit license both halves?

Finally, two things about your own confidence. Flag where your reconstruction is contested rather than settled, and separate that from where you are simply inferring. And if the question as I've posed it is confused — if asking what unit of selection a conservation regime presupposes conflates units of selection with units of value, or if one of my four items is not the same kind of thing as the others and I have manufactured an inconsistency — say so plainly instead of answering it politely.
