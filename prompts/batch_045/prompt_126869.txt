I'm an emergency doc and I also do a lot of the clinical teaching for our residents. My head mostly lives in one pretty specific vocabulary: differential, pre-test probability and testing thresholds, decision rules vs. gestalt, illness scripts, sens/spec and likelihood ratios, effect modification, comorbidity and competing risks, and the whole EBM stack — evidence grades, confidence intervals, number needed to treat, external validity. I took two philosophy classes in college, liked them, but I'm not fluent in the jargon.

Here's the thing I only half remember and want pinned down: the fight about moral particularism vs. generalism. As I have it, the particularist side says there aren't any useful exceptionless moral principles — whether a fact counts as a reason depends entirely on what else is going on in the case, and the very same fact can even flip and count the other way. The generalist side says that if that were true, moral judgment would be unteachable, uncriticizable, maybe not even the kind of thing that can be right or wrong.

Restate that whole argument for me in my vocabulary, both sides at their strongest, no strawmen. And I mean actually do the translation, not hand me an analogy: what is a "reason" in my terms, what is a "principle," what is it for a reason to flip, what exactly is the generalist demanding when they demand principles.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue. I have four guesses about where this breaks and I want to know which ones are right.

1. Modality. Everything in my world is an empirical association with a confidence interval around it and a separate argument about whether it transports to my patients. But I get the feeling moral principles are supposed to hold in some stronger, necessary way — not just "held up in the cases we've seen so far." Is that right? And if so, is there any way to say it in my vocabulary, or is that just a hole my framework has?

2. Outcomes. My vocabulary is downstream of outcomes we can count. When I try to translate "a reason" I keep producing "a feature that predicts a better outcome," which quietly turns every moral question into a consequentialist one. Am I smuggling something in, or is the translation forcing that on me?

3. Teaching and criticism. We teach gestalt by apprenticeship, and we absolutely chew each other's calls apart at M&M without anybody ever producing a rule. So the generalist's move that you can't teach or criticize without principles looks false in my world. Does it collapse, or is there a version of it that survives what we actually do?

4. The claim that two cases alike in every non-moral respect have to be alike morally. I think that's load-bearing for one side. What is it doing in the argument, and does it translate as "the features fully determine the label," or is that a bad rendering?

One concrete detail, because I think it matters: I've started keeping a list on my phone of what I call Janus facts — facts that point one way in most cases and the other way in some. My favorite non-medical one is that a joke's being genuinely funny is normally a reason to tell it, but there are settings where how funny it is is exactly what makes it cruel, and so is a reason to shut up. Is that the same thing the particularists mean by a reason switching polarity, or am I describing something else? And here's what I can't figure out: does it matter that I can write the Janus facts down as a list? Doesn't writing them down turn them back into principles, just ugly ones?

Be blunt where my vocabulary simply can't say something, and tell me whether that's my problem or theirs.
