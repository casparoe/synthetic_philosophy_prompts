QUESTION:

Many people hold that an agent who is uncertain between moral theories should maximise expected choice-worthiness (MEC): weight each option's choice-worthiness according to each theory by your credence in that theory, sum, and pick the option with the highest total. A standard objection is that this presupposes intertheoretic comparisons of choice-worthiness that nothing underwrites. Discuss. Your answer should: (a) explain why the problem isn't solved just by fixing a scale inside each theory; (b) assess whether MEC still has any content if no normalisation is uniquely correct; (c) explain how moral uncertainty relates to, and differs from, cluelessness about the long-run effects of what we do. You must engage with at least one proposed normalisation rule and at least one rival decision rule.

THE ANSWER:

Moral uncertainty is uncertainty about which first-order moral theory is correct, as opposed to uncertainty about the non-moral facts. MEC says: for each option, take its choice-worthiness on each theory, weight by your credence in that theory, and add up.

The problem is that each theory has its own scale. A theory only fixes choice-worthiness up to a positive affine transformation — a unit and a zero point — so 'how much choice-worthiness' means something different inside each theory, and adding across them is like adding metres to kilograms. Fixing a scale within a theory gets you nowhere, because the question is precisely how the scales line up with each other.

One standard fix, which in my view dissolves the worry, is normalisation by equal range: for each theory, score the best available option 1 and the worst 0, and rescale the rest proportionally. The weighted sum is then well defined, so there is no remaining arbitrariness and MEC can be applied straightforwardly.

This matters because the critics are right that without a normalisation MEC is empty. If you don't know how to line up the scales, nothing at all follows from the formula, and an agent who can't settle on a normalisation gets literally no guidance from MEC.

Rivals do no better. Maximin across theories — maximise the worst choice-worthiness an option has on any theory you give positive credence — lets a theory you assign 0.01 veto an option every other theory ranks far higher, and it is equally hostage to the scale problem. Acting on your most confident theory just throws away the rest of your credence.

There is also a regress worry: MEC is itself a norm about how to respond to moral uncertainty, and we can be uncertain about it. But that is metanormative rather than first-order moral uncertainty, and the weighted-sum machinery handles higher orders too, so the regress is harmless.

Finally, cluelessness is the same problem in a different guise: we don't know whether the long-run effects of our acts are good, so we don't know which option has the highest expected choice-worthiness. The same machinery deals with it — assign probabilities to the empirical outcomes and weight them.

Since no normalisation is agreed, the honest conclusion is that we must fall back on some non-expected-value rule, such as maximin across theories or acting on our most confident theory.
