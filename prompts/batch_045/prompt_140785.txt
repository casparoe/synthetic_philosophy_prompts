You are a professional philosopher who specializes in normative ethics and has real expertise in the debate over whether the numbers count in life-and-death cases — the debate that begins from Taurek-style resistance to saving the greater number. Think carefully before committing: I want a considered judgment, not a survey.

Here is my situation. I run a three-person ethics team inside a foundation whose program staff allocate money across global health and long-term-risk causes. They rank interventions by cost-effectiveness, which means they routinely add benefits across different people and trade a smaller number of larger benefits against a larger number of smaller ones. I have about one researcher-year of conceptual (not empirical) capacity to spend, and I have to spend it on a single question, or at most a tightly connected pair. The output must either make that practice more defensible or tell us plainly that some part of it rests on a mistake.

Here is the thing I keep returning to, and I want to know whether I am overweighting it. Resistance to aggregation looks strongly stakes-sensitive in a way the literature mostly ignores: the same people who find it plausible that you may save one stranger rather than five would never accept that you may cure one headache rather than five, or one case of blindness rather than five. Yet the standard arguments against counting — each person's separate claim, the personal point of view, fairness between the few and the many — look as though they should apply to headaches too, if they apply at all. Call this the small-goods gradient. My suspicion is that this is where the real action is: that explaining why aggregation seems compulsory for small goods and merely optional for deaths would settle more of the practical question than another round on the original rescue cases.

Give me a defended agenda. Set out the three or four questions you would put on the list, in the order you would spend the year on them, with the small-goods gradient placed wherever you think it belongs — including the possibility that it belongs nowhere near the top. For each one, say what makes it worth the year rather than merely interesting: how much of the rest of the question hangs on it, whether a philosopher with no new empirical data can genuinely make progress on it in that time, whether it is neglected or already settled by implication from work everyone accepts, and what our staff would do differently if it came out one way rather than the other. Then name the single finding or argument that, if it turned up in month three, would most change your ordering and make you reallocate the remaining time.

Before giving the final ordering, spend two or three paragraphs brainstorming objections to my framing — for instance, that the gradient is an artifact of small goods being divisible and interpersonally comparable in a way that deaths are not, that it dissolves once you distinguish outcome-value from the agent's reasons, or that it only reformulates the old question without adding anything. Then commit.

Do not give me a bibliography or citations, and do not lean on papers or authors I would have to look up: argue the positions directly and assume I know the standard moves on both sides. Write it so it could be defended in front of a skeptical program officer.
