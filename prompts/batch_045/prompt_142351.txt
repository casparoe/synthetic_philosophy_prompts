Hey — I'm a TA on a first-year general-ed module called something like "Ethics in Public Life," and I've got next week's 75-minute slot (realistically 60 of usable time, there's always admin at the start) on the "he was a man of his time" defence. The three cases on the table are Jefferson, Churchill and Columbus, non-majors, whiteboard only, and about a third of the room will arrive already convinced the answer is obvious — split between "obviously you can't judge them" and "obviously you can." I don't want it to just become a free-floating argument about statues; I want them to leave able to actually do something with it: take a figure, run the defence on them, and say where exactly it gives out.

I've been argueing about this with my flatmate, who's doing a history PhD and is very annoying about it, and he made a move I can't get out of my head. He says the real work in "man of his time" isn't the bit about cost — that it would have been expensive, dangerous or socially ruinous to act differently — but a claim about conceptual availability: the agent didn't have the conceptual resources to see the thing as a moral failing in the first place, so blaming him is a kind of category error rather than just harshness. I want that distinction to be load-bearing in the session. Two other things I keep tripping over and want built into the structure rather than bolted on at the end: first, that excusing Jefferson (or whoever) and deciding whether to keep his name on a building look like two different questions even though everyone in the room will run them together; second, a symmetry worry — if the defence works for the bad beliefs it should equally work for the good ones (whatever we're supposed to admire him for), so you can't deploy it selectively, and if it works for them it presumably works for us too, meaning our grandchildren excuse whatever we're doing right now. That last one sounds like it slides straight into "nobody is ever blameworthy for anything," which can't be right, and I really don't want the session to end there with everyone shrugging.

So don't write me the explanation — plan how I teach it. I want: the order the ideas have to go in and what depends on what (I genuinely can't tell whether the excuse-vs-commemoration split has to land before or after the conceptual-availability-vs-cost distinction, so argue for one ordering); the single example out of those three that should carry the whole hour, with reasoning about why that one and what the example has to have and lack — my guess is Columbus is too easy and Jefferson is too loaded, but tell me if that's wrong; the misconceptions in the order they'll actually surface in the room and the specific moment in the sequence where I head each one off; one thing I should flatly refuse to get into at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing; and a check at the end that isn't a show of hands but shows me they can run the defence on a case they haven't been walked through. One more: should my flatmate's distinction ever get a name in the session, or should it just get used informally, and if it gets one, where? Skip any reading list or bibliography — I'm not assigning anything for this one, and I'd rather the plan not depend on me having to adjudicate disputed history in front of thirty people.
