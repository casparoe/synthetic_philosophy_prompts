I write fiction for a living, and I'm drafting a novel in which two characters argue about which markets the law should shut down. I want their disagreement to turn on a distinction that would actually survive being argued with, so I've been collecting my own gut verdicts on a batch of cases and trying to see what they add up to. Here they are, with the verdicts I keep landing on:

A living person sells a kidney to the highest bidder — no, and I'd keep it banned.

The same kidney sale, except that the price is fixed by statute and the recipient is drawn by lottery from the waiting list — still no, but my recoil is much weaker, and I can't say why, since the same organ, the same surgery, and the same money are involved.

Selling one's corneas, to be taken after death, the fee paid to one's estate — fine; no recoil at all.

Paid surrogacy under an enforceable contract — permissible, though uneasy. This is the pair I can't reconcile: it seems to me to involve exactly what the kidney case involves, namely the state eventually backing a claim over a living person's body, yet I judge the two differently.

Selling eggs for IVF — fine.

Two hundred dollars for a vote — no; a crime, and I think rightly so. Yet paying people a modest sum for the time it takes to register and vote, with no condition on how they vote, strikes me as fine, and I know the two are hard to tell apart in their effects.

Eighty dollars for a case of bottled water after a hurricane — no; I'd prohibit it, and the argument that high prices draw in supply doesn't move me. Eighty dollars for the same case at a desert music festival — also no, but markedly less bad.

A thriller I'm commissioned to write for a defense contractor that supplies plot points and holds a veto over how its drone operators are portrayed — not fine. A novel commissioned by a hotel chain that leaves me full editorial control — fine.

A fee to put my name on a novel I did not write — not fine, and worse than writing a novel that carries someone else's name, which is fine.

And the case I find worst of all: a buyer who pays a premium not for the organ but for the seller's willingness — for the meeting, the thanks, the fact that the seller was willing at all. Nothing extra changes hands and no extra coercion is involved, yet this strikes me as worse than the plain sale.

Is there one principle, or a small set, that gets all of these or nearly all? I'd like it stated precisely enough to argue with, with the case that motivates each clause. I especially want to know where you think the repugnance actually lives — in what is transferred, in what the transaction expresses to others, or in what the state would have to do to enforce it — because only one of those can be shown on the page, and I need to know which of my verdicts I'd be sacrificing if I built the argument around it. Tell me honestly which verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you'd have me drop them or bend the principle to keep them. Then give me its verdicts on two cases I haven't settled: a publisher paying my estate for the right, after my death, to produce new novels in which my characters appear, generated by a model trained on my work; and a state monopsony that is the only legal buyer of kidneys, paying a fixed sum, with no resale and no contact between buyer and seller.

Please answer in American English.
