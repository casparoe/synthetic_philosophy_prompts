I sit on the heritage grants committee of a regional council, and I've read enough constructivist history of nationalism that "it was invented" no longer settles anything for me — on the standard account nearly everything gets invented at some point, so the live question is what age and uptake actually buy you. Our funding rule, drafted in 1974, says a tradition must have been performed continuously for at least a century by a self-identifying local community, and must not be a reconstruction or revival.

Our hardest application this year is a midwinter lantern procession through the salt marshes: roughly three thousand participants, the largest civic event in a depressed coastal town, and the only thing that reliably fills its high street. Two things surfaced in review. First, a local historian has published a founding vicar's diary showing the procession was composed in 1897 out of fragments of unrelated older customs, explicitly to give a newly amalgamated parish a shared ceremony. It clears our century mark by twenty-seven years — but it was designed, and recently enough that the designing is documented. Second, the people who now make the lanterns and organize the route are mostly the town's Bangladeshi and Polish families, who joined about thirty years ago; several older families have stopped attending and say the procession is no longer theirs. The applicants also want the grant to cover changes: LED lanterns instead of oil, and a preamble spoken in Bengali and Polish alongside the English one.

Please draft the committee's decision memo — fund, decline, or fund with conditions — with reasons the applicants could actually respond to, and with the strongest version of the losing side stated honestly rather than as a strawman. What I mainly need is a principle our rule doesn't supply: whether a tradition whose origin is conceded to be a deliberate invention forfeits its claim, or whether a century and a quarter of continuous practice by whoever is keeping it alive now generates a claim of its own; and whether the community whose tradition it is means the descendants or the current practitioners. I'd also like the memo to say what we should do, if anything, about the fact that the grant would be funding a change rather than a preservation.
