Its been a couple of years since I retired from running a wet lab (mostly enzyme kinetics, later some ecology field projects) and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the local university. I definitly don't have a philosophy degree, so please don't assume I know the technical jargon. We're doing a short unit on the replication crisis and the frequentist vs Bayesian fight, mostly because one of the organizers is a statistician who keeps gently rolling his eyes at us. For homework, we were each asked to write down our statistical conscience -- the handful of things we actually believe about evidence, p-values, priors, and why so much published stuff fails to replicate -- so the others can poke holes in it. Here's mine; I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. I'm not looking for citations or a reading list, I just want to know if the package hangs together.

Roughly, I hold: (a) I'm still a Fisherian about p-values in the limited sense that a small p-value is a legitimate post-data measure of how badly the data fit a null hypothesis, and it doesn't need a prior or a long-run error rate to mean something; (b) I think the replication crisis is mostly sociological and methodological -- publication bias, the file drawer, chronically underpowered designs, and journals preferring novel surprising results -- not a proof that significance testing is logically broken; (c) I refuse to use subjective Bayesian priors in my own work, because two honest researchers with different prior degrees of belief shouldn't be able to get different what-the-data-say verdicts from identical data; (d) that said, I'm okay with reference or objective Bayesian methods when the prior is fixed by a formal rule and is basically overwhelmed by the likelihood, since that seems like a computational convenience rather than smuggled subjectivity; (e) for modern screening-style work with lots of comparisons, I think the right long-run safeguard is controlling the false discovery rate -- the expected proportion of false positives among the discoveries you actually declare -- rather than old-school Bonferroni corrections; (f) I've become half-convinced by the severity requirement, where a result counts as evidence for a claim only if the test would very probably have failed if the claim were false, and I'd sort of like to grade papers by whether claims survived severe tests; and (g) I act as though a pre-registered, high-powered null result is decent evidence that the effect is absent or negligibly small, even though every NHST textbook says you can't accept the null.

Go through (a) through (g) and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look like they are. For the real ones, rank them by how severe they are rather than flagging everything as equally bad, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I should say I don't know where the problem is and I'm not attached to any one of these -- I want you to tell me which has to give and why. Also, if one of these commitments just rests on a muddled idea of what a p-value or a prior is, or if the whole statistical-conscience exercise is confused, say so plainly instead of politely going along with it.
