I'm a retired sensory physiologist — most of my career was on the vestibular system and on how people stay upright when one of the inputs drops out — and about a year ago I started going to the philosophy of mind reading group at the university in town. They've given me one of the short slots next month, twenty minutes plus discussion, and I'd like to be ready for the questions, because the last person who presented there got eaten alive.

What I'm defending is a deflated version of that thought experiment Avicenna gives about a man brought into existence all at once, already adult, floating in the air with his limbs spread apart and not touching anything, with no sensation of any kind, who would still, on the usual telling, affirm that he exists. I don't want the dualist conclusion — I don't think it gets you an immaterial soul, and I'm not going to argue for one. My claim is narrower: whatever the most basic self-awareness is, it isn't delivered by or built up out of any single sensory channel, so you can't explain it as a construction from interoceptive, proprioceptive or vestibular input. Then I want to add a wrinkle I haven't seen anyone use. Instead of the all-or-nothing case, run it as a gradient: strip away touch, then proprioception below the neck, then vestibular input, then interoception, and at each stage ask whether self-awareness fades by degrees or falls off a cliff at some threshold. My second claim is that the original staging is cheating anyway — we're told the man's limbs are spread apart, and I think that description is covertly supplying exactly the bodily content the argument claims to have removed; call it parasitic imagination. So the apparent force of the thought experiment may be an artifact of how it's staged, and the gradient version is the only honest way to put the question. And I want to insist it's partly an empirical question, answerable from people who've lost the large-fibre proprioception in their limbs and have to watch their own legs to walk, and from patients with bilateral vestibular loss. I've got about six pages of notes, definately more confident on the physiology than the philosophy, and I can't read Arabic, so keep the history light.

The room will be maybe fifteen people: four or five analytic philosophers of mind, mostly physicalists of one stripe or another, a couple of whom work on bodily awareness and self-representation; a cognitive scientist who does the rubber-hand and full-body illusion work; a phenomenologist in the Merleau-Ponty line who thinks bodily intentionality is doing all the work; one medievalist who turns up when the topic touches old texts and who has already emailed me to say I'm converting a claim about what the soul is into a claim about what we can know about ourselves; a couple of retirees like me; and the department's Wittgensteinian, whose stock question is whether a word is doing any work. Can you write me the prep sheet? The eight-ish objections I'll actually get from this lot, roughly in the order they'll arrive, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than in neutral philosophical voice, each with a reply I can give in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft; what I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone puts a hand up; what I should hold even if the whole room pushes back; and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Skip anything I'd only get from a theology crowd or from people who argue about this stuff online.
