hi — novelist here, not a philosopher, and I'm hoping you can tell me whether something I keep tripping over has a name, because I've had it described to me in a way that doesn't quite match what I'm seeing.

Twice now I've had the same argument in a workshop about the same scene with one thing changed. Scene A: a hydrologist named Ilse diverts a river to keep a town from washing away. She knows — and says so, in first-person narration, in the scene — that the diversion will silt up a downstream marsh that a neighbouring village lives off. There's no way to save both. The text is explicit that she has no feelings about the marsh either way; she just wants the town dry. My readers come out of it convinced she destroyed the marsh on purpose, that she wanted it gone, and that this is secretly what the chapter is "about." When I add a line where she says out loud that she's sorry about the marsh and wishes there were another way, they read it as insincere, or as a tell.

Scene B: identical, same sentences, same foresight, same lack of alternatives, except the side effect is good — the diverted water refills a marsh that's been dry for years. She's equally indifferent in the narration. Nobody says she restored the marsh on purpose or wanted to. And here's the bit I actually care about: in B, one line is enough. If she says "I didn't have the marsh in mind at all," readers accept it and stop giving her credit for it. In A, nothing works. I've tryed three drafts. The intentional reading sticks, and every line I add to soften it gets absorbed as further evidence — the denial becomes the proof.

So far as I can tell that's two things, and I can't tell whether they're one: (1) whether a side effect is bad or good decides whether we hear it as something the character did at all, as opposed to something that just happened; and (2) the bad version is sticky in a way the good version isn't — it resists correction and feeds on it, while the good version evaporates the moment you poke it.

Someone at a reading waved at "the Knobe effect," but when I looked into it, it seemed to be about what people say when you ask them a direct question in a survey, which isn't quite the same as readers holding onto an interpretation against the plain sense of the text. And (2) — the part where the disclaimer is read as a confession — I can't find anything for at all.

So: is this one named thing or two? What are the nearest established concepts for each, and how exactly does each differ from what I've described? Does it matter that she had no alternative, that she's stated to be indifferent, that the harm falls on a village rather than a person — i.e. which of those details are load-bearing and which are incidental? If part of it has no name, coin one and define it tightly enough that it doesn't just collapse into the general claim that people are quicker to blame than to praise, and give me a nearby case that would NOT be an instance of it. I don't just want a label — I want the carving, since I'm going to have to make a decision about this scene either way.
