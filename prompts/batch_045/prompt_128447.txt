I'm in a very low-stakes reading group — three of us, we meet at a wine bar, one of the guys is doing a PhD in economics and keeps insisting that the Wealth of Nations is the "easy" Smith book, which I refuse to believe — and this month we read a pop moral-psychology book that has a chapter on Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments. I liked the chapter a lot, but I have no way of telling whether it's fair to Smith. The econ guy muttered something like "Smith did not think sympathy was empathy" and then wouldn't elaborate because his drink arrived. Here's roughly how the chapter sums Smith up: "Morality, for Smith, is built out of sympathy — which is basically what psychologists today call empathy. I can't feel your pain directly, so I imagine what it would be like to be in your situation, and that imagined feeling, a kind of faint copy of yours, is the raw material of moral judgment. I approve of your grief or your anger when my imagined feeling matches yours and disapprove when it doesn't, so 'fitting' just means: our feelings resonate. But other people's real reactions are biased and self-serving — they flatter the rich and despise the poor — so Smith introduces the 'impartial spectator.' You internalize the gaze of a neutral observer and use it to overrule your selfish impulses. Really it's just the crowd's opinion installed inside your head, which is also why Smith thought we care so much about how we're regarded; it's essentially Hume's 'general point of view' with a catchier name." The punchline the author draws is that since psychologists have now shown imagination-based empathy to be hopelessly parochial — we feel for people who are close, similar and vividly depicted, and go cold for statistics and strangers — the impartial spectator is just a biased faculty with a fancy label, and Smith's answer to "how do I know what's right?" collapses into "whatever my imagination happens to hand me."

The bit where I wrote "?!" in the margin is the line about the crowd's opinion being installed inside your head. I've read in a couple of places that Smith specifically cared about being *praiseworthy* rather than merely praised, and this chapter makes him sound like a pure reputation-manager; also I half-remember people quoting him about "the man within the breast," which the chapter never mentions, and that feels like a clue that something is being flattened.
