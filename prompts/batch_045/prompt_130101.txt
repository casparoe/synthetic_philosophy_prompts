I chair the clinical ethics committee of a large public hospital, and we have a case on Thursday that we expect to set precedent, so I want the written opinion argued at the level of principle rather than settled by intuition.

Ms. A. is 79 and has advanced dementia. She no longer recognises her children, speaks little and not coherently, shows no signs of distress, and is fed by hand by nursing staff. She has now developed dysphagia with recurrent aspiration, and her team puts two options to us: place a feeding tube, or continue careful hand feeding while accepting a high risk of aspiration pneumonia and manage comfort thereafter. Nine years ago, while cognitively intact, she signed an advance directive refusing tube feeding and any further hospital admission. Her two sons ask us to set it aside. Their argument, as they put it, is that their mother is not the person who signed that paper. On their account a person is constituted by her relationships and not by the private, temporally extended will of a single biological organism; the person in the bed today is the person their family sustains, someone who responds to being fed by hand, to touch, to being sung to, and whose personhood they enact by continuing to feed her. They accept the consequence that the document is not merely outdated but was never the right sort of thing to bind her. Her daughter disagrees and asks that the directive be honoured. Two further facts complicate things. The sons have visited a handful of times in the last two years while the daughter has been present most days, so the family that claims to speak for her relational self is itself divided about who belongs to it. And several staff have said privately that the relational argument strikes them as a philosophical veneer over the sons' own interest in keeping their mother alive, while other members of our committee think our own default — that the earlier autonomous individual is the one whose word goes — assumes exactly the individualistic metaphysics the sons reject, and is therefore not the neutral position either.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should (i) recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame and a review point, not just a framework; (ii) engage the sons' position on its own terms, including the possibility that if relational personhood is right it undermines the standard justification for advance directives rather than merely adding a cultural exemption to it; (iii) give our staff criteria they could actually apply next time to tell a family speaking for a genuinely relational interest from a family pursuing its own, given that families are rarely unanimous and that the intensity of a relative's involvement seems relevant in a way our current policy has no vocabulary for; and (iv) state the strongest objection to whatever you recommend, and why it loses. Two constraints on form: no citations, no literature review, no discussion of which philosophers have defended which view — I need a document the committee could adopt; and the reasoning should not turn on this family's cultural or religious identity, since a large share of the families at this hospital could make the same kind of claim and I want the policy to generalise to any of them.
