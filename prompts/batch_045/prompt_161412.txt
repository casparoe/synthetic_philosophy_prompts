Working title: What's a glut good for? Gluts and deliberation

The thesis: The costs of dialetheism that everyone talks about are logico-semantic — no detachable conditional, modus ponens only in restricted contexts, disjunctive syllogism under pressure, the whole business of getting classical reasoning back inside consistent contexts. I think the underrated cost is decision-theoretic. Once you let a glutty proposition into the state space of a decision problem, the glut does no work. Either deliberation breaks down, or the agent ends up behaving exactly as if they were maximally ignorant about that proposition — and either way the extra truth value earns nothing. Call this the inertness argument.

Step 1, credence. Take P to be a glut: true and false. Consider an agent who knows this. In a standard accuracy framework your inaccuracy about P is how far your credence sits from P's truth value; but P has two truth values, 1 and 0. If you score against both and add them — which seems like the natural thing to do if you really think P is both — then the inaccuracy-minimizing credence in P is 1/2, since (1−x)² + x² bottoms out at x = 1/2. Same for not-P, also 1/2. So a perfectly informed dialetheist who knows P is a glut should, by accuracy lights, sit at 1/2. But 1/2 is precisely what the accuracy framework prescribes for someone with no evidence either way. The knowledge that P is both does nothing to credence. (Obvious pushback I can't fully evaluate: the dialetheist may reject the score-twice-and-add move as itself classical, since adding the two errors assumes theyre separate and both count.)

Step 2, acts. Now decisions. Suppose the only live uncertainty in a decision problem is whether P. Ordinarily EU(A) = cr(P)u(A,P) + cr(¬P)u(A,¬P), and with both credences at 1/2 you get exactly the maximally uncertain agent. So the decision rule that falls out of step 1 treats the glut as ignorance. Alternative: try to honour the claim that P is true (and not-P too) by evaluating each act twice, once taking P as true and once taking not-P as true, then combining — maximin over the two evaluations, or average them. But averaging just returns the 1/2 case, and maximin is what an agent with no probability information at all would do. Whichever you pick, the advice you hand the agent is indistinguishable from the advice you'd hand someone with no idea whether P. Inertness again.

Step 3, where it bites. Not all gluts are equal. The paradox-driven gluts — liar-ish sentences, set-theoretic ones, the inclosure-schema cases — never show up in practical deliberation; no one's decision table has a cell for this very sentence is not true. For those, inertness is harmless and the dialetheist can shrug. The costly class is the applied gluts, above all vagueness. If borderline predications are glutty — is a vehicle, is a person, counts as a refugee — and if the deontic verdict inherits the glut in the usual way, then in a borderline case the agent is both permitted and not permitted. And that is exactly the moment they wanted guidance. The dialetheist's machinery hands them the same advice as total ignorance. Which is where I want to press the cash-value question: what is both true and false buying us in the one class of cases where it was supposed to buy something?

Step 4, the reply I'm most worried about. The natural dialetheist move is to let the practical verdict be glutty too: A1 is both choice-worthy and not choice-worthy, that's just another true contradiction, pick either, nobody is criticising you either way. I want to say this is worse than it looks. First, it makes deliberation pointless in precisely the cases that motivated the theory — we wanted a story about borderline cases that does work. Second, it looks unstable once you notice that if do A and don't do A are both true then you're criticisable either way threatens to be true as well, which is a substantive and strange moral result rather than a neutral logician's shrug. But I'm not sure how much of that second point smuggles in a classical deontic logic. There's also a version where the glut doesn't transmit at all: the conditional in the deontic rule doesn't detach in the relevant/paraconsistent setting, so glutty predication never yields glutty obligation, and my step 3 test case just misfires.

Other exits I gesture at and don't know what to do with: deny that truth constrains credence at all (then truth gets normatively thinner, but maybe dialetheists are fine with that); build a properly non-classical probability and utility theory where the measure of P and of not-P are both 1 and additivity fails (then there's no clean EU rule, and whatever decision rule you bolt on — upper and lower expectations, whatever — looks like maximin again); or restrict dialetheism to the paradox-driven gluts, in which case my whole complaint is idle.
