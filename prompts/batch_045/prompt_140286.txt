Write a joke about Moloch and multipolar traps that would actually land with someone who knows what those are. Not the gothic register in which Moloch is a demon demanding sacrifice, and not the shrugging version whose entire content is that everyone defects, ha ha. The humor has to be generated by the structure of a race to the bottom itself, and it has to turn on at least two of the following three things.

First, that the regret in a trap is real but unassigned. Every participant, looking back, sincerely prefers the outcome in which nobody competed to the one they all reached; and every participant, asked whether they would have acted differently given what the others in fact did, sincerely says no. Conditional no-regret and unconditional regret are both true simultaneously, and neither one licenses a conclusion about what any individual should have done.

Second, that Moloch is not an agent in the game. Nobody chose the equilibrium, so there is nobody to blame, punish, bribe, sue, or negotiate with. If your joke requires Moloch to want something, it has stopped being about the structure and has become a joke about a villain.

Third — and this is the case I want you to build the joke around, because I have not seen anyone use it — suppose an agency, call it the Office of Counterfactual Restitution, is charged with computing, for each participant in some concrete race to the bottom of your choosing (ad load, credential inflation, working hours, regulatory laxity across jurisdictions, deployment speed, whatever you like), the difference between what they got and what they would have got if everyone had exercised restraint, and with sending each of them a bill for that difference. The arithmetic it immediately runs into is the arithmetic that makes the situation a trap rather than a collective mistake: the benefit each competitor gets from competing is largely a transfer from the others, so the apparent gains cancel in aggregate and nothing was created, while the cost each competitor imposes is spread across everyone including themselves, so the cost any one of them can see is only a fraction of the cost they cause. Every individual bill is therefore small, accurate, and payable; no individual decision looks wrong; and the totals nevertheless show a pure loss with nothing on the other side of the ledger.

Give me the joke as a one-liner or a brief exchange, a few lines at most. Then, in no more than four sentences, say which of the three ingredients the punchline turns on and what exactly the joke gets right about the mechanism — including, if you use it, why a scheme for collecting the bills would reproduce the trap one level up rather than solving it. A groan of recognition is an acceptable result; a joke that would work just as well with a cartoon villain substituted for Moloch is not.
