I'm writing the prospectus chapter for a dissertation in philosophy of science, and I keep coming back to a frustration from a reading group I ran last term on natural kinds. We spent eight weeks on it and ended more or less where we started. The realists said that the track record of mature science — the periodic table, the germ theory of disease, the particulate account of inheritance — is best explained by its categories tracking mind-independent structure, and that if the categories were merely convenient, the reliability of induction and explanation would be a miracle. The skeptics and pluralists replied that for every successful kind that looks like that there is another successful one that does not: species, psychiatric disorders, genes, money — categories that are interest-relative, historically contingent, functionally defined, or multiply realized, and that support plenty of good science anyway. They added that talk of joints is a metaphor doing the work of an argument. Everyone had read everyone, nobody seemed confused, and nobody moved. I do not want another tour of the positions; I want a diagnosis of the structure of the standoff.

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, has two parts. First, I suspect the sides are not using one notion of a natural kind but two: an epistemic or projectibility criterion, on which a kind is natural to the extent that it licenses induction, supports counterfactuals, and stays stable as inquiry proceeds; and a causal-structural criterion, on which a kind is natural if its members share a mind-independent causal structure that explains their co-occurring observable properties. These normally travel together, which is what makes the dispute look substantive, but they can in principle come apart — a category might be projectible without being causally unified, or causally unified while being useless for induction — and if so, much of the apparent disagreement is about which test is the right one rather than about what the world contains. Second, I suspect the two sides are tacitly generalizing from different paradigm sciences, so the debate has the disguised form of a dispute about whether biology, psychology, and the social sciences are relevantly like physics and chemistry. Tell me which parts of this are right, which are wrong, and which parts are simply things both sides already grant and hence cannot do the diagnostic work I want from them.

What I am asking for is roughly 800 words of diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who is right. Distinguish as carefully as you can among these possibilities: that the dispute is verbal; that it is substantive but turns on a hidden empirical question, for instance about how much causal structure the special sciences actually turn up; that it is substantive but rests on different views about what a theory of kinds is *for*; that the two sides are answering different questions under a single label; or that the question as posed is ill-posed. For each side, say what it is genuinely committed to — what it could not absorb without cost, as opposed to what it can concede while remaining itself — and say why its best arguments leave the other side untouched. Then be honest about whether anything could end this: a formal result, a distinction, a shift to a domain-by-domain treatment, a decision to ask a different question, or nothing at all. Please do not give me citations or a bibliography; I will add those. Refer to positions and arguments by description, and if you name a view, name only ones a non-specialist philosopher would recognize.
