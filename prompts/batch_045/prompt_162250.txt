I am writing a novel, and I have written a speech I cannot check.

The scene is a Sunday lunch in Chapter Nine. Alec, a retired bond trader, expounds to his daughter Frances, a junior doctor, and she is meant, in roughly a page, to puncture the whole thing. I have drafted his premises and his conclusion; the flaw, if there is one, I cannot locate. I have written it so that it sounds unanswerable, which is exactly why I distrust it. And if I cannot locate the flaw I cannot give Frances a reply that is more than assertion, and a novelist who hands a character a rigged argument and no answer is cheating the reader.

His argument runs as follows.

(1) A voluntary trade is struck only when each party expects to gain by it, given what each knows at the time of striking.

(2) Take a contract that pays a fixed sum if and only if some event occurs — regulatory approval of a drug, say — and nothing otherwise. Whatever one side gains, the other loses, less costs. So both sides can expect to gain only if they attach different probabilities to the event.

(3) Suppose both parties are rational, share a common prior, and have each correctly processed their own private information.

(4) Where two parties share a common prior and their posteriors are common knowledge between them, those posteriors are equal. And once my willingness to trade at a stated price is visible to you and yours to me, our willingness — and with it, enough of our posteriors — is common knowledge between us.

(5) So, once the bid and the offer are on the screen, both parties assign the same probability and neither can expect to gain. The order book should be empty. This is the no-trade result, and it does not depend on anyone's being stupid.

(6) Now drop the simplifying assumption that the contract is a pure bet. Any trade decomposes into a component driven by differing beliefs and a component driven by differing exposures — insurance, hedging, the shifting of risk to whoever bears it more cheaply. The second component is genuine, and such trade should occur. But it is bounded: there is only so much risk in the world to redistribute, whereas turnover in single-name equities runs to many multiples of anything an exposure-sharing motive could explain. The residue must be belief-driven.

(7) The residue is enormous. So (3) fails in the world: either traders are not rational, or they do not share a common prior.

(8) If they do not share a common prior, what moves the residue is not informational disagreement at all — nobody knows more, everybody merely began elsewhere, and there is no arbiter between starting points. Then the price is not an epistemic achievement. It is a weighted average of opinions, weighted by capital and by stubbornness, and it deserves no more deference than any other poll.

(9) If instead traders are irrational, the price is a weighted average of error, and again deserves no deference.

(10) So a market can be thick with trade, or it can know something, but not both. To the extent the order book is full, the price is dumb. That is the sentence the chapter ends on.

The trouble is that the reasoning also seems to prove things I cannot accept and cannot put into Alec's mouth. If parties who share a common prior cannot disagree, then a junior doctor who dissents from a consultant's diagnosis is thereby irrational or never shared the consultant's prior; juries should not split; scientific minorities are always in the wrong; whistleblowers and reformers are by definition malfunctioning; two editors who have read the same manuscript and exchanged all their notes should never disagree about it, and plainly they do. And — the part that actually unsettles me — Alec cannot rationally hope to change Frances's mind, since on his own showing a rational interlocutor with a shared prior would already agree with him. The speech consumes itself. Frances can say that in half a line. I need considerably more from her than a tu quoque.

So I should be grateful for the following. First, which step you reject, and why that step looked sound enough to get past me and past Alec. Second, what each of the other two or three plausible rejections would cost: if he gives up (6), what becomes of the punchline; if he gives up (8), what does he have to say about prices instead; if he gives up (3), what exactly is he conceding about traders. Third, whether the step you reject is the same step that stops the argument from proving the absurdities about juries, consultants and editors, or whether rejecting it saves the market conclusion while leaving those intact — I want the diagnosis to earn the whole list, not only the last item. Fourth, a single paragraph of reply for Frances that is a real reply rather than a counter-assertion, in a voice a clever non-specialist could actually speak at a lunch table. Fifth, whether sentence (10) can be kept honestly in any form; I would rather keep (1), (2), (4) and (5) than keep (10), but if (10) survives in some honest version I should like to have it, because the chapter needs it.

Please answer in British English.
