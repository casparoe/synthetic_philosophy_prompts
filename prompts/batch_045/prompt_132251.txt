My dad got an early-onset Alzheimer's diagnosis a few months ago and we spent last weekend doing the paperwork: a living will where he says no tube feeding and no heroic measures once he can't recognize us, plus a durable power of attorney naming my mom as his health care agent. He was completely with it when he signed, and I get why that's supposed to matter.

But the more I read about it the more the whole thing looks like a patchwork that doesn't add up, and I don't know any of the vocabulary here, so bear with me.

Here's what bugs me. Under ordinary agency law — as my sister explained it, she's a lawyer, which is the only reason I know any of this, so correct me if she's got it wrong — an agent's authority normally dies when the principal loses capacity, and the principal can fire the agent whenever they feel like it. The "durable" part of a durable power of attorney is the exception to exactly that: it's built to survive the thing that would normaly kill it, and once Dad is incapacitated he can't take it back. So the moment he might most want to change his mind is the moment we've stripped him of the power to change his mind. That seems backwards, and I want to know what it presupposes.

And then everything next door goes the other way. As far as I can tell:

- You can bind your property for decades past your death with a trust, but you can't get a court to personally make somebody show up and do the work — specific performance, my sister says, doesn't run against a person's body or labor.
- You can't sign away your right to declare bankruptcy, no matter how clearly you meant it.
- Consent to anything medical is supposed to be giveable and withdrawable in the moment; you can't sign a form at forty that counts as your consent to something at sixty.
- Except the living will IS a form signed at sixty-two deciding what happens at seventy, and — I think? — if a confused seventy-year-old begs for a feeding tube, the hospital will still refuse him one on the strength of the earlier signature.
- My sister says psychiatric advance directives, where someone in a clear state says what to do during a psychotic break, get overridden by clinicians constantly even where they're legally valid — so the same move that's binding in dementia apparently isn't in psychosis.
- The form the hospital gave us lapses in two years unless it's re-signed, which is basically a sunset clause on Dad's authority over himself.

So: what does this patchwork presuppose? About what a person is across time, about what gives one moment's decision authority over another's, and about whether the old directive binds because the earlier person had a *right* to decide or merely because it's our best *evidence* about what the later person wants? Is there one principle that generates the whole mess — you can precommit your stuff but not your body, or precommitments are honored only where they protect your future agency rather than spend it, or only where third parties can cheaply verify compliance — or is it five unrelated historical accidents that happen to share a name? Which parts does the practice strictly require, and which are just what most people working in it happen to believe? Where is it underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would all license it? And what would actually have to change in the paperwork if we went another way: if we decided the confused seventy-year-old asking for a sandwich is simply a different customer whose current interests are what count, or the opposite, that the sixty-two-year-old's signature is authoritative full stop and needs no renewal?

People keep comparing all this to constitutional precommitment — supermajority amendment rules, unamendable clauses, the usual complaint about the dead hand of the past — and if that's the same animal, I'd like to know whether one reconstruction covers both or whether the analogy is doing me a disservice.
