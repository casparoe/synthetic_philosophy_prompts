I write novels, which mostly means inventing people's inner lives and then getting emails about them. I've now watched the same thing happen three times and I need a word for it, because I want to talk about it in an afterword and I'd rather not grab some term of art and use it wrong.

Instance one. A few years back I gave a character a very specific way of going flat — not sad exactly, more like the volume drops on everything, and she can describe it perfectly well and can't do anything about it. I got maybe forty messages from readers saying some version of: that's me, and I never had a word for it until I read it. Several said they'd gone and got diagnosed with something on the strength of it. Fine. Expected.

Instance two is the one that got under my skin. When I started the sequel I went looking for how clinicians actually describe that flat-volume thing, and the descriptions I found — a patient handout, a long Q&A on a clinic's site — use almost the vocabulary my character used: the same metaphors, the same move of insisting it isn't sadness, everything is just behind glass. I'm definately not claiming anyone read my book. It's more that my book and the clinic were both drawing on the same pool of first-person talk that had been going around in memoirs and forums for years.

So here's the shape I keep seeing. A way of describing an experience shows up first in ordinary language. It gets taken up as clinical description, because there isn't really any better evidence for what something is like from the inside. Once it's in the clinical description it gets handed back to people as the way to recognize the thing in themselves. Those recognitions come back as more first-person talk, which is the raw material for the next version of the category. Each turn the picture gets a bit sharper. The label on the box never changes, so nobody experiences the category as having moved, but the thing in the box is not the thing that was in it twenty years ago.

And the part I actually care about: the loop seems to run in different directions depending on whether anyone wants the label. Where the label is something people seek — it gets you accomodations, or it hands you the story of your own life — the loop seems to tighten: more people arrive, the descriptions converge, the category gets steadily better at catching the mild end. Where the label is something people dodge, it seems to run backwards: the people who end up carrying it are disproportionately the ones who couldn't dodge it, because a court or a school or a family dragged them in, and then the clinical picture gets built out of that group, so the typical case is a sample nobody chose, and the condition ends up looking more severe and more tangled up with trouble than it probably is.

Someone told me there's a name for the general shape of this, and I half-remember it being about the people changing. But my thing is about the picture changing, and I don't think those are the same thing.

What I need: the nearest established concepts, taken one at a time, each with where it fits what I've described and where it doesn't, and then a verdict on which one earns the right to be used in a sentence — or a straight there isn't one, plus a coinage with a definition. A friend said medicalization, but that's about turning ordinary life problems into illnesses, and another said self-fulfilling prophecy, which can't be right because nothing here fails to come true. I don't want a bare label. I want the edges: one case that's clearly inside, one that's clearly outside, and one you'd genuinely hesitate over. And if the direction thing — the loop tightening or inverting depending on whether the label is wanted — is itself a named moderator, I want that separately, because that's the bit with the teeth in it.

One more thing: I'm on deadline in a place with terrible wifi and I can't check any of this. So tell me how confident you are in each name you hand me, flag any you might be half-remembering or quietly inventing, and single out the one claim in your answer you'd be least surprised to find out is wrong.
