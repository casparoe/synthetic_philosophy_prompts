I've been reading LessWrong and the comments sections of the rationalist blogosphere since roughly the days of the Sequences, and for the past two years I've been doing unpaid literature review for a small funder that works on invertebrate welfare, which means I spend a lot of time around people who say things like "sentience comes in degrees" and "there is no bright line between a thermostat and a human." Over time I've come to hold a fairly specific version of that view myself, and I want it stress-tested rather than affirmed. Call it the continuum view: there is a single degree-valued quantity — I'll call it phenomenal integration, meaning the extent to which a system's activity at a given moment is bound into one unified perspective on its own situation rather than being a mere collection of causally coupled subprocesses — that varies continuously across systems; moral weight is a positive, increasing, and probably concave function of it; a thermostat sits at or negligibly near the bottom not because it is made of the wrong kind of stuff but because its causal organization is almost trivially simple, a fruit fly sits somewhat above it, and a human far above; and there is no sharp cutoff anywhere on that ladder because there is no further fact about where the cutoff would fall, only facts about how far along the scale any given system sits.

What I want is a map of what would count against that package, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I have some candidates I'd like you to work through rather than just list. First, the sorites from zero: if the thermostat is at exactly zero, then some system is the first to be non-zero, and I don't know what could make that true; if nothing is at exactly zero, I seem to have become a panpsychist without noticing, and my funders would like to know. Second, what I think of as the grain-of-individuation problem: how finely or coarsely do we draw the boundaries of the thing whose integration we are measuring — a whole animal, a subsystem, a colony — and can degrees be summed across individuals, so that a hive is both ten thousand bees and one somewhat-integrated colony, without my either double-counting or watching the welfare vanish as the grain shifts? Third, the fan-out problem: with any positive continuous function, enough insects should outweigh one human in aggregate, and if I make the function steeply concave or bounded in order to dodge that, I want to know whether I have quietly reinstated a de facto threshold and whether the shape of that function is now doing all the load-bearing work — I would count an unspecifiable-shape-in-advance as a genuine cost. Fourth, a case I invented and can't get rid of: imagine a model-predictive controller for a data center's cooling system that runs a learned world-model, thousands of nested feedback loops, and something structurally akin to a model of its own error signal. Is the continuum view committed to ranking it above a fruit fly, and should I treat that as a reduction I accept or as evidence against the scale? Fifth, the disaggregation ladder: as one severs integrative connections in a single subject — commissurotomy, hemispherectomy, progressive disconnection — total moral weight should slide smoothly downward on my account, but it looks at least as though one full person has become two partial persons, and I'd like to know whether the arithmetic can be made to work.

Please also tell me which of these a committed defender of the continuum view would concede as counting against it and which they would dismiss as resting on a confusion, since I don't trust the objections I've seen from people who were never tempted by the view. Include what the view predicts that a threshold view or a panpsychist view does not, and give me an honest assessment of whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability: because degree of integration is supposed to come apart from behavior, every possible observation may be compatible with every assignment of degrees, and I want to know whether that insulation is a defect here or whether a view of this kind is allowed to be empirically toothless. I should say that I would personally be most moved by arguments from the inside — ones showing that the continuum view collapses into one of its rivals, or that its central quantity is not well defined at the grain where it matters — and least moved by appeals to how mysterious consciousness is. One more thing: please write your reply as continuous prose, the way you would write an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
