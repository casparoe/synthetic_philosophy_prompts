I'm a PhD student finishing a chapter on Moore's axiology, and I've got an argument I built for my reading group that I now think proves something Moore would have refused — but I can't put my finger on the exact step where it goes wrong. The setting is the gap between the method he says must be used to settle questions of intrinsic value (the method of absolute isolation, set out at the start of the chapter on the Ideal in *Principia Ethica*) and his actual verdict that the greatest goods we can imagine are certain states of consciousness — roughly, personal affection and the appreciation of beauty — which he insists are highly complex organic unities, such that it is "these complex wholes themselves, and not any constituent or characteristic of them" that form the rational ultimate end. My worry is that the method and the verdict pull in opposite directions.

Here is the argument as I numbered it: (1) The greatest intrinsic goods, for Moore, are organic unities: wholes made up of a consciousness together with its object (the friend, the beautiful thing), where the value of the whole is not the sum of the values of the parts. (2) Moore's method fixes both which things have intrinsic value and in what degree: take the candidate thing as existing by itself, in absolute isolation, and judge the value attaching to that isolated existence; degrees of value are settled by comparing isolated existences. (3) Run the test on the object-constituent. Moore grants that the mere existence of what is beautiful has some intrinsic value, but says its value is negligible in comparison with that which attaches to the consciousness of beauty — this is his own comparison, not mine. (4) Run the test on the mental constituent. A consciousness of beauty considered in isolation from any real object seems to retain nearly all of the value; and since what is supposed to matter is what the state is intrinsically — what it would be were it alone in the universe — an intrinsically indistinguishable but hallucinatory consciousness of beauty must come out equal in intrinsic value to the genuine article. (5) So the difference in value between (a) a world containing real friendship and real appreciation of real beauty and (b) a "Moorean machine" world containing only intrinsically indistinguishable conscious states, with no real friends and no real beautiful objects anywhere in it, is at most the negligible quantity fixed in (3) — perhaps nil. (6) But Moore cannot accept (5): he says the goods are the complex wholes and not any constituent or characteristic of them, and his defence of unperceived beauty against Sidgwick — the imagined comparison between an uninhabited world of extraordinary beauty and an uninhabited heap of filth, which he insists it is rational to prefer — shows that he wants the object-side to matter more than (3) permits. (7) Nor can he retreat to comparing wholes directly, because absolute isolation only ever tests single items, and if a part's value within a context is never a function of its value alone, the method returns no verdict on any comparison between two complex wholes that differ in more than one constituent — which is every comparison Moore actually wants to make.

Which step do you reject? My own suspicion is that the damage is in the passage from (3) and (4) to (5): I have started calling that the Proxy Assumption, the assumption that a constituent's value in isolation stands in for what it contributes to the whole, and I notice that Moore himself warns, in discussing what he calls the second and more subtle error, that even where pleasure is the only constituent with any value by itself, the whole enjoyment owes its value quite equally to the presence of the other constituents. But if that is the answer, I want to know what Moore is left holding. I have tried replacing isolation with a counterfactual-subtraction test — the contribution of a constituent X to a whole W is the difference between the value of W and the value of W with X removed — and it seems to break down as well, because when you subtract the object from a case of appreciation you either leave the conscious state untouched and get a contribution of zero, which is just (5) again, or you change the state and thereby fail to hold fixed the very thing whose value you were trying to measure. One person in the group says the real rot is at (4): that a consciousness of beauty existing in absolute isolation is not a coherent object for the test at all, since an intentional state with no object is not the same state. To me that relocates the difficulty rather than solving it, but I may be wrong. So: name the step, explain why it looked sound, and price the alternatives — what does Moore lose if he gives up the Proxy Assumption, what does he lose if he gives up isolation as a procedure for ranking complex wholes, and is there any reading of the method on which it still generates his ranking of friendship and beauty above everything else? I am most attached to (1) and (6), least to (3), and I would rather keep the organic-unity doctrine than the method, if I have to choose.
