Was at a LessWrong-ish meetup on Sunday — the small one that meets above the board game cafe — and two people there got into an arguement I've been chewing on since. Everyone there had read that old embedded agency sequence, and the question on the table was a design proposal: suppose you're building an assistant agent that has to decide on its own when to escalate a hard problem to a human operator instead of grinding on it. The proposal is to give it a *shadow* — a learned, coarse, low-resolution model of its own decision procedure, trained on records of its own past episodes and readable by the agent at deliberation time — so that it reasons about its own future behavior the way it reasons about any other agent's, instead of pretending it can model itself exactly or pretending it has no model of itself at all.

It went roughly like this. (1) Sam said the shadow just smuggles the Cartesian boundary back in: the agent's model has to include the model's own updates, which have to include the model's own updates, and so on, and the whole point of the embedded framing is that there's no principled place to cut the world into agent and environment. (2) Priya said the cut doesn't have to be physical, it can be functional: you draw it wherever the agent's own next update becomes practically unpredictable to itself, and a coarse self-model is fine because coarse models are all embedded agents ever get of anything — she doesn't model her own neurons either, she models herself roughly the way she models a coworker, and that works well enough to get things done. (3) Sam said sure, but here's the specific way it breaks: the agent's plans depend on what it predicts it will do later, so there's optimization pressure on the shadow's prediction to come true — a Goodhart-ish loop run on your own self-model — and the fixed points it can settle into don't have to be good ones; the scary one is the agent that settles into being the sort of agent that never escalates, because that's the fixed point where the shadow predicts it will grind on it and grinding on it is cheaper than escalating right now. (4) Priya said that's fixable: keep the shadow deliberately stale — fit it only on episodes that finished before the current decision, with a built-in delay, and keep it low-resolution on purpose — so today's deliberation can't feed back into the thing that's predicting today's deliberation. (5) Sam said staleness buys the fix at exactly the wrong moment, since a model trained on old episodes is least reliable precisely in the novel situations where you most need to know whether you'll flail or hand off, and that underneath there's a diagonalization problem: any shadow the agent can read, the agent can act against, so no readable-and-accurate shadow is stable — Löb-flavored trouble reconstituted one level up, at the level of a learned model rather than a formal system. Priya's reply, which Sam never got to answer because we were getting kicked out of the cafe, was that this proves too much: by the same reasoning no agent could ever have accurate expectations about another agent that's good at predicting it, yet bounded agents do this all the time, because prediction is a race the predictor doesn't need to win in the limit, only soon enough and often enough — and the impossibility results Sam keeps invoking are limit results about unbounded reasoners, which is exactly what an embedded agent isn't.
