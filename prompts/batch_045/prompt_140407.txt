My book club just read Simone Weil's short essay arguing that political parties ought to be abolished outright, and I can't stop chewing on it. The way I took her argument: a party is not just a group of people who happen to agree but a machine whose real interest is its own size and Survival, so that once you're inside one, whatever helps the party starts to look like the good, thinking for yourself starts to feel like disloyalty, and the stated program is never really what moves it. Me and a friend got stuck on whether that's a structural claim about organizations or just cynicism dressed up, so I want to test it on something concrete: the German Social Democrats in 1914. As I understand it (correct me if I've got the details wrong), the SPD was the largest Marxist party in the world, had spent years through the Second International pledging that workers wouldn't fight each other in a capitalist war, and then in August 1914 its Reichstag deputies voted for war credits more or less unanimously despite real opposition inside the caucus, agreed to a no-strikes policy, and by December one deputy, Liebknecht, was voting no on his own; he was expelled from the party later and the anti-war people eventually split off into a party of their own.

Run Weil's argument over that. Who is the agent in her story — the caucus, the leadership, the union apparatus, the voters — and what options does her theory say they actually faced, as opposed to the ones they thought they faced? Where does it explain this case better than the obvious alternatives (pressure from the state, patriotic fever, plain insincerity), and where does it go quiet or get the case wrong? The bit I really can't work out: if parties always sell out their program when the program gets inconvenient, then the anti-war resolutions were themselves a party product, cheap talk that cost nothing to pass — does Weil's argument predict that, and does it have any real bite, or does it explain both the sellout and the split-and-become-irrelevant outcome, in which case is it a theory or a mood? At the end, say how confident you are in your reading overall and flag the one claim you'd least want to have to defend.
