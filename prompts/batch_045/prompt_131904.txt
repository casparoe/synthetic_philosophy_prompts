I write about behavioural science for a living, mostly the decision-making beat, and there's one argument I keep walking into at conferenes that never goes anywhere, and I want to know why it's stuck rather than who's right.

It's the argument about satisficing. Camp one, the Simon people: real choice environments are too big and too messy for optimization to even be computable, so agents carry an aspiration level, search until something clears it, adjust the level up or down depending on how the search is going, and that's the actual mechanism. Rationality is procedural, not the property of the outcome, and behaviour is explained by the fit between the shape of the mind and the shape of the environment — the scissors point, neither blade alone. Camp two, the optimization people: fine, but add a cost to searching or to thinking, write down a reservation value or an optimal stopping rule, and the satisficer comes out as a maximizer with a different budget line. And there's the bigger version of the move — if a choice pattern is coherent at all it can be represented as maximizing something or other, so satisficing isn't a rival theory of rationality, it's a vernacular name for a search policy that happens to be optimal once you price the search.

This has been going since the 1950s and nobody I interview seems to have moved a millimeter. So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it verbal, substantive, two questions wearing one name, a hidden empirical dispute about how aspiration levels actually adapt, or a disagreement about what counts as data — lab tasks where the option distribution is handed to the subject versus real environments where the agent doesn't know the distribution and an optimizer's model quietly needs a prior nobody can specify?

My hypothesis, and please knock it down if it's bad: the two sides are answering different questions, one about the mechanism that produces the stopping and one about whether the resulting behaviour can be represented as optimal relative to some cost structure, and the second question is close to empty because you can always invent a cost that makes it work. If that's right then the whole dispute turns on whether anyone can say what counts as a legitimate cost, and nobody will, because any principled answer to that seems to concede the Simon point about intractability. But I might be wrong that they're answering different questions — it might be one question where the sides just disagree about where the burden of proof sits.

Two things I'd like you to chew on rather than treat as settled background. First, I think people conflate at least three things under one word: stopping-rule satisficing (when do I end the search), acceptance satisficing (I never searched, everything is on the table, and I take the first thing that's good enough), and a third case that's getting more common and which I'd like you to name properly — the case where the consideration set is picked by someone or something else, a gatekeeper, a form with a dropdown, a recommender, an assistant that pre-filters, so the agent optimizes all right but over a menu whose threshold was set by a different agency with different incentives. Does that third case change what the dispute is even about, or is it just the same problem with an extra stage? Second: I have an impression from reporting, and it's only an impression, that people's stopping behaviour doesn't track the cost of searching in the clean way the optimal-stopping story predicts — they quit early when search is nearly free and grind on when it's expensive, apparently because the aspiration level is anchored to something else entirely. If that impression is right, does it cut against one side, or is it exactly the sort of thing the as-if move absorbs by re-specifying the cost?

Give me the diagnosis with the reasons attached, not a survey of the positions — I can write the survey myself. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether that's a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, or a change of question, and whether I should expect any progress at all. And flag how confident you are in each part, and where you're going out on a limb versus just restating the dialectic back to me. If the premise is muddled — if satisficing is three theories and I've asked one question about all three, or if there's a standard reply I've somehow never been told that dissolves the whole thing — say that plainly instead of answering politely. I'd much rather be told the question doesn't hold up.
