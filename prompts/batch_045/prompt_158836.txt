I'm in a philosophy of law seminar this term — the topic is punishment and the state — and our final deliverable is supposed to be an adversarial-collaboration document. The room has basically split into two camps: me and two others who are retributivists (desert, proportionality, the offender getting what's owed), and three people who are thoroughgoing consequentialists (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, whatever actually reduces harm). We've been arguing in circles for like eight weeks and the prof has told us to stop and produce something both sides would actually sign, so I need to draft it.

What I want: a numbered section of claims both camps can agree to — about what punishment is for, about what needs justifying in the first place, about which considerations are ruled out entirely (I assume everyone can sign something about punishing the known-innocent, but you tell me). Then a disagreements table, where each row is one issue and has: the retributivist's position phrased the way a retributivist would phrase it themselves, the consequentialist's position phrased the way they'd phrase it, the crux underneath, a test or case both sides accept as bearing on it, and — recorded in advance, before anybody says how it comes out — what each side predicts about that case. No strawman wording; if a camp wouldn't sign their own row, it's wrong.

The cases I keep pushing in seminar, and which I want used as at least some of the tests:

- The unlabelled sanction: a jurisdiction imposes exactly the same hard treatment on convicted offenders — same loss of liberty, same duration, same daily regime — but under a purely medical/public-health description, with no blame, no condemnation, no criminal record, and nobody outside the institution is told. The consequentialists in my seminar say this is fine or even better. I think it splits my own side: is retributivism a claim about what must be suffered, or about what must be communicated? Please say which, and whether that fight is real or verbal.
- The consent ledger: before any offence, a person can file a standing consent to a fixed sanction for a named offence, in exchange for some small benefit, and the sanction is then imposed without any further trial. Is desert waivable? Transferable? And what would each camp predict about whether this raises or lowers the offence rate?
- The substitution: with the offender's consent, a volunteer serves part of the sentence. Same question about transfer, plus what each side predicts about deterrence uptake.
- Sentence/conviction mismatch: the state convicts of what it can prove but calibrates the sentence to what the person actually did, and everybody knows it does this. Both camps have to say whether this is just, and what they predict it does to compliance and legitimacy.
- The last offender: one culpable offender left, no one will ever learn of the sanction, no future crimes to prevent, no one to rehabilitate toward. Both sides agree this case is diagnostic of something — say exactly what.

Also: I suspect at least one of our fights is about words, not the world — maybe whether retributivism is a claim about what's permissible, about what's required, or about what's non-instrumentally good. Say which part is verbal and why, and don't resolve it by fudging. And please don't cite anything or suggest readings — we're writing this closed-book, so everything has to come from the arguments and cases themselves.
