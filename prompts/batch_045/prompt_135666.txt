I am writing a short essay on what the cogito actually establishes, and I would like a reader before I develop it further. The draft below is closer to structured notes than to finished prose; that is deliberate. After it I list the specific kinds of response I am hoping for.

**Draft: "The punctual cogito"**

The two familiar quarrels about the cogito are whether it is an inference or a self-verifying performance, and whether its *I* denotes a thinking substance or merely the present thinker of the present thought. I want to set both aside and ask a different question: for how long does the certainty last?

My thesis is that the certainty is *punctual* — it belongs to the act of thinking the thought and to nothing else — and that this is not a curiosity about the cogito but the reason the foundation cannot bear any weight. I assume, for the sake of argument, the most favourable reading available to Descartes: that the cogito is not a syllogism with a suppressed major premise, since in the Meditations he himself insists that the certainty is not reached by inference, and that each act of conceiving it is self-standing. My objection is meant to bite even on that reading.

Distinguish three grades of certainty. **(a) Token-certainty.** Whenever I actually think 'I am thinking now', the occurrence of the act guarantees its truth. It is self-verifying; no further premise is needed, and the demon cannot make it false, only unperformed. **(b) Retention-certainty.** At a later time I know that I performed the act earlier, and that it was certain. **(c) Type-certainty.** I know in general that any performance of the act is veridical — that thinking entails existing, always, and not only while I am at it.

The cogito gives (a). I claim it gives neither (b) nor (c), and that Descartes needs both.

*Why (a) does not give (b).* A foundation must be available for reuse: when a later step is checked against it, I must be able to return to it and find it as I left it. But a punctual certainty can only be re-created, never revisited. If I perform the act again at t₂, what I get is a fresh token-certainty that I exist at t₂. Nothing in that performance certifies that the t₁ performance occurred, or that it was certain: memory is exactly as vulnerable to the demon as any other faculty, and the new performance has no more authority over my apparent memories than it has over my apparent perceptions. The earlier performance is therefore evidentially inert. Call this the no-return problem.

*Why (a) does not give (c).* The move from one performance to a general principle is a generalization, and generalizations need a premise. Either the premise is inductive — it presupposes retained instances and the uniformity of nature, both demon-vulnerable — or it is a priori, known by the same clear and distinct intuition whose reliability is precisely what the Cartesian circle is about. So type-certainty is either not foundational or not yet available.

*The deeper reason: inferences have thickness.* Any step of reasoning takes time. A premise must survive until the conclusion is drawn. But a punctual certainty has, so to speak, zero duration: no sooner is the inference under way than the premise has decayed into a retention or into an intuition held in memory, and retention is a different epistemic item with a different epistemic status. So a foundation made entirely of punctual certainties cannot support a multi-step argument — not because it is too weak, but because it is too short. Nor does it help to re-run the cogito at every step. Re-running requires either that each step be instantaneous, which no step of reasoning is, or that the result of the previous run be carried forward, which is retention all over again.

*What the I contributes.* Suppose a system whose successive conscious episodes belong to numerically distinct subjects, each of which sincerely performs the cogito in its turn. Every performance is self-verifying; no subject ever performs it twice; and nothing guarantees a subject persisting across the sequence. If the certainty survives intact in that case, then it establishes nothing about a persisting self, and the *I* does no work beyond the indexical present thinker. But Descartes needs a persisting subject: the real distinction, and the claim that the mind is better known than the body, are claims about a thing that endures through change — and endurance is exactly what a punctual certainty cannot reach.

*Upshot.* Perhaps the cogito was never a foundation, in the sense of a proposition on which a science could be built. Perhaps it exhibits what certainty would be like, and is meant to be re-performed rather than stored: the Meditations as a training in an act rather than a proof of a thesis. The skeptic's demand — produce a proposition that is certain — would then be a demand of the wrong shape. But for the same reason, the project the Meditations are supposed to serve is in trouble, since science is made of propositions, not of acts.
