Don't explain the standard of taste to me, I've read it and I sort of get it. What I need is a plan for how to get *them* to it, and I keep freezing up on the sequencing.

Here's the knot, as I see it: the essay seems to pull both ways at once — beauty is a sentiment in us, not a quality sitting in the object, and yet some verdicts are genuinely correct and others aren't, and we argue about taste in a way we don't argue about whether we like broccoli. Our seminar handout splits the readings into what it calls the consensus reading (the standard is just what qualified people converge on) and the instrument reading (the true judge is a well-tuned measuring device, and the standard is what such a device registers) — my professor keeps saying Hume is much closer to calibration than to voting, and my roommate, who's an art history major, says that's a distinction without a difference and Hume is a relativist with extra steps. I don't need you to settle that for the essay, I need to know what has to be in place in their heads *before* it can even be a live question, and in what order. And what should carry the whole section: Hume's own bit about the wine tasters, or something out of their own world (a song they all hated on first listen and love now, a building everyone now calls beautiful that was panned when it went up, a game with ugly graphics that fans find gorgeous)? Pick one for me and say what such an example has to have, and what it has to *not* have, to bear the weight of the hour — and why Hume's wine story might be the wrong one to build on even though it's right there in the text.
