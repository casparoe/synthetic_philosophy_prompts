I'm working on a piece on few-shot concept learning -- the Bongard-problem revival, whether vision-language models can really induce a rule from twelve little pictures. I made up a problem in the classic format, six figures on the left and six on the right, and put it to two people I interviewed separately. They ended up arguing with each other over email and cc'ing me, and I honestly cant tell who's winning.

The figures: on the left, every box has two polygons, one drawn big and one small, with the small one sitting strictly inside the big one -- big hexagon with a small triangle, big pentagon with a small square, big square with a small triangle, big hexagon with a small square, big pentagon with a small triangle, big hexagon with a small pentagon. On the right, six boxes with two polygons drawn side by side and not touching -- big triangle with a small hexagon, big square with a small pentagon, big triangle with a small pentagon, big square with a small hexagon, big triangle with a small square, big pentagon with a small hexagon.

I also sketched two extra figures and asked how each should be classified: (a) a big triangle with a small hexagon tucked strictly inside it, and (b) a big square with a small pentagon straddling the square's edge, half in and half out. They disagreed about both.

The exchange, more or less as it happened:

(1) Wes: the rule is about number of sides. On the left the polygon with more sides is always the bigger one; on the right the polygon with more sides is always the smaller one. That flips cleanly on all twelve. If the intended rule were just containment, whoever built the set would have had to accidentally reverse the side-count relation six times running in each panel -- that's a 1-in-4096 coincidence. The confound is the tell about how the set was generated.

(2) Marta: that coincidence isn't much of a coincidence. Try drawing a hexagon big enough to see inside a triangle at those proportions, it looks like garbage, so any batch of 'inside' figures will tend to have the rounder, more-sided shape on the outside. Your number assumes the shapes were picked independently, and they weren't. Besides, containment is one relation between two regions; your rule needs you to count sides, compare the counts, and compare sizes. Simplicity is the standard tiebreak here and containment wins it.

(3) Wes: fine, then take a third rule that also fits all twelve -- some or all of the small shape lies inside the big one (left) versus none of it does (right). That's about as simple as your containment rule, and it disagrees with yours on exactly the straddling figure (b). So your tiebreak doesn't pick a winner either. The item is underdetermined by its own data, and the honest answer is that somebody needs to draw more figures.

(4) Marta: 'underdetermined' is true of every induction problem you can name -- infinitely many rules fit any finite sample -- so as a verdict its empty. What's actually interesting is which rules are fragile and where they're fragile. A model that answers containment and also flags that a sides-reversed figure like (a) or a straddling figure like (b) would break it has learned something real about the problem; a model that just throws up its hands and says 'ambiguous, I refuse' has learned nothing, and I don't see how you'd score that as success.

Can you go through the four moves in order and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each -- who's right about what, not just who wins? Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. I need something sharper than 'the problem is underdetermined' for the last paragraph of the piece.
