I write historical fiction, and a scene in my current draft turns on a question I have not been able to settle for myself. In the novel, a vessel was left by a nineteenth-century will to "the ship Theseus and to whoever is her master," and there are now two candidates: the vessel still afloat, which has been continuously maintained and every plank of which has by now been replaced, and a second vessel built in a neighbouring yard out of all the original timber. The scene needs one of my characters to win the argument on the merits, not by rhetoric.

So I asked two people who know more about this than I do — a conservator at a maritime museum and a retired naval architect — and instead of helping me they spent an evening arguing with each other. I have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can. Both were making points I could not refute on the spot, which is exactly my problem.

(1) The conservator opened with the material view: a ship is her fabric, and a vessel with none of her original timber is a different vessel — a replica that happens to have had a long career. Continuity of name and use, she said, is a matter of sentiment and paperwork, not of what the thing is.

(2) The naval architect replied that this gets ordinary repair wrong: a ship is precisely the kind of thing that persists by being maintained, and if replacing a tenth of her planking preserves her, then so does replacing the rest, one plank at a time. What identifies her is the continuous history of a working vessel — the same hull lines, the same name, the same crew's habits, the same run of voyages.

(3) The conservator then raised the reassembly case: if the discarded planks are carefully stored and afterwards put back together into a hull of the same design, the material view and the continuity view seem to collide — the continuity theorist has a claim on the vessel afloat, the material theorist on the reassembled one, and there cannot have been two ships where there was one.

(4) The architect bit one bullet: the reassembled hull is a new ship made of old timber, because it had a gap — for years the planks were a pile in a shed and not a ship at all. Persistence, he said, requires uninterrupted shiphood; a thing that stops being a ship and later becomes one again is a successor, not a survivor.

(5) The conservator then produced a case I have not seen in any version of the puzzle, and it is the one that stopped me. Suppose the refit is done side by side: the first plank comes out on Monday and is fitted the same afternoon into a hull being built on the adjacent slipway; the second comes out on Tuesday and is fitted that evening; and so on through all nine hundred planks, so that on the day the last plank leaves the old hull, the new one is complete. Nothing is ever a heap. The second vessel is never disassembled, never idle in a shed, never a mere collection of timber: it is under continuous construction from first plank to last, and it ends up with every bit of the original matter and — the yard having worked from the original moulds — the same lines. Now, she said, the no-gap test is satisfied on both sides, and it can only separate them by asking when a growing hull becomes a ship, which looks like a stipulation rather than a discovery. Worse: at the midpoint of the refit the two vessels are mirror images, each half original and half new, and your account owes me an answer about which one is Theseus's ship on that day.

(6) The architect shifted ground rather than conceded: what settles it, he said, is not matter or an intrinsic threshold but the social and institutional career — the registry entry, the name on the stern, the family that has sailed her, the chain of custody from the original launch. Identity for artefacts like this is constituted by continuity of use and recognition, not by any one plank.

(7) The conservator pressed two objections. First: identity cannot come in degrees, but every one of your criteria — form, function, use, recognition — does come in degrees, so you have quietly stopped talking about identity and started talking about something graded and call it the same thing. Second: if it is really a matter of what the relevant community holds, then two communities can disagree — the museum and the family that sails her — and there is nothing left for them to be right or wrong about.

(8) The architect granted the first point and re-described his position: he is not claiming strict identity at all. There are several legitimate continuance relations here, he said, and which one matters is interest-relative — insurance and title on one reading, commemoration and display on another — and the phrase 'the same ship' is shorthand for whichever relation serves the purpose in hand.

(9) The conservator said that if that is so, then the bequest in my scene cannot be decided on the merits at all: the judge is not discovering which vessel is Theseus's ship, only choosing a convention, and either verdict would be equally arbitrary.

(10) The architect's reply was that choosing is not the same as being arbitrary: the practice exists for reasons, the will was written for a purpose, and a decision can be better or worse relative to that purpose even if the metaphysics is indeterminate — my scene, he said, is decidable; it is just not decidable by doing metaphysics.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the ten moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why — and if either of them is relying on a premise that is false or on a distinction that does no work, name it. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and give me your overall verdict on who has the better of the argument. One further thing I need for the book: say whether the answer a court or a maritime register would give should be expected to track the philosophical one, or whether the two can come apart, and if so where.

Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the move-by-move reasoning; no preamble and no restating the question, and please keep it as plain as the subject allows.
