I work as a software engineer in the United States, with no formal training in philosophy, economics, or health policy. I am trying to work out what I actually think about where the line falls between medicine as a public guarantee and medicine as a market good, and I do not trust myself at the level of slogans. So here are my gut verdicts on a set of concrete cases. I would like you to reconstruct the principle, if there is one, that I am implicitly applying; tell me where it forces me to give up a verdict; and then run it on several cases I have not settled.

1. Insulin and continuous glucose monitoring for a type 1 diabetic: covered, free at the point of use, no question.
2. A two-million-dollar one-time gene therapy for a fatal disease affecting roughly fifteen infants a year: covered. I understand this fails any cost-per-quality-adjusted-life-year test by a wide margin, and I still think we pay.
3. A drug that buys about three additional weeks of life for eighty thousand dollars in late-stage cancer: covered, but with noticeably less conviction than in case 2.
4. Bariatric surgery for someone whose type 2 diabetes is downstream of two decades of obesity: covered. I notice that I do not want to apply any discount for self-causation here.
5. Routine dental cleaning and periodontal treatment: covered.
6. A second IVF cycle after the first has failed: covered. I would cap the number of cycles at some point, but the cap feels administrative rather than principled.
7. Laser eye surgery for someone who simply prefers not to wear glasses: not covered, and I am untroubled by their buying it.
8. Cosmetic rhinoplasty: not covered. Fine to buy.
9. An unproven stem-cell treatment for chronic back pain: not covered, and I would prefer it not be sold at all.
10. The same unproven treatment, with the same risk profile, for someone with months to live who has exhausted every approved option: I want to say yes. This sits badly with case 9 and I know that it does.
11. Paying people for plasma: fine. Paying them for a kidney: not fine, and it bothers me that I cannot say why in one sentence. Reimbursing a surrogate for lost wages and medical costs: fine. Paying her a fee for the pregnancy itself: not fine.
12. A public hospital selling a private-room upgrade: fine. A public hospital selling a shorter wait for an MRI to whoever pays: not fine. A concierge practice in which you pay an annual retainer for same-day access to your own physician: fine. That third verdict is very close to the second and I cannot articulate the difference.
13. Five hundred thousand dollars for a named two-year-old's surgery, publicized, donations pouring in: I feel the pull very strongly. Five hundred thousand dollars for a program that would statistically prevent thirty deaths from carbon-monoxide poisoning in substandard housing: I judge this to be the better use of the money, and I would probably still open my wallet for the child. I would like a principle that says something about that gap rather than one that declares my gut irrational and stops there.

The best I have managed on my own is this: the public system covers whatever restores or maintains normal species-typical functioning; markets handle anything that is enhancement, comfort, or cosmetic; and the pull of an identifiable victim in extremis is carved out as a separate budget line that I admit is not justified by the rest of the principle. I do not think this survives contact with my own list. Cases 5 and 6 are neither restoration of function nor rescue. The split between 9 and 10 seems to be about hope and desperation rather than functioning. Case 11 is not about functioning at all. And case 12 is not about which services exist but about what money is permitted to buy access to inside a system that is supposed to treat people equally.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts or nearly does? Please state it precisely enough that I could turn it into an eligibility rule rather than a platitude; tell me which clause is doing the work on which case; tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me overturn; and say what it predicts about the cases I have not settled: a GLP-1 weight-loss drug prescribed to someone who is obese and prediabetic, versus the same drug for someone who wants to lose fifteen pounds before a wedding; gender-affirming surgery; an AI diagnostic system that outperforms the average radiologist, where the question is whether access to it belongs in the guaranteed package or is a paid upgrade; and weekly talk therapy for mild, non-clinical distress versus for severe depression. Finally, I would like your view on the question I keep dodging in case 12: whether a service that is publicly guaranteed may also be privately purchasable in a faster or better-funded tier, or whether allowing both tiers is itself the thing my verdicts should have ruled out.
