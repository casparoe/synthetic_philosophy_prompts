I've been working through the arrow of time with a friend, and the blog-level treatments of the Past Hypothesis left both of us unsatisfied, so we tried to build the argument ourselves. I've ended up with something whose conclusion I cannot accept and whose steps I cannot break. I'd like a diagnosis: which step goes, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere?

Set-up, which I would rather not have to give up but am happy to have challenged: grant, for the sake of the argument, that the microdynamics is time-reversal invariant. Then the fine-grained Gibbs entropy of a closed system is constant along the dynamics, so whatever the second law is about, it is not that quantity.

(1) What the second law is about is Boltzmann entropy: a function of the volume of the region of phase space in which the system's state sits, where that region is picked out by a coarse-graining, i.e. a partition of the state space into macrostates.

(2) The microdynamics does not hand you a partition. Which microstates count as the same macrostate is not read off from the Hamiltonian.

(3) So the entropy of a system at a time, and the sign of the entropy difference between two times, is a function of a history together with a partition — not of the history by itself. Two partitions can disagree about the sign along exactly the same sequence of states. Trivially: time-reverse any partition and you invert the gradient along the same trajectory. Less trivially: consider whether the Big Bang is a low-entropy state at all. Relative to the macro-variables we actually observe — the density, temperature and composition of the plasma and of the radiation field, whose near-perfect blackbody spectrum is the best-confirmed thermal fact we have — the early universe was as close to thermal equilibrium, and hence to maximum entropy, as anything we have ever measured. It looks staggeringly special only once gravitational degrees of freedom are counted: the clumping into stars, galaxies and black holes is where nearly all of the entropy produced since now sits. And there is no generally agreed way to assign an entropy to the gravitational field locally. So whether there is even a Past Hypothesis to state looks partition-relative.

(4) The only non-question-begging constraint anyone has offered on which partition is the right one is something like naturalness: the partition's macro-variables should obey approximately closed, simple, projectible evolution laws, with no gerrymandered, disjunctive or time-indexed cells.

(5) Here is the part I invented, and I want it treated as a possibility rather than waved away. Suppose we were to find a second partition — call it the shadow coarse-graining — that satisfies the naturalness constraint in (4) at least as well as ours does, whose macro-variables are as simply specifiable and as smooth as ours, and relative to which the Big Bang is a maximum-entropy state, entropy has been falling ever since, and the thermodynamic gradient points the other way. Suppose too that nothing we can observe distinguishes the two: the disagreement is invisible from the inside.

(6) Then there is no partition-free fact about which way entropy increases. The direction of the gradient is a feature of a description, not of the world — the way whether two distant events are simultaneous is a feature of a frame.

(7) But the other arrows do not look like that. That I have records of yesterday and none of tomorrow; that I can affect tomorrow and not yesterday; that radiation diverges from sources rather than converging onto them; that I am answerable for what I do later today. None of these seems to be settled by how anyone labels cells of phase space. So if they reduce to the thermodynamic arrow, they reduce to a convention; if they do not reduce to it, the reductionist program that everyone advertises was never doing its job.

(8) So either there is no fact of the matter about the direction of time, or the reduction of the other arrows to the thermodynamic one is not the explanation it is taken to be.

I cannot accept (8), and the reasoning also seems to prove too much: by the same logic, any claim about the time-profile of any coarse-grained quantity — that the pressure in my oven is rising, that a gas is equilibrating — would lack a partition-free truth value, and I do not believe that about pressure. If that really is where the argument leads, it has proved far more than that time lacks a direction.

Which step do you reject? I would most like to keep (1), since the Boltzmannian account is what makes the second law something you derive rather than assert, and (7). My suspicion is that the live choice is between rejecting (3) and rejecting (5), but I genuinely cannot tell whether the trouble here is a premise that is false or an inference that only looks valid, and I cannot see what either option commits me to downstream. If you think (5) is incoherent, say why — and say what it costs to lean on that incoherence, because any partition that can support agents or records must agree about the sign sounds to me like it turns the arrow into a selection effect, and I do not know whether that is a defense of the standard view or a capitulation. And if you think the relativity in (6) is real but harmless, I want to know whether it is harmless in the way a choice of frame is harmless or only in the way a choice of units is harmless, because those cash out very differently for (7).

For each step you might give up, please say both why it looked strong enough to get into the argument in the first place and what else I would have to accept or abandon if I gave it up.
