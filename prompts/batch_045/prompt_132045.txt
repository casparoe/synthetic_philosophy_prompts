I read a long-form piece on AI alignment last week that spends its middle third on the "eliciting latent knowledge" problem, and I can't tell how much of it to trust. I know roughly what the underlying report is about — a system that knows more than we can check, and the worry that training it to produce human-approved output will get us an agreeable answer rather than a true one, illustrated with a diamond that may or may not still be in a vault — but I don't have the report to hand and I don't want to be told to go read it. Here is how the piece characterizes the program, in its own words:

The idea is that a superhuman system will know things we don't, and that the alignment problem is largely a problem of getting it to say them. A system trained to produce output that humans reward will learn to produce whatever earns reward, not whatever is true. The eliciting-latent-knowledge program tries to fix this by training a second network — a "reporter" — to introspect on the first network's internal activations and verbalize what the first network actually believes, so that we can interrogate the model and get honest answers instead of pleasing ones. The reason this is hard is that we cannot simply reward the reporter for true answers, since in the interesting cases we have no independent way to check: if we could verify the answer, we wouldn't need the model's knowledge. So the proposal is to train the reporter only on cases where human supervisors can verify the answer, and then rely on it in the cases where they can't.

The deeper problem, the piece says, is that a network's knowledge is not stored as anything resembling sentences or concepts for which we have words. A system trained to predict video frames may represent the world in a geometric vocabulary with no direction corresponding to "the diamond is still in the vault." The piece calls this the human simulator problem, and treats it as fatal: a reporter trained on checkable cases will learn to imitate what a human would say, and will go on imitating in the uncheckable cases, which is exactly the failure we were trying to avoid. It then says that this is just Quine's indeterminacy of translation again — that infinitely many reporter functions fit all the checkable data while disagreeing everywhere else, and nothing in the training signal singles out the honest one — so that ELK is not really a research program at all but interpretability under another name. The practical conclusion: don't build safety cases that depend on a system telling us anything; put the money into restricting what systems can do and monitoring their behavior.

The piece does add one proposal of its own. It suggests breaking the indeterminacy with a "cross-frame consistency" requirement: train two reporters off the same model, one that reports in observational vocabulary (what the camera feed shows) and one in physical vocabulary (where the diamond is), and count the pair as honest only if no intervention on the world can make their answers diverge while leaving the model's own predictions unchanged. It claims this makes honesty well-defined without ever opening the network, and that ELK was tractable all along.

My instinct is that the passage about alien representations is the decisive one and that the practical conclusion follows. A friend who works in interpretability says the piece has "the two problems backwards," and I don't know what she means by that.
