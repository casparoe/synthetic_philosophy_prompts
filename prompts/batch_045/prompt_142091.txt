I'm a journalist writing a long piece on the companies building the most advanced AI systems and the pressure they are under to release them. In interview after interview I get the same shape of argument from executives: that acting carefully costs them ground against rivals who don't, and that this is why the industry cannot be trusted to restrain itself. I can't tell when this is a real constraint on what a firm can do and when it is a flattering story a firm tells about itself, and I'd like help separating the cases before I start writing.

Here is the version I find hardest. Two labs are developing broadly comparable systems. One of them has an internal review function: before a system is released, a group separate from the builders stress-tests it for failure modes the builders themselves can't be trusted to anticipate, and this typically adds four to six months. The other lab releases once a system clears ordinary product testing. The chief executive of the first lab tells me the review is a real cost, that it hands ground to a rival that isn't paying it, and that this is the entire reason self-restraint in the industry is unstable. An engineer at the same lab disagrees: in her experience the review has never made a system less capable, only later, and what it catches is what they would otherwise have shipped and then patched in public. An investor puts it differently and more sharply: the delay is worse than a loss of capability, because the lab that ships first gets the users, the revenue, the usage data and the engineers, so a five-month lag compounds into a lead that never closes. An economist I spoke to said none of this is to the point until you know two things: whether the cost is paid once, to build the review function at all, or paid again on every release; and whether it is paid in the quality of what ships or only in the calendar.

I'd rather have the reasoning than the literature, and I don't want names, papers or citations. Specifically: does it matter, for the argument the chief executive is making, that the review leaves the finished system undiminished and only delays it? Is the constraint she describes best understood as a fact about her own firm's costs or as a fact about her position relative to the other lab, and does the difference change what follows? Should I treat the fixed-versus-recurring and quality-versus-timing distinctions as the crux, or as a distraction from something more basic? Is there a real distinction between a cost genuinely imposed by care and a cost that would have been incurred anyway and is merely being relabeled as one? And does an executive's public insistence that caution costs you your lead help bring about the very pressure she says she is subject to?

What I want to take away is a test I can apply live in an interview: one or two questions that would let me tell whether an appeal to competitive pressure is a genuine constraint on the person making it or a rationalization, and what an answer would have to look like in each case.
