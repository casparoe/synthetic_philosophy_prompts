Hey — I need help with a speech, and it's one of those things that sounds like a joke until you're the one holding the microphone.

I'm deputy clerk for a town of about nine hundred people, which means I get volunteered for things. On Saturday we're opening the new boardwalk at Kellner's Lake — tiny, deep, no oxygen at the bottom, so the mud at the bottom is laid down in visible annual layers, one per year, undisturbed, going back centuries. A group of stratigraphers has been coring it for a decade; there's a plutonium fallout spike in the layers from the early fifties and a pollen change just above it, and for a while it was one of the candidate sites for the "golden spike" — the one place on earth you point at and say: this is where the new interval starts. That whole proposal got voted down in 2024 by the stratigraphy people, so officially we're all still Holocene. We still got the boardwalk.

The audience is going to be roughly: a hundred and fifty townspeople including a grade five class and the volunteer fire department; four or five of the geologists, who flew in for it and are being very polite; members of the neighbouring First Nation, whose territory the lake is on and whose reserve is across the bay, and who have been saying for years that a boundary date in the 1950s quietly writes out everything that happened to them starting in the 1700s; the regional councillor whose money paid for the boardwalk; and my own family, including my grandfather, who put thirty-one years into the smelter forty kilometres upwind — the metal particulates from that smelter are in the same core, a few centimetres up from the plutonium.

Here's why I actually need help. The plaque isn't engraved yet — that's happening in the spring — and I've been asked to close the ceremony by saying what I think it should say. Out loud, in front of all those people, most of whom disagree with each other. So the philosophical thing isn't decoration, it's the whole job: the speech has to take a position on a date and defend it, and there are a bunch of things I can't do. I can't say science has settled it, because it hasn't. I can't say it's all just a story we tell so any date is as good as another, because that insults everyone in the room including the geologists. I can't do a "we respectfully acknowledge" paragraph that folds the Nation's objection in as flavouring and then keeps the 1950s date anyway — and I obviously can't speak for them either. I can't make it a doom speech, and I can't make it a blame speech, not with my grandfather standing there and half the room working shift rotation.

The two ideas I keep going around on, if they're useful to you: the date where the evidence is cleanest isn't the date when the thing you actually care about began — those never line up, and choosing one over the other quietly changes what the monument is even claiming. And the second one, which I think matters more here: every other boundary in the geological time scale is drawn by looking at what's on top of it, but we're drawing the bottom line of a period whose top hasn't happened yet, so whoever picks the date is picking it blind. Which makes me think the real question isn't "when did it start" but "who has to live under the answer" — a date on a plaque in a town that still works in the mine isn't a fact about mud, it's a claim about who the next fifty years are supposed to answer to.

Can you write it? Six minutes, outdoors, kids horsing around at the back, barbecue afterwards, so no jargon and no lecturing — but I'd like it to end with an actual proposed wording for the plaque, and I'd like it to be the kind of wording a person could stand behind without pretending the argument is over.
