I'm a second-year PhD student, and this term I'm the TA for a seminar on skepticism; the session I lead next week is on Moore's lecture "Proof of an External World," the argument where he holds up his hands and concludes that there are at least two things in space external to our minds, insisting that this is a perfectly rigorous proof because he knows the premises and the conclusion follows from them, even though the argument cannot convince anyone who does not already accept its first premise. In preparing it I got into an argument with a friend who works on the psychology of memory, and I have not been able to work out whether her analogy is devastating or irrelevant. She asks me to imagine the parallel "proof" against a skeptic about the past: I remember my eighth birthday, and I remember eating breakfast this morning, and from the mere fact that I remember such things it follows that there was a past and that my memory is not globally unreliable. Our seminar is split. Half of the students think the parallel shows the hand argument is worthless — if the memory version is obviously a manoeuvre rather than a proof, then so is the original, and the original only looks respectable because we are all already on its side. The other half think the parallel shows only that "proof" is the wrong word for what Moore is doing, and that what Moore is really offering is something like a methodological stance: that our ordinary certainties enjoy a default authority that no philosophical argument can overturn, and that the appearance of a proof is incidental. I want to know what the comparison actually establishes, and I would rather have your own analysis than a reading list, since I have plenty of reading and no clear view.

So please compare the two arguments carefully, in a form I could turn into a two-page handout for my students. I'd suggest setting both out first with their premises made explicit, since I think the structures are less similar than they look: the move from the existence of hands to the existence of external objects needs a bridge about what hands are, while "I remember that p" is arguably factive in a way that "I see a hand" is not, and in each case the skeptic has an obvious retreat to a weaker premise about how things seem. Then take up, at least, the following three questions. First: Moore's own answer to the charge that he has not proved his first premise is that he knows it without being able to prove it — does that answer have the same force in the memory case, where one might think the premise's status depends on the very faculty under suspicion? Second: grant, for the sake of argument, that memory is reconstructive and frequently confabulates, which is a kind of first-order evidence of unreliability that has no analogue in the perceptual case; does that asymmetry make the memory argument worse than Moore's, or does it show that such evidence is simply beside the point for arguments of this shape? Third: the memory skeptic can add the hypothesis that the world, along with all my apparent memories, came into existence five minutes ago, which threatens not just the premises of the argument but the activity of arguing at all, since running an inference requires retaining a premise from one step to the next — is that a liability for the memory-style proof, or a sign that the skeptic has sawed off the branch she is sitting on, and is there an analogous self-undermining worry available against the dream skeptic? Close with a verdict: which of the two arguments, if either, does better against its skeptic, and what does the answer tell us about what a Moorean argument can be — a proof, a shifting of the burden of argument, an expression of a commitment to common sense, or something else? About twelve hundred words, please, and feel free to disagree with both halves of my seminar.
