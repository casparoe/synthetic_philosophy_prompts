I am the principal policy adviser to a statutory commission on cultural and religious pluralism. Two or three times a month we receive a submission from a minority community asking us to recommend that the law accommodate one of its practices — language schooling, customary family law, religious arbitration, communal land tenure, ritual objects. Our recommendations are the basis of the report the ministry legislates on, and they have to be drafted so that they fit inside a constitutional charter of individual rights and a set of anti-discrimination statutes. That means every submission is restated, before it reaches the commissioners, as a claim about individuals: what a person may do, or what burden a person must not be made to suffer. I have become convinced that this restatement is not a neutral transcription, and I want to know whether the thing I am seeing has been identified and named.

Two recent submissions made it plain to me.

A linguistic minority asked for public funding for its own-language schools and for legal recognition of its own marriage customs. Its stated reason was about persistence: if the next generation is educated in the majority language, there will be no one left to be a member of this people in two generations, and that disappearance is itself the harm to be prevented. By the time the paper reached the commissioners, the claim read "members of the minority have a right to education in their own language." Nothing in that sentence carries the interest in persistence, and part of it is in tension with it: a right belongs to a person and may be waived, exit must remain open, and the number of people who exercise it is irrelevant to whether the right exists.

A religious community asked that its internal dispute resolution be given legal effect. Its reason was that adjudication is partly constitutive of the community: a community that brings its disputes to the state's courts has, in its own terms, already stopped being the community it is. Translated, the claim became "individual members have a right of access to religious arbitration." The predictable objection — what of the member who would rather go to the state courts, or who is under family pressure — then bites, and the community cannot answer it, because the option of exit for each individual is precisely what threatens the practice it is asking us to protect.

The pattern, as I have come to see it, has several parts. Claims whose content was already individualistic — a person wants to wear a particular garment, wants a dietary accommodation — pass through almost unchanged. Claims about the persistence of a form of life come out the other side as claims about individual options, having lost the part the claimants said mattered. Claimants and their representatives learn this, and after a few years the persistence reason stops appearing in our records altogether; a later commission reading only those records would conclude that these communities never had such an interest. And in at least one case the restatement struck me as an improvement rather than a loss: the original submission bundled in a demand that the community be able to sanction members who marry outside it, and the rights frame correctly stripped that out — but it stripped out the non-coercive part of the same claim along with it.

I have tried the obvious labels and they do not fit. Hermeneutical injustice seems wrong, because the vocabulary is not missing: the claimants have the words and use them in their own setting; the difficulty is that our forms have no line for them. It is not a credibility problem either — no one doubts what they say about themselves. A colleague suggested that what I am describing is simply the way institutions render things "legible" in order to act on them, but in the accounts of that I have seen, the loss is one of detail and measurability, whereas here what disappears is the point of the claim, and the claimants are the ones doing the simplifying. I have also seen liberal theorists argue that group protections are defensible when what is at stake is the cultural framework individuals need in order to exercise choice — but that justification still puts the individual's options at the centre and treats the persistence of the group as a by-product, which is the substitution I am trying to describe rather than an answer to it.

So my question is not "what is this called." I want to know what the phenomenon is. Specifically:

1. Is there an established concept in political theory, legal theory, or the philosophy of language for the deformation of a claim by the institutional vocabulary it must pass through, where what is systematically lost is the part about the persistence of a form of life and what survives is the part about individual options? If there is, characterise it precisely enough that I can tell which of my cases fall under it and how far its ordinary use extends. If there is not, give me a term and a definition tight enough to use in a memorandum, together with the conditions a submission must meet to count as an instance.

2. How is it to be distinguished, case by case, from its neighbours: from ordinary strategic reframing, where a claimant says what will work and loses nothing; from a genuine clarification, where the restatement makes a confused claim precise; from the familiar liberal objection that a claim on behalf of a group is really a claim to control its members; and from the general costs of administering any rule at all?

3. What would let my commission detect the loss in a live case — what test we could apply to a submission, or what question we could put to the claimants, that would show whether the restatement has deleted something rather than merely restated it? I would rather have something we can run in a morning, by people with no training in the relevant theory, than something that requires us to become theorists.

4. Whether it is a reason to change our intake procedure at all, or only a reason to be candid about what our procedure cannot register. I am open to being told that the loss is unavoidable given the charter we operate under, but I want that said explicitly, with what it costs us.

Be direct about which parts of your answer are established usage and which are your own proposal. I am not asking for references, but I will be quoting this to commissioners, and I do not want to present a coinage as settled terminology.
