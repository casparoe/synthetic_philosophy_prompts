In a reading group on AI alignment, one member leaned on an analogy I keep hearing: inferring human preferences from behavior is really just detective work. Human behavior is the evidence left at the scene; the reward function is the suspect's motive; the model's assumption that people act approximately optimally (the softmax-over-value likelihood everyone puts in these models) is the detective's assumption that the culprit is rational; and just as enough clues let a detective converge on a single motive, enough behavioral data will converge on the true reward. He used this to argue that preference inference is mainly a data-collection problem, citing a demo in which a system watched forty hours of in-car video of one driver and reported that she weights arriving on time more heavily than minor traffic violations, and he added that whatever ambiguity remains is just ordinary scientific underdetermination, which we already know how to resolve with a simplicity prior over reward functions.

I want to know how much work the analogy is doing. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the mapping stated as compact correspondences separated by semicolons — what plays the role of the clue, the motive, the suspect, the case file, and the verdict; (2) the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion, as distinct from cosmetic ones; (3) whether the convergence claim survives, given what I half-remember — that any finite behavioral record is consistent with many reward functions, and that two rewards differing by a term that depends only on the state can make exactly the same behavior optimal — and whether either point bites once the environment itself changes; (4) whether the Occam's-razor rider actually helps, or whether a simplicity prior over reward functions is sensitive to how rewards are parameterized in a way that simplicity in ordinary science is not; (5) a better analogy if one is available, or a note on what no analogy can carry here.
