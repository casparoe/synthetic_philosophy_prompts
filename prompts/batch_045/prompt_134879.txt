I teach a 90-minute seminar in formal epistemology, 18 students, all of whom have had intro probability and a bit of decision theory but none of whom have seen interval-valued credences. I want an in-class game where they run into the following before anybody names it: conditionalizing a *set* of probabilities can leave you strictly less precise about an event you currently have a perfectly sharp credence in, and you can see it coming in advance and it still happens.

Here's the mechanism Ive built, and my arithmetic is shaky so check it and fix the marble counts if they don't do what I think (keep them as whole marbles out of 100, and tell me what you changed and why):

Two opaque bags, 100 marbles each, four colours.
Bag P: 30 red, 30 blue, 20 green, 20 yellow.
Bag Q: 10 red, 50 blue, 30 green, 10 yellow.
I pick one bag by a method written on a card in a sealed envelope that never gets opened, and I don't tell them which and I won't. So all they know is that the distribution is either P or Q, and they have no sharp view about which. I draw one marble from the chosen bag and keep the colour hidden.
Target event A: the marble is red or blue. Signal event S: the marble is red or green.

My working says P(A) = 0.6 under both bags, so before anything is revealed they should be willing to buy *and* sell a $1-if-A ticket at 0.6 and at no other price — a sharp credence sitting on top of total ignorance about which bag, which is already a small jolt. Then if I tell them S holds, their interval for A should open to [0.25, 0.6]; if I tell them S fails, it should open to [0.6, 0.83]. Either way a single number becomes a spread, and they could have predicted that beforehand. If that's wrong, fix the counts.

What I want is the runnable version: the running order, the exact wording of what I say and of what's on the cards, a payoff scheme in points (quiz credit, no real money) where declining to trade is a live option rather than a shrug, and the moment where they hit the thing before its named. I think the phenomenon is called dilation but I'm not confident, and I don't want the word said out loud until the last ten minutes.

Three things I specifically want built in:

1. A pre-mortem. Before I reveal anything about S, each student writes on an index card whether their bid-ask spread for A will get wider, narrower, or stay the same once they hear whether S holds, and why. They commit before they see it.

2. A forced choice about the information itself. Before they hear anything, I offer to tell them whether S holds — for free, or for a point, or I offer *them* a point to let me not tell them; whichever framing makes it land. The point is that they had a usable sharp 0.6 and the news is going to cost them that, so they should feel themselves wanting to refuse free information. Tell me which way to set it up so it doesn't feel rigged.

3. A bookie round. Once the interval for A is open, a student bookie offers trades at prices strictly inside it — sell me a $1-if-A ticket at 0.55, buy one back at 0.45 — each offer individually licensed by the thought that *some* probability in their set makes it look fine. I'm fairly confident this is where they get pumped for a sure loss, but I'm genuinely unsure whether the pump bites on every version of the view or only on the permissive reading (a trade is fine if some measure in the set likes it) as opposed to the strict one (it has to be licensed by all of them). If it only bites on one, build the round around that one, say which, and give me wording for the two rules that students would recognise as the thing they were doing.

Then the debrief: map each moment in the game onto the bit of theory it corresponds to, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I know one of them will just split the difference and say 0.5, and someone will demand I open the envelope), and be frank about what the activity can't do. My worry is that because I've staged the imprecision as ignorance about a hidden fact, the resident sharp Bayesian can say they simply *have* a prior over which bag and merely decline to state it, and nothing in the game answers them. Also: which feature of my two bags is actually doing the work — is it that the bags agree on A unconditionally but come apart once you condition on S, or something else? I want to know whether I could have picked these numbers at random or whether the effect needs contriving.

Start with the thing itself — the participant handout and my moves in running order, written so I can photocopy one and read the other straight off the page — then the debrief and the honest notes. No preface, no telling me what you're about to give me.
