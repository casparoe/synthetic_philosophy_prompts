Last night I argued until well past midnight with a friend about Hofstadter and Sander's 2013 book Surfaces and Essences, in which they defend the thesis that analogy-making, rather than rule-application or mere association, is the core of cognition: that to categorize anything is already to liken it to something remembered, and that our concepts are the slowly blurred residue of the particular episodes through which we met them. We kept talking past each other because we were, I think, demanding different things of a theory. So before we resume, I want the standard written down.

Assume the theory is wanted for a specific purpose: a machine that acquires concepts the way a young child does, from a handful of examples, with no curriculum, no labels and no hand-coded features, and whose failures are recognizably human failures. Write the desiderata such a theory has to satisfy, each with the case or consideration behind it. Among the cases I want covered: being handed one of those two-panel visual puzzles of the kind where you must say what separates the twelve figures on the left from the twelve on the right, where the distinguishing property may be purely relational and is not marked in advance; understanding in a single untroubled pass what someone means when they say that starting a new job feels like being the new kid at school, while later coming to reject an analogy that once seemed perfect, such as comparing an atom to a solar system; explaining one domain to a friend by mapping it onto a second domain that initially shares no vocabulary with it, where the shared structure seems to be created by the comparison rather than discovered by it; and getting from a single exposure to a usable concept and then retracting the overextension on your own, without anyone telling you that you were wrong. I also want the principled constraints: that the combinatorial explosion of candidate mappings be tamed by something other than the answer being presupposed, that the theory say what makes an analogy resistible and what we learn from our resistance to it, and that it predict characteristic errors rather than only successes.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable given that purpose and which are really preferences of one camp, such as the demand for a single unified mechanism or the demand that everything be stateable in one representational format. Most importantly, tell me which subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied: the plasticity that lets one example suffice seems to be the same plasticity that produces overextension and confabulated likeness, and a mechanism that can invent the structure it finds is hard to hold to predictions in advance. Finally, in a sentence or two each, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the families of accounts people actually defend, and why. Skip a full scorecard; that part we can do ourselves.
