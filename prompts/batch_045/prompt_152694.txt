hey — i'm putting together discussion material for a seminar i'm TAing (evolution & philosophy of biology, mixed undergrads/grads) and i want a dialogue i can hand out. the topic is adaptationism vs the Gould-Lewontin style critique, but i really don't want another generic "adaptationist says it's for X, spandrel-person says maybe it's not, they agree to disagree" thing. so i've made up a case with stipulated facts, and i want the two characters to actually work through *this* case. please don't have anyone look anything up or cite anything — everything you need is below.

setup: two senior researchers, Mara and Theo, have been arguing for years about one feature in one fossil taxon. call the animal whatever you like; the feature is a recurved flange of bone on the cheek that forms a half-tube and, in life, seems to have housed a soft-tissue duct. they're standing at a specimen tray. Mara is the adaptationist / reverse-engineering type; Theo is the structuralist in the spandrels tradition. neither is an idiot and neither is a caricature.

the facts they both accept (stipulate these in the dialogue somehow, naturally, not as a list):
- the flange shows up abruptly in the group, is in every known specimen, and has no obvious precursor.
- its fit with the duct is strikingly precise — the kind of precision that makes people reach for "designed for."
- developmentally, the flange comes out of the same mesenchymal condensation as the jaw joint region; in the sister taxon that condensation produces a plain ridge with no channel at all. you don't seem to be able to get the jaw shape without making *something* there.
- in living relatives with a flange, blocking the channel costs about 20% prey-detection accuracy in turbid water and does nothing in clear water. estimated selection coefficient is small.
- flange depth varies continuously across individuals; deeper flanges give slightly better detection but slightly weaker bite force. so there's a real trade-off.
- someone finally ran a "swap" experiment: replace the flange with a printed prosthetic of the same outer geometry but different internal material. the animals do fine. nobody agrees what that shows.

what i want them to actually do:

Mara should run a genuine reverse-engineering argument, not a just-so story — an optimality model over the detection/bite-force trade-off, an independent prediction about where flange depth should sit in turbid vs clear-water populations, and an answer to the "Panglossian" jibe: her claim should be that the objection is about hypothesis *generation*, not about a single tested model, and that "no adaptive story found" isn't evidence of anything.

Theo should have a *positive* structural hypothesis, not just scepticism — something like: the flange is a consequence of what it takes to keep the cheek plate rigid under bite load. it should carry its own quantitative prediction that differs from Mara's (e.g. flange depth should track bite-force demands across the clade rather than turbidity), so the two views are discriminable rather than just verbally different.

both have to deal honestly with two distinctions that i think students always collapse:
1. origin vs maintenance — a thing can arise as a by-product and later be kept and refined by selection. Mara should be allowed to say "fine, then it's an adaptation now and my model still explains its present form," and Theo needs a real reply, not a shrug.
2. spandrel vs exaptation vs plain constraint/pleiotropy. if anyone uses these interchangeably in the dialogue they're doing it wrong.

near the end I want Theo to raise a point i've been calling the "carving" objection, and Mara to answer it seriously: the flange may not be the kind of thing selection could have acted *on*, because it isn't separately variable or separately heritable — the variational unit is the whole cheek module, so the flange's boundaries are ours, not evolution's. Mara's comeback shouldn't be "but it has a function"; it should engage with whether the model's variables have to correspond to real variational units.

each of them should concede something they actually dislike conceding. Mara: that the "what is this for?" heuristic makes the spandrel verdict nearly unreachable in practice, even when it's true. Theo: that "spandrel" is not the default or the null — it needs a positive developmental/structural account with numbers, and the precision of the channel-to-duct fit is a real explanandum that bare constraint-talk usually waves away.

they should end by jointly designing the swap experiment properly (what they'd measure, what would falsify whom) while still disagreeing about what a positive result would *mean* — Mara: it only shows the material doesn't matter, not that the shape wasn't selected for; Theo: if many materials satisfy the function equally, the optimality model has no grip. no resolution, no kumbaya. if you want, a student can walk in at the very end and ask "so — is it an adaptation or not?" and they give two different two-line answers. that's optional.

length: 900-1200 words of dialogue, plus whatever stage directions. keep it talking, not lecturing — short turns, real interruptions, they should quote each other's earlier lines back at each other rather than deliver speeches. and please get the vocab right (spandrel, exaptation, constraint, pleiotropy, optimality model, trade-off, selection coefficient, variational module) — i'm going to be reading this in front of people who know the literature. sorry if this is over-specified lol, i just want something i can actually hand out without fixing it first.
