so im in this seminar this term — half philosophy of biology, half cog sci, officially titled something like "Minds Without Magic" — and we've spent the last three weeks on what our prof keeps calling competence without comprehension: the idea that you get apparently intelligent, apparently purposive, apparently understanding behavior out of processes in which nothing understands anything. Darwin's side is the appearance of design without a designer. Turing's side is the inversion — instead of starting from an intuition about what real understanding must be and then checking whether a machine has it, you get the competences first, and any claim about "understanding" either has to be built out of them or is just extra decoration.

The thing I can't get straight is how much of the argument is being carried by one analogy. Our prof, and the readings, and I guess the whole Dennett-ish tradition, talk about natural selection as if it were a designer: it "explores" a space of possible solutions, "tests" them, "trades off" costs, "figures out" that a thicker shell is worth a slower escape, "solves" the problem of seeing in a given medium — the example he keeps coming back to is the camera-style eye, which he says selection built as well as an engineer would have and faster. Then the same move on the Turing side: training a net is "shaping" it, the loss function is what we "want," the model "learns" the task. And all of this is supposed to license the conclusion that there's nothing left over: no comprehension required anywhere in the story, no extra ingredient, and in particular that the person who says "fine, you've explained the competence, but you haven't explained the understanding" is demanding something that was never going to be there.

My friend in the class — physics/CS double major, deeply suspicious of all of this — says the analogy *is* the argument: designer vocabulary smuggles in foresight, a representation of the goal, the ability to weigh counterfactuals and trade things off globally, and above all somebody who understands, and then you turn around and announce that nobody needs to understand. I think she's partly right but also that the analogy is doing something legitimate I can't name.

Our prof also made a distinction I haven't found anywhere else, which I think is his own: "shallow" inversions, where a mechanism's competence is explained by a process none of whose steps involve comprehension but somebody outside the system did understand and set it up — an artifact, or a net we trained — versus "deep" inversions, where nobody ever understood, at any point, which is supposed to be the Darwin case. He says only the deep ones are philosophically interesting and that designer talk is fine as long as you remember it's shallow talk about a deep process.

Can you audit this? I want the correspondence table for the designer analogy as it's actually being used: what plays the role of the client, the brief, the blueprint, the design space, the prototype, the test, the tradeoff, the revision, the finished product — in the Darwin case, then in the trained-net case. Then where the mapping breaks, and specifically which breaks matter for the conclusion it's being used for and which are harmless scenery. Does his shallow/deep distinction help, or does it make things worse? My worry is that the analogy only has real content in the shallow case while the conclusion is only supposed to follow in the deep one. And if the analogy is carrying the conclusion, is there a better one, or is the honest answer that there's nothing to map onto?
