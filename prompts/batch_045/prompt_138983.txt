I've been reading a well-reviewed popular-science book on consciousness, and the chapter on illusionism characterizes the view roughly as follows (I've numbered the sentences; this is my own close paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation):

1. Illusionism, associated above all with Daniel Dennett and Keith Frankish, is the view that phenomenal consciousness does not exist: there are no qualia, and there is nothing it is like to be you.
2. It is called illusionism because it treats our conviction that we have qualia as an illusion: an introspective illusion produced by the brain's self-monitoring systems, which misrepresent ordinary sensory states as having ineffable, intrinsic qualities.
3. The illusion works exactly like a visual illusion such as the Müller-Lyer: the experience itself is real and vivid, it merely misleads you about what is there, so the reality of the experience is never in question, only its accuracy.
4. The motivation is the explanatory gap: since no account of neural processing could ever explain why it feels like anything, illusionists conclude that it doesn't, and treat the apparent hardness of the hard problem as itself the phenomenon to be explained.
5. Illusionism is therefore a form of eliminativism: eliminativism about consciousness plus a psychological story about why we find it so hard to accept.
6. And the view is generally held to be self-undermining, which is why it has so few defenders: the illusionist must concede that things seem some way to us, and that seeming just is the phenomenal fact being denied.
7. One further consequence follows: if consciousness is an illusion, then nothing is ever really harmed by anything, and the entire moral significance we attach to experience rests on a mistake.

Sentence 3 seems to use illusion in the ordinary sense in which an illusion is itself a conscious experience — which is precisely the sort of thing the illusionist is denying, isn't it? On the other hand, I'm half inclined to accept 7: if there is nothing it is like to be in pain, it is hard to see why pain matters morally.
