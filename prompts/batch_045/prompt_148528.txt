I need a favour and it's a slightly weird one. I'm a historian at a small college in coastal Connecticut, and in nine days I have to give the dedicatory address for the new Pryce Archive — a reading room and special collections wing paid for by Martha Pryce Halloway, who is the great-great-granddaughter of Josiah Pryce, the shipping magnate whose money founded the place in 1861 (guano and sugar, carried on ships that also moved indentured labourers between islands in the Pacific; that's the part everyone now knows and nobody says out loud at these things). The board voted 7–6 to keep his name on the building, I was on the losing side of that vote, and I agreed to speak only on the understanding that I could say what I actually think. The room will be: Martha and about thirty assorted Pryces, two people who came a genuinely long way from one of the islands the labour came from, the college president and a state rep who both want this to be over in eight minutes, a couple hundred alumni who want to feel good about their alma mater, and my own department, who know my methodological commitments and who will absolutely catch me if I fudge them.

Here's why I can't just write something gracious and go home. The view I've argued for in my own work — I call it the two-ledger view, sorry — is that blame and commemoration run on separate books: whether you may condemn a past agent is governed by the norms and evidence actually available to them, but whether we should go on naming buildings after them is governed entirely by ours, since a dedication is an act performed by the living for the living. Which means a person can be genuinely non-culpable by the standards of his own time and still be someone we shouldn't be honouring, and on my own view I'm not allowed to call Josiah Pryce a villain and I'm also not allowed to thank him. There's a second trap I keep falling into: everything I draft turns into a quiet little story about how much more enlightened we are now, which is exactly the Whiggish self-flattery the two-ledger thing was supposed to block, especially since the college endowment still holds money traceable to that trade and my students' financial aid comes out of it. And there's a third wrinkle — Pryce also endowed a school for girls in 1872, which looks progressive now and was done for reasons that were mostly about wanting literate clerks for his counting houses and not wanting to lose status to a rival across the river — so the speech has to handle a right act done for mixed and not-very-lovable reasons without either canonising him or sneering at him.

So: write me the address, six to eight minutes, in a register that Martha Pryce Halloway can sit through without being insulted to her face at her own gift, that the two visitors from the islands can hear as honest rather than evasive, and that my colleagues won't be able to dismiss as a sellout. The philosophical problem should be doing the structural work here — deciding what the speech can and can't say — not just getting named in passing. Then, after the text, add a short note (a couple hundred words) on where the speech leans on a contested reading of the presentism problem or takes a liberty with it, how confident you are that this is the right way to handle the occasion, and which single line in it you're least sure about.
