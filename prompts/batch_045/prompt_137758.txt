I've reconstructed the Lewis-style triviality argument in my own notation for a seminar paper, and it now seems to me to prove a great deal more than the result is supposed to prove. I'd like a step-by-step diagnosis: which step do you reject, why does that step look compulsory, and what does rejecting it cost?

**Setup.** Let W be a finite set of worlds; sentences express subsets of W; an agent's credence is a probability measure P on the algebra of all subsets of W. An *account* of the indicative conditional delivers, for each measure P and each pair of sentences A, C, a number f_P(A ▷ C) in [0,1]: the credence the account says an agent with credence P should have in 'if A, then C'. Call an account PROPOSITIONAL (PROP) if there is an operation * on pairs of propositions such that for every P, f_P(A ▷ C) = P(‖A‖ * ‖C‖): the conditional expresses a proposition X, fixed by the propositions A and C express, and its probability is just P(X). Call an account SUPPOSITIONAL (SUPP) if it stipulates f_P(A ▷ C) := P(C | A) and denies that ▷ expresses a proposition at all.

**The argument.**

(1) *The Equation.* Any adequate account satisfies (E): for every P with P(A) > 0, f_P(A ▷ C) = P(C | A). This is the datum the literature starts from — conditional betting rates track conditional probabilities, the material reading gives the wrong verdicts, and the Ramsey test looks like the procedure we actually use.

(2) PROP.

(3) *Closure under learning.* (E) must hold not only at the agent's current P but at P( · | E) for every E the agent could learn by conditioning, since each such function is itself a rationally permissible credence function and (E) was stated as a norm on all of them.

(4) Fix A and C, and let W contain the four nonempty atoms w1 = ‖A ∧ C‖, w2 = ‖A ∧ ¬C‖, w3 = ‖¬A ∧ C‖, w4 = ‖¬A ∧ ¬C‖. Let X = ‖A * C‖ and let P have full support.

(5) Apply (E) at P( · | A ∧ C): there C has probability 1 given A, so P(X | A ∧ C) = 1, whence ‖A ∧ C‖ ⊆ X. Apply (E) at P( · | A ∧ ¬C): there C has probability 0 given A, so P(X | A ∧ ¬C) = 0, whence X ∩ ‖A ∧ ¬C‖ = ∅. Both times it is the *same* X, since by clause (ii) of PROP it is fixed by ‖A‖ and ‖C‖ alone. So X ∩ ‖A‖ = ‖A ∧ C‖, i.e. X = ‖A ∧ C‖ ∪ S with S ⊆ ‖¬A‖.

(6) Now impose (E) at P itself, P(X) = P(C | A), and run the four cases for S. (a) S = ∅: P(A ∧ C) = P(A ∧ C)/P(A), which for full support with P(A) < 1 forces P(A ∧ C) = 0 — impossible. (b) S = ‖¬A ∧ C‖, so X = ‖C‖: requires P(C) = P(C | A) at every admissible P, i.e. universal independence of A and C. (c) S = ‖¬A ∧ ¬C‖, so X = ‖A ≡ C‖: with P(w1) = 1/2 and P(w2) = P(w3) = P(w4) = 1/6, P(X) = 2/3 while P(C | A) = 3/4. (d) S = ‖¬A‖, so X = ‖A ⊃ C‖: at the uniform measure P(X) = 3/4 while P(C | A) = 1/2. Every case fails, so PROP is inconsistent with (E).

(7) *The step that worries me.* PROP sounds like a substantive semantic thesis, but in the argument it did only one job: it guaranteed that f_P(A ▷ C) is the measure, under P, of one fixed proposition. And that is not a semantic thesis at all — it is just what it is for an account to attach a probability to a sentence in a form the probability calculus can operate on. Any account whatsoever delivers a number f_P(A ▷ C) as a function of P; that is what an account is. What (4)–(6) establish is that P ↦ P(C | A) is not the measure of any fixed proposition: such measures are linear in P, and a ratio of linear functions is not. So P ↦ P(C | A) cannot be the credence any account mandates, and (E) fails for every account, not merely for propositional ones.

(8) *The consequence I can't accept.* SUPP was built to satisfy (E): it sets f_P(A ▷ C) := P(C | A) by stipulation and gives up the proposition. If (7) is right, that manoeuvre buys nothing, and the Ramsey datum is not a norm on conditional credence at all — which is absurd, since (E) is the datum the triviality result was supposed to *protect* by driving us to SUPP.

Two readers have been unhelpful in different ways. One says the break is at (3). The other says the argument only establishes that PROP is false and that SUPP is untouched — which strikes me as refusing to engage with (7) rather than answering it, because (7) seems to me to assume nothing beyond 'an account assigns numbers'. On the first: I tried dropping (3), and the collapse in (4)–(6) indeed never starts, since it needs conditionalizations. But then I noticed that in a coarse space the Equation fails anyway — with four equiprobable worlds, A = three of them and C = one of those, P(C | A) = 1/3 and no proposition in that space has probability 1/3, so PROP dies without any help from (3). My friend replies that we should take the space to be atomless, and then for any single P one can always find a set X of the required measure. I don't know whether that is a real answer or a way of burying the problem.

Please: (i) name the step you reject and explain why it looked fine; (ii) price the two or three most attractive alternatives to your own diagnosis, including, if your diagnosis spares SUPP, what SUPP pays for denying that conditional credence is the measure of a proposition; (iii) tell me whether my reconstruction is technically sound — do (4)–(6) really go through, and is the atomless reply to the granularity worry correct?; and (iv) say what would change your mind: which consideration would lead you to abandon your diagnosis, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail? For what it's worth, I would much rather keep (1) than keep (2).
