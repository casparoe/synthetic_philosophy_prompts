I was going though some old epistemology notes from a class I took last year, and there's a summary of the dogmatism paradox that I can't tell is fair or not. The notes (or maybe it was a blog post, I forget) explain it like this:

'The dogmatism paradox starts with the principle of dogmatism, which is just the idea that knowledge is closed under entailment: if you know that p, and p entails q, then you already know q, even if you have never actually drawn the inference. Because of this, a knower automatically acquires knowledge of any consequence of what they know, just by logic doing its work in the background. To get the paradox, let p be something you know, say that snow is white. Now consider the self-referential sentence q: "snow is white and you do not know q." You cannot know q. For if you knew q, then q would be true, so its second conjunct would be true, meaning you do not know q—a contradiction. So you can know that you do not know q. But you also know p, and p together with the fact that you do not know q entails q itself. Since knowledge is closed under entailment, you therefore automatically know q after all. That contradicts the earlier result that you know you don't know q. The conclusion is that the principle of dogmatism is flatly refuted, and really the sentence q is another liar-paradox style sentence that cannot be true at all. Since self-referential claims like q never come up outside of logic puzzles, this doesn't threaten ordinary claims to knowledge.'

Two things make me suspicious. First, the line about already knowing q even if you have never drawn the inference sounds like a strawman to me: I thought the principle of dogmatism was about being able to come to know a consequence by inferring it, not about automatically knowing every remote consequence without thinking. Second, the last bit where it says q can't be true seems wrong, because I thought the standard setup says q is true but unknowable, not that it is untrue. But I honestly might be mixing it up with something else, and for all I know the middle part is wrong too. Its a bit over my head.

How much of this summary is fair? Can you go through it and separate what's accurate, what's a subtle misrepresentation (like a conflation of two claims or an overstatement of a modest thesis), and what a defender of the usual way of setting up the paradox would object to? Does the notes' final conclusion that the principle is plainly refuted follow from the corrected version, or is that too quick? And if the notes' statement of the principle is off, what's the right way to state the principle of dogmatism so that the paradox still has its bite?
