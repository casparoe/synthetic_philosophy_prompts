Hi — I need a hand turning a half-formed idea into a proposal for a one-semester master's dissertation (about 10,000 words, one supervisor, due at the end of spring). Topic: Frege's political diary and whether it bears on his philosophy.

Background so you don't have to reconstruct it: the diary Frege kept over roughly a month in 1924, which only surfaced in the 1990s (German publication in 1994, English translation a couple of years later), contains all the things that made people uncomfortable — virulent antisemitism, including the thought that Jews should lose political rights in Germany, anti-Catholicism, hatred of social democracy and of parliamentary government, a monarchist and aggressively nationalist outlook, and a lot of bitterness about the lost war and the Weimar settlement. The argument it kicked off is, in one form: does any of that have anything to do with his logic, his anti-psychologism, his Platonism about thoughts, his context principle, or is it just a nasty old man's private notebook? And in its other form: what does a department actually do with a founder whose politics were like that.

My half-formed idea, which may be too clever for its own good: I think the debate stalls because everyone uses one vague relation, bear on, where there are at least four. First, a causal or genetic one — did the politics shape the doctrines, or vice versa? Second, an evidential one — does knowing about the diary give me a reason to doubt some specific claim, and if so which one, and by what route? Third, a hermeneutic one — does the diary make something in the philosophy legible that otherwise looks like pure tectonics (his recurring first-person plural, his obsession with sharp boundaries and with contamination of science by psychology, his insistence that truth isn't settled by what most people accept)? Fourth, an institutional one — what should a prize, a lecture series, or a syllabus do? My suspicion is that these get different answers, and that the interesting one is the hermeneutic one, where the connection, if there is one, isn't at the level of doctrine content at all.

Here's the angle I actually want to defend. Instead of asking whether any Fregean doctrine implies or is implied by any diary remark, ask whether the *way* he draws and polices distinctions is the same in both places. His philosophy is built almost entirely out of sharp, exhaustive dichotomies defended with a rhetoric of purity and corruption — thoughts that get sullied if you confuse them with ideas, concepts that must have sharp boundaries or stop being concepts, no compromise with psychologism or relativism or with the crowd. The diary draws its own hard dichotomies with what looks like the same vocabulary of infection, defence, and things that must be kept out. The testable version of that: fix a short list of his distinctions and code the passages where he introduces them for that pattern, then run the same coding over comparable polemics by other German academics of the period, because otherwise I've only rediscovered how professors wrote in 1900.

The other half is reflexive and I can't tell if its a strength or a trap. Frege's own tool kit might or might not let us quarantine the diary. On one hand he insists, as a methodological maxim, that how someone arrived at a judgement has nothing to do with what justifies it — which looks like exactly the licence to say the diary is irrelevant to the philosophy. On the other hand his anti-psychologism means the diary can't be dismissed as mere private mental goings-on, since what's in it is asserted content, and on his own view an expressed thought is publicly available and judgeable rather than the author's property. So: is the man-versus-work move easier or harder to make with Frege's own distinctions than people assume, and does using his maxim here commit me to more than I want — for instance that nothing about an author's life, good or bad, can ever count? And is that maxim a logical principle or a piece of intellectual temperament, which is, awkwardly, the same kind of question the whole project is asking.

What I want out of it is something decision-shaped rather than a verdict on Frege's soul: a taxonomy people could actually use when deciding whether to rename something or how to contextualise him in a course, plus an argument about which of the four relations is the one worth caring about.

In roughly 900 words, give me: the question in one sentence; a short section on why the two standard postures (no connection whatsoever, versus deep damning continuity) both leave it open; the method with a rough month-by-month over four months; the two or three conclusions I might reach and what would decide between them; what would count as the project failing rather than just coming out negative; and a frank paragraph on what to cut so it fits in a semester. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I've got the primary texts and I'll add references myself, and I'd rather the proposal argue the framing than cite at it. Thanks alot.
