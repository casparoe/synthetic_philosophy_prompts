I was at a psych department's journal club last week — I'm in philosophy, over from another building, so I was mostly out of my depth — and two people there got into an argument I can't stop poking at. There was a talk first. The speaker claimed we have a dedicated system for tracking how well our bids for attention are landing: the story was that the crappy feeling you get when something you post gets way less response than you expected is a signal from a mechanism calibrated to the response rates you'd get in a small ancestral band, not to the internet. These are the findings she reported and they're the only facts you need:

- the mood drop tracks the *ratio* of responses to audience size, not the raw count: two replies out of forty hurts more than two out of two hundred
- past an audience of roughly 150 the denominator stops counting: a one-in-twenty return on forty people feels worse than a one-in-twenty return on two thousand, even though by the ratio story they should feel the same
- it doesn't seem to care about stakes: being ignored by forty people who can't affect anything in your life stings about as much as being ignored by forty who can
- heavy users, people posting twenty times a day for years, still show the same drop — it hasn't recalibrated
- the same ratio structure run through a non-social task (a little game where rewards arrive at a rate worse than you were told to expect) doesn't produce the mood drop

Then Marcus, who's sympathetic to the evolutionary psych side, and Elin went at it. Roughly:

1. Marcus: Ratio-tracking plus a ceiling at 150. A general learner has no reason to put a ceiling there — most of us are exposed to audiences far bigger than that, so if you're picking up the base rate from experience, the ceiling should sit wherever your actual experience put it, not at a band size nobody in this room has ever lived in.

2. Elin: You're reading a processing limit as a fossil. Nobody tracks a ratio when the denominator is in the thousands, and past 150 the audience isn't people who know you anymore — it's followers, a different situation with different norms about replying. Your ceiling could be sitting on top of a confound.

3. Marcus: Fine, drop the ceiling. The stakes finding is the strong one. If this were general-purpose learning about what produces good outcomes for you, forty people who can't touch your life should barely register next to forty who can. They don't. The system is running on a proxy — response rate — not on consequences. That's what you'd expect from something built to track where you stood in a group where everyone's opinion did matter.

4. Elin: Loads of domain-general systems run on proxies; that's the most ordinary result in the heuristics literature, it isn't a module detector. Also "how much do these people matter to you" was measured by asking people, and self-report about what matters is exactly the sort of thing that comes out flat.

5. Marcus: Then the non-updating. Heavy users have had thousands of trials. A learner should have quietly reset its expectation to the real base rate and stopped being surprised. It hasn't. Stubbornness of that kind is what an innately-set expectation looks like.

6. Elin: Variable-ratio reinforcement is famously resistant to extinction — that's textbook, not exotic. Social media is a slot machine. Non-updating is what a general learner does on that schedule, so if the finding cuts anywhere it cuts my way.

7. Marcus: And the non-social version produces nothing. Same ratio structure, worse-than-expected return, no mood effect. If this were just prediction error the game should sting like the post stings. That's domain specificity, and domain specificity is the one thing your story can't hand you.

8. Elin: Your game differed from posting in a dozen ways — you controlled it, nobody was watching, there was no self-presentation. One null result can't carry domain specificity. And even if you got it, showing the mechanism is social isn't showing it's an adaptation. "We have a system for monitoring audience response because monitoring audience response was useful" is the finding with a story stapled to it. It could be a byproduct; it could be Gould and Lewontin's spandrel, general machinery that happened to get wired to social feedback. Give me one thing your hypothesis predicts that mine doesn't, that you'd have been willing to lose on.

9. Marcus: I've given you five. You've given each one a local fix, but you have to cover all five at once, and you keep swapping which general mechanism you're appealing to — heuristics here, extinction schedules there. That's not a rival theory, it's a pile of excuses.

10. Elin: A pile of mechanisms that each handle one finding is what a non-modular mind looks like.

They asked me afterwards who I thought was winning and I honestly couldn't tell — I have a feeling they're talking past each other somewhere in the middle but I can't locate where. So: can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, not just who wins overall? A rating from clearly wrong to clearly right on each move with a line of justification would be ideal. Two things on top of that, since they're the bits my own department would care about: (a) I think "just-so story" is doing maybe four different jobs in this exchange, and I want them pulled apart, and told which of them Marcus has genuinely met and which he's just talked around; (b) nobody ever said what would tell us whether the feeling has a function *now* as opposed to having had one way back when, and it bugged me that both of them seemed to assume those were the same question. Are they?
