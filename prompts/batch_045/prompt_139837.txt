Act as a formal epistemologist who works on chance and credence. Take your time, brainstorm the space of possible answers before you commit to one, and please tell me plainly if my setup is confused rather than just picking a side.

I'm building a small forecasting app and I want to get the epistemic plumbing right. Users get yes/no questions about next week's events. For each question I can show them one of two things: (a) a number from a chance oracle, a black box I have certified as calibrated — across thousands of past questions, of the ones it labelled 0.7, about 70% came true, and likewise for its other labels; or (b) a bare verdict from a tracker that just prints WILL HAPPEN or WON'T, has been right about 90% of the time, and never gives numbers.

Here is what I can't sort out, and its driving me nuts. As I understand the Principal Principle, rationality requires you to defer to objective chance: your credence in A should equal the chance of A, end of story. So if the oracle really is handing out the chances, the tracker is irrelevant and I should probably suppress it — showing it would just tempt people away from the correct number and leave them worse calibrated. But that seems insane when the tracker is right 90% of the time. And when I try to square it I end up at the opposite conclusion: if the tracker is right 90% of the time, then roughly 90% of the events it flagged did in fact happen, so the real chance for those events was 0.9 and the oracle must be badly miscalibrated on exactly that subset — so the oracle was never giving the chances at all, and the tracker is the thing to display.

Can you settle it? Is the Principal Principle telling me to throw the tracker away, or is it telling me the oracle was never reporting chances? And if both of those readings are wrong, what should I actually be showing users and why?
