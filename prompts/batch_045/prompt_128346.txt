I am a journalist working on a long feature about how an artificial agent ought to decide when it may be copied, simulated, predicted, or run more than once — a cluster of questions that has migrated out of seminar rooms and into the design of actual systems. Two of my sources, one an advocate of updateless decision theory and one an advocate of updateful (causal) decision theory, have been arguing by email for the better part of a year and have at last agreed to an adversarial collaboration. They have asked me for a template; I would rather hand them something substantive to react to. Please draft the joint statement they could both sign.

A few stipulations they have already accepted, and which you should treat as fixed: the trustee in each scenario below is a single decision procedure, its source code is known to the other parties in the scenario, and its copies or simulations are exact; there are no causal back-channels between runs; money is denominated in a single endowment pot unless stated otherwise; and the word "rational" is to be used descriptively until the document says otherwise. Please do not adjudicate between the two positions — I do not want a verdict, I want the document. Do not cite or name papers, authors, or schools beyond the two camps themselves; the whole thing should stand on argument alone.

The two cases I would like used as the shared test-bed are these.

**Case A, the odd digit.** Yesterday, a benefactor ran an exact simulation of the trustee's decision procedure and put to it the very question that may be put to the trustee today: pay £1,000 out of the endowment, or decline. If the simulation paid, the benefactor deposited £1,000,000 into the endowment then and there; if it declined, nothing was deposited. A bit B is then fixed by a mathematical fact the trustee cannot currently compute — say, whether the trillionth digit in the decimal expansion of pi is odd. Both camps stipulate that the trustee's prior over B is one half, that B is in no way causally influenced by what the trustee does, and that B is in principle computable. If B takes one value, the trustee is never asked anything and the matter closes with whatever sum was deposited. If B takes the other value, the trustee is asked, and it knows which branch it is in: pay £1,000 now, or decline, with no further money at stake either way. What should the trustee do, and what should it have wanted, yesterday, to be disposed to do?

**Case B, the two centres.** The trustee's programme is instantiated twice, in Reading and in Inverness, on identical hardware with identical inputs; nothing available to either instantiation distinguishes the two, and they cannot communicate. Each is told that if exactly one instantiation presses within the hour, the endowment receives £1,000,000, that if both or neither press it receives nothing, and that pressing costs nothing. What should the policy be, and what should an instantiation do once it is running? I would also like the document to say what changes if the prize is paid per instantiation to a local budget rather than once to the common pot, and whether that is a question of decision theory or a question about what the trustee is for.

**Case C, the retrofit.** In Case A, at the moment the trustee is asked, it may pay £100 to delete its record of the interim evidence — including its knowledge that it was asked at all — and hand the decision to a policy registered before B was resolved. Would either camp's agent take that option? Both of them, I suspect, have something invested in the answer.

What I want, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including about the two cases themselves, about what a money-pump or "why aren't you rich" style argument can and cannot establish, about whether "you should have bound yourself in advance" is a criticism of a decision theory or a remark about an agent's options, and about what counts as a solution to a decision problem at all. Please make them substantive: I will spot claims so thin that no one could deny them.
2. A disagreements table, with one row per crux and columns for: the crux, stated as a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the updateless commitment, phrased as an updateless theorist would phrase it; the updateful commitment, phrased as an updateful theorist would phrase it; the case or argument both accept as bearing on that crux; and, written down in advance, what each side predicts that case or argument will show. The cruxes I would expect to see addressed include: whether the object of evaluation is a policy or an act, and from which standpoint it is evaluated; whether the outputs of other computations implementing one's own decision procedure are things one controls, or merely events correlated with one's action; whether indexical or self-locating information is admissible evidence, and how value aggregates across instantiations; what counterfactuals each theory needs — counterfactuals about logical facts on one side, counterfactuals about what a copy of oneself would have done on the other — and whether that need is a genuine cost; and, finally, whether a decision theory that advises its agents to self-modify into agents of another sort has thereby indicted itself, and where the ex ante standpoint comes from for a system that was trained rather than designed and has no identifiable first moment. Please also register where the updateful side must disambiguate which updateful theory it speaks for, and where it will want to gesture at evidential decision theory as a third position not at the table.
3. A short closing section, "What would move us": what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own view, and whether there is anything both agree would actually settle part of the dispute — a formal equivalence result, a solved theory of logical counterfactuals, a tournament, or something else.
4. A brief note on which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive, and why.

Where you cannot find a formulation of one side's view that the other side would accept as a fair statement of it, say so in the table rather than papering over the gap; that itself will be useful to me.
