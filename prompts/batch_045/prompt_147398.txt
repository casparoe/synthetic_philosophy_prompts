I'm revising the opening chapter of my PhD thesis on the normative status of expected utility theory, and my supervisor has asked me to write down, in one place, everything I am committed to in this area so that she can see where the load-bearing joints are before the argument starts. Before I hand it over I would like an independent audit. I have a suspicion that something I say in the first chapter sits badly with something I say in the third, but I cannot locate the fault line, and I would rather not hand in a chapter with a visible seam in it.

Here is the set, stated as neutrally as I can manage.

(1) For choices under risk, where the probabilities are objectively known, the correct normative theory is expected utility maximization with the von Neumann–Morgenstern independence axiom. I hold this as a claim about what it is rational to prefer, not as a description of anyone.

(2) The Allais pattern is a genuine error rather than an unusual-but-permissible taste. Someone who prefers a certain million to the gamble that pays five million with probability 0.10, one million with probability 0.89 and nothing with probability 0.01, and who also prefers a 0.10 chance at five million to a 0.11 chance at one million, has made a mistake. I am fairly comfortable with the analogy to a visual illusion: the certainty effect moves people without giving them a reason.

(3) I do not think structural axioms can be defended by appeal to their intuitiveness. The only arguments for them that I find convincing are pragmatic ones: violate the axiom and you can be maneuvered into a sure loss, or into paying to reverse a swap you have just made, or into adopting a plan you will later want to abandon. An axiom with no argument of that shape behind it is, as far as I am concerned, a taste in axioms.

(4) I am a subjectivist about ends. Being rational is a matter of having your preferences hang together; there is no such thing as an irrational taste as such, only an incoherent one. Rationality constrains the structure of your preferences, not their objects.

(5) I do not think the Allais preferences can be convicted by re-describing the one-shot choice as a two-stage decision tree. In the sequential version the agent at the second node knows that she is in the 11 per cent branch, which is information she does not have in the one-shot version, and that makes it a different decision problem rather than the same problem in disguise. The one-shot presentation is the fair test of the one-shot case.

(6) I think ambiguity aversion is rationally permissible. An agent who prefers betting on the urn of known composition to betting on the urn of unknown composition, in the Ellsberg set-up, is not thereby making a mistake, even though this violates the same style of sure-thing reasoning I rely on elsewhere and looks formally parallel to the Allais pattern.

(7) I think there is nothing irrational about being risk-averse with respect to money, including in the specific form of being markedly more reluctant to gamble when the stakes are large relative to your wealth than when they are small.

(8) I also have a methodological scruple: I think a normative theory should be answerable to the reasons agents actually take themselves to have, and I am uneasy with theories whose treatment of the reasons people cite is to class them as psychological noise. This is why I resisted a friend's suggestion that I simply stipulate that outcomes are final probability distributions over money and nothing else.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Tell me which of these are in genuine tension, ordered by severity, and for each tension say exactly which premise would have to be given up or which additional premise would have to be added, and what that would cost me elsewhere on the list. I would particularly like to know whether any of the tensions can be repaired by adding something I have not stated rather than by dropping something I have, since I would rather add than drop. I also want the real tensions separated cleanly from the merely apparent ones: my office-mate's view is that all eight pull against each other, which I take to be a sign that he has not looked very hard, and two or three properly diagnosed tensions are worth more to me than eight waved at. Where a tension turns on a distinction I have not drawn, between risk and uncertainty, or between coherence and substance, or between a preference over outcomes and a preference over the way an outcome was reached, say which distinction is doing the work and what happens to my list if I draw it. And if you think the set is consistent only because one of my commitments is stated too weakly to do the work I need it to do, tell me which one and restate it in the form that would actually force the issue.
