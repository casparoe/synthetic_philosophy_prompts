I've been reading rationalist-adjacent decision-theory blogs for something like fifteen years, which means I have probably read more words about Pascal's Wager than about any other argument in the history of philosophy — and nearly all of them make the same three moves in the same order. I'd like something that comes at it sideways.

Write a parody of a hedge fund's quarterly letter to limited partners — the annual update from a firm on the order of "Infinite Upside Capital" — reporting on its strategy of wagering on the existence of deities. Roughly 800 words, in the register of a real investor letter: calm first-person plural, euphemistic, rich in confident-sounding numbers. Format it with section headings: a performance summary, a description of the strategy, a schedule of holdings (call it "Schedule of Deities"), a risk-factors section, and two or three footnotes.

The jokes should be decision-theoretic rather than theological. I'm not interested in "ha ha, religion"; the target is the wager's own machinery, taken seriously and allowed to break in public. Material I'd like drawn on, though don't feel obliged to tick off every item:

- The payoff structure. Infinite gains and losses don't cancel and can't be ranked against each other, so the Fund's claim to have "optimized" is incoherent and its allocation across deities is arbitrary in a way the letter presents as rigorous.
- The many-gods problem, solved by indexing: the Fund holds a position in every deity anyone has proposed, including deities that punish worship undertaken for expected-value reasons, and one that punishes worship full stop — and then concedes in a footnote that the net expected value has no defined sign.
- The gap between betting and believing. Belief isn't something you can just decide to have, and a deity stipulated to care about genuine faith will see through a hedged position. Please include the disclosure loop: the Fund is legally required to explain its strategy to investors, the explanation itself ruins the strategy, and the Fund maintains that the two facts are unrelated.
- The small-probability variant — the one where a stranger in a doorway offers an astronomical payoff at a one-in-a-trillion chance — held alongside the infinite-payoff positions, and funded on a rule that is transparently insensitive to scale.

Add at least one invented wrinkle of your own. My candidate, which you're welcome to steal or ignore: the management fee is payable only in worlds where no deity exists, so the Fund's incentives are inverted relative to its investors'.

Two constraints. First, stay deadpan: no winking at the reader, no "as the astute reader will have noticed." The letter should be recognizable as satire from the content alone. Second, exaggerate real features of the wager literature rather than inventing defects — someone who has actually worked through the argument should judge the joke fair rather than a strawman.

Be committed rather than even-handed. Pick one objection to make load-bearing — decide what the central absurdity actually is and build the piece around it, instead of giving every objection equal airtime — and then add a short author's note (100–150 words) stating which objection you made load-bearing, why you chose it, and where you took liberties: if you leaned on a contested reading (that infinite payoffs break the dominance argument, that what is wagered for is genuine belief rather than mere practice, that the many-gods problem is decisive rather than answerable), say so plainly instead of hedging.
