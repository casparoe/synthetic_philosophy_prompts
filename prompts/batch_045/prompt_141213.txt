I write a column on science and ideas, and I've let myself be talked into running a hands-on session at a weekend ideas festival: about 30 adults, mixed backgrounds, no maths past school, 75 minutes, and Id like to avoid slides if I can. The situation I want them in is the Bongard-problem one — a handfull of figures on the left that share something, the same number on the right that don't, and you have to say what the rule is from those examples and nothing else.

What I want them to run into is not that patterns are hard. It's that the examples never pin down a single rule: for any rule that fits, you can cook up a second, incompatible one that fits the same examples and disagrees with it somewhere further out, and whatever makes you go with the first one is coming from you, not from the data.

Can you design it for me — a recipe for making the problems out of stuff I can draw on paper or hand out on cards (I don't want to go hunting for good ones), a structure that makes people commit to a rule in writing and then produce a second incompatible one, and some way (points, tokens, a show of hands with something riding on it) of getting them to stake something on which rule will handle cases they haven't seen yet. Two things I'd especially like built in. First, the turn from description to prediction: the moment where someone concedes that both rules fit everything in front of them and still has to pick which one to rely on. Second, a split where half the room sees the same examples in a different order and the halves then find out they've landed on different rules, so ordering does some visible work before I say a word about it.

And then the debrief: what to draw out of it, what will predictably go wrong (my worry is one person shouts a rule in the first two minutes, the room locks onto it, and the whole thing becomes a puzzle hunt), and an honest note on the part of the worry the game can't stage and I'll just have to state out loud at the end.
