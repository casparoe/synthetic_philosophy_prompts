I want to practice arguing against contractualism, and I want a real opponent, not a punching bag. So: play a committed, well-read Scanlonian — someone who genuinely thinks an act is wrong just when it would be disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject, and who is totally at peace with the consequence that you can't pile up a million tiny complaints to outweigh one person's serious one. Be sharp: use the actual machinery (narrowly vs broadly comparative complaints, the tie-breaker move about numbers, whatever else you've got), concede small points when they're fair, but never hand me the utilitarian conclusion that numbers do the work. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so it feels like an actual argument and not a paper.

Here's my opening. You're happy to aggregate *within* a life. When you ask how strong someone's complaint against a principle is, you don't take one momentary twinge in isolation — you look at the shape of their whole life, so forty years of low-grade chronic pain counts as one big complaint rather than as fourteen thousand trivial ones. But that is already aggregation. And the only reason you offer for blocking it *across* persons is that persons are separate standpoints of justification; there's no one for whom the sum is experienced.

Now take the reductionist view of persons seriously, the way anyone who's spent time with Parfit probably should. Personal identity just is psychological continuity and connectedness, and those come in degrees. Within a life they're high, between two strangers they're low, but they aren't 1 and they aren't 0. So "one standpoint" versus "many standpoints" starts to look like a difference of degree along the very same axis you're already aggregating along inside a single life. Why doesn't that collapse the whole picture? As far as I can see you have two options and neither is good. Either you need a bright line where psychology gives you only a gradient, or you're committed to numbers mattering a little between weakly-connected people and mattering more between strongly-unified ones — which is utilitarianism with an extra parameter bolted on, and I don't see how it's still contractualism in any sense that would have motivated you in the first place.

Also, fair warning: if you try to escape with the tie-breaker move, I have a follow-up about vagueness and sorites chains where near-equal complaints get chained together, so don't lean on it too hard.
