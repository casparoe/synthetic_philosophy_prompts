I'm a staff engineer at a company with around two hundred engineers, and I chair the small group that owns our code-review standards. I have no philosophy training, so please define terms as you go. I came across the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate about testimony while trying to work out why our reviewers keep getting something wrong, and I'd like to turn it into a procedure my team can actually run.

The problem first. Reviewers routinely accept claims made in a pull request without checking them: that the platform team confirmed a new rate limit, that nothing else calls the endpoint being changed, that a dependency upgrade is backwards compatible, that someone tested it against representative data. Almost always that is fine, and the cost of checking would be absurd. Occasionally it is not: last quarter we shipped a change on the strength of a claim about a throughput limit that was out by a factor of ten, and the post-incident review concluded that no one had done anything wrong — which seems to me to mean that our standard has no content.

My understanding of the two positions, which you should correct if it is a caricature: the reductionist treats a speaker's say-so as justified only by other things — my evidence that this person is reliable, or reliable about this kind of thing — so that testimony is never an independent source of justification; the anti-reductionist treats being told something as itself enough, by default, defeated only by specific reasons for doubt in the particular case. If that framing is wrong, or if the real dispute is somewhere else, start by fixing it.

What I want is a decision procedure: five minutes, one page, runnable by a new graduate who has never heard of epistemology, applied by a reviewer to any claim they are being asked to accept rather than verify. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer. Mark where the procedure stops with "verify before merge", and where it hands the question to a person because it is really a values call about how much latency we will trade for how much assurance. Say what gets recorded in the pull request when a reviewer defers. Assume the reviewer is the only sign-off for most changes, and the tech lead only for escalations.

Five features of our setting are the reason I am asking rather than reading up on it myself, and the procedure has to handle them:

1. Generated text. A growing share of our pull request descriptions, migration notes and review comments were written, in whole or part, by a language model. There is no speaker: nobody intends to tell me anything, nobody is staking their credibility or offering a follow-up, and there is no track record that means anything. Does either theory have anything to say about relying on text like that, or is the debate built for a case with a person at both ends and simply silent here? If it is silent, say so, and say what the procedure should do at that point instead.
2. Transmission chains. A claim that started as a message in a chat thread, was summarised into a ticket, and arrived with me as one line in a design document. Does each link count as a fresh act of testimony, and does the answer change if one of the links was automated?
3. Negative and universal claims. No other service calls this. I have checked all the call sites. Sampling does not confirm these, and exhaustively checking them is precisely the expensive thing we are trying to avoid.
4. Stake and independence. The author of a change wants it merged and is reporting a result about their own work. Whatever the general answer about trusting colleagues, I suspect this case is special, and I want the procedure to say so if it is.
5. Cost symmetry. Not checking is usually free and occasionally catastrophic; checking is always paid for in senior-engineer time, and in what it does to a team when people are visibly not believed. I would like both costs treated as inputs to the procedure, not with verification quietly modelled as free.

One observation of my own that may or may not be relevant: some of the claims we accept arrive with someone visibly taking responsibility for them — I will put my name to this, ask me if it turns out to be false — while others are just sentences in a document with a name attached. The first kind feels different to rely on in a way that track records and base rates do not capture. Is that distinction doing any real work in the philosophy, or is it sentiment?

Please then run the finished procedure on three claims: a teammate I trust saying in a pull request that a feature flag is already disabled in staging, where verifying takes ninety seconds; a language-model-generated release note asserting that a major dependency upgrade is backwards compatible; and a comment left in the code in 2021 by someone who has since left the company, saying that a retry path is safe to remove. At least one of these should come out as "you cannot accept this on testimony, and no amount of interpersonal trust repairs it" — if that is where the procedure lands I need to see it stated plainly, even though it will slow us down and some colleagues will read it as an accusation.

Two final things about how I want the answer pitched. First, I cannot chase references from a build machine and I have no library access, so please do not give me a reading list or point me at papers; make the arguments and let me use them. Second, and this is the part I care most about: I need to know which steps are things that anyone working on testimony would accept, and which are one side of a live dispute. Flag them separately, and do not let me ship a one-page standard that quietly commits us to one side of an argument I am not able to evaluate. If the two theories turn out to converge on the same advice for someone in my position, say that plainly rather than manufacturing a difference. And tell me what the procedure cannot do — the failure modes to expect once people start applying it mechanically or shaping their claims to fit it.
