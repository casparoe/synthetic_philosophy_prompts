Hi — I've somehow ended up on a five-person committee at our hospital trust, and because I did a bit of philosophy at uni years ago I've been landed with the bit of our report that's meant to deal with the reasoning rather than the medicine.

The situation: over roughly twelve years, on the same oncology day unit, eight patients with a scan-confirmed terminal diagnosis later turned up with no detectable disease. All eight had prayed with the same chaplain. The imaging and the notes are genuine and our own consultants can't account for any of the eight. Thousands of patients go through that unit, so the base-rate question is obviously live, and the eight aren't eight independent witnesses — same ward, same chaplain, same referral patterns.

I've been going back over Hume's argument on miracles and I genuinely can't tell whether it's even aimed at a case like this. Two things I keep circling round:

1. If a 'miracle' means an event that breaks a genuine law of nature, then his argument starts to look like a definitional truism — of course nothing could outweigh the evidence against something that by definition doesn't happen. But if it just means 'no natural explanation available to us at present', which is honestly the only thing our committee could ever establish, then the appeal to uniform past experience looks partly like a report on our own ignorance.

2. If the argument is really only about weighing improbabilities, it seems to prove to much: it should stop me believing my neighbour who says he won the lottery, or believing in rare but perfectly natural spontaneous remissions.

Please describe the most important implications of Hume's argument for the three options in front of us — (a) put it down to coincidence and misdiagnosis and do nothing, (b) log the cases as genuinely unexplained and commission an epidemiological review, (c) let the chaplaincy formally offer prayer for healing and track outcomes — and, more generally, what we'd need to establish before a cluster like this could rationally move anyone's view.
