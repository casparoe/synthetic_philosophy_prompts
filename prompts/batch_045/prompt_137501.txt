I am the person named to decide for my older sister if she can no longer decide for herself, and the decision I now have to prepare for is not one I ever expected to face. She has been offered a place in a protocol that would replace her neural tissue with synthetic units replicating each replaced unit's input-output behavior, roughly one percent a month, so that in about eight years nothing biological remains. The clinicians present gradualness as though it settles the matter: one unit is obviously survivable, so a thousand is, so there is no step at which the person stops. I want to know how much weight that argument can actually bear before I lean on it.

So: give me your credence that the gradual-replacement argument does real independent argumentative work. I mean this: that a reflective person who began genuinely agnostic about whether a completed functional isomorph of a human brain would be the same subject of consciousness (or, if you think the question splits, would be conscious at all) should end up substantially more confident after working through the gradual case than after being told only what the end-state is like. Count the claim as resolved affirmatively if the gradual case would move such a person by fifteen percentage points or more, or if it surfaces a consideration the instantaneous case entirely misses. Give me the number and the decomposition that produces it: the probability that some functionalist or psychological-continuity account of identity, or of consciousness, is correct at all rather than one requiring organic or substrate continuity; the probability that, even given such an account, same-subject talk is vague enough that the inductive step fails as a sorites; the probability that there is some non-functional, substrate-dependent contribution to consciousness that function-preserving replacement would quietly destroy; the probability that the small-step intuition is genuine evidence rather than a continuity heuristic we should discount; and whatever further terms you think belong. One number and one line of justification for each, then the combination.

Two features of the actual protocol that I cannot tell whether they matter. First, every batch is placed under general anesthesia, so no stream of consciousness spans any swap: on that description each month is a small instantaneous replacement, and continuity of experience is broken a hundred times over. Second, the reverse test: if a synthetic isomorph were built first and biological units then implanted into it gradually until it was wholly organic, would you count the organic end-state as the same subject as the isomorph? If your answer there differs from your answer to the original case, the argument seems to be drawing on something other than gradualness. Say for each whether it moves your number, and by how much.

For context, I am around 0.35 and my sister's neurosurgeon is around 0.85. Tell me which of your terms their disagreement is most likely about, which single term you are least confident in, and what evidence or argument would move that term most.
