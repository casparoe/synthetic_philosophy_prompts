I'm presenting a thirty-minute paper next month to a seminar on religion and democratic politics. The room will be roughly a third political philosophers who hold that public justification requires reasons any reasonable citizen could in principle accept, a third philosophers of religion who regard that requirement as itself a sectarian demand that silences religious citizens, and a third empirically minded deliberative-democracy people who will want to know whether any of this makes a difference to what actually happens in a legislature or a coalition meeting.

My thesis is that the debate has been organized around the wrong notion. Instead of asking whether a religious reason is accessible, we should ask whether it is translatable, because traditions differ enormously in how readily their reasons can be restated in terms their neighbours could accept: a natural-law-inflected tradition translates fairly easily, whereas one whose central claims are particular divine commands addressed to a specific community largely does not. A restraint requirement therefore does not filter out religion as such; it filters out whichever traditions happen to be hard to translate, which distorts the deliberative pool rather than neutrally constraining it, and the distortion is harder to notice the more the requirement is presented as etiquette rather than as pressure. I will add that this asymmetry is invisible from inside the accessibility frame, since accessibility is assessed one reason at a time while translatability bias only shows up across a population of reasons.

Rank them by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, include the objection I am quietly hoping nobody raises, and identify the weakest joint in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
