I'm one of five trustees of a small family foundation. A donor's bequest of roughly $3.5 million has to be committed at our meeting in three weeks, and his will directs us to use it where it will do the most good. We have narrowed the field to two proposals.

The first is a ten-year scholarship and advising program at one under-resourced high school in our county: about $300,000 a year, with independent evidence about how much it raises graduation rates and later earnings, and with results we can audit ourselves.

The second is a grant of similar size to a small research group working on making advanced AI systems safer before they are widely deployed. Our own adviser gave us the honest version: the possible effects are enormous compared with anything else on our desk, but nobody can say whether this group's work makes the long-run outcome better or worse. Careful safety work might matter a great deal, might be ignored entirely, or might backfire by feeding into deployment or by drawing attention to hazards. No trustee disputes that description; the disagreement is about what follows from it.

Two of us think that when we cannot tell the sign of an effect, we should set the long-run consequences aside and compare only the near-term benefits we can actually measure. Two others think the stakes are so large that even a small chance of mattering dwarfs anything a scholarship program can do. I have tried to put my own view into words: the scholarship case does not depend on the long-run sign, because it has what I would call a visible floor of benefit, whereas the other proposal's whole case is a bet on a sign we have already admitted we cannot read. One trustee replied that a visible floor is not a floor at all if unseen effects can be negative and large enough to cancel it, and that our scholarship program has untraceable long-run effects of its own. I do not have a good answer to that.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — how much of the bequest to commit to each proposal, over what period, and under what conditions or later review — and it should give reasons that the trustees on the other side could actually respond to rather than merely restating my framing. Please also say plainly, at the end, where the recommendation is weakest and what argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different one. Do not cite studies or name authors; I need the reasoning, not sources.
