My friend and I got into a massive argument at a barbecue on Sunday — yes, while eating burgers, it was that kind of conversation — about whether eating animals is actually defensible, and it ended with her sending me a link to a podcast episode she says lays out the philosophical case properly. She's a med student, I did a philosophy minor years ago and mostly remember being confused, and I've been back and forth about giving up meat again for a while now, so I really want to know how much of this thing I should trust. I should of known better than to get into it without having read anything on it, but here we are.

The episode — I won't name it, the guy sounded like he knew his stuff but I've got no idea who he is — argues roughly like this. Moral status can't really be about what species you belong to, and the argument from marginal cases is what shows it: some humans, babies and people with advanced dementia and people with profound intellectual disabilities, have mental lives no richer than a pig's or a chimpanzee's, so if those humans get full moral status then consistency forces you to give it to the animals too, and if you won't give it to the animals you have to take it away from the humans, which nobody is willing to do. So what actually does the work isn't species, it's what's going on in the head: more or less how much neural machinery you've got and how much that lets you do. And the old story that humans are special because of our big cortex is dead, because neither brain size nor even neuron count puts us on top — elephants and several whale species have bigger brains than we do and by some counts more neurons, so if the criterion is neural machinery we aren't even the winner. Two things then follow. First: killing a cow for dinner is morally on a par with killing a human being whose mental life is comparable, so vegetarianism isn't a lifestyle choice, it's just what the argument forces. Second: if moral status is a scalar quantity that tracks neural machinery, then it adds up — a hundred pigs have a hundred times the moral claim of one pig, so anything that saves one human at the cost of a hundred pigs is straightforwardly wrong, and no hand-waving about human dignity gets you out of that.

Can you go through that and tell me what's fair and what isn't? I don't want citations or a reading list, I just want to know which bits someone who actually works on moral status or animal ethics would nod along to, which are the kind of simplification a popularizer makes that quietly changes the conclusion, and which are just wrong — and then whether the two consequences survive once the dodgy bits are repaired. I have a feeling he does something slippery when he moves from species membership can't be the criterion to neuron count is the criterion, and something else slippery when he says moral status just adds up, but I can't put my finger on either. I'm also pretty sure I've half-misremembered the whale and elephant neuron business, and I'd rather you not hang anything on exact numbers: treat the empirical claim as some animals are at least in our ballpark on whatever brain measure you pick, and tell me if the argument changes depending on how that actually turns out. Two more things I care about: is the argument from marginal cases really the knockdown dilemma he makes it sound like, where one horn is unthinkable, or is it something narrower and more technical than that? And I found myself reaching for a threshold view — anything above a certain level of capacity gets full status, below that gets partial weight in proportion — and my friend said that was just speciesism with extra steps. Is it? If there's a version of that move, or of any other move, that lets someone keep treating severely cognitively disabled humans as full equals without becoming a vegetarian, I want to hear the strongest form of it rather than a strawman, because the thing I actually can't tell is whether I'm being reasoned into something or talked into something.
