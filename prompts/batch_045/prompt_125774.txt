hey — so I'm taking a practical rationality seminar this term and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I can't find a name for, and it's starting to annoy me because it feels like it should already have one. We went over Parfit's hitchhiker last week, so I know that case, but what I'm after seems like a bigger family and the hitchhiker is only one member of it.

The pattern is: traits that pay off *only because* you can't switch them off at the moment they cost you.

Three instances I've actually seen:

1. My roommate is basically incapable of not paying people back — small amounts, people who'd never chase him for it, times when he's completely broke. Because of it people trust him with money, with keys, with their laptops, and he gets offered a lot of stuff. But if he could decide case by case whether to be like that, he'd obviously turn it off in half those cases, and I think the whole thing would evaporate — people seem to be picking up somehow on the fact that it isn't a decision for him.

2. Me and running. If I skip a scheduled run I feel genuinely awful for the rest of the day, guilt-awful, not just "oh well". It works way better than any app or stake I've tried, and those failed precisely because I could switch them off or just ignore them. Nobody is watching in this case, it's totally private, and I'd still rather have the guilt than not have it.

3. A guy I work shifts with at a coffee place gives refunds no questions asked, including to people who are obviously scamming him, and he's visibly hacked off about it every single time. The regulars all know, and the place does much better than the chain two doors down.

Conditions as I'd tentatively list them: (a) the trait makes you do something that on that occasion is against your own interests and that you'd rather not do; (b) it's not under voluntary control at the moment — and that's load bearing, not incidental; (c) it pays off across many occasions, and only if it's strong enough to win in each individual one, so being "a bit" like this is worth nothing; (d) the payoff normally runs through other people being able to rely on you — except in case 2, where it doesn't.

Is there an established concept this is an instance of? A friend says it's just costly signalling / the handicap principle, but case 2 has no audience at all, and in signalling the agent *chooses* to send the signal whereas here the point is that you can't choose at the moment. Someone else said "commitment device", but that's something you install on purpose and it works by messing with your options or your payoffs; this doesn't touch either. It isn't akrasia (that's a failure with no upside) and it isn't "rational irrationality" as I've seen it used, which is about holding false beliefs because being wrong is cheap — being disposed like this is expensive, that's the whole trick.

If there's no name for it, I want one, plus a definition and boundary cases: is an ordinary habit (brushing your teeth without deciding to) an instance? An addiction? A rigid principle you could in principle revise but won't? And is it part of the same thing or a separate thing that these traits seem to matter less where an institution does the job — like if everyone had a public reliability score, my roommate's disposition would be worth a lot less. Please don't just hand me a label: I want the nearest established concepts, exactly how each one misses, and where you'd draw the edges.
