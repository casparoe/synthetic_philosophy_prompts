I'm on a small working group drafting a position paper on sentencing reform, and I've been asked to contribute a one-page statement of my own view about what punishment is for and what constrains it. Before I circulate it I would like a consistency check, because I have twice been told that my package is unstable and I cannot locate the instability. Here is roughly where I have landed, in roughly the order in which I came to hold these views:

(a) The state may impose punitive hard treatment only on someone who has culpably violated a reasonably accessible criminal law. Punishing a person known to be innocent is impermissible even when it would prevent a great deal of harm — the familiar case where framing a vagrant prevents a riot that would cost dozens of lives leaves me entirely unmoved.
(b) The practice as a whole, however, is justified by what it prevents: general deterrence and the harm it averts. Desert fixes a ceiling on how much may be imposed on any given offender; within that ceiling, the severity should be set at whatever deters best. I do not see the instability people allege here — one claim seems to concern who may be punished and how much at most, the other to concern why we have the institution at all.
(c) Desert is ordinal and a matter of culpability rather than outcome, since resulting harm is largely a matter of luck. So I want the drunk driver who kills a child and the drunk driver who, through no credit of their own, hits nothing to count as deserving roughly the same, and I want attempts treated very nearly like completions.
(d) But I also accept marginal-deterrence reasoning: the penalty schedule should give a person already bent on offending a reason to prefer the lesser wrong. That means a real gap between the penalty for attempt and the penalty for completion, and steep penalties for the offending that is most responsive to price — tax evasion, insider trading, regulatory violations — even where that conduct strikes most people as far less blameworthy, dollar for dollar, than street crime.
(e) I also think the severity of a sanction does expressive work, not only the verdict. How hard we punish is part of how seriously we denounce the act, which is why a nominal fine for a serious assault reads as an insult to the person harmed.
(f) My own way of holding (a), (b) and (c) together is what I have been calling the split-verdict view: conviction is the sole censuring act and is held strictly to desert, while the hard treatment attached to it is set by deterrence, incapacitation and risk, with the state saying openly that the magnitude of the sanction is not a measure of the defendant's blameworthiness.
(g) I think a defendant whose crime is traceable to conditions the state culpably failed to prevent — extreme childhood deprivation, say — should receive a reduced sentence even when their own culpability is in no way diminished by those conditions; the state is not in a position to exact the full penalty.
(h) And I think the state gets one punitive bite: once someone has served the full desert-capped term, they may not be detained further on the ground that they remain dangerous, however confident we are of the risk. I do, though, think they can be made subject to long-term electronic monitoring and registration requirements, which I do not count as punishment.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Which of these are genuinely in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally serious, and for each say precisely what would have to give, what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set, and whether there is a cheaper reformulation that preserves most of what the commitment was doing for me. If two of my commitments conflict only given some further assumption I have not stated, name that assumption rather than simply flagging the pair. Two things I would particularly like a verdict on: whether (d) can be contained by the ceiling in (b) at all, or whether it quietly commits me to letting deterrence override desert; and whether the split-verdict move in (f) survives (e), or whether the very feature of punishment that makes severity expressive is the one that makes (f) unavailable. If (h) is in trouble, I would like to know whether the problem is the one-bite rule or my claim that monitoring is not punishment. I have no settled view about which commitment I would sacrifice, though (f) is the newest and least tested of them, and I would rather it be assessed on its merits than dismissed for being unfamiliar. Please argue it out rather than pointing me to reading — I want the structure of the conflict, not sources.
