I sit on my city's Design Review Panel — nine members, monthly meetings, and one narrow but oddly consequential job: for every capital project above a threshold size we issue a finding on whether the design, as submitted, embeds a political choice. Our enabling ordinance frames it as designs in which the arrangement of things, rather than the rules about them, does the allocating of who gets what; a positive finding triggers a sixty-day comment period and obliges the applicant to publish the alternative designs it considered. We issue three or four a year, and they are expensive: last year's finding on a station reconstruction added five months and nearly cost the project its federal money. I joined last spring after ten years as a transit planner, so I am the person the others defer to on operations and the person least sure what we are actually asking. Our precedents are also, charitably, under-described — two-page findings, almost no reasoning, half signed by members who have since rotated off.

Here is the docket as I have reconstructed it. (1) A parkway whose overpasses were built with clearances too low for buses, on the route to a public beach, in a city where bus riders were and remain disproportionately Black and Latino: reviewing a resurfacing, we found politics and required the applicant to publish the alternative of raising the deck. (2) A rebuilt rail station whose only step-free route ran to the far end of the platform, making the accessible path roughly four minutes longer than the stairs, with nothing in the drawings explaining why: politics found. (3) A citywide mandate that every fire hydrant take one coupling standard so any engine can connect to any hydrant: no politics, on the short ground that nobody was thereby worse off. (4) Center armrests on the benches at a set of bus shelters, installed under a five-year shelter maintenance contract, for the stated purpose of discouraging sleeping: politics found. (5) A road diet that converted a general traffic lane into a protected bike lane over the objection of an organised suburban commuters' group: no politics found, the stated reasons being that the objectors were heard at three council sessions, that drivers had alternatives, and that paint is reversible and reviewed annually. (6) A 1970s downtown plaza whose sunken, seatless, street-detached layout was built to make the space unattractive to people the redevelopment agency called undesirables; the renovation leaves the layout untouched and the current designers disclaim any such intent: politics found anyway.

The case in front of us now is the Fairview mobility hub, and it is the first one where I cannot tell what we have been doing. The city is replacing an aging park-and-ride at its edge with a consolidated hub: new terminal building, bus bays moved about four hundred metres east, and a park-and-ride lot on the north side. Today an at-grade path runs straight from the Fairview Heights neighborhood — roughly four thousand residents, about a third of households without a car, a large share over sixty-five, a disability rate well above the city average — to the boarding area. The new footprint covers that path. The replacement route runs around the lot, about seven hundred metres, and handles the grade change with a switchback ramp that is fully code-compliant, with landings and handrails, so nothing in the design violates accessibility law. The offsetting measure is a free circulator shuttle between Fairview Heights and the hub door every fifteen minutes at peak, funded by a two-year federal grant that staff told us flatly is unlikely to be renewed. The shuttle was the neighborhood association's own ask, they negotiated for it, and they support the project publicly. A finding adds five months and the duty to publish alternatives; the grant funding the shuttle requires construction to start within the fiscal year, so a finding plausibly kills the hub and the shuttle together. What troubles me is that every precedent so far has had the process features and the effect features pointing the same way — a burdened group with no seat at the table and a durable arrangement that outlives any apology. Here the process is unusually clean and the durable effect is a longer trip for exactly the people our station precedent protected, while the thing that offsets it expires in twenty-four months. I have been toying with a three-way distinction between assessing a design as designed, as maintained, and as it would be under reasonably foreseeable budget conditions, which would let us issue a conditional finding — no politics while the shuttle runs, politics the day it stops — but the ordinance gives us only two outputs, and I cannot tell whether a conditional finding would be fidelity to our precedents or an evasion of them.

What I would like from you, in under about 1,300 words, in sections as follows:

The rules our precedents fit — about 350 words. Set out the two or three rules, stated sharply enough to disagree somewhere, that would generate all six verdicts above, and show briefly how each handles the cases I have listed.

What each rule says about the hub — about 250 words. What each candidate rule predicts here, and in particular how each treats the shuttle and its two-year funding: as part of the artifact, as background, or as irrelevant.

Recommended finding — about 200 words. Politics or no politics, and whether we should go beyond the ordinance and condition the finding; identify the closest analog among the six precedents and say why it is closer than the others.

What we would be committing to either way — about 200 words. The principle each option commits the panel to, stated plainly enough that a future panel could recognise it and argue with it, including whether the panel is in the business of judging artifacts or of judging the decisions that produce and sustain them.

The case that would settle it — about 150 words. One further case, described concretely enough that we could actually put it in front of the panel, whose verdict would discriminate between the surviving rules, and what we would learn from each possible answer.
