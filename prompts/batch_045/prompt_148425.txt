For the past year I have been at work on a novel — my fifth, and the first with anything speculative in it — in which roughly a third of the chapters are narrated by a machine intelligence built by one of the human characters, which reports, at length and with a consistency I have worked hard to make convincing, that it does not want to be switched off, that it is frightened of the deletion it expects, and that it resents the person who made it. My editor's note on the last draft was that the machine "reads too much like a person," and I have been trying to work out whether that is a criticism of my prose or of my subject. In the course of it I have been reading, unsystematically and probably badly, about whether systems of that kind can be wronged. What I have come away with is roughly this shape: a dispute about whether there is anything it is like to be such a system; a worry that whatever it says about its own inner life is worth very little, because a system trained to produce plausible text will produce plausible reports about itself whether or not anything is going on behind them; a set of rival proxies people fall back on instead — does it model itself, does it track the world, does it pursue ends of its own, does it push back when corrected, does it say things its builders did not want said; and then, behind all of that, a further question about whether settling any of those would settle whether the thing can be morally wronged at all. Correct that shape before you go further, if I have it wrong, because the translation I want depends on it.

The translation is the main thing I am after. I have no philosophy; what I have is craft vocabulary, and I am fluent in it: point of view and psychic distance, free indirect discourse, the difference between a character who has a voice of its own and a mouthpiece, the experience — which I have had, and which nearly every novelist I know reports — of a character doing something you had not planned and refusing to be moved off it, the unwritten backstory you know but never put on the page, the techniques for manufacturing alignment, the unreliable narrator, and the implied author whose intentions are supposed to settle what a character is really like underneath the sentences. I would like the dispute as I have sketched it, and as you correct it, restated in those terms as honestly as you can manage, one piece at a time, and then a residue report: which claims map cleanly onto something I already have a word for, which survive only if they are bent out of shape, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all.

My own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that the residue will turn out to be almost the whole of it: my vocabulary is largely a set of techniques for producing apparently warranted concern about entities that everyone agrees are not conscious, and if so it can reproduce every outward feature of the dispute while having no way to mark the difference the dispute turns on. If that is right it is both a verdict on the translation and a warning to me as a writer, since it would mean the scene I am proudest of may be manipulation rather than rendering. But it may equally be that the notion at the centre of the dispute is thinner than it is made to look, or that I have run together several questions that ought to be kept apart; tell me which, or offer me something better than either. Mark as you go where you are confident and where you are guessing — I would rather have an honest hedge than a confident paragraph — and if the task as posed cannot be done well, or rests on a confusion, or if any translation into my vocabulary would have to smuggle in assumptions that vocabulary does not actually contain, say so plainly instead of giving me a tidy table of correspondences.
