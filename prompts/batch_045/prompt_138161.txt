I'm writing a ~5,000 word paper for a grad seminar on democratic theory, and I'd like it to double as a prospectus for a bigger project later, so it needs to read like a plan for work I haven't done yet, not like an op-ed.

The background: I got into an argument with my flatmate about compulsory voting (she's Australian so she thinks it's totally normal, I was raised on the forcing-people-to-vote-is-illiberal line), the conversation slid into whether 16-year-olds should vote, and then into whether long-term legal residents who aren't citizens should get the vote in national elections. What struck me afterwards is that these three debates almost never talk to each other and they run on completely different arguments. Compulsory voting is defended in terms of civic duty, or civic virtue, or as anti-corruption / anti-free-riding. Lowering the voting age is defended either by claiming teenagers are competent enough or by appealing to something like the all-affected-interests principle (they'll live longest with the consequences, climate etc.). Non-citizen voting gets the all-affected principle plus some denizenship or stakeholder story about being subject to the law. And the objections are different too: coercion or illiberalism in the first case, competence in the second, membership and collective self-determination in the third. My supervisor's read is that this is just three separate applied questions and I should pick one, and I don't want to do that.

Here's the angle I want to try, which I'm not sure survives. Standard thinking treats voting as something an individual does — expresses a preference, exercises autonomy, participates in authorizing a government — and on that picture the three questions really are separate. But there's another picture on which what matters is the aggregate output, and the electorate matters as a kind of sample of the governed: with voluntary turnout the people who actually show up are systematically unrepresentative (older, richer, more educated, more partisan), and that makes outcomes worse in a way that has nothing to do with anyone's individual duty or self-development. Call it the electorate-as-sample view. It has some consequences I can't get my head around and that's the part I want to make into a question. (i) It's not a duty argument at all, so it should be immune to the liberal coercion objection, but it also seems to make voting *optional in principle* if you could get representativeness some other way, which feels like it gives up too much. (ii) Sampling logic doesn't require any individual unit to be competent or well-informed — a poll doesn't need every respondent to be an expert, it needs an unbiased sample and enough of them — so the competence objection to lowering the voting age loses most of its bite, which seems too fast and is probably where the view breaks. (iii) Sampling logic forces you to say who exactly the population is that you're sampling from, which pushes toward including non-citizen residents (they're governed too) and then toward making them vote as well, and toward residence thresholds that look arbitrary in a way that matters much more under this view than under a membership view. There's probably a fourth thing: if the sample is what matters, it's unclear why we should care about *ballots* rather than, say, compulsory turnout with a formal abstention option.

Give me the question stated precisely enough that it has some determinate answer rather than being three questions stapled together; say why the existing positions (duty-based and virtue-based defences of compulsory voting, competence-based and affectedness-based arguments about who gets the franchise, membership-based objections to non-citizen voting) leave it open; give me the method — what I'd actually do section by section over a semester, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives (I specifically don't want a comparative survey of which countries do what, and I don't want to spend the paper on empirical turnout research — assume I can stipulate the empirical claims and say so); say what a positive result would look like and what a useful negative result would look like; and be honest about what would count as the project failing. My supervisor's worry, which I partly share, is that electorate-as-sample is just sortition with extra steps, or a metaphor dressed up as an argument, and that even if it works it can't generate determinate answers about age or residence thresholds.
