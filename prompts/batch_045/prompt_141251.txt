I've been reading about moral particularism and generalism on my own — no classes, just books and a long-running argument with a friend — and the argument has stalled in a way I think only a well-built case can fix. So I'd like a case constructed to order.

My friend's position: as he understands it, particularism says only that reasons can be outweighed or defeated, that you can't read an all-things-considered verdict off a single feature. Everyone already believes that, generalists included, he says. So particularism is either a truism or it collapses into the claim that morality can't be taught, argued about, or learned from cases — which he takes to be obviously false. I want a case that shows the interesting claim is stronger than defeat, and I want it built so that he can't wave it off as a stipulation.

Specifications:

1. Two ordinary situations, one paragraph each: a workplace, a family, a clinic, a neighborhood. No trolleys, no science fiction, no exotic roles or institutions. The two cases should share a thin non-moral description — same kind of agent, same kind of act — and differ by the addition of exactly one further non-moral fact. Call the shared feature F and the act X.
2. In case A, F is a reason to do X, and doing X is what should be done. In case B — A plus the one extra fact — F is *still* a reason to do X; I don't want it to go silent, and I don't want a plain defeat where it simply gets outweighed by something independent of it. But in B, F is also part of what makes some other feature G into a reason *not* to do X. So F favours doing X and, in the same situation, enables a reason against doing X. And in B, all things considered, X shouldn't be done. That double role is the thing my friend denies can happen.
3. The difference between the cases must not be traceable to consequences, or to a change in the agent's aims or motives. Hold outcomes fixed across the two cases as far as you honestly can.
4. The pull has to come from the setup, not from my say-so. I don't want to have to add "and intuitively, here F is a reason against." A careful reader should feel the flip before it's named.
5. Anticipate his repair: he'll say the generalist just states a more specific principle, or adds a ceteris paribus or "unless defeated" clause, and gets the right answer anyway. So show what that repair costs: whether the extra condition in the more specific principle can be stated in general terms at all, rather than being a disguised proper name for this case; and whether a hedged principle can still guide someone facing the case for the first time, before they know how it comes out.

After the scenario, walk through how a strong generalist, a hedged or defeasible generalist, and a particularist each describe both cases; then say which structural feature of the case is doing the double-role work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how my friend would best attack it. If some part of my specification can't be met without cheating, say so plainly and give me the nearest specification that can be met.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in what you claim about what each view is committed to, and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd rather have an honest boundary marker I can concede than a confident overreach I can't defend when he pushes back.
