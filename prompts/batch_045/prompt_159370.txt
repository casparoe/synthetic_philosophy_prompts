I am writing the third chapter of my dissertation on how we explain participation in mass killing, and my supervisor—who will read it, and whose position on this literature is well known to me in outline but not in its principle—has given a crisp verdict every time one of these cases has come up in our reading group. I would like to anticipate her verdict on the case I have to write up next. I am less interested in the verdict than in the rule she is running, in the other rules that fit her record just as well, and in which of them the new case actually separates.

Her verdicts so far:

1. The reserve police battalion at Józefów in July 1942—middle-aged Hamburg policemen too old for the front, not selected for zeal, who shot the Jews of a Polish village and then went on to a year and a half of killing and deportation work in occupied Poland—she explains situationally: peer conformity, deference to authority, routinization, alcohol, and the reluctance to be the man who breaks ranks. She makes much of Major Trapp's offer to let any man who found the work too hard step out, and of the fact that only a handful of roughly five hundred took it without being punished for it; and of the men who afterwards drifted to the edge of the pit, shot wide, or found errands elsewhere. The cruelty she treats as real but secondary: a hard core, plus the bottle.

2. The guards of the death marches and of the camps in the last months of the war, who beat and killed prisoners whom the labour authorities wanted kept alive, she explains by internalized motivation: the cruelty is surplus to any order, and it continued while authority and oversight were collapsing. She adds that these men had already passed through earlier rounds of killing and had not taken the chances to get out that earlier rounds offered.

3. A locally recruited volunteer militia in a district where a pogrom had taken place under the previous occupying power two years earlier she explains by local culture, grievance, and opportunity for loot—but she insisted the culture be specified locally and tested against differences between districts, and she has repeatedly refused to explain anything by a national culture treated as a monolith.

4. The transport and administration officers who arranged deportations from desks and never fired a shot she explains by careerism, deference to expertise, and the fragmentation of responsibility; and she has said more than once that the ordinary-men and willing-executioners boxes are not exhaustive.

5. On method: the laboratory obedience literature can establish that a mechanism is possible under specified conditions, but not that it operated in any given field case; a field attribution requires that the mechanism's preconditions be documented in that case. Character explanations she regards as circular unless the character is identified independently of the deeds.

The case I have to write up next is a composite I have built from several units, and it has these features: about three hundred men recruited in the autumn of 1942 from a prisoner-of-war camp in which inmates were dying of starvation and disease in their thousands every month, where enlisting meant food, boots and a chance of survival, and where recruits were told only that they would do guard duty; mixed in nationality, including men from groups the occupying power classified as racially inferior and a few from groups it was itself killing; six weeks of drill and guard instruction with no ideological curriculum beyond two lectures; a first killing action on the fourth day after arrival, with no ramp-up through lesser acts; two men who refused on that first day, shot in front of the unit, and no recorded refusals afterwards; seven months of near-universal participation in roundups and shooting operations; gratuitous cruelty—beatings, forced humiliations, killing of prisoners certified as needed for labour—concentrated in roughly a fifth of the men, while the remainder shot, guarded and returned to barracks; an eleven-day midwinter withdrawal of the German personnel during which the killings continued at the same rate while the gratuitous cruelty fell sharply and resumed when the Germans returned; and postwar testimony in which some say they had no choice, some describe the work as boring routine, and a small number recall satisfaction with it.

What I want from you: your best guess at her verdict, stated plainly; the single rule she seems to be running; at least two further rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts equally well, with what each of them predicts about this case—they should not all predict the same thing; and one further case, specified in the same vocabulary of features, that would best tell the live rules apart. If you think her precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, say what she would be committing herself to under each option, and say whether the honest verdict here is neither of her two usual boxes but a third mechanism—brutalization, careerism, role-internalization, emulation of the supervisors—since she has left that door open.

Close with a single final line of exactly this form, with the letter and a phrase of no more than about eight words: \answer{X — short phrase}, where X is one of: A, situational or structural, no inference to internalized motivation warranted; B, internalized motivation, eliminative or ideological; C, dispositional, men brutalized or hardened by prior exposure rather than ideologically motivated; D, split verdict, a motivated minority inside a coerced majority; E, she would decline to rule and demand further evidence.
