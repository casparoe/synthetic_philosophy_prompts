I keep teaching graduate seminars on Fear and Trembling and I keep watching the same deadlock form, and after the third time round I no longer think it's my students' fault. Half the room reads the binding as a real suspension: the single individual relates absolutely to the absolute, so there's an absolute duty to God that can override the universal, and the reason Abraham can't explain himself to Sarah or Isaac on the three-day ride isn't cowardice but the fact that there is no universalizable story to tell. The other half insists that nothing is really suspended at all — either Abraham believes, by virtue of the absurd, that he'll get Isaac back, so he never intends to kill anybody and the whole thing is a test of trust rather than a moral conflict; or the book is a sustained irony and Johannes de Silentio is advertising his own failure to understand the very faith he's praising; or it's just a tragic dilemma, two genuine obligations colliding, and we don't need a third sphere to describe it. My colleague in religious studies then says we've all missed the point, that the interesting question was never about permission but about what kind of self the movement of faith is supposed to produce. Everyone leaves the room where they came in.

The thing I keep banging on is what I've started calling the unstayed hand problem. Run the story with no angel and no ram: Abraham's knife comes down. If the suspension reading has to say that he was within his rights, religious commitment appears to swallow moral assessment whole, and I don't see how you then criticize anybody who kills for a voice. If it says he was not within his rights, then the suspension never did any normative work at all and the dramatic claim evaporates into a bluff. Alongside that there's an audit problem: because the criterion for faith, if there is one, has to be private in the way Abraham's silence is private, any test we propose is either public and therefore the wrong test, or private and therefore no test at all. My working hypothesis — and I want it treated as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that this isn't a substantive moral disagreement but three questions sharing a heading: what faith is, what's permitted, and what the word for the ethical even picks out, given that it could mean Hegelian ethical life, a universal moral law, or just whatever the neighbours would say. There may also be a hidden question about how much of the book is ventriloquism doing the real work.

So: diagnose it. Verbal or substantive, competing questions under one name, or a standoff between bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Defend it against what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress for a room like mine — a distinction, a reformulation, a change of question — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why. Assume I know the 1843 text but basically none of the secondary literature, and I don't want names, papers or a reading list, just the structure of the disagreement. Lead with one sentence giving your diagnosis, then spend the rest of it defending that and answering the progress question. Hard limit: 650 words.
