I am giving the remarks at the unveiling of a commemorative plaque next month at Bramwell Memorial Hospital, for a prenatal nutrition study that ran in its clinic from 1963 to 1969. I chair the committee that drafted the plaque, and I will be standing beside it when it is uncovered. I need the text of the speech: roughly nine hundred to a thousand words, six to eight minutes delivered, in American English.

The room will hold about forty surviving participants together with their children and grandchildren; the hospital board and its current institutional review board; two local reporters; and a bioethicist from the state university who advised us and who will notice if I say something careless. At the public listening sessions, several of the women said that they are not victims and resent being described as victims, and said in the same breath that they would have signed the form anyway if they had been told the truth, because the clinic was the only place within sixty miles that would see them without payment. Several others have not spoken at all.

The following is established in our report and should be treated as given. The study's stated purpose, on a single-page form, was to test whether a vitamin tonic improved pregnancy outcomes, and participants were told they would receive the tonic. In fact half of them were assigned to an inert pill, and the design's point was that arm: the investigators wanted a baseline for what happens in pregnancy without supplementation. Women in that arm were not told they might receive nothing, and the form did not mention the possibility. The form existed only in English; for patients whose first language was not English, a bilingual nurse explained it, and in at least some of those conversations she used the word in their language for a strengthening medicine rather than the word for an experimental preparation. One participant was seventeen; the age of majority in the state was then twenty-one, and her mother was not asked. Finally, a number of participants kept returning to the clinic for other care for years afterward, and some went on seeing the same physicians after a local paper reported the study in 1971.

The question of what makes consent valid is not decoration here; it decides what we can honestly put on the plaque. The usual three grounds — deception, coercion, impaired competence — are all in play, and none of them fits cleanly, which is the difficulty I want the speech to face rather than smooth over. I have my own candidate for the central failure, which I have been arguing in committee and which you should feel free to reject: that what went wrong is neither that the women were misinformed nor that they were pressured, but a failure of scope — they were asked to agree to one kind of thing, receiving a tonic as part of a study of tonics, and were enrolled in a different kind of thing, a study of what happens when the tonic is withheld, which is not something a fully informed person could have agreed to by agreeing to the first. If you think that is just deception relabeled, say so in the note below and write the speech on whatever footing you think is right.

Constraints on the text. It must say plainly what was done, with no appeals to the standards of the time. It must not call the women victims or imply that their later attendance ratified anything, and it must not say anything about them that a woman in the room could reasonably call false. It must not read to the participants' families as a lecture on the conditions of valid consent, and it must not be inaccurate in a way the bioethicist would object to. Do not build it around any famous historical case or well-known ethical scandal; comparisons to those would read as borrowed outrage and would insult the people in the room.

Also write the plaque text the speech leads up to, no more than forty words, which a participant's grandchild could read without wincing and the hospital could still defend.

After the speech and the plaque text, add a note of at most 250 words: state how confident you are in the normative claims the speech commits me to, and single out the one you are least confident in, including whether my scope framing does real work or is a relabeling of something more familiar, and whether any part of the disagreement it papers over is merely verbal.
