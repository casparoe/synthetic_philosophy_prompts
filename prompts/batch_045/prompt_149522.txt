A phonologist friend of mine says the Letter Spirit question — what makes every 'a' an 'a', what has to stay put across fonts for a letter to still be that letter — is just the phoneme/allophone distinction in a nicer jacket. On his version: 'a' is to its glyphs as /t/ is to [tʰ], [t], [ɾ], [ʔ]; there's no essence to discover, just an equivalence class a system hands you. He uses it to say the old project of getting a program to bootstrap a whole alphabet from a few seed letters (Hofstadter and co.) is chasing a ghost: once you've been told which glyphs count as a's, nothing is left to explain. He also thinks the glottal-stop case strenghtens him — a London [ʔ] shares no place or manner with a [t] and we still call it /t/ — but I suspect that example cuts the other way.

I can't say why compactly, so: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table (roles, the letter inventory, the font, the type designer, the reader, is 'a' vs 'o' really a minimal pair), then what it smuggles in that letterforms may not have — a closed finite inventory defined by contrast, allophones sharing articulatory substance, categories nobody designed and nobody can botch — where the mapping actually breaks, which of those breaks matter for his "nothing left to explain" conclusion and which don't, and whether that conclusion survives. And if phonemes are the wrong source, what's a better one: melody across performances, a dish across kitchens, something else?
