I spent thirty-one years in experimental physical chemistry, mostly on reaction kinetics, and since retiring I have been auditing philosophy seminars and, for the past eighteen months, convening a discussion group of my own. I would be grateful for a session design I can actually run.

The group: fourteen people, nearly all retired, ninety minutes on a Tuesday morning, in a room with four round tables and no technology I care to use. About half have some technical background (two engineers, a physician, a retired actuary); the rest are readers of history and literature. They will follow arithmetic if it is written on the board, they will not follow formalism, and they turn hostile to jargon within about four minutes. No real money may change hands; poker chips, index cards, dice, envelopes, and a cardboard screen are all available.

The material I have in mind is a decision problem I met in a book on rational choice and have been turning over ever since. A motorist drives a highway with two exits. Leaving at the first exit pays 0, leaving at the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 1. He can adopt any rule he likes before setting out, and he will remember the rule; he may also carry a randomizing device and use it fresh at each intersection. What he cannot do is tell, on arriving at an intersection, whether it is the first or the second, or recall whether he has already passed one.

My own arithmetic, which I would like checked and corrected if it is wrong: committing in advance to continue with probability 2/3 is worth 4/3 per trip. At an intersection, however, three-fifths of the driver's visits to intersections are visits to the first one, and on that reckoning taking the exit looks worth 8/5 against 1 for continuing, so he abandons the plan — and if he abandons it at the first intersection he collects nothing at all.

What I want participants to feel is not that there is a trick somewhere, but that one small problem licenses two recommendations, each of which seems unanswerable when you are standing in the position it addresses, and that the disagreement can be located in exactly one place: what the number "the chance that this is the first intersection" is a chance over. My suspicion is that 3/5 arises because intersection visits are being sampled rather than trips, the same length-biased sampling that makes the bus you are waiting for appear to run late. I do not want that said in the room. I want it to be something a participant can stumble on.

The wrinkle I insist on building in is that recall should be a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing. Suppose that at each intersection there is a lamp, drawn independently at each intersection and visible to the driver: lit with probability 0.8 at the second intersection and 0.2 at the first. The driver sees the lamp but not the intersection number, and may make his rule depend on it. I have worked two cases by hand. At reliability 0.8 the best rule I can find is "continue if the lamp is dark, leave if it is lit," worth 2.72 per trip (against 4/3 with no lamp and 4 with perfect recall), and each branch of that rule endorses itself when evaluated at the intersection. At reliability 0.6 the best rule has, to within a rounding error, the same shape and is worth 1.68, but the lit branch no longer endorses itself: continuing looks worth about 1.95 against about 1.89 for leaving. So the two perspectives appear to come apart at 0.6 and to agree at 0.8, with the shape of the rule unchanged between them. I have not located the boundary and I do not want the session to hang on my having found it; please check these numbers, tell me plainly if they are wrong, and design around the corrected ones.

Please give me:

1. The activity itself: the roles, the rules, the materials, the payoff accounting in chips, how the driver's ignorance is actually enforced in a room where everyone can see everyone, how randomization is enforced, how the tally is kept, and how many trips per condition with a reason for that number — enough that the difference between a plan worth 4/3 and a deviation worth 0 is visible in the chips, few enough to run the whole thing twice in ninety minutes. It must work for people who have never computed an expected value, and it must not require me to explain anything first.

2. How the lamp variant is staged so that participants discover, rather than being told, that the interesting thing about partial recall is not the value of the information but the fact that somewhere between reliability 0.6 and 0.8 the argument made at the intersection stops conflicting with the plan even though the rule being used has not changed shape.

3. A debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific features of the structure, including what to do with the person who announces that the driver should simply keep a notepad, and what to do with the person who works out where he is by counting how many times I have said "you are at an intersection." Say which condition runs first and why, and how I keep the first condition from contaminating the second.

4. An honest note on what the game cannot reproduce and that only I can say: in particular that nobody in the room can literally forget, that the belief at the intersection has to be handed to the participants by me, and that whether the reasoning of the person at the intersection is simply a mistake or has a genuine claim on us is not something the session can settle.

5. At least four predictable ways this goes wrong the first time I run it, each with what I should do in the moment rather than in advance.

6. One closing paragraph, and only one, on what any of this has to do with acting on a fact about your own history that you can only partly observe — I have in mind a daily dose of a drug where a double dose is dangerous and memory is not reliable. The group will not sit still for more than a paragraph.

After the design, give me a run-of-show table with exactly these columns: Phase; Clock time from the start; What participants do; What I do; What they should be noticing by the end of the phase; Materials needed.

Then a single JSON object with exactly these keys: "core_mechanism" (one sentence: the feature of the rules that generates the clash); "moment_the_idea_lands" (the moment in the session when participants first feel it, and what they typically say out loud); "predictable_failures" (an array of objects with keys "failure", "why_it_happens", "in_the_moment_fix", at least four entries); "facilitator_must_supply" (array of strings); "cannot_reproduce" (array of strings); "leaky_variant" (an object with keys "reliability_values_used", "what_participants_find", "what_i_say_if_it_fails_to_reproduce"); "arithmetic_check" (an array of objects with keys "my_claim", "verdict" — one of "correct" or "incorrect" — and "correction", covering the 4/3, the 3/5, the 8/5, the 2.72, the 1.68, and the two 0.6-branch figures).

Please write in American English.
