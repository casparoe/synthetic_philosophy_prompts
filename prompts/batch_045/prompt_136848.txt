I should be grateful for a sustained argument in defence of one side of a puzzle I have been worrying at since retiring from forty years of physical chemistry and taking up some reading in decision theory.

The puzzle is a variant of the familiar case of the absent-minded driver, which I shall restate in my own terms so that nothing hangs on anyone's recollection of how the case is usually told.

A motorist is driving home along a motorway at night. The route has two junctions, indistinguishable one from another, and he must turn off at the second: turning off at the first lands him in a thoroughly unpleasant district, which we may score as 0; turning off at the second brings him home, scored as 4; driving past both means a night in a motel, scored as 1. Take these as utilities. His difficulty is that he cannot tell the junctions apart by their appearance, he retains no memory of having passed the first, and he has no other cue.

Now the variant. He has fitted a mechanical tally counter to the steering column, of the sort one presses with the thumb. He presses it each time he passes a junction without turning off, and he glances at the drum on drawing up to each junction, before deciding whether to turn off. The mechanism is worn: each press has an independent probability q of failing to advance the drum, and it never advances spuriously, so the counter can only undercount. The motorist, being a careful fellow, has measured q in his garage and knows its value. The counter therefore reads 0 when he draws up to the first junction with certainty, and reads 1 when he draws up to the second just in case the press at the first registered. Assume the mechanics of the story as stated: no marking of the drum, no stopping to think, no other instrumentation.

The claim I should like to see defended is this: that for every failure rate q, the plan which maximises the motorist's expected score when he sets out is also a best response when he is in fact sitting at a junction with the drum showing 0 — provided that, on drawing up there, he apportions his credence between the two junctions in proportion to how often, under the plan he is actually executing, a junction-visit with that reading occurs at each of them. On that way of apportioning credence the plan turns out to be exactly borderline in the interesting region of parameter space, and the familiar impression that the absent-minded motorist must revise his plan at the roadside is an artefact either of a mistaken rule for self-locating belief or of crediting him with a discrimination between the two junctions that the story does not allow.

In defending it, please do the following.

First, set out the strategy space explicitly — what the motorist may do on a reading of 0 and on a reading of 1 — and derive his expected score at the outset as a function of his strategy and of q, and hence the optimum. I suspect the optimum falls into two regimes according to q, but correct me if I am wrong.

Second, state the credence rule being proposed, distinguish it clearly from the rival rule which treats the two junctions as equiprobable on arrival, and give a reason for preferring it that does not simply assume the conclusion.

Third, verify the best-response claim at the roadside, saying precisely in which regime the motorist is indifferent between his planned action and its alternative, and in which regime he strictly prefers the planned action.

Fourth, confront the strongest objection you can find. I have in mind two lines in particular: the motorist who says that, conditional on being at a junction at all, he is free to re-optimise his future behaviour and so need not take his own continuation probability as fixed; and the objection that a credence which depends on the plan one happens to be executing is circular, or has been gerrymandered to produce agreement with the ex-ante calculation.

Fifth, check the two limiting cases: a perfectly reliable counter, and a counter so unreliable as to be evidentially worthless, which should return you to the unmodified puzzle — confirm that your numbers agree with whatever you take the standard answer there to be.

Finally, draw out the moral in a few sentences: what does the exercise show about the relation between optimality ex ante and optimality at the moment of action, and about how much weight the notion of a plan can bear when an agent cannot tell his own decision points apart?

Please argue from the mathematics and the structure of the case rather than from the literature: I am interested in the reasoning, not in who has said what, and I would rather have your own derivation than a survey of opinions.
