I am in the third year of a doctorate on vagueness, and by an accident of committee seniority I have become the postgraduate member of my faculty's Academic Conduct Committee, which is rewriting its Code of Conduct this academic year. Because of what I work on, I have ended up as the person who vets the wording of every clause before it goes to the full committee and then to the university's legal officer. I would be grateful for a procedure I can actually hand to the other members.

The constraints matter. There are about sixty clauses to check, and realistically no one will spend more than fifteen minutes on each. The other members are a chemist, a historian and a deputy registrar; the legal officer is unsympathetic to anything that sounds like theory, and reads vagueness as drafting laziness. Findings under the Code are appealed on the ground that a student could not reasonably have known what was prohibited, so the stakes are real, and the wording will bind us for five years.

What I want is a decision procedure, in the form of ordered questions, that returns one of three verdicts on a clause's operative predicate: sharp enough as drafted; vague but defensibly so; or impermissibly vague, which means sharpen it with a stated cut-off or delete it. For each question, say what would count as a sufficient answer, and say where the procedure stops with a no and where it must hand the decision back to the committee because the issue is one of values rather than of meaning. Please be explicit about which distinctions survive the simplification and which you have deliberately dropped as unusable by non-philosophers.

Four features of the draft Code are giving me trouble, and I would like the procedure to handle them rather than treat them as noise:

1. Chained definitions, where the clause's predicate inherits its vagueness by cross-reference — for instance a prohibition defined by reference to accepted practice in the discipline, which is itself glossed elsewhere as what a reasonable practitioner would do. The vagueness is not local to the clause, so testing the clause in isolation gives the wrong answer.
2. Aggregate predicates such as a persistent pattern of minor breaches or systematic disregard, where every individual instance is clearly below threshold and what is judged is the sequence. I am not convinced this is the same disease: the predicate does not look tolerant in the sorites sense, since one further instance can matter decisively. If it is a different problem, the procedure should say so and handle it separately.
3. Multi-dimensional predicates, where two vague dimensions trade off against each other — significance in severity against significance in duration — so that there is no single dimension along which to run a sorites series.
4. Deliberate vagueness and precedent drift. Sometimes we want the penumbra: it delegates judgement, avoids over- and under-inclusion, and is cheaper than precision. But our habit of resolving vagueness case by case means each decision fixes a new borderline, so after twenty decisions the line has migrated without anyone having chosen to move it. The procedure should say when vagueness should be retained, when it should be resolved by precedent rather than by definition, and what to do about the drift — a scheduled re-anchoring, a sunset clause, or something else.

Please apply the finished procedure to three clauses from the current draft and show the working:

- No member of the University shall cause significant disruption to teaching or examination.
- Uncollegial behaviour towards other members of the University is prohibited.
- A persistent pattern of minor breaches may be treated as a single finding of misconduct.

Include at least one clause where sharpening makes things worse — where a bright line invites gaming, so that seven hours and fifty-nine minutes of notice becomes attractive — and say how the procedure tells a drafter when a cut-off is worse than a penumbra. And please give me one verdict I will not like: my suspicion is that the procedure will condemn the Committee's catch-all referring to conduct contrary to the spirit of the Code, which the Chair is attached to, but if your procedure in fact condemns something else, say that instead and say plainly that we will not enjoy hearing it.

Two requests about how you write. First, be committed rather than survey-like: do not set out the menu of theories of vagueness and leave me to choose. Take a definite position on the practical upshots you build into the procedure — for example, whether borderline cases are to be treated as cases in which there is an unknowable fact for the Committee to find, or as cases in which the Committee is choosing where to put a line — and give your reasons. Then tell me, in a short closing note, which contested commitments the procedure builds in and what breaks if I drop them. Second, please write in British English. I would expect something in the region of 1,000 to 1,200 words for the procedure and applications together, plus the note.
