I'm on the maintenance committee of a small housing co-op — 24 units, we fix everything ourselves, nobody here has any kind of academic background — and we've been going round in circles for three meetings about the bike room. Right now it's a cheap mechanical lock with a code that everyone in the building knows and that we change roughly never. The proposal is to replace it with a phone-app smart lock: the app logs who opened the door and when, and whoever is set as admin can revoke someone's access instantly from their phone. One member voted no on the grounds that "artifacts have politics" — her point being that the app lock is a managerial, surveilling kind of object and that picking it is already a political decision, quite apart from who ends up as admin. Another member said a lock is a lock, it's a neutral tool, and since we would be the admin it can't do anything to us. A friend of mine who reads this kind of thing told me the phrase is the title of an old essay by someone called Langdon Winner (1980, I think) and that he supposedly splits artifacts into ones that *require* a particular social arrangement and ones that are merely *strongly compatible* with one, but she couldn't tell me why that split matters or which half a bike-room lock falls into, and honestly it sounded like the same distinction in fancier words.

So: can you lay out, as a list I can bring to the next meeting, the different precise things "artifacts have politics" could be claiming, ordered from the boring/trivial end to the wild/radical end — along with which of them are basically platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are probably false, and which one the no-voter needs in order for her argument to actually work rather than just sounding good? I especially want to know which readings would survive the objection that *we* would be the admin, and which ones quietly depend on the artifact being owned by someone hostile, because that feels like the crux and I can't tell if the slogan is saying something about the object itself or about the object-plus-who-controls-it.

I've also got a few half-baked distinctions of my own that I'd like you to either sharpen or shoot down, and maybe turn into tests we can actually run on a proposal in ten minutes while standing in the bike room: (1) the *omissions* thing — the new system has no physical key override and no way to see the log as a resident, and it feels like what a thing refuses to do counts for as much as what it does, but I can't say why; (2) the *time* thing — we're the admin now, but the building could be sold to a landlord in ten years and the same hardware would still be in the door, so is the politics supposed to be stable across changes in ownership or is it allowed to switch; (3) the *scale* thing — one smart lock is nothing, but the vendor's dashboard aggregates every building on it, so maybe "has politics" is a threshold claim that only kicks in at some size; (4) the *breakdown* thing — when the app is down or the vendor goes bust, whatever social arrangement we fall back on might be the real tell. If some of these are just different readings in disguise, say so; and please flag clearly which readings are unfalsifiable, i.e. which ones no test could ever disprove and which are therefore just a way of winning arguments. Sorry this is long, and definately let me know if I've missed a reading that the actual debate turns on.
