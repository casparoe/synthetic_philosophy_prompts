Bit of an odd one, but here goes. I'm a philosophy Phil professor, and I'm putting together a paper on whether "collapse" is a descriptive notion at all or whether it's doing evaluative work under cover of a description. My archaeologist colleague down the corridor keeps leaning on this analogy whenever I raise the point: a society is like an organism — it's born, it grows, it matures, it senesces, it dies. He uses it to licence two conclusions at once, which is what makes me suspicious. First: collapse is endogenous, it's senescence, so the blame-the-drought / blame-the-Sea-Peoples stories are missing the structural point and an organism needs no external cause to die. Second: collapse is a real ending, the death of a thing that mattered, so the people who say these societies "merely changed" are being squeamish.

Help me audit it. Three cases I keep coming back to: the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE (palace centres and their administrative scripts gone, long-distance trade contracting hard, but plenty of continuity in settlement and material culture in the Levant), the Classic Maya (the monumental dated inscriptions stop, the divine kingship goes, but Maya people, languages and farming carry straight on to today), and Rapa Nui (monument-building ends, the population figures are fiercely contested, and there's a nasty contact-era confound). I want the correspondence table — what plays the role of the genome, the birth, the metabolism, the senescence, the death, the corpse, the offspring — and then a separate list of where the mapping breaks.

The bit I actually want you to get your teeth into is this distinction I've been using, which isn't in the literature as far as I know: I want to sever three things that normally travel together. Call them the three Ds. Depopulation: fewer bodies. De-complexification: fewer moving parts — fewer levels of settlement hierarchy, no writing, no standing administration, in the Tainter-ish idiom a drop in investment with diminishing returns. And de-legitimation: the "we" dissolves, nobody any more takes themselves to be acting as the thing, no one claims to be its successor. My hunch is that the organism analogy quietly fuses all three because in an organism they can't come apart, and that this is doing all the argumentative work — that, plus what I've started calling the undertaker's fallacy: you infer a death from a corpse-like state, when what you've got is a transformation you failed to recognise because you were identifying the thing by its most visible institutions (the palace, the script, the monument cult).

So: which of the disanalogies actually matter for the two conclusions he wants, and which are just noise? Does the endogenous-senescence claim survive once you note that societies have no genome, no fixed lifespan, and no mechanism of senescence — or does it collapse into "complexity gets expensive"? Does the "real ending" claim survive once you notice that when an organism dies its parts die with it, whereas when a polity "dies" the population very often survives and does fine? And what does the analogy smuggle in about wholes and parts, and about whether the thing that persists is the same thing under a new description?

If the organism is a bad frame, give me a better one and say what it can carry that the organism can't. I've been toying with Latin becoming the Romance languages (the thing persists, but the speakers' own description of what they are changes, and there's no date you could put on the death), with a company dissolved into a merger (the legal person ceases, nothing is lost, the employees turn up at work on Monday), and with fire as disturbance rather than death in a forest. Tell me which of those, if any, is the honest frame for which of the three cases — or whether I should be refusing the analogy format altogether and just doing the conceptual work directly.

Couple of practical things: in British English please, I'm marking in UK spelling and I don't want to have to convert. Don't bother with reading lists or citations — I'm not fussed about there provenance, I want the argument. And feel free to tell me the three Ds are a bad idea; I'd rather find out now than at a conference.
