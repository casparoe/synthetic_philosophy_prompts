I have no academic background in statistics, computer science, or philosophy — I work in insurance operations — but I have been reading about algorithmic risk scores on my own ever since a news story about their use in criminal sentencing. I recently read a long magazine piece on what it called the impossibility results, and I cannot tell how much of it to trust. I am not asking for citations or suggestions for further reading, only for the reasoning itself.

The piece's account of the result, as closely as I can reconstruct it, ran roughly like this:

"A few years ago computer scientists proved, mathematically, that an algorithmic risk score cannot be fair. The requirements look harmless enough. First, a score should mean the same thing regardless of who receives it: if the algorithm puts a defendant in the "7" bucket, then about seven in ten people in that bucket should actually go on to reoffend, in every group. Second, the algorithm should be equally likely to make each kind of mistake in each group: it should flag people who would not have reoffended at the same rate in both groups, and miss people who would have at the same rate in both groups. Third, the share of flagged people who turn out to be positive should be the same in both groups. The proof shows that no risk score can satisfy all three at once, and this is not an artifact of one particular data set: it holds for every risk score, whatever it was trained on, now or in the future.

The reason, in a sentence, is that the groups do not have the same rate of the thing being predicted — in the criminal-justice case, not the same reoffending rates. Once the base rates differ, a score calibrated for one group is automatically miscalibrated for the other, and the error rates cannot be equalized at the same time. So the obstacle is not bias in the data or sloppiness in the model; it is arithmetic.

That leaves a choice. Since you cannot have all three, one has to go, and most people working on this think the one to give up is the first: what a number means is negotiable in a way that error rates are not. The practical version of that is to stop letting the system see race or sex at all, so that it cannot sort people into groups in the first place.

The upshot the piece draws is that "algorithmic fairness" is not an engineering problem awaiting a cleverer technique but a contradiction in terms. Once you grant that the groups genuinely differ — and if they did not differ, there would be nothing to be fair about — no amount of better data, better features, or better mathematics will help, because the conflict lives inside probability theory itself."
