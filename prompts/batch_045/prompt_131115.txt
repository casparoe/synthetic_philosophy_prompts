I have been trying to state as strongly as I can the case against low-impact approaches to AI safety — the hope that one can recover some of what a correct utility function would buy you by instead penalising an agent for "affecting the world" — and my reconstruction now seems to prove considerably more than I am willing to accept. I would like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Set-up. An agent is given a primary objective (some task) and, added to it, a penalty on a quantity I will call impact. The appeal of the arrangement is supposed to be that the penalty need not know what is good: it discourages side effects without anyone having first to say which effects matter.

(1) A low-impact objective is worth wanting only if the penalty is not itself a theory of value. The entire attraction is that one can write down "do the task, and otherwise leave things as they are" without having solved the prior problem of what is worth having.

(2) Any impact penalty must compare the course of events the agent actually brings about with some baseline course of events (what would have happened otherwise), and must say how far apart the two are.

(3) To say how far apart two courses of events are, one needs a metric on histories; and a metric on histories must weigh kinds of difference against one another. It must say, for instance, that moving a teacup counts for less than moving a piano, or that a difference in who is in the room counts for more than a difference in the arrangement of dust.

(4) Weights of that kind just are a ranking of states by how much they matter; and a ranking of states by how much they matter is a partial specification of value. So whoever designs an impact measure thereby writes down a partial theory of value, however grudgingly and however incomplete.

(5) The penalty is moreover symmetric in a way that value is not. Whatever the designer's weights single out as mattering, the agent is discouraged both from destroying and from bringing about: it will not break the vase, but it will not fetch a new one either, and it has no reason to stop a third party from breaking it unless stopping them happens to fall under the task.

(6) The weights therefore have to be right, and right not merely about what matters but about how much, relative to the primary objective. Get the exchange rate wrong in one direction and the penalty swallows the task, so the agent does nothing; wrong in the other and the penalty is negligible whenever the task is worth anything. Calibrating that exchange rate is calibrating the whole thing.

(7) Hence the dilemma: either the impact measure is silent about what matters, in which case it is blind and often actively harmful — it will refuse to switch off an unattended stove, since switching it off is a sizeable change — or it says enough about what matters to do any work, in which case the "value-free" selling point was an illusion and we needed a specification of value after all. Low-impact methods are a detour that rejoins the road they were meant to avoid.

(8) And nothing in (2)–(6) turns on the word "impact". Any behavioural constraint on an agent — a geofence, an off-switch, a monitored quantity with a tripwire, a prohibition on accumulating computing hardware — singles out some quantity that someone had to choose, and choosing it is a value judgment in the sense of (4). So by the same reasoning no safety technique whatsoever avoids encoding a partial theory of value, and the project of building safe systems without first solving value theory is empty.

(9) But (8) is not credible. Engineers constrain systems whose outputs they cannot evaluate all the time, and the constraints do real work: a pressure-release valve embeds no theory of the good life, yet it prevents explosions.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than accept (8), and I would rather keep (9) than accept (7). So something between (3) and (8) has to go, and I cannot see what.

Here is the candidate escape I have been turning over, which is meant to break the chain at (4). Forget weights and metrics over states. Fix a modest budget of effort and resources available to the operator, and fix a list L of conditions the operator might later want to bring about: the vase intact, the front door unlocked, the backups restorable, the operator alive, and so on. For each condition in L, ask whether it is still attainable within that budget from the state of the world the agent would leave behind, and compare the answer with what it would have been from the baseline state. Penalise the agent by the number of conditions in L that went from attainable to unattainable, or better, by the erosion of slack: how much more of the operator's budget each condition would now consume than it would have. The proposal never weighs anything. L is a set, not a ranking; the only arithmetic is counting and comparing resource costs, and resource costs are physical rather than evaluative.

A friend says this escapes at (4). I think it escapes nothing: the value has merely migrated from the weights to the list, the budget, and the choice of baseline, and in migrating it has become harder rather than easier to audit, since my measure has no way to record that some items on L matter enormously and others hardly at all. There is also something about it I cannot locate in the argument at all — a penalty on shrinking the operator's menu looks like it gives the agent a reason to make itself indispensable, since an agent that has been switched off cannot keep anyone's menu open — and I do not know whether that is an instance of (5) or a separate objection to my construction.

Which step do you reject, and why does the step you reject look innocuous? If you think the escape is real, say what my construction pays for it, and where. If you think it is not, tell me what a specification of value has to contain for a constraint to count as encoding one, and what a constraint may contain without yet encoding it.
