I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation with an annual budget of roughly two million dollars, and we are deciding whether to adopt the importance / tractability / neglectedness framework — the scheme that is standard in cause-prioritization work — as our standing method for choosing where the money goes. In preparation I read a much-shared blog post explaining the framework by a writer who covers philanthropy for a general audience and is broadly sympathetic to it. The passage that matters I have reconstructed below close to how it read:

"Choosing a cause is a multiplication problem. Estimate three factors and multiply them. Importance is the size of the prize: how much better the world would be if the problem were solved outright. Tractability is the exchange rate between resources and progress — what fraction of the problem a given amount of money or talent can eliminate. Neglectedness is how little is already being spent on it, and it matters because the first dollar into a problem does far more good than the ten-thousandth, so a well-funded cause has already picked its low-hanging fruit and a neglected one has not. Multiply and you have the expected good per dollar, which is the only number a donor has any business looking at. The practical upshot is that the best causes are, as a rule, the unfashionable ones: if a problem is important and tractable, the only explanation for its still being neglected is that no one has got round to it yet, and that neglect is precisely what makes your dollar go furthest. Two things follow. First, concentrate: a cause ranked second is by definition worse per dollar than the one ranked first, so a donor who spreads money across several causes is simply buying less good with the same cheque. Second, stay out of crowded causes: a new dollar for something like cancer research, which already commands enormous budgets, buys next to nothing, and is therefore close to worthless to a donor whose aim is to do good."

A colleague on the committee, who is skeptical of the whole framework, says the multiplication is a category mistake: neglectedness is not a third independent factor but a claim about where we currently sit on the tractability curve, so multiplying by it counts the same thing twice. He adds that once you take diminishing returns seriously the object to be chosen is not a ranked list of causes but an allocation across a portfolio, one that equalizes marginal cost-effectiveness across causes — which will normally mean funding several at once rather than piling everything into the winner. On the cancer remark he says the post forgets about other people's money: if a well-funded institution receives my dollar it may spend less of its own, so the counterfactual effect of my dollar is not simply the marginal project it nominally purchases. I find all three points persuasive, which worries me — I may be flattered by a clever objection rather than told the truth — and I don't know enough about the framework to tell whether he is attacking it or attacking a version of it that only exists in the post.

Please audit the passage rather than the framework in general. Work through it claim by claim and sort what you find into: what a careful proponent of the framework would endorse as stated; what is a legitimate simplification for a popular audience; what is a genuine misrepresentation or non sequitur; and what is contested among people who actually use the framework. Then tell me whether the two conclusions the post draws — put everything into your top-ranked cause, and treat crowded causes as worthless — survive correction of the passages they rest on, and how much weaker the defensible versions of those claims are.

Two constraints on how you answer. First, I know the terms importance, tractability and neglectedness are defined slightly differently by different writers, and you have no way to check: wherever your verdict turns on which definition is in force, say so explicitly and give me the verdict under each reading rather than picking one silently. Second, I want the audit to come with its own error bars: attach a confidence level to each of your main judgments, name the one place where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and state what argument or consideration — a definition, an empirical fact about how steeply marginal returns actually decay, a feature of the framework's intended use I haven't mentioned — would lead you to a different verdict on the multiplication objection and on the concentration conclusion.
