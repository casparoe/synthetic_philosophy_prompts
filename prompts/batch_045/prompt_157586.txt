I taught and did research in experimental physiology for most of my working life and have only come to philosophy of mind since retiring, where I now host a small reading group of fellow amateurs. For our session on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness I would like to close with a poem: a sestina, six six-line stanzas in which the six end-words rotate by the standard permutation, followed by a three-line envoi.

The rotation should do philosophical work. I want the same six end-words — words along the lines of seems, red, nothing, report, grain, like — to be used first on one side of the dispute and then on the other, so that by the envoi no word has been claimed by either party and the vocabulary of the debate turns out to be shared property, which is close to what I take illusionism itself to assert: that all the words can be kept while the property is denied.

Alongside the standard dialectic — the claim that introspection misrepresents its own states, the residual seeming that survives the denial, the objection that the seeming is itself a datum in need of explanation — please build the poem around a wrinkle of my own that I have not found addressed anywhere. Call it role illusionism: suppose introspection is wholly veridical about the qualitative character of experience but systematically misrepresents experience as disclosing its own causal and functional embedding, as though from the inside one could tell that one's seeing of red is what moves the hand, or where that seeing sits in a wider network of dispositions. The turn of the poem, whether you place it at the last stanza or in the envoi, should be the moment it becomes clear that the argumentative move the illusionist deploys against qualia is structurally available against the seeming of role too, so that a realist cannot appeal to introspection selectively and an illusionist cannot either.

Two further constraints. First, one worry from my old trade that I cannot shake: a misrepresentation generated by a coarse-grained functional state owes us some account of the fine grain of what it misrepresents, this particular shade rather than that one, this felt texture rather than another, and I would like the poem to keep faith with that worry rather than smooth it over. Second, accuracy matters more than verdict: someone who actually works on illusionism should find the dialectic right rather than merely alluded to, and someone who has never heard of it should still find a poem. Please write in American English and keep the whole thing, title included, under about forty-five lines.
