I'm drafting a long piece on punishment for the kind of audience that reads LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, and I want to try something I haven't seen done before: reading Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment* as a contribution to the argument between Dennett-style compatibilists and Caruso-style skeptics about just deserts.

Here is how I'd state the two sides, and you can correct me if I've drawn them badly. The skeptic holds that if our choices are the upshot of heredity, environment, and luck, then no one is morally responsible in the basic-desert sense, and punishment can only be justified by what it does next: deterrence, rehabilitation, and — where someone remains a threat — something like the public-health quarantine model, licensed by the same considerations that license isolating carriers rather than by any thought that the person has it coming. The compatibilist replies that desert-talk never needed indeterminism; what matters is that practices of holding people responsible are what make us, over a lifetime, into agents who can be reasoned with, and that a practice which addresses someone as answerable secures something a purely medical model cannot — it gives the person a role in his own case. In the comment-section shorthand I keep running into, the skeptic is said to have the arguments and the compatibilist to have only the rhetoric.

I want to test that shorthand against the novel, without flattening either it or the debate. Four things in the book seem to me to be doing real philosophical work:

First, Raskolnikov justifies the murder twice over — once by a crude act-consequentialist arithmetic about a useless old woman, and once by his published article's claim that a certain kind of extraordinary person is permitted to transgress. The novel demolishes both, but not, so far as I can tell, by saying he deserved to suffer. What collapses is a self-conception. Is the "extraordinary man" theory best read as a claim about being self-exempt from moral accounting, and is the novel's answer to it the compatibilist one — that you don't get to author your own exemption, because answerability is something a community confers and withdraws? Or is the novel's refusal to invoke desert itself evidence for the skeptic?

Second, Porfiry Petrovich. By his own admission he could charge Raskolnikov and doesn't; across their interviews he instead manoeuvres him toward confessing. Call this the Porfiry constraint: an adequate account of punishment should be able to say why what Porfiry does is better than an immediate arrest. Quarantine has no use for the interviews, and retributivism has to treat the delay as desert merely deferred. My hunch is that Porfiry is manufacturing addressability — engineering the conditions under which Raskolnikov can become someone who can be spoken to about what he did — and that this is a function of criminal process that neither model names. Is that a fair description, and is it available to the compatibilist alone?

Third, the housepainter Mikolka, who falsely confesses to the murder and seems to want the punishment for it. Call it the Mikolka datum: people sometimes present themselves for punishment they have not earned, and are in some way damaged by being refused it. How does each model handle someone who seeks to be held responsible? Does the datum point to a good that punishment provides — being counted an agent, being answerable to someone — which a purely forward-looking system has no way to supply?

Fourth, the epilogue. Raskolnikov is not much tormented in Siberia; the change in him arrives elsewhere and is not produced by the sentence. So the novel appears to deny that punishment retires a debt, which is the skeptic's conclusion reached by an entirely different route. Is *Crime and Punishment* then a Carusian document? Or does it suggest that desert is not the only backward-looking thing punishment might be for — expiation, acknowledgment, the reconstitution of the person as someone who can be addressed — so that "no basic desert" would not by itself settle whether we still need practices of this shape?

Where I most want you to push back: Dostoevsky's own framing is religious, with suffering as redemptive in a way neither Dennett nor Caruso can use, and I don't want to baptize the novel as a secular theory it isn't. Tell me where the reading strains, where the book simply has no view, and which scenes you'd point a skeptical audience to if you wanted them to take the question seriously. I'd rather end up with a smaller, honest claim than a tidy thesis.
