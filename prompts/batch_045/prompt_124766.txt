I'm on the editorial board of my school paper. Last month we ran a column arguing against a policy most of the school supports; a group of students organized a petition, the administration took the piece down from the website, and the writer was asked to step away from a student-government post. When he said he was being silenced, one of the organizers answered: nobody's censoring you, you're free to say it, you just have to live with what people do about it — that's not censorship, that's consequences. It landed as if it settled the matter, but I can't tell what it actually asserts.

Could you set out, as a list, the different precise claims that one-liner might be making, from the trivial to the strong? Three things I especially want separated out: how much of its force comes from picturing the reaction as something that merely happens to a speaker rather than something people deliberately organize and coordinate; whether a response wildly out of proportion to what was said still counts as "consequences," and so still counts as not-censorship, on that reading; and whether the answer changes when every individual reaction — declining to share, complaining to an editor, withdrawing an invitation — is something each person is entitled to do, yet the reactions together strip a speaker's platform far more thoroughly than any one of them would alone.

For each reading say whether it is true more or less by definition, whether it is a substantive moral claim that someone could reasonably reject, and whether it is actually strong enough to show that what happened to our columnist raises no free-speech issue at all. I'd like to bring something sharper than the slogan to our next board meeting.
