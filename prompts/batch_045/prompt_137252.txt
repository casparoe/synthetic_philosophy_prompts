Hi — I work incident response at a mid-size cyber insurance and managed-detection outfit. We handle somewhere around 150 ransomware and data-extortion cases a year: usually me plus a duty analyst on a call, two to six hours to decide whether we authorize a payment, legal and a director signing off, and the client (often a small hospital, a school district, a county) sitting in the room wanting an answer yesterday. What I need is a procedure, not an essay.

The reason I'm asking about this stuff specifically: our internal arguments have been going in circles for about six months, and from what I can tell we've independently reinvented a loop that decision theorists already have names for. One side says: the files are already encrypted, the data is already out, the intrusion already cost them money, so nothing we pay now changes any of that — the only live question is what paying does from this moment forward. The other side says: fine, but the reason this client got picked, and the reason the note is sitting in the inbox at all, has something to do with what they expected us to do, and if we're the kind of outfit that pays we get picked more, and the future version of us is worse off. Then the first side says the decision today can't change whether we were already the kind of outfit that pays. And round we go.

Two setups were described to me as capturing the structure, roughly:

- Counterfactual mugging: a very reliable predictor flips a coin in private. If it comes up heads and the predictor expected you to hand over $100 in the tails case, it quietly deposits $10,000 and you're never asked for anything. If it comes up tails, it turns up and asks for $100. You've been asked. Nothing you do now changes how the coin landed. Do you pay?
- XOR blackmail: a letter arrives offering to save your house from termites for a fee. You know the letter gets sent exactly when either there's an infestation and the sender expects you to refuse, or there isn't one and they expect you to pay — one or the other, not both. The letter is here. Do you pay?

What I want is the usable content of those two puzzles, and of whatever the fight between the two ways of answering them actually turns on, turned into something my team can run at 3am. Concretely: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the points where the answer is just "no", and the points where the procedure has to stop and hand things to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Assume the analyst running it has no decision-theory background at all, has twenty to thirty minutes, and has to write the output down in a form the director and our compliance file can both live with.

Four ways our real cases aren't the puzzles, and the procedure has to survive them:

1. Our predictors are bad. Attackers aren't anywhere near perfect predictors. They read proxies: whether the client carries cyber insurance, what sector it's in, anything public we've said, whether somebody paid under similar circumstances last year. So whatever the argument for paying (or for refusing) is, it should degrade smoothly as prediction gets worse instead of flipping at some point, and it needs to say what to do when we genuinely have no idea how good the proxy is — which is most of the time.
2. Two audiences, not one. Whoever we're dealing with right now will definitely find out what we did, and may well come back at the same target again (repeat extortion is common). The wider population of attackers will mostly learn nothing beyond a leak or a court filing eight months later. I'm fairly sure those are separate channels that should get separate weights, and I don't want a procedure that silently adds them together.
3. The note itself carries information. Sometimes the demand is boilerplate; sometimes it names specific files, or a backup server, or the client's insurer by name, and the timing and the phrasing are themselves clues about how we were selected and what they think they know. I want a step that asks what the arrival and the content of the demand tell us, and what they make irrelevant — not just "did a crime happen".
4. Legibility isn't free. We've talked about publishing a no-concessions charter, except that half our clients' policies only reimburse a ransom if we haven't publicly committed to never paying one, and a public charter only does any work if attackers can read it and believe it. So "just credibly precommit in advance" is not an answer I can use; I need the procedure to treat precommitment as something with a price and a reliability, and to say where it belongs in the ordering.

Please also give me worked applications to three cases, with the reasoning actually shown rather than just the verdicts: one ordinary hospital ransomware case (encrypted systems, backups partly intact, no exfiltration claimed), one where a small client has already had the same actor come back a second time after we paid once, and one where the procedure spits out an answer I'm going to hate. My guess is that last one is either paying in a case where the money plainly does nothing for the victim sitting in front of me, or refusing where somebody's going to lose records they can't get back — if you think the unwelcome answer is somewhere else entirely, use that instead and say why.

And a closing section on what the procedure can't do: where it will misfire, what it's structurally blind to, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Be explicit wherever you're smuggling in a choice about whose welfare counts, what counts as a cost, or what we're trading against what — I'd rather see the smuggling than have it hidden inside a checklist.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are in the different moving parts of what you've given me, and single out the one bit you're least sure of. If the honest answer is that one of my four complications has no good answer in the usual way people think about these two puzzles, say that plainly instead of inventing something smooth — I'd rather have a hole I know about than a step my analysts trust and shouldn't.
