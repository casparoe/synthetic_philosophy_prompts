I am writing a long-form piece on what the WEIRD problem in moral psychology actually establishes, and on whether the cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgment documented by anthropologists and experimental economists is evidence against moral objectivity or merely evidence about who has been sampled. I would rather carry that argument through a novel than open with a laboratory study, and I want to use Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* as the spine of the piece. So I need a reading of the novel that can bear real philosophical weight rather than decorate an argument I could have made without it.

The material I want taken seriously is the argument from variation: people raised in small-scale, kin-based, subsistence societies reliably differ from Western undergraduates in how they punish, how they share, and what they treat as a moral matter at all rather than a matter of custom, ritual, or status; and if our moral responses are the output of such local training, the realist's claim that they track something stance-independent starts to look like an unearned privilege. I have found it useful to sort the live responses into three: the mirroring view, on which variation is mostly noise or mostly surface and the underlying judgments converge once descriptions are matched; the projection view, on which disagreement between moralities is closer to disagreement between palates; and a third option I want to call the calibration view, on which a moral sensibility is a socially trained detector whose reliability can be assessed only by asking whether independently trained detectors converge once they share information, share stakes, and are released from coercion — so that the WEIRD finding is a fact about a badly drawn sample of detectors, not a proof that there is no signal. I would also like to apply a constraint I have been calling the double-criticism constraint: a metaethical position earns its keep only if it can make sense of a reader who simultaneously rejects the colonial verdict on Umuofia and rejects some of Umuofia's own practices, without falling back on the empty formula that each side had its own valid morality.

Please read the novel through this material. Which of the three responses does the book actually dramatize, if any? Where does the calibration view illuminate a specific scene — Obierika's troubled questioning of his own community's exile of a man for an inadvertent offence, the killing of Ikemefuna, the converts drawn from among those the clan had cast out, Okonkwo's suicide, and the District Commissioner's closing plan to dispose of the whole life in a paragraph of a chapter on pacifying the region? Is Achebe there making a claim about moral knowledge, or only about who gets to keep the record?

I also want you to say where the reading fails. Achebe wrote to restore the human reality of a society that colonial literature had caricatured, not to supply a metaethics seminar with an intuition pump; the novel is also a tragedy about a man whose rigidity helps destroy the thing he is defending, and what it depicts is in part his extreme version of the morality rather than its average. Be explicit about what I would have to distort in order to make the novel fit.

Finally, tell me which two or three scenes I should actually put on the page for readers, what I should warn them against — in particular, how to avoid the condescension of treating a real people's moral life as a specimen — and what, if anything, the novel adds to this debate that the arguments on their own do not.
