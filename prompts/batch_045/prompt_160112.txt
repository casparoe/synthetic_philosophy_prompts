I'm a retired plant physiologist — forty years of measuring things — and since I packed it in I've been going to a U3A philosophy group on Tuesday mornings, which is how I've ended up stuck in the middle of all this Mary's Room business. I keep wanting to ask what the mechanism is and getting told that's the wrong sort of question, so I'd rather hand this one to someone who likes that sort of thing.

The woman who runs the group, Brenda, has given verdicts on a run of Mary-type cases, and they don't line up neatly with any of the labels in the books I've borrowed, so I want to know what she's actually doing. Her verdicts so far (paraphrasing, but this is the gist):

- Ordinary Mary, black-and-white room, all the physical facts, red tomato on release: she gains something on release, but not a new fact — nothing she could have written down in the room turns out to be false. She also won't let anyone wriggle out of it by saying Mary couldn't really have known all the physics; we're to grant that she does.
- A robot with a colour camera and the complete physical theory, sorting red from green perfectly, never having had any experience at all: the robot does not know what red looks like, and no story about its dispositions or its use of the word makes up the difference. So she's a realist about something, whatever it is.
- Mary who is allowed, in the room, to run a perfect simulation of her own visual system right up to the moment of release: if it's a real running of her own colour machinery then yes, she now knows, and that's cheating; if it's the equations on paper then no. The difference, she says, is in whether the system has actually been exercised, not in the facts.
- Inverted Mary: two people identical in every third-person respect except that the wiring from the receptors into the colour areas is crossed, so their experiences of red and green are swapped. Conceivable; a real difference if it happened; and yet nothing in either person's physical description would be false.

The case we've got next week is the one I care about, and there's a tenner on it. Call her Partial Mary. She's a protanope — born without the long-wavelength cone — so she has ordinary colour experience of blues, yellows and greens and has never had the red end of things. In the room she learns the complete physical story, including the physics of the pigment she's missing and the full neuroscience of normal trichromatic vision. Then (stipulate that it wires up cleanly and works straight away) she's given the opsin gene, the cone starts working, and she looks at a red tomato. Does she learn anything new?

Why I think this is the interesting one: as far as I can make out, protanopes don't lose a whole modality, they lose a direction in a space they already have — they call reds dark yellow or brown, and they're just less sensitive at the long end. So Partial Mary starts out already acquainted with colour, and red may be a short step (or a short step plus a change in brightness) from hues she knows perfectly well. That's where the rules I can see behind Brenda's verdicts come apart, and I can't tell which one she's running:

1. something in the ability camp — what changes on release is what she can do: imagine, remember, recognise;
2. something in the phenomenal-concept camp — she acquires a new concept that only the experience itself can supply, so the fact was old but her grip on it is new, and she couldn't have anticipated it;
3. something more like the quality-space or acquaintance line — she already occupies the space, so with the physical story plus the structure she already knows she can locate red in it, and any surprise is a failure of imagination or of computing power rather than of knowledge.

What I'd like: for each of those three, what it predicts about Partial Mary, and whether it predicts she should be *less* surprised than ordinary Mary or exactly as surprised; your best guess at what Brenda will say on Tuesday, with the reasons for preferring that reading of her track record (and do say if one of her earlier verdicts, especially the simulation one, is doing more work than I think); and then the one further case — Mary variant or otherwise — that would separate whichever two rules are still tied after this one. Don't bother naming authors or papers, I can't remember them anyway; just give me the case and what it would show.
