I'm a journalist working on a piece about how professional neutrals actually divide money when neither side wants to go to trial. I've been given the anonymized award record of one mediator — I'll call her M. — together with the case that is in front of her this month, and I want to know what she is likely to do with it. I'm less interested in the number itself than in the rule she appears to be running, in the other rules that fit her record equally well, and in what I'd have to see to tell them apart.

Every case in her record has the same structure. Two parties, call them A and B, are dividing a fund; if they fail to agree, each falls back to a known outside outcome. What the two can achieve jointly, above those fallbacks, is given as a broken line, and I'll give you its corner points: the achievable set is everything on or below and to the left of that line, since anyone can always leave money on the table. Entries are (A's share, B's share) in millions of dollars. The shapes come from gross-up provisions and statutory caps, which isn't the part I need help with.

Her four published awards:

(1) Fallback (0, 0). Frontier a straight line from (40, 0) to (0, 10). She awarded (20, 5).
(2) Fallback (0, 0). Frontier a straight line from (10, 0) to (0, 40). She awarded (5, 20).
(3) Fallback (2, 8). Frontier a straight line from (42, 8) to (2, 18). She awarded (22, 13).
(4) Fallback (0, 0). Frontier (10, 0) → (8, 8) → (0, 10). She awarded (8, 8).

What strikes me about the record is this: every award sits on the frontier, none of them is an equal split, and in the first three the frontier was a single straight line while in the fourth it was kinked but perfectly symmetric. So on her whole published record the two rules that everyone keeps pointing me to — the one that maximizes the product of the two sides' gains over their fallbacks, and the one that runs a straight line from the fallback point to the pair of best cases and takes the furthest point along it — give exactly the same answer everywhere. I gather these are the Nash and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions, and that the first is tied to an axiom saying that options you don't choose shouldn't matter while the second is tied to a monotonicity condition, roughly that a side whose best case improves shouldn't end up with less. Correct me if I have that backwards.

The new case: both fallbacks are zero, since neither side has a realistic outside option. The frontier is (100, 0) → (60, 9) → (0, 10). A's best case is 100; B's share is capped at 10 by statute; and the kink sits at (60, 9).

Please answer in five titled sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

**The candidates** (~200 words): the division rules consistent with all four of her awards, including any none of us has thought of, and which plausible rules her record eliminates and where exactly it eliminates them.

**What each predicts here** (~250 words): what each surviving rule awards on the new case, with enough arithmetic that I can check it, and how far apart the predictions actually are.

**Your best guess** (~150 words): the award you expect, the reason for preferring that rule over its rivals, and how confident you are. If you think the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so and tell me what adopting each candidate would commit her to.

**The case I'd want next** (~200 words): one further case, with numbers, that would separate the surviving rules — what each would award there and why that case does the work. My hunch is that the sharpest test isn't another capped-share case but a monotonicity test: improve A's best case alone, leave B's cap where it is, and see whether A's award goes up or down. Tell me whether that hunch is right and, if it is, give me numbers that make the divergence vivid.

**What her record can't tell me** (~150 words): the limits of this exercise — what no number of past awards could settle, including anything about whether these figures should be treated as dollars or as something else, and what is normatively at stake in the choice between the two rules.
