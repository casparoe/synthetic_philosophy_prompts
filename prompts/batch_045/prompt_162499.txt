Consider the phenomenon of simultaneous invention. It is often said that some discoveries are in the air: multiple investigators, working independently, arrive at the same result at roughly the same time. The standard textbook illustrations are Newton and Leibniz with the calculus and Darwin and Wallace with evolution by natural selection. If discoveries of this kind are common, or even inevitable given the state of a field, then it is natural to ask whether it really matters who made them, and what our institutions should do when two people independently arrive at the same thing.

Intellectual property law gives two apparently conflicting answers to this question in neighboring domains. In copyright law, the fact that a defendant independently created a work identical to the plaintiff's is a complete defense to infringement: infringement requires copying, and an independent creator has not copied anyone. Each independent creator therefore owns an ordinary copyright in their own identical work, and neither can sue the other. Copyright, moreover, does not require that a work be novel or unlike anything that already exists; it requires only that the author not have copied it from someone else. In patent law, by contrast, independent invention is no defense to infringement. A defendant who independently invents and then makes or sells something covered by an existing patent is still an infringer, even if they never heard of the patentee or the patent. And the second independent inventor cannot obtain a patent on the same invention, because the first inventor's earlier public disclosure or patent filing has made the invention part of the prior art and thus no longer new to the world. Patent law thus treats the invention as a single thing that can be invented only once, with the legal right to practice it belonging to the first to file a patent application (or, under older rules, the first to invent), while copyright allows the same expression to be created again and again by independent authors.

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind this asymmetry. What philosophical commitments does the practice presuppose about the nature of invention, discovery, authorship, originality, and credit? In particular, does the patent rule presuppose a metaphysics in which an invention is a datable, abstract entity that is discovered once and thereafter merely replicated by later independent arrivals, whereas the copyright rule presupposes that an expressive work is created afresh in each act of independent origination, so that simultaneity is metaphysically unproblematic? Or is the asymmetry better explained by the different instrumental aims of the two regimes—patents being justified by the need to induce disclosure and commercialization through a temporary monopoly, and copyrights by the need to prevent misappropriation of expressive labor or to protect authorial personality—rather than by any deep view about discovery? Distinguish what the asymmetry strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the two sides of the asymmetry are consistent with one another or whether they quietly rely on incompatible theories. Finally, explain what would have to change, and what would follow for the Newton-Leibniz and Darwin-Wallace-style cases, if we swapped the rules: if independent invention became a defense in patent law, or if copyright abandoned the independent-creation defense and made identical independently created works infringing.
