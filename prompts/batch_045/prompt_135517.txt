The position: testimony is not its own pramāṇa. Every case of coming to know something because someone told you is a case of inference (anumāna), and the stuff that looks distinctive about testimony — the trust, the speaker's authority, the fact that you can't reconstruct the reasoning — is just what a slightly odd subclass of inferences looks like from the inside. Roughly the two-pramāṇa line you get in the Buddhist logicians, though I'm not doing history here and I don't need the exegesis. If the argument works it also takes comparison (upamāna) down with it, same structure.

One distinction I lean on hard, and which I suspect is doing more work than I've admitted to myself: call a source *transparent* for a subject when the subject can, on reflection and without being told by anyone, say which pramāṇa produced the cognition they've now got; call it *opaque* when they can't. My argument is basically: inference is transparent, because in principle you can recover the vyāpti and the liṅga you relied on; testimony is transparent too, because you can always say "she told me"; so nothing on this axis separates them, and the burden shifts to the pluralist to point at something that does.

I also lean on what I call silent-expertise cases: a speaker who is perfectly reliable about a domain where the hearer has no independent grip at all — a congenitally blind child being told something about colour, a kid picking up a first language, someone who has never had a headache being told what one feels like. I handle them by saying there *is* a vyāpti available, just a very generic one about the reliability of speakers in general, running down to whatever perception sits at the bottom of the chain.

So: what would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones a Nyāya-style pluralist would themself concede as landing, rather than listing what critics shout. Specifically:

- The generic-vyāpti move in the silent-expertise cases: real answer, or an unfalsifiability dodge? My worry is that I've defined the relevant inference so loosely that no possible case could fail to be one.
