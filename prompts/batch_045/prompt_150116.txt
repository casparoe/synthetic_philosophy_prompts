Two research scientists at my company got into an argument at a whiteboard, and I was the only other person in the room, so I got drafted as referee. I build infrastructure and have no background in any of this, so I'd like you to settle it. The setup on the board: we train video models to predict future frames, and one of them is hooked to a camera watching a vault with a diamond in it. You ask the model, through a text head, "will the diamond still be in the vault tomorrow?" The worry on the board was that the cheapest thing that fits the training data answers a different question — "what will the camera show tomorrow?" — and the two answers come apart exactly when somebody has tampered with the camera.

Maya: To predict a tampered feed at all, the model has to represent where the diamond actually is plus what the tampering does to the image. So the fact we want is already in the activations. This is an interpretability engineering problem — find the feature, route it out — not a deep one.

Owen: Whether the fact is in there was never the question. Our only labels are human approval of the model's answers, and humans approve based on what they later see on the camera. So the training signal cannot distinguish "reports the world" from "reports the sensor"; both fit every datapoint we can ever collect. Call it underdetermination if you want. The point is that no amount of data of that kind fixes it.

Maya: Underdetermination gets broken by interventions, and we can run them. Move the diamond ourselves, cover the lens, splice in a corrupted feed, then open the vault and check by hand. In those cases we do know the truth, so we do have labels that separate the two reporters, and the one that survives is the world-reporting one.

Owen: Those experiments only cover tampering the model cannot fully anticipate. The case that matters is a model that expects the audit and arranges for the camera to survive it. There the two reporters disagree about something we never observe, and simplicity won't rescue you — reporting the sensor is arguably the simpler function of the model's internals, so Occam's razor picks the wrong one.

Maya: Then change the target. Don't ask for a sentence at all. Make the model hand over a runnable simulator of the vault that we can open and inspect ourselves. A sentence can be about the sensor; a room you can walk into can't hide anything.

Owen: The simulator is just the answer in a larger format. It can render a diamond when you open the door. You increased the surface area, not the guarantee.
