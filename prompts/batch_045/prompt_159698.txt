A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether cost-benefit analysis can ever be legitimate, and we ended up agreeing on more than either of us expected, which makes me suspect we converged on the same mistake.

The case we kept returning to was a night curfew at a regional airport: roughly nine thousand nearby residents would sleep through the night, and roughly forty people who work night freight would lose their jobs.

We agreed on two things. First, that utility is only a representation of each person's own preferences, that there is no shared unit and no common zero in which to weigh one person's gain against another's loss, and so that any assessment which says that the gains exceed the losses asserts something the formalism does not license. Second, that the honest fallback is the Pareto test: approve a change only if someone gains and nobody loses.

Then the argument started. My friend said the Pareto test is not the neutral fallback at all. It takes the existing allocation as its baseline, and it hands any single loser an effective veto over an enormous gain to everyone else; both of those are themselves interpersonal comparisons, and of a rather extreme kind, in which any loss whatsoever outranks any finite gain. I said that was a confusion: declining to compare is the neutral option, and the appearance of bias is just what it costs to refrain from helping yourself to numbers you are not entitled to. He replied with a reversal: suppose the curfew is already in force and the question is whether to lift it. Now the test protects the firms and blocks the residents' gains. The same pair of outcomes, opposite verdicts, purely as a function of who happens to hold the entitlements — which, he said, shows that the test is not abstaining from judgement but ratifying the status quo. I had no answer to that, and I still don't.

So my first question: is refusing to make interpersonal comparisons actually a way of avoiding them, or is it one weighting among others? And if it is one weighting among others, what exactly is it weighting, and how much of the disagreement between us survives once that is on the table?

Second. We floated a repair: put every gain and loss on a money scale by asking what each side would pay to have its way, require the winners actually to compensate the losers, and treat the result as a genuine improvement. My friend objected that willingness to pay is denominated in money and therefore inherits the wealth of whoever is doing the paying, and that compensation which is merely hypothetical compensates nobody. I said the first only shows that a dollar in a poor person's pocket should count for more, not that the method is broken, and I cannot see what is wrong with the second. Which of us is wrong, and where?

Third, a thought I have been turning over since, which neither of us raised at the time. Instead of choosing one way of lining up two people's scales, why not ask whether a verdict survives every lining-up the formalism permits: approve a policy only if it comes out ahead under every admissible interpersonal rescaling, and say nothing when the verdict flips. That seems to extract real verdicts without pretending to a common unit. It also seems too good to be true, and I would like to know precisely where it breaks — whether it is genuinely less committal than simply picking a normalization, whether it quietly collapses back into the Pareto test, or whether it falls silent in nearly every case that matters in practice.

I don't need names, papers, or references. I want to know where the argument actually goes wrong.
