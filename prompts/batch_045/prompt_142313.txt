I've been going back and forth on white lies and bluntness and I want to run the case method on myself before I read any more about it. Background: I grew up outside Utrecht, I've been in Japan for six years, I'm married to a Japanese person, and at work I'm the one who translates between the Dutch client and our team — literally, but also in the other sense, where you take what someone means and put it into a form the other side can hear. So my verdicts come out of living in both places, and I don't trust all of them.

What I actually think, case by case:

- My mother-in-law spends three hours on dinner and asks how it is. It's bland. I say it's delicious. Fine — I'd go further and say refusing to say it would be the rude thing.
- My Dutch brother emails me a draft and asks what I think. I tell him it's a mess and needs restructuring. Fine, and I'd feel I'd failed him if I'd softened it.
- Same brother shows me a draft and doesn't ask. I tell him it's a mess anyway. Not fine. The honesty permission seems to be tied to being asked, which is odd if what you care about is truth getting transmitted.
- A colleague asks how I am in the hallway. I'm having a bad week. I say fine. Fine. The same question from my Dutch friend: I tell him about the week. Also fine. Same sentence, opposite answers, both right — I don't want a principle that says one of those is a mistake.
- I relay a message. The team means no and can't make the deadline, and I tell the Dutch client they're "still looking into it," knowing he'll hear maybe-yes. Not fine, and worse than most of the above. I don't think I said anything false.
- Ending a rejection email with "looking forward to hearing from you again." Fine. Pure formula; I'd write it to someone I never want to hear from again.
- Rejection email saying "we were impressed by your application" to someone we rejected after forty seconds. Not fine — even though it's the same kind of boilerplate. I want to say the first has stopped carrying a claim and the second hasn't, but I can't say what makes that so.
- My grandmother has dementia and asks every ten minutes where her husband is. He died in 2019. I say he's resting. Fine, I think, though it costs me something each time.
- My nephew's hamster dies and my sister wants me to back her up on "he went to live on a farm." Not fine. I don't want to be the one who tells a seven-year-old, but I really don't want to be the one who lies to him either.

Those last two are the pair I can't square: both are protective fictions about a death, offered to someone who can't handle the truth, and I go opposite ways. The rejection-email pair is the other one that bothers me.

Two more things that make me suspect my own verdicts. I'm blunt with my intern in a way I'm not blunt with my boss, and I've started to think that isn't honesty, it's just that she can't push back. And I'm blunt in English with colleagues who speak English worse than I do, which feels like the same problem wearing a different hat. Also, sometimes I enjoy being blunt — there's a little glow of being the honest one — and a verdict I enjoy is a verdict I should discount.

My rough rule, such as it is: what matters isn't whether the sentence is true, it's wether I've left the other person in a position to act on what they need, given what this kind of exchange is for, with a discount for who has the power. But "what they need" is doing all the work and I have no idea how to fill it in.

So: is there one principle — or a small set of candidates — that gets most of this? I want it stated precisely enough that I can test it on new cases, with the clause each of my cases is buying, and an honest list of the verdicts it can't save and which you'd overturn. Two cases I haven't decided, and I want to know what falls out of the principle rather than what you'd independently say: a junior colleague here who says to me, in English, "please be honest with me" — do I take that at face value, and does it change anything that saying it is itself a politeness formula? And the AI assistant that tells me "great question!" — nobody's feelings are at stake, nobody expects anything, and it still irritates me; does a decent principle have anything to say about it, or is my reaction just noise? And if the account leans on "what the exchange is for," can that be cashed out so it isn't an all-purpose excuse for whatever anyone felt like doing?

Be blunt with me, obviously.
