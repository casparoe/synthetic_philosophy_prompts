I teach an introductory metaethics course, and next term I want to open the unit on moral disagreement and objectivity with Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart instead of with a survey of the psychology literature. I'd like a reading of the novel that takes it seriously as a contribution to one specific argument: the move from the fact that moral judgment varies systematically across cultures — the sample on which most moral-psychology results rest being narrow, Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic, and much of the variation that does show up tracking honour-based moralities rather than guilt- or dignity-based ones — to the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about which morality is right.

Keep three claims apart in the reading, because I think students collapse them: the descriptive claim about variation; the methodological claim that this variation discredits the intuitions philosophers reason from; and the metaethical conclusion. Then tell me where the novel, instead of illustrating all three at once, resists them. I have two hypotheses I'd rather have tested than flattered. First: the closing scene in which the district commissioner mentally reduces Okonkwo's life to a paragraph in an ethnographic study is routinely taught as a lesson in relativism, when it might equally be a portrait of the opposite failing — an observer entirely confident of his own standpoint, whose problem is indifference rather than relativism. Second: the novel's most interesting material for this argument is not the collision between Igbo and missionary morality but the disagreement inside Umuofia — the misgivings voiced by villagers themselves about the disposal of twins, about the exile imposed for an accidental killing, about Ikemefuna. If moral objection is only intelligible from inside a form of life, then intra-cultural disagreement should do the same argumentative work as cross-cultural disagreement, and the novel may show us something the psychology literature's culture-as-a-single-data-point framing misses.

Where does the reading break down? The novel has a polemical purpose, written against European representations of Africa, and it is at least as concerned with who gets to tell a people's story as with the metaphysics of value; I don't want to conscript it into a seminar-room argument it was never having. Say what the novel can add to the debate about variation and objectivity that the standard hypothetical cases can't, and what an honest reader has to concede it doesn't show.
