I sit in on a small weekly reading group on moral desert, responsibility, and luck, and last week's session was on karma. The discussion kept being settled by a single proverb — you reap what you sow — and I came away convinced that everyone at the table was using it to mean something slightly different. I would like a disambiguation that I can bring to the next meeting.

The sentence was made to do at least three jobs, and I want to know what each job actually requires of it. First, it was offered as an answer to the objection from luck: if suffering is the harvest of one's own sowing, then it is not a matter of luck that it arrives, and the familiar complaint that desert claims are hostage to circumstance is supposed to dissolve. Second, it was used to mark off karma-as-desert from what one member called the psychological reading — the thought that cruelty tends to breed guilt, isolation, and reprisal, so that bad people often come to bad ends — with the insistence that the proverb says something stronger and more moralized than that. Third, in a discussion of inherited disadvantage, it was used to underwrite the claim that a person's present misery is deserved: not merely explicable, not merely the predictable downstream effect of her own earlier choices, but something she merits.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that the proverb could be asserting, ordered roughly from the thinnest to the strongest, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but so far undefended, or straightforwardly false? I do not want one reading selected and explained; I want the candidate readings separated, with the differences between them made explicit, including the differences I have not thought of.

In particular, I would like the following treated as candidate readings to be sorted and assessed rather than as settled distinctions:

- Whether the return must be in the same currency as the sowing — deception repaid in being deceived, betrayal in being betrayed — or only of equivalent disvalue, so that any suffering of matching magnitude settles the account. Call these currency-matching and value-matching readings.
- Whether the ledger is atomic, with each deed generating its own discrete return that must eventually be discharged, or merely aggregative, requiring only that the totals over a life or a series of lives balance.
- Whether the return tracks the agent's intention or the deed's actual consequences, and what follows, on the intention-tracking reading, for a case where the intention was thoroughly bad and nothing whatever came of it.
- Whether the point of the return is retributive, because the suffering is owed; educative or purgative, because it is administered for the agent's own good; or merely equilibrative, a bookkeeping necessity with no point beyond balance itself.
- Whether the reaper must be the sower in any sense stronger than bare causal continuity, and if the return may land on someone else or on a later bearer of the same stream, what becomes of the claim that this is desert at all.
- Whether the mechanism is an impersonal law or something administered, and whether that difference affects whether a return could be remitted, transferred, or forgiven.

For each of the three uses I described, tell me which of the readings on the menu would be strong enough to do the work asked of it, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. My suspicion is that the third use needs a reading so strong that almost nobody would endorse it once it is stated plainly, and I would like to see whether that is right, or whether there is a defensible version of it that I am failing to distinguish from the others.
