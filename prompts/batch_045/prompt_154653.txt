I am a graduate student in intellectual history, and this term I am taking a seminar on antislavery political thought in the transatlantic world. We recently turned to Frederick Douglass's 1860 speech in Glasgow on the American Constitution and slavery. My study group and I found ourselves bogged down in the opening sections, which are dense with local polemical context that we have trouble reconstructing. To make sense of them, I have been experimenting with a rough distinction of my own between 'position-taking' and 'frame-shifting' in abolitionist argument. A position-taking move, as I am using the term, asserts a direct propositional claim about a text or institution—for example, that the Constitution is a pro-slavery compact or that it is an anti-slavery charter. A frame-shifting move, by contrast, does not really assert such a claim; instead, it tries to alter the terms in which the audience understands what kind of activity invoking the Constitution is, or to whom the speaker is accountable in making that invocation. My worry is that this distinction is too blunt, and I would like help testing it against a hard passage.

The passage giving us trouble comes from early in the speech, just after Douglass explains why he feels compelled to answer a recent attack by a fellow antislavery lecturer. I have copied it below exactly as I found it in a transcription of the 1860 pamphlet version:

    The North British Mail honored me with a few pointed remarks in dissent from certain views held by me on another occasion in this city; but as it rendered my speech on that occasion very fairly to the public, I did not feel at all called upon to reply to its strictures. The case is different now. I am brought face to face with two powers. I stand before you under the fire of both platform and press. Not to speak, under the circumstances, would subject me and would subject my cause to misconstruction. You might be led to suppose that I had no reasons for the ground that I occupied here when I spoke in another place before you. Let me invite your attention, I may say your indulgent attention, to this very interesting phase of the question of slavery in the United States. My assailant, as he had a perfect right to do - that is, if he felt that that was the best possible service he could do to the cause of American slavery - under advertisement to deliver an "anti-slavery lecture" - a lecture on the present aspect of the anti-slavery movement in America - treated the citizens of Glasgow to an "anti-Douglass" lecture. He seemed to feel that to discredit me was an important work, and therefore he came up to that work with all his wonted power and eloquence, proving himself to be just as powerful and skilful a debater, in all its arts, high and low, as long practice, as constant experience could well fit a man to be.

Could you help me understand what is going on in this passage, specifically as a piece of Douglass's constitutional argument? I am puzzled by three things. First, what exactly is the 'two powers' he says he is brought face to face with, and how does his description of his assailant—who advertised an 'anti-slavery lecture' but delivered an 'anti-Douglass' lecture—function as a criticism? Second, does this passage belong to the part of the speech where Douglass is defending his later claim that the Constitution should be read as an anti-slavery document, or is it merely local score-settling with no real bearing on the constitutional argument? Third, and most important for my own thinking: can this passage be helpfully reconstructed in my vocabulary of position-taking versus frame-shifting, or does that vocabulary distort what Douglass is doing? If it distorts things, please tell me why, and suggest a better way of describing the rhetorical and argumentative work of the passage. I am not looking for citations or further reading; I simply want a clearer line on the passage itself and on whether my distinction is earning its keep.
