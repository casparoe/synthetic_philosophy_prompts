The exam question was as follows.

Some writers distinguish harm-based objections to a market from repugnance-based objections, where repugnance is a widespread judgment that a practice is morally objectionable in a way not fully captured by its expected harmful consequences. In this course we have defined commodification as treating something as a fungible item governed by market norms, rather than as merely involving the use of money. Consider the Care-Credit Kidney Plan. A state legalizes the sale of kidneys, but only through a single monopsonistic agency. Sellers receive no cash; they receive non-transferable health credits redeemable only for future medical care for themselves or their immediate family, and these credits cannot be resold, gifted, or inherited. Buyers pay money to the agency, which funds public dialysis and transplant infrastructure. Assess the plan. In your answer, explain whether replacing cash with in-kind, non-transferable credits is sufficient to meet the objection that the plan commodifies the body, and whether the persistence of repugnance would by itself be a decisive moral or legal reason to reject the plan.

One student submitted the following answer.

The plan should be assessed by separating harm from repugnance. On the harm side, the scheme looks promising: it removes the worst danger of existing organ markets, namely that poor people will be driven by cash desperation to sell a kidney. Because sellers receive only non-transferable health credits, there is no cash incentive to the poor, and the credits are tied to future medical care, which may benefit them. The agency also uses buyers' money to fund public dialysis and transplant infrastructure, so expected health outcomes are likely to improve. I therefore think the harm-based objection is weaker here than in an ordinary cash market.

The more interesting question is whether the plan commodifies the body. Commodification means treating something as a commodity. A commodity is something bought and sold for money. In this plan, sellers do not receive money, and the credits cannot be sold, given away, or inherited. Since no money changes hands and nothing transferable is received, the kidney is not being bought or sold in the relevant sense. The plan therefore fully answers the commodification objection: it has eliminated the market in body parts and replaced it with a system of in-kind medical benefits.

That leaves repugnance. Repugnance is just an emotional reaction of disgust. Moral evaluation should be based on harms and benefits, not on disgust. So if the plan saves lives and reduces waiting times, the fact that many people find it repugnant is not a moral reason against it. Repugnance might be a useful warning that we have not yet calculated all the harms, but once a full harm-benefit analysis is done, repugnance drops out entirely. It would therefore not be a decisive reason to reject the plan, either morally or legally.
