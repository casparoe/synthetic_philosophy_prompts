QUESTION (Question 3, 30 marks) — The doomsday argument
(a) State the self-sampling assumption and explain what work it does in the doomsday argument. [6 marks]
(b) Someone alive today has a birth rank of roughly 60 billion. Using Bayes' theorem, show how that fact shifts an ordinary prior about how many humans will ever live. [12 marks]
(c) Outline one serious objection to the argument and say whether you think it defeats it. [12 marks]

STUDENT'S ANSWER:

(a) The argument rests on the self-indication assumption (SIA): that I should reason as if I were a random sample drawn from all the people who will ever live. Once you grant that, the rest follows more or less automatically, because a random draw is far more likely to land somewhere in the middle of a list than right at the very beginning of it. If humanity has a long future ahead of it, with trillions more people to come, then someone at birth rank 60 billion is freakishly early — and that is the surprising fact the argument trades on. Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" argument is a nice illustration of why the assumption has to be right: if one cosmological theory says the universe contains vastly more observers than another, then the mere fact that you find yourself existing is strong evidence for the theory with more observers. That is the same mechanism that does the work here, which is why Bostrom is happy to accept the doomsday argument.

(b) Set it up with Bayes. Let H1 be the "doom fairly soon" hypothesis, on which the total number of humans who will ever live is around 200 billion, and H2 the "long future" hypothesis, on which it is more like 200 trillion. Before I think about my birth rank my prior is maybe 5% on H1 — I have no special reason to think we're doomed — so prior odds of roughly 1 to 19 against doom soon. Now the likelihoods: if the total is N, the probability that I would find myself at rank 60 billion is 1/N, so P(my rank | H1) = 1/(2×10^11) and P(my rank | H2) = 1/(2×10^14). That is a likelihood ratio of 1000 to 1 in favour of H1. Multiplying, the posterior odds come out around 1000 to 19, i.e. roughly 50 to 1 on doom soon, or about 98%. So a prior of 5% gets turned into something close to certainty just by noticing where I am in the queue.

There's also a neat second route to the same 95%-ish number, which is reassuring because it doesn't use birth ranks at all — Gott's "delta t" argument. If I've turned up at a random moment during the human species' lifetime, then with 95% confidence the total duration is between 1/39 and 39 times its present age. Humans have been around for something like 200,000 years, which gives a remaining lifespan of somewhere between about 5,000 and 8 million years. Same conclusion as the birth-rank version, arrived at by counting years rather than people: two independent arguments agreeing is a good sign that neither is just an artefact of the set-up.

(c) The most serious objection is the reference class problem. The sampling assumption only does any work once you've decided what you're being sampled from: all humans who ever live? all observers? all possible minds, including future artificial ones and aliens? Each choice gives a different answer, and nobody has a non-arbitrary way to pick between them. I don't think this defeats the argument outright, but I do think it means the conclusion can't be stated as one sharp number. Some people hope that widening the reference class defuses the argument, but I'm not convinced that it does.

So, summing up: unless you already had strong independent reasons to expect doom, there is something like a 95% chance that humanity goes extinct within the next 9,000 years or so. I don't believe that, but I can't find the mistake, and that discomfort is most of what makes the argument interesting.
