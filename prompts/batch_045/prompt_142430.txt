hey — I'm TAing a first-year seminar on freedom this term and I want a "which theory of liberty do you actually hold" quiz for the last session. I need the leaves to include negative liberty, positive liberty in both its flavors (self-mastery and the exercise/self-realization kind), Pettit-style non-domination, and the feminist non-subordination cousin, plus up to two more you think belong in the space.

The whole point is that it should sort students by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so: no question may mention a theorist or a theory name, and no question may be phrased as "do you think freedom means X". 8–10 questions, each a short concrete case with three answer options — keep the options parallel across questions so the scoring isn't a mess.

Here's why I'm not just writing it myself. Every version of this I've seen runs the same three warhorses, and they're all easy to answer from vibes. I want the cases to carry the load, so:

- Set all of them inside one invented institution so they hang together. My idea was a deep-sea research station with a captain who holds formal emergency powers she has never once used, but a tenant co-op run by an allocation algorithm, or a monastery under a rule nobody can amend, might work better — pick one and stick to it for the whole quiz.
- Build each case to isolate a single parameter. These are the ones I want separated:
  1. interference that actually happens vs. power that is held but never exercised;
  2. whether the power is arbitrary (its holder needn't track your avowable interests) vs. bound to track them — e.g. an administrator who governs well and consults everyone but whose decisions there is no procedure for contesting;
  3. whether the power is cheap for its holder to exercise vs. ruinously costly — is a capacity no one would rationally use still a capacity?;
  4. internal obstacles (compulsion, weakness of will, preferences that adapted to a dominating power) vs. external ones;
  5. interference with an option the agent was never going to take, and the related question whether freedom is a scalar (how wide is the open field) or a status (does anyone stand over you);
  6. whether the agent knows about the power — a monitoring system nobody told you about, and its mirror image, a guard quietly retired years ago whose cameras are still switched on;
  7. structural cases with nobody in charge: the constraint comes from a rule with no author you can point at and no procedure for changing it. Does domination need a dominus?

Then give me the scoring key as a decision tree rather than a points tally: branch nodes are the parameters above (or better ones if you spot them), leaves are positions, each leaf with one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a defender has to swallow. Work out the tree from the logical space first and then slot the named positions into it, and for the cells nobody occupies, say whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

I also want you to flag where the tree genuinely can't seperate two leaves instead of faking a distinction. My guess is that non-domination and positive liberty converge on the adapted-preferences case and that republicanism splits internally over whether tracking interests is enough or contestability is required, and that a probabilistic or expected-cost version of domination collapses into a maximizing negative-liberty view — but do the work and tell me where you actually get stuck, not where I guessed.

Students haven't read anything yet, so no names in the quiz itself (names in the key are fine), and keep each case under about 60 words. The key can be as long as it needs to be.
