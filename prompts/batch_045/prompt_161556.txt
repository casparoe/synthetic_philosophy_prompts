hey — novelist here, philosophy-adjacent at best, and ive got a scene I can't get right and it's driving me nuts.

Setup, briefly: my new book has a retired logician, Marguerite, who is a hardcore deflationist about truth — she thinks there's nothing to truth beyond the equivalence schema, no substantive property out there doing explanatory work. The person she's arguing with across a kitchen table is an old friend who's a substantivist: truth is a genuine property of beliefs/sentences, and it earns its keep by explaining things — why true beliefs tend to make action come out well, why assertion and belief are normatively tied to truth, that sort of thing.

The wrinkle I want them to actually fight over, because I think it's where my book lives: the novel contains a manuscript Marguerite finds after her husband dies, narrated by someone who is plainly unreliable, and I keep needing sentences like "it's true in the manuscript that the letters were burned" — fictional truth, one level down. And then there are places where the invented story and the real one line up, so "true in the manuscript" and "true, full stop" collide in the same breath, and Marguerite can't tell which one she's committed to. I want to know whether her tidy deflationary story survives nested fictional truth, or whether the substantivist gets to say "your schema doesn't reach in here."

So: draft the joint statement the two of them would produce after a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration. Claims both can sign, the disagreements stated so precisely that each would accept the wording of their own side, the real cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case they'd both agree bears on it with each side's prediction written down in advance. If some of it is just a verbal dispute, say which part and why — I suspect at least one of their fights is about the word "property" and I don't want my characters shouting past each other for two hundred pages.

Sections, in this order:

1. "What both of them can sign" — numbered, about 450 words. Include what's genuinely not at issue and what they agree about ordinary truth talk and about truth-in-fiction.
2. "Where they still differ" — a table, about 350 words, one row per disagreement, each side's version phrased so that side would sign it.
3. "Cruxes and predictions" — about 500 words, two or three cruxes, each with the case or argument both accept as evidence and what each predicts it'll show.
4. "Lines for the scene" — about 200 words: three or four things Marguerite could actually say out loud that a real substantivist wouldn't call a strawman, plus one line she'd be tempted to say that a real deflationist would wince at.
