So here is where I've landed on Pascal's mugging, and I want you to try to break it. Bit of background: I'm retired now, spent a career in experimental physics doing calibration work and error budgets, and I've drifted into a philosophy reading group at the local lifelong learning place. My position is basically this: any hypothesis whose probability falls below some fixed floor gets treated as zero, exactly the way I'd treat a signal below the noise floor of an instrument. Call it the clipping rule. Pick the floor wherever you like, I've been using something like one in a trillion, which is roughly where I'd stop trusting my own apparatus. Once you clip, Pascal's mugging just dissolves, and so does every other argument that tries to move me with a huge payoff times a vanishing probability.

I want the honest defeater map for this, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward. Some specific things I want you to engage with rather than dodge:

One, the bundling problem. A single mugger offers me one shot at a trillion-trillion lives for a one-in-a-trillion-trillion chance. Fine, clipped. But now suppose there are a million independent muggers, or one mugger who offers to repeat the miracle a million times, or who offers a package of a million independent small miracles. The chance that at least one of them pays off is not below my floor. So does the clip apply per hypothesis or per decision? If per decision, fanaticism is straight back through the door and I've achieved nothing. If per hypothesis, I'm throwing away a non-negligible shot at an astronomically large good because of how the offer happens to be carved up into descriptions, which looks indefensible. Tell me which horn I should take and what each costs me.

Two, the graduated mugger. Suppose the mugger first demonstrates a hundred small credible things I can verify myself, so that by the time the big claim arrives my probability for it is high and not clipped at all. My rule then has nothing to say and I pay up. Does that show my view was never a view about the size of the stakes at all, just ordinary epistemology wearing a lab coat, and if so is it vacuous or is it fine?

Three, can anyone actually Dutch-book or money-pump a clipper, or construct a case where the rule guarantees a loss? I don't see one, but I'd like to know why not. Relatedly: clipping is discontinuous, so what does it do to the value of information? Would a clipper ever pay for evidence, and is refusing to is itself a reductio?

Four, scope. Does the rule cover known tiny probabilities too, like a real lottery with verifiable odds of one in a billion and a huge prize? If it does, I'm declining clearly favourable bets, which seems crazy. If it doesn't, and it only applies when the probability is itself uncertain or unverifiable, then say plainly what kind of rule that is and whether it collapses into one of the standard fixes, capping utility, or dropping expected-value maximisation, or a rejection of fanaticism generally. If it collapses into one of those, tell me what, if anything, I actually added.

Five, arguments from my own trade. I'm aware experimentalists do care about tails: importance sampling exists precisely because rare events carry the variance, and the look-elsewhere effect is a real thing. Say which of these analogies genuinely supports clipping and which cuts against me.

What would move me most is a concrete case at stakes I can actually picture, not ten to the hundred lives, where the clipping rule gives advice I'd call obviously wrong. Not a formal result alone. Also tell me what someone who accepts fanaticism would concede counts against their own side, not just what they say about mine, and give me your honest read on whether I've insulated this into unfalsifiability and whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind.
