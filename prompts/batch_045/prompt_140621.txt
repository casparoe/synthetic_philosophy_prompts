I have no background in any of this, I just got stuck on something a while ago and its been rattling around my head ever since.

My brother has played guitar in a cover band for about fifteen years. He says that on the best nights he isn't deciding anything: the solo just comes out, and if you asked him afterwards why he picked a particular note he couldn't tell you, and if he starts actually thinking about his fingering he falls apart. Meanwhile I once read one of those stories about digger wasps, where the female paralyses a cricket, dumps it outside her burrow, goes in to check the burrow, comes back out and drags the cricket in, and if you move the cricket a few inches while she's inside she goes back in and does the check again, and you can keep making her repeat it. I have no idea whether that story is accurate or got embellished in the retelling, but the word for that kind of rigid routine, sphexishness, stuck with me. What bugs me is that from the outside, my brother on a good night and the wasp have the same shape: cue in, smooth unbroken sequence out, nothing being weighed. So I ended up building this argument:

(1) You act freely only if, in the exact situation you were actually in, you could have done otherwise.

(2) And "could have done otherwise in that exact situation" has to mean: the very same situation, and your response still wasn't already settled by it.

(3) The wasp's problem is exactly that the same situation produces the same response every time, even once the routine has stopped making any sense. That's what makes her the picture of unfreedom.

(4) But my brother's playing is even less variable than a beginner's. Practice makes you good by making you reliable, which is just another word for rigid, so by the test in (2) his best solo is more sphexish than a student's first attempt. I've started calling this the competence trap: the property we most admire in a skill is the very property we use to say a mechanism isn't free.

(5) The obvious reply is that he's different because he would have adjusted instantly if the amp had blown or someone had called a different key, whereas the wasp grinds on. But that's a claim about a situation that didn't happen, and (1) said the test concerns the situation he was actually in. So that reply is cheating.

(6) So either the best playing of his life wasn't free, which is crazy, because it's the case I'd most want to call free, or (1) is the wrong test.

(7) Fine, drop (1): what matters is flexibility, really tracking how things are rather than running a fixed routine. But then a twenty dollar thermostat beats my brother, because the thermostat genuinely varies what it does with conditions while he just executed a routine.

(8) So the rigid-versus-flexible axis doesn't carve out freedom at all, and between the wasp and us there's no difference in kind, only a difference in how many gears are involved.

I want to keep (4), I really don't want to say that getting good at something makes you less free. But I also don't want a thermostat to come out freer than a person, and I don't want to swallow (8). Which step is the broken one, why does it look fine when you read it quickly, and what does fixing it cost me?

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no intro and no summing-up at the end.
