I sit on the investment committee of a small university endowment, and one of our senior trustees has basically one argument that he uses for everything. Active management, he says, is like sitting down at a poker table: if you can't tell which player is the mark within twenty minutes, then you're the mark. And since the market price already reflects everything anybody actually knows, we should fire our active managers, go fully indexed, and drop the fossil-fuel tilt we adopted last year too, because tilting is just paying someone to be wrong about something nobody can be right about. Most of the committee finds this persuasive and I dont think they should, but I can't put my finger on why.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the correspondence table first -- what plays the cards, the other players, the ante, the mark, and what exactly would have to be true of us and our counterparties for there to have to be a mark at all. Then where the mapping breaks. The ones I keep circling on: trades that are really risk-sharing or hedging rather than anyone claiming to know something, our private-asset book where the prices are appraisals that only get refreshed a couple of times a year, and the fact that a price never comes with reasons attached, so we can never ask it why it believes what it believes. Then tell me which of those disanalogies actually matter for the two conclusions he's drawing and which are decoration -- his indexing conclusion and his drop-the-tilt conclusion seem to me to be doing different kinds of work, and I suspect the poker table only supports one of them. Does either survive, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? American English please, and keep it to about two pages since I'm circulating it before Thursday.
