I'm a high school senior working on a paper about whether utilitarianism's habit of adding everyone's happiness together is a mistake, and I have built an argument I no longer trust, because it seems to hand the conclusion to the utilitarians.

The target is the objection that goes by the name of the separateness of persons: summing across people is supposed to be illegitimate because there is nobody whose good the sum is — no one lives the total, only this person and that person live their own portions — so a larger total cannot be what makes an outcome better. I want to keep that. But here is the argument I ended up with:

(1) An outcome is better or worse only in virtue of being better or worse for someone.

(2) Suppose one person loses a great deal while ten people each gain a little. The gains are had by the ten, one each; the loss is had by the one; the favourable balance is had by nobody. By (1), a balance that nobody has cannot make the outcome better. So there is no licit adding up across persons.

(3) What licenses adding up within a single life — painful dental work now, decades of healthy teeth later; hours of practice for a recital much later — can only be the unity of a life. And the unity of a life is a matter of degree: the stage that sits in the dentist's chair is tied to the stage that enjoys the teeth by memory, intention, and character, and ties like those come in degrees.

(4) Degrees of unity do not stop at the skin. Take the Pair: two sisters who since childhood have had access to each other's experience in real time, who deliberate together about everything, share every plan, and have never formed an intention the other did not share. Now take Nadia at eighteen and Nadia at sixty-eight: by then she shares almost no memories, desires, or traits with the teenager, having been changed slowly, one small alteration at a time, so that no stage along the way differed noticeably from the one before it. By the measures in (3), the sisters are more unified with each other than the woman is with herself across those fifty years.

(5) If unity is what licenses the ledger, and unity is a matter of degree, then in (4) the ledger should run between the sisters at least as readily as it runs across those fifty years.

(6) But adding up within a life is plainly in order, and the separateness objection is supposed to protect individuals precisely by protecting their lives over time.

(7) So either the objection collapses into the view it was aimed at — adding up is fine, and the only question left is how much unity a subject needs — or the objection stands and prudence is as unjustified as utilitarianism's sacrifices, which is absurd.

I dislike (7) but cannot locate the mistake. My best guess is that something shifts between (1) and (3) — perhaps better for someone and permitted to trade off are two different relations and I have run them together — but I cannot say what the difference is. A second worry, which may be the same worry in another shape: if (2) is right, I also cannot say that you ought to pull five strangers out of a burning building rather than one stranger, since that too appeals to totals, and everyone I have asked thinks that is obviously what you ought to do, including people who press the objection.

I would most like to keep (6) and the thought that utilitarianism gets something deeply wrong when it spends one person for a larger total. I am willing to give up almost anything else, including (1). Which step do you reject, why does that step look innocent when you read it quickly, and what does rejecting it cost me — including what the other options would cost, if you can say?
