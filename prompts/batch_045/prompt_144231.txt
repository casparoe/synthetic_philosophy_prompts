I work in interpretability. My team pulls candidate features — directions, clusters, circuits — out of trained systems, and then has to decide which of them to write down in a report as features of the world, as opposed to artifacts of a training distribution or of our own clustering machinery. The same question in a different costume comes up whenever someone proposes a new diagnostic category, or defends an existing one, or when two fields carve up the same objects differently and each insists their cut is the real one. I have read the rationalist canon on this — clusters in thingspace, the idea that a category is worth only as much as the inferences it licenses, the ontological-crisis material, the standard suspicions about essences — and I do not think it adds up to a specification. So before I read any more theories of natural kinds, I want to know what I should be demanding of one.

Fix the purpose as mine. The theory is to grade candidate categories and tell me which ones I am entitled to do four things with:

1. project inductively from sampled instances to unsampled ones;
2. treat as invariant when I intervene at some other level of description;
3. expect to re-identify after the underlying descriptive vocabulary is replaced by a better one;
4. rely on in the awkward case where the category itself becomes a target of optimization, so that future members are selected *because* they are members.

A theory that returns no verdict on those four jobs is useless to me regardless of its metaphysical virtues. Note also that a theory which can only ever say yes is defective: part of what I want is a criterion capable of delivering a negative verdict about a category that working scientists currently take seriously, and I want to be told which of the requirements is doing that work.

So: write the desiderata. For each, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable given the four jobs above or as a preference internal to one camp — the micro-essentialists, the homeostatic-cluster people, the degrees-of-naturalness people, the real-pattern/compression people, the promiscuous pluralists. Among the candidates I want adjudicated:

- Projectibility, with the grue-style cases that motivate it.
- Sparseness: kinds must be few, and arbitrarily disjunctive or gerrymandered properties must not qualify.
- Causal or homeostatic unity as opposed to mere similarity of appearance.
- Intrinsicality, or at least non-indexicality, to rule out things like tallest person in the room, pest, weed, local.
- Mind-independence from human interests and from the investigative tools we happen to have.
- Tolerance of multiple realizability — depression, money, the gene, computation — versus the demand that a kind be microstructurally uniform.
- Discoverability: that a kind be recoverable from finite noisy samples by something like clustering. I suspect this one is a prejudice of my own camp rather than a desideratum, but I want the argument either way.
- Method-independence: that what you learn about instances gathered by one detection method should still hold for instances gathered by a different one, so that a category defined by a single instrument does not count.
- Optimization-stability: that the properties which made a category projectible should not evaporate when membership in it is selected for, in the way a proxy stops tracking its target once you push on it.
- Portability across a change of fundamental ontology, so that the criterion can tell me what was preserved and what was lost when the furniture of the world gets replaced.
- Grain-honesty: the theory must say at what grain the joints are, and must not imply that maximally fine-grained fundamental properties and coarse special-science ones are natural in the same way or to the same degree.

Alongside these, give the distinctions a theory in this area must not collapse — natural versus merely projectible, natural versus useful-to-us, being a kind at all versus how natural a kind is, a real kind versus a stable pattern, a kind versus an individual — and the findings it is not allowed to contradict: that species are historical lineages rather than microstructurally uniform classes, that many scientifically indispensable predicates are multiply realized, that the same objects admit many partitionings that predict equally well, that some categories are unified at one level and hopelessly heterogeneous at another.

Then the part I most want. Tell me which subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and give the argument — if the list itself is a proof that no theory can have everything, that is more valuable to me than any scorecard. Flag every pair you suspect of tension, and say which single requirement excludes the largest share of otherwise-attractive candidates.

Cases the list has to be testable against: water and jade; chemical elements; a biological species; cancer; a psychiatric diagnosis; intelligence; money; a weed; sand; a direction in a latent space; conscious experience. Assume no library access and no references — I want the criteria and the cases, not who proposed what. A short table at the end grading the five or six main camps against the list is fine, but keep it to an appendix; the thinking should be in the requirements.
