Hi — I'm a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I've got a book on the go in which two seperate groups of people emerge in a small port town over about thirty years, and the division ends up deciding who gets hired, who gets believed by the doctor, who ends up dead at fifty — but nobody in the town ever decides on it, names it, notices it, or has any belief about it whatsoever. My editor keeps saying the townspeople read as too passive, which is sort of the point but also might mean I haven't built the mechanism right. I was chewing this over a bad lunch with a friend who does social anthropology and she said something like "that's just a statistical cluster, not a kind," and I've been stuck on whether she's right ever since.

So here's what I need. Build me a thought experiment, set somewhere a reader would believe — a workplace, a hospital, a boarding school, that port town, no sci-fi, no fantasy, no demons or gods — in which a social kind of person comes to exist and to have real causal power purely as the unintended upshot of lots of ordinary people's mundane, self-regarding acts, whilst all of the following hold: nobody in the story has any belief at all about the kind; nobody applies a term or a concept to its members; nobody has ever conferred a status on them, formally or otherwise; and no one's behaviour is explained by there own sense of belonging to it. Then check it against three views, in plain language and with no citations: (1) a collective-acceptance or status-function view, on which something is a social kind because we collectively treat or accept it as such; (2) a constitutive, Haslanger-ish view, on which a kind is socially constructed when social practices and relations partly constitute something as being of that kind and its being that kind structures advantage or disadvantage in a context; (3) a looping or interactive-kind view, on which a human kind is social because being classified under it changes the people classified, who then react, which changes the classification, and so on. I want (2) to say "yes, constructed" and (1) and (3) to say "no," and I want those verdicts to fall out of the theories rather than you baking them into the set-up — if you have to stipulate that a view says something, say so instead.

After the scenario and the three verdicts, give me one closing paragraph on which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I put a word for the kind into the townspeople's mouths halfway through the book (I suspect that flips view 3 but I can't see exactly why), and how a defender of (1) and of (3) would each try to argue that what I've described isn't a kind at all, only a pattern someone could draw a line around. Keep it tight: scenario in one paragraph, max 130 words; each verdict max 70 words; the closing paragraph max 100 words; under 500 in total. British English please, and no bibliography, no names with dates after them, no "see especially chapter four" — I'll sort that out myself if I ever need footnotes. If you think my three-way split is muddled as I've stated it, tell me in one line before you start rather than quietly repairing it.
