I write fiction — three novels, the most recent narrated by a woman who spends four hundred pages being completely certain about the wrong thing — and I've agreed, somewhat against my better judgment, to run a single ninety-minute evening session next month at a writers' centre. About fourteen adults, mostly novelists and a couple of poets, no philosophers among them, and the only theme I was given is "doubt and certainty," which is obviously a trap. I've been reading around the late remarks on certainty — the posthumously published ones usually titled On Certainty — and what I want my group to run into, physically rather than by being told, is this: that a doubt needs somewhere to stand, that the claims we most want to doubt are the ones our doubting quietly depends on, that the regress of reasons doesn't hit bedrock but something more like a riverbed that shifts slowly and is shown in what people do rather than asserted by them, and that a doubt which reaches that deep doesn't actually land on anything — it just spins. I don't want an evening of people saying "but how do you know?" in increasingly tiresous voices, and I don't want to name any view, any book, or any philosopher until the last ten minutes: the experience has to come first and the vocabulary afterwards.

Here is what I've half-designed and don't trust. Players hold a handful of claim cards, and you may only put a claim into play by citing another claim already on the table as its backing; each claim accumulates a score equal to the number of later claims that cite it; then an "audit" reveals that three or four cards are doing nearly all the load-bearing work and have never themselves been backed; then I confiscate the highest-scoring card and make them rebuild on the ruins. I also want, somewhere in there, a scarce "doubt budget" rather than unlimited scepticism, a rule that any doubt must be accompanied by a concrete story about what the world would look like if the doubted claim were false (so that idle verbal doubt can be told apart from doubt with traction), and a closing coda in which they're asked to do something utterly mundane — write their name on a slip and hand it in, read the time off their own phone — immediately after having conceded, in the game, that they can't rule out the alternative. I don't know whether those pieces fit together, whether the scoring rewards the wrong behaviour, or whether the confiscation round will feel like a trick rather than a discovery. Materials have to fit in a tote bag — index cards, pens, a timer, nothing printed in colour, no money and no real stakes.

So: take the skeleton above, repair it or replace it with something better, and give me rules that work with fourteen literate sceptics in ninety minutes, a timeline with minute marks, the exact facilitator moves, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the idea — including the moment I should finally say out loud what it is they've been doing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, tell me which rules to bend if the group turns out to be quicker or slower than I expect, and be frank about which feature of this family of views the game simply cannot stage and which I'd have to supply in words. One more thing, since I intend to write about this afterwards and would rather not publish something a reviewer can pick apart: in the debrief notes, please separate what is settled among people who actually work on these remarks from what is live dispute — for instance whether the load-bearing claims are beliefs at all, whether they are true, whether they can be rationally assessed, whether the whole picture collapses into some form of relativism about reasons, and whether it is compatible with thinking that some of our ordinary practices of justification really do track the world. Where you're telling me something contested, say so, and don't let a reading you find elegant stand in for the consensus.
