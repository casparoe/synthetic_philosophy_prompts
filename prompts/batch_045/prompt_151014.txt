Hey — I help run a monthly "ethics and comics" night for our undergrad philosophy society, and we're doing Watchmen next week. Most of the group has only seen the movie, not read the comic, so if the two differ somewhere that actually matters for the reading, flag it. What I want to spend the evening on is Ozymandias's plan read through the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism — the Rawls/Nozick-flavoured complaint that aggregative moral reasoning treats distinct people as if they were just locations of value inside one big life, so that a gain over here can "make up for" a loss over there, and that this is the deep thing consequentialism gets wrong, not just an unfortunate side effect. Concretely: the comic seems hand-built for this argument. Weighing three million dead New Yorkers against hundreds of millions (arguably billions) of survivors looks exactly like the move the objection says is illegitimate, and Rorschach's refusal to compromise even in the face of Armageddon reads like the objection in human form. But I don't want us to spend an hour on the yes/no verdict on whether Veidt was right. I want us to get clearer on what the objection actually is — how it differs from plain deontology, from "there are some things you just don't do," from the demandingness complaint, and from the boring thought that numbers don't matter — and then on whether it really reaches him, or whether it's mostly rhetorical heat around a tradeoff that everyone is already making in their heads.

Three things I keep going back and forth on and would love you to press on. First, the sacrifice in the story is hidden: the people who are saved don't know they were saved, the people who die don't know they were sacrificed, and the whole fragile peace rests on that not coming out. Does that make the objection bite harder or softer? On one hand there's nobody left to say the justifying thing to, and no violation anyone can acknowledge, so maybe the complaint about "what can be avowed to each person" quietly collapses. On the other hand, being used in a way that can never be named, honoured, compensated or mourned by the people who benefit looks like being treated as a mere instrument in the most literal possible sense — the dead don't even get to be martyrs. Second, a chunk of what the plan buys isn't the continued lives of existing people but the existence of future people who would otherwise never be born. Is that a different kind of good for the purposes of the objection? The complaint is usually about someone bearing a cost so that someone else gets a benefit, and it's not obvious there's a "someone" on the receiving end here in the same way. Third, the Parfit-style deflation: if persons aren't deeply separate facts in the way the objection seems to assume — if personal identity is a matter of degree, if there are borderline and split cases — then what happens to the objection? And the story gives you a character who is arguably in exactly that position: Doctor Manhattan, who experiences all of his times at once and for whom other people's boundaries clearly don't have the grip they have on us. Is the comic suggesting that genuine impartial aggregation is only available from a standpoint no human occuppies, i.e. that the separateness of persons point is at bottom a claim about the kinds of creatures we are and the moral psychology we can actually have, rather than a metaphysical claim about what matters?

What I'd like from you is a reading, not a survey: where the objection genuinely illuminates a scene or a character's choice (I'd love two or three specific moments I can put on the board for the group, including at least one from Rorschach's own storyline, since his absolutism has its own ugly edges that the objection doesn't obviously endorse), where the comic resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so, and finally whether the story adds anything to the philosophical argument that the papers don't — something about scale, or about what it feels like from the inside of the aggregator's head, or about how a shared trauma manufactures the unity that the objection then presupposes. If you think the whole lens is the wrong one and something else in the ethics literature gets Veidt better, say that too, but argue for it.
