I would be grateful for your help with an argument I have been using in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of medicine. A respondent has convinced me that it proves too much, and after several weeks I still cannot locate the break.

(1) Induction over a kind requires what I will call inferential robustness. A predicate F licenses expectations about unobserved instances only if the features in virtue of which an individual satisfies F are not themselves altered by the circulation of what is believed about Fs.

(2) Kinds of people are not robust in that sense. The criteria, screening instruments, self-help literature, and insurance codes through which a psychiatric category travels are taken up by the people whom the category classifies, and the uptake alters how they experience themselves, what they report, and how they present. It alters the classifiers as well: my own threshold for what counts as clinically significant distress moves with the patients who come through the door.

(3) There is no description of a kind of person that is guaranteed in advance to sit outside the loop. For any candidate — a symptom profile, a self-report scale, a biomarker, a pattern of passively collected phone data — coming to know it changes how some people seek, produce, simulate, suppress, or display it. Nor does modeling the feedback let us out: the model is published too, and the loop becomes second-order.

(4) Therefore no kind of person is inferentially robust, and nothing established about a psychiatric category in one decade underwrites the application of that category in the next.

I take the conclusion to be unacceptable, and not merely because we plainly do learn things. It is self-consuming: clinician, self-report, prevalence, and uptake are themselves kinds of people, so if (4) is true the studies I cite in support of (2) do not license (2) beyond the decade of their publication. And (4) was never really about psychiatry: it would hold equally for smoker, unemployed, literate, or carrier of a communicable disease.

Two constraints on what I am willing to surrender. I want to keep the falsity of (4): some of what psychiatry has learned has traveled across decades and across health systems, and I am not prepared to call that an accident. I also want to keep (2), because the literature on looping effects, in Hacking's sense, strikes me as empirically sound and I teach it. I would prefer not to give up (1), since I use it in another project to mark what is distinctive about classifying people as opposed to classifying cells or minerals; but I would rather give up (1) than keep a valid argument to a false conclusion, and I would like to know which of these attachments is the expensive one.

My own repair fails, and I suspect the failure is diagnostic. I want to distinguish uptake that changes which individuals fall under a category from uptake that changes what falling under it consists in, and to claim that (2) and (3) establish only the first. But the categories I care about are defined by their criteria — there is no underlying mechanism to which the criteria answer, which is why the familiar proposal to rebuild classification on mechanisms rather than symptoms is so attractive — and for a criteria-constituted category the first kind of uptake just is the second. So my repair seems to cut psychiatry off from whatever makes other medical kinds tractable, and I cannot tell whether that is a discovery about psychiatric kinds or a sign that I have been looking for the break in the wrong place.

Which step is the break? Name it, explain why it looked sound, and set out what I pay for each of the alternatives I might have rejected instead. And tell me whether the contrast between criteria-constituted and mechanism-constituted kinds is doing the work I think it is, or whether I have invented a distinction in order to protect a premise.
