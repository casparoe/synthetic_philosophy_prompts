THE QUESTION (20 marks)

(a) Distinguish first-order from higher-order evidence, and explain what is meant by epistemic akrasia. (b) Consider the following case. D is an experienced analyst who has run the same assay several hundred times. Her result on a sample says the batch is above the legal limit for a contaminant. Before signing it off she learns two things: (i) the pipette used in that run was subsequently found to have a calibration fault, and (ii) in a blind re-analysis of archived samples from her bench that week, roughly two thirds of the results were wrong. She re-checks the arithmetic and re-reads the trace; the peak looks clean and the number is unchanged. She ends up about 75% confident that the batch is above the limit and, at the same time, judging that someone in her epistemic position ought to be only about 40% confident of that. Call the response that says she should bring her confidence into line with that judgement conciliatory, and the response that says she may reasonably stay where she is steadfast. (1) Is D being irrational? (2) Is her belief that the batch is over the limit justified? Say which of the two responses you accept and why, and be explicit about what is doing the work: the fact that she now has evidence about her own reliability, or something else. Finally, address one complication: D's re-check of the arithmetic and the trace uses the very capacities — reading an instrument, drawing an inference from a peak — that (i) and (ii) give her reason to distrust.

THE ANSWER

First-order evidence is evidence that bears directly on how the world is. Here that is the instrument reading and the shape of the peak, which bear directly on whether the contaminant is above the limit. Higher-order evidence is evidence about one's evidence, or about the quality of one's reasoning: it bears not on the level of the contaminant but on whether D's first-order evidence is a good guide to the truth. (i) and (ii) are higher-order in this sense, since they tell her that the process which produced her result is error-prone without telling her anything new about the sample.

Epistemic akrasia is the state of believing a proposition while at the same time believing or judging that one ought not to believe it — that one's evidence does not support it, or supports it less than one is in fact supporting it. It is called akrasia by analogy with practical akrasia, where an agent knowingly acts against her own judgement about what she should do. The epistemic case is supposed to be the same kind of failure of self-government in the sphere of belief, and it is supposed to be puzzling because belief, unlike action, aims at the truth, so it ought to line up with however well supported the proposition actually is. Note that D's two attitudes do not strictly contradict each other: the 75% is a confidence in the batch being over the limit, and the 40% is a judgement about what-confidence-someone-in-her-position-should-have, which is a different content. The conflict is therefore not a flat logical inconsistency, and that is what makes the case interesting.

I think D is being irrational, and I think her belief is not justified. The crucial point is that her total evidence now favours the proposition that the batch is under the limit. If two thirds of the results from her bench that week were wrong, then the probability that her result is wrong is about two thirds, and conditionalising on that she should be at around 40%, which is below the threshold for belief; so by her own lights she should believe that the batch is under the limit, or at any rate suspend judgement on it. She does not do this, so she is in a state of epistemic akrasia. And since akrasia is a failure of rationality, and something cannot be justified if it is irrational, her belief that the batch is over the limit is not justified. This is what the conciliatory response says, and I think it is right.

The steadfast response, as I understand it, denies that higher-order evidence is really evidence at all. On that view, evidence is just first-order evidence about the world, and learning that one's pipette was miscalibrated, or that one's bench had a bad week, is at most a psychological influence on one's confidence which one is free to set aside; that is why the steadfast response lets D stay at 75%. But this cannot be right. (i) and (ii) are perfectly ordinary empirical findings about the world — about a piece of glassware and about an error rate — so they are just as much evidence as anything else in the case, and a view that says otherwise is not really engaging with the example. The steadfast response is therefore no better than wishful thinking.

Last, the complication. I don't think it changes anything. The re-check is not independent, because it uses the same capacities that (i) and (ii) give her reason to distrust, so it cannot get her back up to 75%. A check only helps if it is independent of whatever went wrong. But this doesn't rescue the steadfast view either: it just means D is stuck, and the conciliatory response stands. The worry doesn't spread to the higher-order evidence itself, because (i) and (ii) come from other technicians using other instruments, so there is no regress.
