After nearly four decades in experimental physical chemistry — much of it spent fitting models to spectra that were noisy, incomplete, and far sparser than the textbook derivations assumed — I have begun auditing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science. One session has stuck with me. The question was whether the argument from the poverty of the stimulus shows that a child must bring domain-specific grammatical knowledge to the acquisition of a language, or only that we have underestimated how much structure is already present in what the child actually hears. In my own work I never had to suppose that the spectrometer knew spectroscopy: one supplied a sensible model, a great deal of data, and an inference procedure, and the rest followed. My instinct therefore lies with the second camp. But the first camp's case seems to contain more than my instinct can absorb, and I cannot locate where the real disagreement sits.

What I would like is a written dialogue — something I could read aloud to three retired colleagues who argue about this over lunch — between a defender of the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument and a defender of the opposite view, that the stimulus is not impoverished but merely under-analysed. Both should be first-rate, both should know the other's literature, and neither should be a foil: each should be able to state the other's strongest argument in a form the other accepts before answering it. Set it where you like; a walk across the campus after the seminar would suit me.

Three things I would ask you to build into the exchange.

First, pin down which poverty claim is at issue, because I suspect the seminar conflated several. There is the claim that the relevant data type simply never occurs in the child's input; the claim that it does occur but too sparsely, noisily, or unreliably for any general-purpose procedure to extract the pattern from the sample a child actually gets; and the claim that the data are present and usable but underdetermine the outcome, because indefinitely many incompatible grammars fit them equally well and nothing in the child's experience adjudicates between them. These are different claims, and evidence for one does not obviously transfer to the others. I would like each character to say which one they take to be the live thesis and why, and to be made to answer the version the other one is defending.

Second, give them a concrete case to fight over, and let it be a case that cuts both ways. Suppose — as a thought experiment, since no committee would approve it — that children are reared in an environment where every adult utterance passes through a filter that removes subordination entirely: no relative clauses, no complement clauses, no embedded questions, complex thoughts expressed only as chains of short clauses linked by anaphora. The finding, when these children are four, is mixed in an awkward way: they produce and comprehend genuinely hierarchical constructions that occur nowhere in what they heard, but they build the marker of subordination out of a discourse particle which in the filtered input appeared only at utterance edges and never clause-internally. Structure invented; material recycled. I want the nativist to say what in this datum the domain-general story cannot explain, and the rich-input theorist to explain the same datum without remainder — and then I want each of them to answer the other's account of it rather than restate their own.

Third, let the nativist deal with a puzzle from my own lifetime. Systems trained on text alone, with no innate grammar and no referential grounding, now show a remarkable command of long-distance dependencies and of distinctions that seminar treated as unlearnable from what a child hears. That looks like an existence proof against the argument. I want the nativist's reply to be the best available one rather than a dismissal, and I also want the rich-input theorist to be honest about the respects in which such systems are not in the business a child is in — the volume of data, the absence of a shared world, and the difference between what is learnable from a trillion words and what is learnable from the few million a child gets by the age of four.

Two constraints to keep it honest. The word 'innate' should not do unpaid work: have them separate, at some point, the claim that the outcome is a canalised species-typical development, the claim that there is domain-specific architecture — sequence memory, auditory priors, limits on what can be held in mind at once — from the claim that there is domain-specific representational content, and let the rich-input theorist concede the first two and deny the third while the nativist says why, exactly, the first two cannot do the work asked of them. And please do not let either side lean on the classic identification-in-the-limit results from formal learning theory — the ones showing that a learner fed only positive examples cannot identify an arbitrary recursively enumerable language from an arbitrary enumeration of it — without saying what those results do and do not cover; if the nativist cites them, the other should be given the standard reply, and if the nativist concedes the reply, they should say what remains of the argument.

Do not let them converge. I would rather the dialogue end with each having located the precise sentence the other one cannot grant, and with each saying what experiment — feasible or not — would settle it. Twelve hundred words or so would be ideal.
