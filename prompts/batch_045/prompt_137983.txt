Every few years the same exchange restarts in my corner of the internet: someone writes that the genome is a code, a program, a set of instructions for building an organism; someone else replies that this is a metaphor in a lab coat, that DNA is just a polymer doing chemistry, and that the reading is done by machinery which is itself built from what it reads. It never converges, because nobody ever says what would count as settling it. I'd rather fix the standard first than read another round.

So: write the desiderata for an account of genetic information — what a theory has to deliver in order to be worth arguing about. The purpose I care about is this one: deciding whether a working molecular biologist is entitled to her working vocabulary (genes specifying traits, cells reading and misreading signals, machinery looking up a codon), or whether that vocabulary adds nothing over a purely chemical description. Fix adequacy to that purpose.

Cases I want on the list, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement:

- No collapse. On the usual correlational or statistical notion of information, a whale's genome carries information about the ocean and a forest fire's smoke carries a message about the fire. Say what relation is supposed to be special here, and why the account doesn't turn out to be true of every causal process.
- No external interpreter. The translational machinery is partly encoded in the sequences it reads, and the mapping is contingent rather than chemically forced — there are variant codes in mitochondria and recoded stop codons — so content has to be located somewhere other than in the intrinsic properties of the bases, without smuggling in a homunculus.
- One message per token is probably wrong. The same stretch of sequence can be coding region in one context, splice-regulatory element in another, structural or template element in a third. And suppose we discovered a lineage whose decoding is context-dependent, so that what a codon specifies varies with the cell's metabolic state: does the account still have anything to say?
- The possibility of error. A semantic theory needs a difference between the codon meaning X and the machinery producing the wrong product. Mutation, mistranslation, and readthrough have to come apart, or the theory isn't semantic at all.
- Environment relativity. Same genome, different phenotype: information has to be relativized to something — a developmental context, a lineage-typical environment, an evolutionary history — and the theory has to say which.
- Non-DNA heredity: methylation marks, inherited prion states, parental-effect gene products. Either these carry the relevant kind of content as well, or the theory explains why not.
- Selection and function: most of a large genome is not under selection in any simple sense, so say whether content tracks selected function, actual downstream effects, or something else — and whether a novel sequence that has never been selected can mean anything.
- Payoff: does the account license any inference from sequence to phenotype that chemistry alone wouldn't?

Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a camp. Then tell me which single requirement excludes the most of the familiar options — the deflationary just-chemistry reading, the instruction-or-program reading, the selected-function reading — and whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. That last part is what I most want. A table is fine; keep the weight on the criteria rather than on scoring the positions.
