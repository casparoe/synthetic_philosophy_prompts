I teach and write in moral philosophy, and I am trying to find out what I actually think about killing insects in the course of pest control. Here are my verdicts, given on gut rather than on a theory. Indoor residual spraying against malaria mosquitoes across a whole district: permissible, and not even regrettable. Fumigating a bedbug-infested apartment: permissible. Killing the ants that come into my kitchen with a bait: permissible, but it strikes me as slightly shabby in a way the bedbug case does not, and sealing the points where they get in strikes me as better than poisoning them — not, I think, merely because it is more effective. Aerial spraying against a locust swarm threatening subsistence crops: permissible. Neonicotinoid use to keep pests off the ornamental shrubs of a golf course: not permissible. A gene-drive release designed to suppress a malaria vector across its entire range: I withhold permission, even though it promises far more human benefit than the spraying I have just permitted. Killing rats in a grain store troubles me much more than killing insect pests in comparable numbers, but I do not want to explain that by saying their lives matter more simply because they are vertebrates. And I do care how the killing is done: a slow poison that leaves cockroaches convulsing for days strikes me as worse than one that kills quickly, which seems to commit me to insect suffering mattering — yet I felt nothing about the mosquitoes.

My tentative principle is that what matters is expected suffering, the expectation taken over genuine uncertainty about insect sentience, with many small sufferings adding up linearly. That principle appears to condemn the spraying I cannot bring myself to condemn, and it seems to say nothing about why a species-level intervention feels different from an annual one.

Is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts — drawing on sentience uncertainty, on the difference between killing and preventing existence as in the sterile insect technique, on collective or ecological goods that are not sums of individual welfare, or on something I have not considered? State it precisely, say which clause does the work in which case, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot save and whether I should give them up, and tell me what it implies for a case I have not listed: rearing crickets or black soldier fly larvae at industrial scale as a protein source, where vast numbers are bred and then killed by freezing or grinding, and where the insects are not pests at all.
