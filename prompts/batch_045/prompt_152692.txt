I am writing a book chapter on whether the informational vocabulary of molecular biology — the gene as a message, the sequence as a code, translation as reading, mutation as error — can be taken literally, or whether it is at bottom a convenient way of describing causal structure. Before I commit to a position I want to know what my own verdicts on cases amount to. I have listed them below, including one pair I cannot reconcile. Please extract the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that they are tracking; tell me which verdicts it cannot preserve and whether I should abandon those; state what it predicts about cases I have not listed; and say plainly if no single principle will do and whether I should then settle for pluralism about information in biology.

My verdicts:

1. A codon triplet in messenger RNA, mapped to an amino acid by the translation apparatus. Verdict: a literal code. The mapping is chemically unconstrained, the codon stands for the amino acid, and an explanation of what happens at the ribosome is incomplete unless it mentions what the codon is for.

2. A transcription-factor binding site, which recruits a protein because of the fit between two molecular surfaces. Verdict: no code, no semantics — a causal and structural fact, even though genomicists speak of a regulatory code. I want to say that this usage is shorthand for a complicated causal profile.

3. Combinatorial patterns of histone modifications, the so-called histone code: distinct enzymes write the marks, other proteins read them, and the mapping from a combination of marks to a downstream effect is largely independent of the chemistry of the marks themselves. Verdict: a code only by courtesy. Yet it has writer/reader asymmetry, arbitrariness, combinatorics, and partial transmission through cell division.

4. Methylation at CpG sites: transmitted through cell division, because maintenance machinery recognises hemimethylated sites and methylates the newly synthesised strand, and in some organisms transmitted across generations. Verdict: I lean towards not genetic information, but noticeably less confidently than in case 3.

5. A prion conformation: heritable, imposed on other molecules of the same protein by direct templating, but not a polymer sequence. Verdict: no code.

6. An engineered system, of the kind synthetic biology is currently assembling in pieces: a cell carrying a polymer of non-natural nucleotides, with a dedicated polymerase that copies it base by base and a dedicated adaptor and translation apparatus that reads its triplets and incorporates a nonstandard amino acid. The polymer is not transmitted reliably beyond a handful of cell divisions, and it has no evolutionary history. Verdict: a genuine code, not a metaphor.

7. One triplet, in one molecule, read under two different code tables — a codon reassignment of the sort that has been engineered in the laboratory, in a cell that still uses the standard table for its other messages — yielding different amino acids. Verdict: the content changes with the reading system, but I do not want to conclude that content is merely reader-relative; I want to say there is a privileged reading system, and I cannot say what privileges it.

8. A stretch of DNA produced by drift, with no history of selection, never transcribed, that would act as a promoter if it were relocated next to a gene. Verdict: it means nothing now, but would mean something if a reader were supplied.

9. The bits in a file stored on a memory stick. Verdict: paradigm code, and I take the reason to be the intentions of those who made and use the format — which the genetic code lacks.

The pair I cannot reconcile is cases 3 and 6: the case I refuse to call a code displays more of the standard marks of coding than the case I insist is one, the salient difference being that one is template-copied and inherited while the other is designed.

The principle I have been toying with: a sequence carries genetic information just in case (i) its individual positions make separable contributions to the product, (ii) the mapping from position to contribution is chemically unconstrained, and (iii) the mapping is stabilised by something external to the molecule — a history of selection, or a designer's intention — in virtue of which the molecule can be misread. Test this against the cases and repair it. In particular: is clause (iii) doing all the work; is heredity required in addition to, or instead of, (iii); and does the notion of error in (iii) really mark a distinction, given that any reading mechanism can fail?

What I want from you, concretely: a principle stated precisely enough to apply to new cases, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed, including positional information in a morphogen gradient, the somatically generated sequences of immune receptors, a stretch of DNA that is both translated and under selection for a structural or packaging role, and a minimal synthetic cell assembled from scratch; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to withdraw. If the right answer is that information talk in biology is irreducibly plural, say what that implies for the deflationary claim that such talk adds nothing to ordinary causal description.

Do not provide a literature review or references; I will supply those myself. Argue from the cases and from what the candidate principles entail.
