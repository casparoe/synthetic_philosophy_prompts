I am in the Lower Sixth and I chair my school's history and politics society, which records one interview episode a term; next term's guest is a historian of comparative school curricula — I shall call her Dr Imogen Sarkar, since I would rather not name her here — whose recent book argues that the familiar textbook controversies are not, at bottom, disputes about what happened. On her account, the two sides in each of her cases broadly share a factual record and are really arguing about what she calls the subject of the sentence: which collective is permitted to appear as the grammatical and moral agent of the narrative — the nation-as-founder, the nation-as-victim, the coloniser, the enslaved, the state. She applies this both to the American quarrel between the 1619 Project and the 1776 report and to the recurring disputes between Japanese and Korean accounts of the colonial period, and she concludes that the usual remedies — more archives, a balanced panel of historians, a footnote on every page, a bilateral commission — are aimed at the wrong target. What is needed instead, she argues, is double-subject narration: a single textbook passage written so that agency passes between two collectives without the text ever declaring which of them is the true subject of the history.

I would be grateful for help drafting eight to ten questions for her, ordered so that the first two or three would be intelligible to any sixth-former listening and the later ones press harder. I do not want questions that let her restate the thesis; I want questions that start from inside it and make it do work. In particular I should like: one on her empirical premise, whether the rival sides really do agree on the facts, or whether that claim is itself a way of taking the 1619 side's framing as settled; one on whether the grammatical vocabulary is carrying moral weight it cannot bear, i.e. whether saying that Korea or Japan is the subject of a sentence is anything more than a vivid way of repeating the original disagreement; one that makes her produce a specimen — what a double-subject paragraph for a GCSE-age reader would actually look like on, say, the arrival of the first enslaved Africans in Virginia in 1619, or on the annexation of Korea, and what a pupil should be able to do afterwards that they could not do before; one on symmetry, since her framework looks equally available to a ministry or screening authority that wants the state itself as the subject; one on whether teachers ought to tell pupils explicitly that the choice of subject is a political choice or let the narration do it silently; one on assessment, since I cannot see how one marks an answer when the subject of the sentence has been deliberately destabilised; and one on scope, whether this is a claim about school textbooks in particular or about historical writing generally, and what, if it is the latter, is left that is distinctive about the classroom. I should also like a closing question about what would count against her — what classroom outcome, or what finding about how pupils actually read such passages, would make her abandon the prescription.

For each question, please indicate briefly the answer she is most likely to give and then supply one follow-up that engages with that answer rather than circling back to my own question. Please phrase the questions as a polite but persistent seventeen-year-old would put them: I do not want to sound like a hostile panel chair, but I am conscious that she is practised at absorbing objections and returning to her preferred vocabulary, and I would like at least two places in the sequence where that manoeuvre becomes visibly costly.
