Two people in my reading group spent most of last night's session going round in circles about whether dreams are experiences — one of them was in a broadly Malcolm-ish mood, the other more Dennett-ish, though neither used the names. I said I'd referee and then realised I couldnt. Roughly how it went:

1. Sam: there's no criterion for saying I *had* an experience in the night. My report can't be checked against any sleep-time event, so 'I dreamt that p' doesn't entail that anything happened to me while I was out. Dreams are something we do on waking — a kind of report — not experiences we undergo.
2. Priya: that's backwards. Treat the report as a fallible report of something that went on, then ask what best explains the reports: their detail, the way they hang together in time, the fact that people wake from nightmares sweating with their heart going, and the way reports turn up when you're woken at some points in the night and not others.
3. Sam: the sweating proves nothing. Whatever disposes you to produce the report could also cause the sweating, so it's a common cause, not a window — arousal happens in reflexes and seizures without anybody wanting to call it experience. And every third-person thing you can point to is only ever correlated with reports, so you never get outside the circle.
4. Priya: then your standard is just scepticism about any experience you can't verify, and it would stop you saying anything about what coma or seizure patients go through. Better to recast the whole question predictively — what should we expect the sleeper to say, how should we treat them — and say there's no further fact after that.
5. Sam: fine, but on that recast 'experience' earns nothing. And you're running two questions together: is there something it's like to dream, and is dreaming a matter of seeming to perceive things? Reports can't settle the second, because supplying a tidy narrative is exactly what waking people do.
6. Priya: the split helps me. Reports are bizzare and gappy, and they never include the bit where you arrive at the scene. A memory invented whole at waking would be tidier than that, not messier — the mess looks like a fragmentary stream reconstructed under pressure to say something.
7. Sam: confabulation is gappy as well, and the pressure to produce a story explains those gaps just as well as your reconstruction does. You've made composition-at-waking uglier, not ruled it out.

A third person mumbled that maybe dreaming is just imagination happening during sleep, and both of them waved that away, and I honestly can't tell whether it dissolves the row or just moves the bump somewhere else.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall — and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
