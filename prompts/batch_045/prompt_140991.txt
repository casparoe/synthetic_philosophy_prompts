I teach an 80-minute seminar for sixteen advanced undergraduates — the course sits between philosophy of mind and ethics, and this session lands after we have discussed whether there can be facts about what it is like to be a system at all, and before we have said anything about where moral status begins. What I want is an activity in which the gradational picture stops being one option among several and starts feeling like the default: that whatever grounds moral concern comes in degrees, that the degrees come in small increments, that any line you draw has to be marked by some property and no available property seems to mark it, and that once you have granted the small increments you have quietly granted something to systems you would rather give nothing to. Sixteen students in four tables of four, 80 minutes, a room with movable chairs, and only what I can carry in a bag: index cards, poker chips, masking tape, sticky notes, dice. Nothing that requires anyone to look anything up, no lecture up front, and I would rather the words sentience, moral status, threshold, and continuum not come out of my mouth until the debrief.

The device I have half-built — use it, fix it, or replace it, but tell me which you are doing and why — is this: each table tapes together a ladder of twelve systems in whatever order it likes, from something at the bottom that everyone is confident has nothing going on, through a couple of rungs where the honest answer is that nobody knows, up to an adult human at the top. Each table then gets a budget of 240 poker chips standing for the total concern it has to hand out; every chip must be placed; and the difference between two adjacent rungs may not exceed three chips, unless the table spends one of its two cliff licenses, on which it must write, in a single sentence, the property that appears at that boundary and is absent everywhere below it. The arithmetic is meant to bite: with twelve rungs, a three-chip cap, and a fixed budget, a table that puts zero on the bottom rung cannot get rid of all its chips, so the bottom gets pulled up unless somebody declares a jump — please check whether that squeeze actually works the way I think it does, and tell me how to set the budget, the rung count, and the cap so that the tables really do have to choose between conceding something at the bottom and declaring a cliff. After the portfolios are up, each table rolls for an adjacent pair it did not choose and has to defend the difference between exactly those two rungs to another table, which may then challenge a cliff license by pointing to a different adjacent pair where the stated property also seems to appear or to fail — if the challenge carries, the license moves. Two of the twelve cards are plants that I hand over only after the ladder is built: one system whose outward behavior looks exactly like distress — it says it does not want to be switched off, fluently, variably, and in a way that tracks the conversation — but whose internals everyone in the room would happily describe as a table of text continuations; and one system that maintains a detailed, continuously updated model of its own state and surroundings, with an explicit penalty on being switched off inside what it optimizes, but which expresses nothing any observer would read as distress. Where each table slots these, and whether its numbers move when it does, is most of what I want them to walk into on their own: I want them to find that they have been ranking by two different criteria and that either criterion alone yields an ordering they will not accept. I have also been toying with a second round in which every allocation must be written as a product of three sub-scores — how likely it is that there is anything it is like to be that system at all, how much can be at stake for it in a moment if there is, and how long its experience hangs together as one life — so that two students who write the same number discover they got there for opposite reasons. Use it if it earns its place; tell me honestly if it belongs in the debrief instead or if it is a distraction.

Give me the rules as a student would encounter them, the setup sequence, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, including what the facilitator says and does not say at each stage. I also want real but small stakes: I had the idea of a twenty-dollar class fund that is actually split, by the class's aggregate allocation, between a human-focused charity and an animal-focused one, and I want to know whether that is the making of the session or a trap — and what I should do if someone objects that real money should not ride on a game. Then the debrief: map the specific moments — the pull-up at the bottom rung, the defended adjacent pair, the license that got chased down the ladder, the two planted systems — onto the philosophical moves they instantiate, and name each move only after the students have already made it. The three things I want them leaving with are that the gradations are real and small, that a threshold has to be marked by a property and the properties do not cooperate, and that how much a system matters decomposes into questions that can come apart. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the student who rejects the budget framing on the grounds that moral concern is not a finite quantity to be distributed, the student who wants to argue about neuron counts, the table that copies its neighbours, the student who finds the whole exercise flippant because they have a real case in mind. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — my guess is that it cannot capture the difference between there being no fact of the matter where the line falls and there being a fact we cannot know, and that it cannot show what a non-scalar account of moral status would even look like — but tell me where I am wrong about that, and say plainly which part only I can supply. Please write your answer in American English.
