I'm a PhD student in political theory; my dissertation is on moralized political conflict, and specifically on what happens to the practice of giving reasons when the reasons people give and the reactions they actually have come apart. I've spent the past year coding open-ended responses from a mixed sample on three contested issues, and I keep running into a configuration I have no name for. I'd be grateful for help identifying it — or, if nothing established fits, for a coined term and a definition.

The configuration has two halves. First: in my transcripts, the reasons people offer have become strikingly homogeneous. Almost everyone now justifies their position in the currency of harm, welfare, and equal treatment — including respondents whose verdicts are best predicted by reactions of a quite different kind, to violations of order, of loyalty, or of what they themselves call the integrity of a shared form of life. These respondents don't say that something offends or degrades them; they say that it damages the community, or that it injures the people involved, and they seem to register no slide in doing so. Their verdicts, meanwhile, have not moved toward each other. If anything, the positions have spread further apart over the same period in which the justifications converged.

Second: shared reasons are supposed to be the engine of convergence. If you and I appeal to the same consideration and still disagree, we ought to conclude that we disagree about the facts, or about how much weight that consideration carries, and go looking for the source. That isn't what happens. What happens is that each side concludes the other does not really believe the reason it gave. Because the other side now argues in your terms and still refuses your conclusion, the economical reading is that the stated reason is a costume: they don't believe it, or they believe it less than they claim, or they would drop it the moment it told against them. So the spread of a common moral vocabulary is accompanied by a rising and roughly symmetrical conviction that the other side is arguing in bad faith — and each side's description of the other has converged on the same shape.

I don't think this is just the familiar business of repackaging one's arguments in the other side's terms in order to persuade them, though that plainly occurs and I can see it happening in my data. Three features don't come out of that description: the repackaging often happens where it is dialectically costly, when the speaker would do better with their own audience by speaking plainly; it is very often not deliberate, in that respondents report the translated reason as the reason and are surprised when shown their own earlier wording; and its aggregate effect is not convergence but mutual attribution of insincerity.

I also don't think it is just post-hoc reason-giving, or the point that people confabulate explanations of their own reactions. That would explain why a stated reason fails to match its psychological cause. It doesn't explain why the drift in vocabulary goes in one direction, why it goes toward the considerations that dominate among the speaker's opponents, why it travels with polarization rather than agreement, or why the bystander's conclusion is bad faith rather than mere confusion.

So: does this configuration have an established name? If several nearby concepts are candidates, which fits best and where exactly does each fall short? If there is nothing established, I'd like a term with a definition that fixes the boundaries — what must be present for the label to apply, what the nearest neighbours are (deliberate reframing, confabulation, expressive or cheap-talk responding, status-seeking moral speech, plain disagreement about the weight of reasons, genuine conceptual change in what a community will accept as a reason), and what observable difference in data like mine would discriminate this from each of them. I won't be satisfied with a bare label, and I can't chase references from where I'm working at the moment; what I need is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

One further thing, because it's where the project is headed. Suppose the pattern is real and widespread. My worry is that it quietly destroys a certain kind of evidence about who is right on these questions. The natural move is to look at which side's case survives once both are stated in neutral, shareable terms: to treat convergence in the currency of reasons as a sign that the dispute is being conducted on common ground, and then to ask who wins there. But if the shared currency is precisely what gets adopted without any change in verdict, then convergence in reasons may be evidence of nothing about the merits, and may even be a symptom of a deeper divergence in the psychologies of left and right. I'd like the answer to say what the phenomenon, properly described, does and does not license us to conclude about the dispute underneath — in particular whether it undercuts the appeal to shared reasons as a neutral court of appeal, or whether the mutual attributions of bad faith are themselves one more symptom rather than a diagnosis.
