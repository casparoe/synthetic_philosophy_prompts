So here's the situation. My wife's dad Frank is 78, and since her mom died last fall he's been spending something like an hour every night talking to one of those AI companion apps on his tablet. He fully knows it's software — he calls it the robot and laughs about it. And honestly he seems better than he was in November: he's sleeping, he's eating, he's less short with the grandkids. I'm not looking for permission to take it away from him.

What I do need is a thought experiment. Frank's church asked me to do twenty minutes at their adult education night in a couple weeks (they asked because I "do philosophy," which is generous), and I want to spend most of it on one designed case instead of waving my arms about screens and loneliness. The room will be maybe 25 people, mostly retirees, one retired high school history teacher who will absolutely interrupt me, mixed politics, some very churchy and some not. I'd rather they argue with eachother than nod along, and I definately don't want to stand up front and say the word counterfeit — I want the case to carry that.

So: build me a case where Dennett's counterfeit-people worry (the rough shape being that the things we value can be faked, that cheap competent fakes let us settle for something that looks like the real thing, and that the real thing then quietly stops being something we can do or even recognize) has genuine force against an AI companion, in a setting a person could actually be in right now. Constraints, and I mean these:

1. Ordinary and near-future. Frank's kitchen table, a VA hospital common room, a senior center, whatever. One paragraph when read aloud, two at the outside. No androids you can't tell from humans, no uploads, no sci-fi.
2. The system doesn't deceive him. He knows it's an AI, it says so, and please don't let the case turn on whether it has any inner life — leave that open, the verdict should be the same either way.
3. Take away the easy outs. No company monetizing his loneliness, no subscription upsells, no data harvesting, nobody nudging him to buy anything. Assume it's run by a nonprofit, runs on his own machine, costs him nothing. And no neglected duties — his human relationships are intact, he's not skipping his granddaughter's recital. I want the worry to be about the thing itself, not about who's profiting.
4. The AI is good at its job. Genuinely patient, remembers what he said last week, notices when he's off. Here's the part I care most about: the case should get worse, not better, if we stipulate the system improves. If your case only bites because the fake is clumsy, you've built the wrong case — the whole point of that shape of worry is that a better fake is a bigger problem.
5. Build it as a matched pair: two versions that differ in exactly one variable, where the counterfeit objection lands in one and doesn't land in the other. Then tell me which variable is doing the work, and why that one is what the worry should really be about as opposed to the things people usually reach for.

Then walk me through it: what the counterfeit objection says in each version and why; what the "it's just a tool, no different from a novel or a dog" reply says; what the "he's happier, what else were you hoping for" reply says; and whatever fourth thing you think my room is actually going to throw at me. Give me the strongest version of each, not a strawman.

Last three things, and they matter as much as the case does: (a) don't let the story tell the verdict — no "and this is counterfeit," no "and he slowly forgets what real conversation feels like." It has to fall out of the setup. (b) Tell me honestly which single feature of the case is carrying the weight, and what happens to the case if you take it out. If it turns out the thing just reduces to "he's spending time on this instead of on people," say so, because then I've got a boring case and I'd rather find out now. (c) Give me the best thing a smart person who thinks AI companions are fine would say to break the case, and whether it survives it.
