So here's where I'm at. Over the last year I've drifted into the paracomplete / groundedness picture of the Liar: the Liar sentence is neither true nor false; a sentence gets a truth value only if there's a non-circular, grounded derivation that confers one (think Kripke-style fixed points with the strong Kleene tables, and the minimal fixed point as the favoured one); classical logic has to give somewhere, so we lose excluded middle for truth ascriptions and the transparency of the truth predicate gets restricted; and the revenge phenomena are handled by refusing to let the object language express the strong "not true" that revenge arguments need, with the cost admitted rather than hidden. A friend of mine (she's a dialetheist, LP-with-gluts) says this whole package has been qualified into unfalsifiability and that I should be embarrassed. I don't yet know whether she's right.

What I want is: what would count against this view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed paracomplete theorist would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as resting on a rival's assumptions. Include things that have arguably already happened. And please be honest at the end about whether the view is in fact insulated — and whether insulation is even a damning property for a theory of this kind, since it isn't an empirical hypothesis about, say, reaction times. Three pressures I specifically want dealt with rather than gestured at. (1) Take this sentence: 'This sentence is not true, and the Q3 audit was signed off before the deadline' — and suppose the audit was in fact signed off three weeks late. My gut says the sentence is straightforwardly false, and I can know it's false just by looking at the second conjunct; but as far as I can tell the fixed-point machinery gaps it regardless, because a conjunction with an undefined conjunct is undefined. Is that a defeater or a feature, and what would settle which? (2) I ran a very informal show-of-hands in a grad seminar last month: for plain old 'this sentence is not true', out of about thirty people, nineteen said false, six said neither, five said the question was broken. If that pattern actually replicated across populations and cultures, would it count? Or is it defusable by saying that ordinary speakers use 'false' as a blanket rejection meaning roughly 'not true', in which case the data never touched the view? (3) Imagine a linguistic community whose language cannot diagonalize — say every sentence has to be shorter than the name that refers to it, so no self-referential sentence is statable and no Liar can even be formulated. What should the paracomplete theorist say about their truth predicate? Is it grounded? Does the theory predict that it must behave classically there, and if the theory has nothing to say about the case, is that an exposure or just a scope limit?
