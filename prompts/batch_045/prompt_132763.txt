so my daughter is 7, nonspeaking, and about three months ago she started spelling on a laminated letter board — one letter at a time, someone holding the board up for her. It works, sort of, or at least it produces sentences that sound like her, and ever since I fell into a rabbit whole reading about the symbol grounding problem because I genuinely can't tell what I'm supposed to conclude from it. Like: are the words she's spelling hooked up to anything in her, or are they hooked up to the person holding the board? Those feel like different questions to me and I can't get anyone to say so.

Here's what I actually want. Read The Miracle Worker through this — Gibson's play, or the 1962 film with Anne Bancroft and Patty Duke, whichever you know better — specifically the pump scene, where Annie has been spelling into Helen's hand for days and Helen treats the hand-shapes as a kind of meaningless finger game, and then water runs over one hand while Annie spells w-a-t-e-r into the other and something clicks. I want the reading to go both ways honestly: what the film gets right about grounding, and where it's just doing something else (sentimental biopic, teacher-as-savior, breakthrough-as-love) and the reading doesn't fit.

A few things I keep going back and forth on and want you to take a position on:

Before the pump, Helen is manipulating signs correctly — she can produce the hand shapes, she can copy, she maybe even uses them to get things — but nothing is attached. Is the film right that the attachment event is a single moment, or is grounding the kind of thing that has to be gradual and distributed, so that the pump is just where it becomes visible? Does the film's need for one dramatic scene actually mislead people like me about what learning a first symbol is like?

And then the thing nobody addresses: the symbols are already grounded in Annie. Helen gets them out of Annie's hands, literally. So is what happens at the pump grounding at all, or is it transfer — the kind of parasitic, borrowed meaning that Searle's Chinese Room person is supposed to have? If it's transfer, is there a principled stopping point, or does everyone's vocabulary end up borrowed at some degree of remove? My daughter's board is held by a person. Helen's hand was held by a person. I don't think that makes them equivalent but I want the difference stated properly.

I've been using three rough labels to keep myself straight and I'd rather you use mine than the literature's: contact grounding (the symbol gets attached because you are yourself in the sensorimotor state it names — water on your own skin), practice grounding (the symbol gets its grip from what you do with it in a shared activity, rules, correction, being wrong and being corrected — think of the long breakfast-table fight, which is about conduct and not about reference at all), and handoff grounding (it's grounded in you because it was grounded in someone who gave it to you). A couple of questions: does anything in the film show contact grounding without already assuming practice grounding, i.e. is the pump scene even intelligible without the week of spelling that came before it? Does handoff grounding deserve to be called grounding, or is it a category error? And where do abstract words land — the later bits where the words stop being objects you can put in her hand? Those can't be contact-grounded in any obvious sense, and the film seems to find them easy, which makes me suspicious.

Please end with an actual ranking: put contact, practice, and handoff in order by how much of Helen's achievement each one explains, one line of justification each, and give each a score out of 10 for how much the pump scene alone gives you. Then — this is the part I care about — tell me which of the three my daughter's letter board is a case of, or say if asking it that way is a mistake. Where the film can't help me, say so plainly instead of forcing it.
