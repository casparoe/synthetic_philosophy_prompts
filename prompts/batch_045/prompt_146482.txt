I've been sitting on an argument for about a month now, and it has grown a conclusion I don't want, so I'd like it diagnosed rather than agreed with.

The setup is a betting mechanism people in my circle have been using at meetups, which I'll call the iterated opt-in. Two traders, A and B, bet on an event E that resolves publicly on a known date. Both are risk-neutral over money at the stakes involved; neither has any prior exposure to the outcome, or any use for the contract beyond its payoff; neither has a hedging, liquidity, or consumption motive; and both know all of this about the other. Both have public long-run forecasting track records and are known to each other to be calibrated. The venue runs like this: at each of a fixed, publicly known number of rounds t = 1…T, each side privately submits a bit — 1 means "I'm still willing to take my side at price p" — and after each round the venue announces only whether both bits were 1. The trade executes if and only if both bits were 1 in every round. Nothing else comes out: not the posterior, not the reason, not who moved first. Each side also files a sealed one-line statement of their reasoning, which is never opened. In practice people set T high — 30, 40; one group ran 200 — on the theory that after that many rounds of publicly reaffirmed willingness, whatever private information sits on the other side, it can no longer be adverse in a way that matters.

(1) Common prior. A's and B's credences are both obtained from a single probability function by conditioning on their respective private information. There is no irreducible "my prior versus your prior"; there is only "my evidence versus your evidence."

(2) Interim rationality. A takes the buy side at p only if, conditional on everything A then knows, the contract is worth more than p; B takes the sell side only if, conditional on everything B then knows, it is worth less. Both are expected-value maximizers, both know this of the other, and the mechanism screens out every other reason to trade.

(3) Adverse selection. If A is willing to buy at p, that is a fact about A's private information, and likewise for B's willingness to sell. So each side's willingness is a signal the other should update on: learning that A was willing to buy pushes B's estimate of E up; learning that B was willing to sell pushes A's estimate down.

(4) The mechanism delivers that signal in full. In the iterated version it isn't merely true that each knows the other was willing; each knows that the other knows that they know, and so on for T rounds, with T large. For the purposes of the inference in (3) that is as good as common knowledge: by the end, nothing about the other side's reasons is left unaccounted for.

(5) No trade. If A and B have a common prior, if neither has any non-speculative reason to trade, and if it is common knowledge that both are willing to trade at p, then the trade cannot be strictly beneficial to both. Conditional on the event that both are willing — which, if it is common knowledge, is already part of what each of them knows — each side's estimate has been pulled back across the price, and at least one of them is taking a position they would decline on their own information.

(6) But the trades happen. People who know each other's track records, know the mechanism, and know that the other person is in the same epistemic position take the other side of each other's bets constantly, at every value of T anyone has bothered to run. Volume doesn't visibly thin out as T goes up or as the participants get better informed, and nobody walks away feeling exploited.

(7) Which proves too much. On these premises, essentially all the volume in such a venue is error: for every contract that changes hands, at least one side is taking an expected loss given everything they know at the moment of the trade, and so either isn't rational or is playing against someone who isn't. And it generalizes past betting. Any institution whose job is to aggregate dispersed information by letting people take positions — prediction markets, forecasting tournaments with traded contracts, a team that pools estimates before making a call — becomes, on these premises, a machine for relieving at least one rational participant of money. That can't be what such institutions are; the point of them is that dispersed information gets aggregated, not that somebody gets fleeced.

(8) So the common prior assumption is false. Two people can share all their evidence and still rationally disagree, because there is such a thing as your prior and my prior, and it is not the kind of thing evidence settles.

I'd most like to keep (1): it does a lot of work in how I think about disagreement generally, and I don't want to be stuck saying that persistent, informed, good-faith disagreement needs no explanation at all. I'd also like to keep (6). But (5) has the clean look of something that follows from the definitions, and I can't locate the break. (4) feels like the weak joint to me, and yet I can't say what goes wrong there, since forty rounds of public reaffirmation really does feel like common knowledge.

Tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it commits me to. If the chain is valid and a premise simply has to go, tell me which is cheapest to give up and what I lose with it — in particular whether giving up (1) costs me the thought that disagreement is always traceable either to somebody's evidence or to somebody's error.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step or premise you reject — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my argument back to me.
