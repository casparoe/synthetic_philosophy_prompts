A well-reviewed popular-science book on animal minds summarises the case for taking brain size to be morally relevant roughly as follows:

"How much a creature matters is settled by how much there is, for it, to be like at a given moment. Call this its phenomenal bandwidth: the number of distinguishable experiences it can bind into a single conscious scene at one time. Brains are the only machinery we know of that delivers it, and because the binding has to happen inside one integrated physical system, bandwidth grows faster than the tissue does. This is what makes the argument from marginal cases so strong: a chimpanzee and a severely cognitively impaired human infant differ in species, and species is a merely biological category, like race. The only difference that could do moral work is bandwidth, and bandwidth is a matter of brain. So eating a pig while objecting to experiments on the impaired infant is exactly parallel to refusing to employ someone because of their race, and the consistent vegetarian has the whole of the argument. It follows directly that moral status is a sliding scale rather than a threshold: a whale, with a brain several times the mass of ours, sits above us, a chicken far below, and — uncomfortably but unavoidably — a person with a smaller brain sits below a person with a larger one."
