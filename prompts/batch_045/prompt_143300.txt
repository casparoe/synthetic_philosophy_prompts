I'm on the executive committee of a scientific society — about 3,000 members — and every couple of years we put out an official position statement on some contested question in the field (does effect X replicate, is technique Y safe at scale, should regulators treat Z as settled). Right now a 12-person elected council ratifies it's wording by simple majority, and the chair breaks ties. Two motions are on the floor for the AGM, which is in three weeks, and I have to circulate the committee's one-page recommendation by Friday.

Motion A, from a group of members: put every statement to an online vote of the whole membership — a two-week comment thread first so people can see each other's arguments, then a simple majority of whoever turns up. Their argument is basically the Condorcet jury theorem: lots of roughly independent voters, each a bit better than chance, and the majority ends up very likely to be right.

Motion B, from our treasurer, who's worried about reputational damage: keep it with the council but require two-thirds.

Some things I've noticed that I want the memo to actually deal with.

First, we're 12 — an even number. If ties are broken by a coin flip, I think a 12-person council is exactly as reliable as an 11-person one; I checked it on toy cases (1 vs 2, and 3 vs 4, both came out identical when everyone's reliability is 0.6) but I can't prove it in general. If that's right, the 12th vote is worth nothing and we should either go to 11 or change the tie rule. Is defaulting to "we issue no statement" better than the chair's casting vote, and does that depend on what fraction of proposed statements are true in the first place?

Second, the comment thread. If everyone reads the same three loud people before voting, we don't have 3,000 independent votes, we have fewer — and I've been told that once votes are correlated the majority stops converging on the truth at all and plateaus somewhere below 1, which would take the legs out of motion A entirely. But the obvious reply is that our 12-person council is far more correlated than the membership: they've been in the same room for three years.

Third, and this is the bit that actually worries me: on a specialist question most of our members are in subfields that have nothing to do with it, so the average member's reliability might be near chance or below it — and if it's below, the same theorem says a bigger electorate makes us worse, not better. I have no idea which side of 0.5 we're on, and we have no track record of scoring our own statements against what later happened, so no one can honestly say. What do you do with a decision rule when you can't tell whether it's helping or hurting?

Fourth, one of our committee members — she's an econometrician — says the simple-majority framing is wrong anyway: if votes are independent, the right rule weights each person by the log-odds of their being right, and a few hundred members who are each only marginally better than a coin flip will beat twelve experts under it. She wants self-reported expertise added as a weighting to the online vote. I don't know if the weighting claim is right, and I definately don't know how anyone would report their own expertise honestly.

Fifth, our statements come in two parts: a factual bit (does the effect replicate) and a recommendation bit (so here's what practitioners and regulators should do). The jury-theorem reasoning seems to touch only the first part, and I suspect that's where the two-thirds rule really belongs, if it belongs anywhere — but I also suspect the threshold ought to fall out of the base rate of statements being right and how much worse a false statement is than silence, rather than from a round number somebody liked.

What I need is the memo: a position on each motion (adopt, reject, adopt with conditions), what we do at the AGM in three weeks, what we do over the next year instead — pilot something? record votes before the comment thread opens and compare them to the post-deliberation ones? split each statement into two separate votes? drop to 11 members and fix the tie rule? — and reasons our members could actually argue with rather than just nod at. Please be straight about the strongest argument for whichever motion you're telling us to reject; the referendum people will be in the room and they're not stupid. If it helps you put numbers on any of this, assume a decent specialist is right about 60% of the time on these questions.
