I had a long and unsatisfying argument with a friend last night about refugees, and I think I now see why it went nowhere: we were each answering a different question and calling it by the same name. I want to try it the other way round. Below are my case-by-case verdicts, given as honestly as I can. What I want from you is the principle, if there is one, that they add up to.

The first thing I noticed is that "refugee" seems to be doing two jobs at once: it marks a fact about a person (something has gone badly wrong for them) and it triggers a duty on someone else's part (you must let them in and not send them back). My verdicts below suggest those two come apart, and I don't know whether to conclude that there are two principles or that I am confused.

On who counts:

1. A shopkeeper who refuses to pay the local gang's extortion, is shot at, and finds the police uninterested because the gang has bought them. Counts. My friend says no, because there is no state persecutor; I say the state's refusal to protect is the whole problem.

2. A family leaving a delta region where the salt water has reached their fields and their village will be gone within a decade. No one is targeting anyone. Doesn't count — but I resist the inference from "not a refugee" to "we owe them nothing," and I can't say why that inference fails here while it seems fine in the next case.

3. A woman who would be confined, beaten and socially destroyed by her own family and community for leaving a marriage. The law is on her side; the police are not. Counts. My friend says this is private violence and therefore not the state's business; that strikes me as drawing the line in exactly the wrong place.

4. A man who fled a city under indiscriminate bombardment. Nobody targeted him; he just lived there. Counts — and, curiously, I feel this more strongly than case 1, even though there is less in the way of anyone *doing* anything to him.

5. An eighteen-year-old from a poor, peaceful, badly governed country who would simply do much better here. Doesn't count. And unlike in case 2, I do think this answer settles the practical question.

6. A mixed case: a family that left after the drought killed their herd *and* after being pushed out of local office because they belong to a disfavoured minority. I think each factor alone would have been enough. Counts — but my confidence drains away as I imagine the persecution getting pettier and the drought getting worse, which suggests I am doing something like adding up harms, and I don't know whether that is legitimate.

7. A former party loyalist who arrives here and, over two years, genuinely converts to a faith his home regime persecutes. Counts. A second man who, before leaving, posted videos denouncing that regime precisely in order to build a claim: doesn't count. A third who converted on the cynical advice of an agent, but who now would genuinely be in danger if returned: I find myself saying no, and then immediately noticing that I have no consistent story about *what* has to be genuine — the belief or the risk.

8. A woman harassed and dispossessed in her home province who could live safely in the capital, nine hundred kilometres away, at the cost of her land, her language community and her livelihood. Counts, in my judgment — but this is the verdict I am least sure of, and it is the one my friend pressed hardest on.

9. A former intelligence officer of a fallen brutal regime, now wanted by its successor; the new government would like to try him, and would torture him. Doesn't count. My reason is that he forfeited the claim — but a principle that is otherwise entirely about what will happen to the person has no obvious slot for forfeiture, and I want to know whether I have imported a second idea.

10. A sixteen-year-old born here to parents whose claim was never resolved; speaks the homeland language poorly, has never lived there. Sending the family back feels wrong, but not, I think, because the child has a claim of the kind in cases 1 to 9. It feels like a different kind of reason altogether.

On who must take them in:

11. A neighbouring country hosting two million people from the same war, with schools on double shifts and a collapsed labour market; a rich country four thousand kilometres away hosting forty thousand. I think the rich country is doing far less than it owes — but I don't think the individual has a claim to be admitted *there*, which is apparently consistent with my thinking that they have a claim to be admitted somewhere.

12. A person who transited four safe countries and claimed in the one with the most generous support and a large community from home. Much weaker claim, perhaps none. What troubles me is that this is case 11 with the choice made explicit, and I dislike the verdict less when the choice is hidden.

13. A country that armed one of the factions in the war. I want to say its duty is greater, but not that it owes more *to this claimant* in particular — the wrong seems to be a fact about which duty-bearer should act, not about the content of anyone's claim.

14. Forty million people denied citizenship and barred from most work across a whole region. I cannot accept that admission is the only way to discharge anything here; I equally cannot accept that therefore none of them count. My friend says this shows any individual-level principle fails to scale, and I suspect he is right.

15. A group of citizens here who offer to fund and host two hundred families. I don't know whether that discharges a duty, transfers one, or merely changes who bears a cost, and my principle has no view.

The best I have managed on my own is a kind of protection-deficit view: what matters is the size of the gap between the rights a person can actually exercise where they are and the rights they could exercise here, with duties of admission falling on whoever can close that gap at least cost. But I can't make it produce my verdict in case 8, it says nothing about case 10, and it silently assumes the answer in case 14. My friend's candidate is different: you count if your own state will not secure your basic rights, and the duty falls on whichever state is best placed to secure them, weighted by capacity and by causal contribution to the failure.

So: give me a principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself. Tell me which clause each of my cases is doing the work for, and which verdicts you would overturn. Then tell me what it implies for cases I haven't listed, including at least two you generate yourself and think I would find uncomfortable, and at least one where you expect the principle to give a verdict I would reject. I don't want a survey of the legal categories or of what the literature says — I want the principle underneath, and the price of having one.
