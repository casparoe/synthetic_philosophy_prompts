I host a scripted podcast series in which each episode interviews a historical figure, performed by an actor who may answer only from that figure's own published arguments. The next episode is John von Neumann on brains and computers, and the source text is the short, unfinished book of Yale Silliman lectures published posthumously in 1958 — the 2012 reissue of which carries a foreword by Ray Kurzweil, who reads the book as an early argument for mind uploading.

I need eight interview questions, each with a one-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. They should press on the machinery of his comparison rather than on biography or on computers in general. The ground I want covered:

1. He holds that the nervous system is neither cleanly digital nor cleanly analog but mixed — nerve pulses are all-or-none, yet magnitudes seem to be carried by their frequency or density. Ask what is actually being claimed by "mixed": an empirical finding about the neural code, or a label for a gap he cannot fill.
2. He accounts for the brain's performance despite its slow components by massive parallelism bought at the cost of low arithmetic precision. Ask whether that trade-off makes the brain a different kind of computer or merely a differently engineered one — and follow up on what we would learn by building a machine with the brain's parallelism and precision profile.
3. He argues that the brain's language is not the language of mathematics or formal logic, and that its codes are unknown and probably fixed by heredity. Ask what work the word "computer" is still doing in the comparison if the code is unknown.
4. In the machines he knew, instructions are stored and separable from the machinery; in the brain, the wiring is the program. Ask what that implies for uploading: whether it makes it a copying problem rather than a porting problem.
5. The question I most want asked. Today's neural networks are massively parallel, run at deliberately low numerical precision, and have no program distinct from their learned connectivity — they resemble the brain in just the respects he singles out as the brain's differences from the machines of 1958. Ask him straight out whether his disanalogies have quietly become similarities, and whether that strengthens his comparison or hollows it out — and follow up on the reply I expect, that architectural resemblance does not settle whether the same function is being computed.
6. He wrote on synthesizing reliable systems out of unreliable components, and treats the brain as reliable despite unreliable neurons. Ask whether that relocates the comparison from the neuron to the network, and what then counts as a component.
7. He gestures at limits on a system's capacity to describe itself. Ask whether that limit is one of complexity in general or something specific to the nervous system.
8. Finally, put Kurzweil's reading to him: does the lecture series support the idea of uploading a mind, or is it the strongest available argument against it?

Phrase each question so that it could be answered from the book's own argument, and so that no listener who had not grappled with that argument would have thought to ask it. Give me the questions only — no specimen answers, no stage directions. No question longer than forty words, and the whole set under 650 words.
