And here's one of the answers (I've stripped the candidate number):

--

The question is whether genetic talk of codes and information is literal or merely metaphorical. I will argue that it is literal at the level of codon-to-amino-acid translation, but metaphorical once it is stretched to cover phenotypes.

What would it take for something to be a code in the strict sense? Three things, I think: a mapping between two systems of elements that are independent of each other; that mapping being arbitrary rather than forced by the intrinsic chemistry of the elements; and a physically realised decoder that actually implements the mapping. The genetic code satisfies all three. There is a mapping from sixty-four codons to the twenty amino acids plus stop. It is degenerate, and degeneracy is really just another word for ambiguity: which amino acid a codon yields depends on which tRNAs the cell happens to contain and on context-dependent recoding, so even at the level of individual codons there is no single determinate mapping. The mapping is also contingent rather than chemically necessary. Crick's frozen accident hypothesis holds that the assignments were fixed early and then could not be altered, and the near-universality of the code across the tree of life (with a scattering of mitochondrial and ciliate variants) is exactly what one would expect if any later reassignment would corrupt every protein in the cell at once. And the decoder is physically realised, in tRNAs, their synthetases and the ribosome.

The most interesting objection is the who reads the reader worry. The decoder is itself made of molecules that are produced by translation, so the code seems to presuppose the very machinery it is supposed to direct, and this looks like a vicious circle. But the objection does not get off the ground. The translation machinery is itself specified by DNA: tRNAs and ribosomal RNAs are transcribed from genes, and the synthetases are translated from genes. So the decoder is not supplied from outside the system at all; the system encodes its own reading mechanism and thereby bootstraps itself. There is no regress to worry about.

A second objection concerns information. Shannon information is cheap: any reliable correlation carries it, and a rain gauge carries Shannon information about the rainfall. So it cannot be Shannon information that makes DNA special; what is at issue is semantic or instructional content, and whether anything in biology has that in the full sense is a real question, since the content would have to be fixed by selection history on teleosemantic accounts, which is a substantive commitment. I will not settle that here.

What does not survive scrutiny is the claim that genes code for phenotypes. The same stretch of DNA yields different products in different cellular contexts, depending on which transcription factors are present and how transcripts are spliced, so the same sequence specifies different things depending on what else is in the cell. DNA codes, literally, for the sequence of a polypeptide. It does not code for the organism.

--
