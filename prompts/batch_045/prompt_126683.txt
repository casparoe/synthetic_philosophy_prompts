I've just come out of a long argument with a friend about the socialist calculation debate — three hours, no movement, the kind where you both end up restating your opening position more slowly — and I've decided that what I want to send her is a poem rather than another email. Would you write it?

Her side, stated as fairly as I can. Mises's challenge was that without market prices for the factors of production, the planners have no common unit in which to weigh one production plan against another; they cannot compare, and so they cannot calculate — not badly, not slowly, but not at all. Her answer is Lange's, and it is that this conflates the existence of a solution with the process that finds one. The Walrasian system of simultaneous equations has a formal solution. Whether the numbers are reached by actual trading or by a central board posting a price vector, watching what is demanded and supplied, raising the price of whatever is in excess demand and lowering the price of whatever is in excess supply, and iterating, is a question about how the computation is carried out, not about whether it can be. Whatever the market computes, the board can simulate. If that is right, the anti-socialist argument shrinks to a contingent claim about administrative clumsiness, and clumsiness is what computation is for.

I conceded a good deal of that, and I still think it fails, for three reasons I want the poem to carry rather than assert. First, the naming problem: before anyone can solve the system, someone must write it down, which means a row for every commodity and a column for every technique. The system can therefore allocate superbly among goods that have already been named and uses that have already been imagined; what it cannot do is what markets partly do, which is discover that there is a demand for something nobody had on the list. Second, the board's prices are quotes for a production period that has not happened yet, and HERE and TODAY keep moving under the quote; the iteration chases a target that does not stand still, and the tidy convergence stories depend on assumptions — goods being substitutes rather than complements, production sets being convex — that are exactly what is in question. Third, where average cost falls across the whole relevant range, setting price equal to marginal cost will not cover cost, so the board must hand out a subsidy or let the manager mark up, and either way somebody is exercising a judgment the rule does not determine.

The form I want is a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, loose iambic pentameter, then a three-line envoi. The six end-words are fixed and must appear exactly as given, no synonyms or near-misses: PRICE, LIST, COST, NEED, HERE, TODAY. Let at least two of them shift part of speech across the poem — PRICE and COST as verbs, NEED as a modal — and let that shift matter.

The reason for a sestina is that its rotation is a rule: it takes six tokens through a fixed permutation and brings them home in the envoi without ever consulting what any of them means. That is Lange's board in miniature, a syntax that arrives at the right arrangement while remaining indifferent to semantics, and I want the poem to let it work — genuinely work — through the first four stanzas, which should state Mises's challenge and then run the simulation, with the iteration visible in the verse: the same six words doing new work in new positions, prices adjusting, quantities responding, the rule converging. Then let the poem turn. Stanza five should be where LIST becomes a problem: the words the rule is permuting are the only words there are. Stanza six should be where HERE and TODAY slip out from under the posted vector, so that the rule is visibly tracking a world that has moved. And the envoi must complete the rotation — the words back where they started, the solution found — and make that homecoming the bad news. The rule can guarantee the return of the words. It cannot tell you that two of them now name things that no longer exist.

Two final constraints. It has to be a poem first: it should read aloud well to someone who has never heard of Mises or Lange, with no endnotes, no prose postscript, no italicised stage directions, and no resolution — my friend is not a fool, and I don't want a poem that wins the argument, only one that locates precisely where the argument is. But it should be exact enough that someone who knows the debate can point to the line where the simulation claim is granted and the line where it stops buying anything, and say why that is the right line to draw.
