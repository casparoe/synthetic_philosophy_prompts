I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on something that's partly craft and partly (I think) an actual philosophical mess, and my writing group has been useless about it in a fun way. I'm writing a book of three linked novellas, each set in one disaster year in the same fictional valley on the slope of a volcano: 1347, when a ship puts in at the port with something in the cargo; 1816, the year without a summer after that huge eruption in the East Indies, when nobody in the valley knows why the sky is wrong; and a near-future one where the power grid has been down for a month and the water isn't safe. Same families, same fields, three hundred years apart. What keeps tripping me up is that in each book a non-human thing is doing most of the plot work — a bacterium, a volcano, a blight, a flood — and I can't settle on how much of a character it's allowed to be. Sometimes I catch myself writing sentences where the frost decides and the microbe waits and the river remembers, and I honestly can't tell whether that's the good kind of animism or just laziness with verbs.

So here's what I want: a sorting instrument I can photocopy for my group, a decision tree or taxonomy that starts from something we all have an opinion about — say, "these three novellas would be different books if the volcano hadn't erupted" — and ends at a named position about non-human causes and agency, eight or nine branch nodes, each phrased as a question about a concrete case so that nobody can tell which leaf it's leading to. Please build it from the logical space first: what are the questions, what combinations of answers do they permit, then which combinations are actually occupied and which are empty (and for the empty ones, say whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unoccupied because nobody's bothered). Every leaf needs a name — including, at minimum, strong environmental determinism, the conjunctural/vulnerability view where the germ or the drought only bites because of how the people were already arranged, the flat symmetrical actor-network view where the bacillus and the bishop are the same kind of thing for purposes of the story, and the humanist view that non-humans are conditions and occasions but not agents — plus, for each, one sentence on the commitment that would embarrass a novelist who held it.

A few things I want in it specifically, because I made them up in a notebook and can't tell if they're real distinctions or just me being tired: what I call the swap test (if you replace this plague with a different plague, or this volcano with a different volcano, and the story ends the same way, was it ever an actor or just a stage?); the answerability condition (does a thing only count as acting if there was somebody who could in principle have answered it — you can negotiate with a drought by planting differently, you can't negotiate with a virus); ventriloquy, meaning the practice of lending the non-human a verb and then quietly forgetting you did; the lag problem, where the cause operates on a timescale none of the characters can perceive, so it can't be a character; and the reader's counterfactual, i.e. whether the book invites you to imagine the valley without the microbe at all. Put those in as branch questions where they belong, drop any that turn out to be the same question wearing a hat, and use at least these cases: the arriving ship, the failed harvest after the eruption, the blight in a potato field where a landlord is still exporting grain, a cholera outbreak where one well is clean and one isn't, and the valley's flood plain. Also please flag any two leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate rather than splitting them with a fake distinction, and add a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto leaves so we can each find out what we've been defending for the past four months. Keep it usable in a two-hour workshop — no reading list, nothing I'd need a library for, and please don't make any question a matter of knowing whether Braudel or Latour or Diamond said what; sort us by what we think happens in the valley, not by who we've read.
