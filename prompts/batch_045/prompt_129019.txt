I host a small independent philosophy podcast — my listeners, like me, have no academic philosophy background — and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a version of Rorty's antirepresentationalism that she calls "vocabulary pluralism." As I understand her from her published work: there is no way the world is, independent of some vocabulary or other, for our sentences to answer to; truth is not correspondence with mind-independent reality but comes to nothing more than what a community's procedures of justification will certify; and philosophy does better as edifying redescription than as an attempt to get reality right. When people call this relativism, she distinguishes the arbitrary from the unconstrained: a vocabulary can be answerable to the resistance the world puts up and to the norms of the people using it, she says, without being answerable *to* the world in any representational sense. She also thinks the old worry about whether science latches onto mind-independent reality simply dissolves once you drop the picture of the mind as a mirror of nature — there is nothing left there to explain.

I don't want an episode where she restates any of this. Give me eight to ten questions that press where it actually hurts, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Specifically, I want questions on: whether the claim that there is no language-independent way the world is is itself offered as true in the strong sense she denies; whether the "resistance" the world offers is representation smuggled back in under a causal-sounding name; what her account of truth does with claims no one will ever be in a position to verify; and whether a vocabulary can be criticized as distorted rather than merely inconvenient when the community using it is the one being misled.

I also want one case of my own worked in, because I think it isolates the problem better than the usual examples. Suppose we meet a civilization whose technology plainly works — it gets us to the stars, it cures our diseases — but whose basic vocabulary shares no concepts with ours and could not be translated into ours even in principle. On her view, is there any sense in which their vocabulary and ours describe the same world, or is "the same world" itself something she has to deflate? Turn that into a question, and build in the follow-ups for the ways I expect her to dodge it: that the case is incoherent, that the success just means it works for us, or that failure of translation is beside the point.

Finally, after each question add a short note saying whether the worry you are having me press is one specialists on this topic actually raise, roughly how many of them accept it versus reject it, and where the matter is genuinely unsettled. I don't want to ambush her with a difficulty I invented, and I don't want to let her wave off a real one as a straw man.
