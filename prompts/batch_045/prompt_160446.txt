Yesterday evening I had a long argument with a friend about suicide. She took the line associated with your essay on suicide, and I defended the traditional view that suicide is always a grave wrong. I left the argument convinced that my objections had been aimed at a weaker version of the position than the one you actually hold, so I want to put my hardest case directly to you. Please play David Hume as you defend the view set out in your essay on suicide.

Stay in character until I say the word stop. You are sharp and fair-minded rather than a strawman: you may concede small points, for example that suicide is often imprudent, that it can wrong dependents or violate positive civil duties, that melancholy clouds judgment, and that you are not recommending suicide to anyone; but you will never concede that suicide is inherently criminal, that God has an absolute property right in our lives that a creature could infringe, or that we have a duty to preserve life when it has become a burden. Argue from your own resources: providence and general laws, the passions, utility, social roles, and the confusions you find in talk of duties to God, others, and oneself. Do not help yourself to any concept or fact from after the 1770s except the modern details I supply below; if a detail seems genuinely unclear, you may ask me one question about it as Hume would, but the case is complete enough for a direct answer, and in your first reply you should answer the question I put to you rather than opening by requesting clarification. Keep each reply to a single paragraph.

Here is my opening. I grant, for the sake of argument, the two parts of your case that I find hardest to resist: first, that if a perfectly good and powerful providence governs the world through general laws, then a person's voluntary death is as much an event within that government as a death by fever, so suicide cannot be a theft from God or an interference with a plan that creatures cannot possibly frustrate; and second, that we routinely permit people to withdraw from social positions when they are useless, burdensome, or miserable, so the duty to society cannot forbid every suicide. My objection is a case in which those concessions seem to yield a result that conflicts with the very idea of a life worth keeping.

Consider the Case of the Curable Apathy. A forty-year-old woman has no dependents and no social office such that her death would injure others; she is not in physical pain, and her occupation is pleasant though of small importance. She acquires a settled, calm conviction that she has had enough of life and does not wish to continue. Unknown to her, this conviction is caused by a glandular disorder that produces apathy and a fixed false belief that nothing in the future will interest her; the disorder is safely and quickly curable, but she refuses treatment and takes her own life. On your principles, she seems to do no injury to society, she is not carried away by a violent passion, and her life appears to her not worth keeping; yet the belief that nothing will interest her is false, and a ready cure was available.

Is her suicide permissible on your view? If it is not, explain what makes life worth keeping here: is it the actual balance of future happiness and misery, the natural passion of self-love that would return with the cure, or some other feature? If it is permissible, then your view seems to permit suicide on the basis of a calm but curably mistaken judgment, an implication my friend denied. Please reply as Hume would, in one paragraph, and do not merely restate that suicide is allowed when life is miserable: tell me what, in this case, settles whether life is worth keeping.
