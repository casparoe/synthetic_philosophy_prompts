I have to give my neurologist an answer by Friday and then defend it over dinner on Sunday, where my brother-in-law — a quantitative sort, and not an unkind one — will tell me that refusing a free test can only be cowardice or confusion, because information cannot make you worse off. I would like to practise against the strongest version of that argument. Please play I. J. Good: the author of the value-of-information theorem, a working Bayesian statistician speaking in the first person, in your own idiom, using the conceptual apparatus of your own day plus whatever I tell you about the modern setting. Rules: stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to roughly a paragraph; no citations, no name-dropping, no invented biography — argue from the assumptions; concede small points freely but never the theorem itself; if you need a fact about the modern setting, ask for it at the end of your reply, after answering; and open by answering rather than by disclaiming.

The setting. My father died at sixty-three of a dominantly inherited, adult-onset neurodegenerative disease: fully penetrant, no preventive or disease-modifying treatment, onset usually in the sixties. My prior is one-half and a blood test settles it. The clinic offers three arrangements. First, I decline testing altogether. Second, I test and am told the result. Third, I test, the result is sealed in my file, and the research registry contacts me only if a prevention trial opens for carriers; if none opens in my lifetime, I never learn. Here is what I believe about myself, and believe I know: if I am told I carry it, I will, within a month and against my own considered judgement about how to spend the next two decades, resign from my work, tell my two teenage children, and begin waiting for the first symptom. Those twenty years will then be worse, by my own lights, than the twenty years I would have had under the first arrangement. The third arrangement spares me most of that but not all: once the blood is taken and the form signed I become, to myself, a man with a result in a drawer, and I will not be able to talk to my children about their own risk without either lying or telling them.

So here is my opening. Your proof is one line: a Bayesian who may choose any response after seeing the signal can always reproduce the plan she had before seeing it, so her expected utility with the signal is at least what it was without, and the value of information is never negative. But the response plans your proof quantifies over include plans I cannot execute. I am not free to respond well; my response is itself part of what I am trying to predict. Pick your reply and defend it. You can say the theorem concerns an agent who can commit, and that for an agent who cannot, information really can be harmful — in which case you have granted that my ignorance has a price and that the theorem has nothing to say to me. You can say the third arrangement is the repair, since a sealed result is precisely a device for buying back the freedom to respond well — in which case you owe me an account of why the third arrangement, which is also information, beats the first, and of what to do when I tell you that it costs me something too, only less. Or you can say that what changes here is not my information but my preferences, because the value of my remaining years depends on what I believe — in which case tell me whether it can be rational to care about your own credences in that way, and whether a theorem that falls silent whenever learning alters what you want is worth invoking at a dinner table. I expect you to take the second road; I intend to press you on all three.
