I work on the research side of a disarmament NGO, and I am drafting a short internal note on an analogy that has quietly become load-bearing in our discussions. The line is that nuclear deterrence is hostage-taking on a civilizational scale: each state holds the other's population hostage and threatens to kill tens of millions of civilians unless it gets what it wants. A colleague deploys it in two directions at once — first for the moral verdict, which is that hostage-taking is wrong not merely when the hostages die but in the issuing of the threat itself, so that deterrence is impermissible whatever its consequences turn out to be; and then, in the same breath, for an empirical claim, which is that nobody incinerates a country at the price of national suicide, so the threat is bluff, so we could disarm without courting attack. My instinct is that the second use eats the first, but I cannot tell whether the two are genuinely in tension or merely independent.

Audit the analogy rather than endorsing or dismissing it. Specifically:

Give me the correspondence table — captor, hostage, the demand, the ransom, the threatened killing, the killing itself, any third party with the standing to intervene, the escape route — and tell me which of those has no real counterpart in a deterrent relationship.

Separate the disanalogies into those that bear on the moral verdict, those that bear on the credibility claim, and those that bear on neither. My own candidate is that a captor can always release the hostages and walk away, whereas a nuclear state cannot release another state's cities; I cannot tell whether that is a genuine disanalogy or only a special case of hostage-taking, and I would like it run down rather than conceded.

Say what the source picture smuggles into the target that the target lacks: an agent who intends to kill and survives the killing, a discrete and satisfiable demand, a background legal order that makes the threat a crime and offers an alternative to it, hostages who are bystanders to the dispute rather than participants in it, and anything else you find doing quiet work.

Then take the two conclusions separately: does either survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the moral verdict survives while the bluff claim does not, or the reverse, identify exactly which disanalogy decides each.

Two complications I want handled rather than set aside. First, the analogy is used as though the state seized its hostages, when in fact the arsenals, the targeting plans and the vulnerable cities are largely inherited and the state's only act is to maintain them; I want to know whether the analogy requires an act of seizure or whether maintenance is enough, and what the answer implies about a state that inherits a posture it disapproves of. Second, the credibility limb of my colleague's argument rests on the threatener's survival instinct, and delegation — launch-on-warning, automated or dead-hand retaliation, anything that removes the human decision under uncertainty — removes that instinct from the picture. So tell me whether the analogy gets better or worse as the threat is delegated, and what that does to each of the two conclusions: it looks to me as if automation would strengthen the moral verdict and destroy the bluff claim, which would mean my colleague cannot have both, and I would like that checked.

Finally, if the hostage picture is the wrong frame, say what the right one is. My suspicion is that the morally relevant category here is not coercion but the imposition of risk, and that a better source picture would be something like two neighbouring towns each storing an unstable explosive that the other cannot make safe — but I want that tested rather than flattered, including what we would lose rhetorically by switching to it, since risk language is much weaker than hostage language and I need to know whether the weakness is rhetorical only or whether it tracks a real loss of moral content.
