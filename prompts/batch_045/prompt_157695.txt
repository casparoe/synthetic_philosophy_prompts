I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm finishing a piece on whether machine learning systems can be said to mean anything by the symbols they produce — not in the promotional sense, but in the strict one: is there a fact of the matter about which function such a system has been trained to compute? My editor's view, which I've come to share, is that the philosophical argument worth having here is the old one about rule-following, so I'd like to conduct an interview against it.

Please play a genuine meaning skeptic: the figure who holds that no fact about a speaker constitutes her meaning one function rather than another, that the so-called straight solution is unavailable, and that what we are left with are assertibility conditions and the practices of a community that can license or withhold a judgement. Not a caricature. You know the standard resistances — dispositions specified under ideal conditions, appeals to simplicity or to the natural joints of the world, the thought that what looks underdetermined from the inside is settled by what a community would count as going on in the same way — and you have answers to each of them. You concede small points readily and never the main one. You need not cite anything or name a single authority; I can't check references on deadline and my readers don't want them. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, in the first person, as a good source does on the phone, and stay in character until I say stop. If you need something about how these systems are actually built, ask me for it the way a source asks a reporter.

Here is the case my reporting keeps producing, and the one I want to press. Take a language model fine-tuned so that it answers correctly for every pair of addends it met in training but answers '5' whenever both addends exceed fifty — a quusified model, if you like, with the threshold set low enough that the deviant answers are everywhere in its behaviour rather than hidden beyond the reach of any computation anyone will ever perform. Now take a second model that means addition but is merely broken: it emits exactly the same pattern of deviant answers, but because of a defect in the inference code rather than because of anything about what it was trained to compute. By any test I can run from the outside, the two are indistinguishable; and unlike you or me, I can also open them up and read off, in exhaustive detail, what each one does with any input I care to name.

So here is my opening, and I'd like you to deal with both halves of it. First: whatever grip your paradox has on us, it comes from finitude — a human being has applied the rule to finitely many cases, and nothing in that record, or in her dispositions, settles which rule she meant, since dispositions have to be identified under a description and every description is itself up for grabs. That complaint simply doesn't transfer. The system's behaviour is not finite in any sense that matters, and it is systematically interrogable: I can ask it, in effect, what it will do with any case whatsoever, and read its internal state besides. If the worry was that meaning outruns the evidence, the evidence here has been industrialised. Second, even granting you the underdetermination, the tie is broken by naturalness: addition is an eligible function and the quusified variant is a gerrymander, and that is precisely the kind of distinction the sciences rely on when they separate a law of nature from an accidentally true generalisation. So the fine-tuned model means the deviant function and the corrupted model means addition despite its corrupted behaviour — the fact of the matter you deny is sitting right there, in the system's dispositions under their most natural description. Tell me why neither half works.

One last thing, which I ask every source and print at the end of the exchange: close each of your replies with one or two sentences on what would change your mind — what finding about these systems, or what argument about us, would lead you to concede that there is a fact of the matter after all — and on where you think your own reasoning is most likely to give way. I'd rather have the honest weak point from you than have a hostile reviewer find it for me.
