ok so I'm slowly working my way through Aristotle's Politics book 1 (the part around 1257a–1258a, in some old public domain translation) for a reading group, and I keep getting stuck on his story about how money shows up. Here are the bits I mean:

> Take for example a shoe—there is its wear as a shoe and there is its use as an article of exchange; for both are ways of using a shoe, inasmuch as even he that barters a shoe for money or food with the customer that wants a shoe uses it as a shoe, though not for the use peculiar to a shoe, since shoes have not come into existence for the purpose of barter.

> For when they had come to supply themselves more from abroad by importing things in which they were deficient and exporting those of which they had a surplus, the employment of money necessarily came to be devised. For the natural necessaries are not in every case readily portable; hence for the purpose of barter men made a mutual compact to give and accept some substance of such a sort as being itself a useful commodity was easy to handle in use for general life, iron for instance, silver and other metals, at the first stage defined merely by size and weight, but finally also by impressing on it a stamp in order that this might relieve them of having to measure it; for the stamp was put on as a token of the amount.

> But at other times, on the contrary, it is thought that money is nonsense, and nothing by nature but entirely a convention, because when those who use it have changed the currency it is worth nothing, and because it is of no use for any of the necessary needs of life and a man well supplied with money may often be destitute of the bare necessities of subsistence, yet it is anomalous that wealth should be of such a kind that a man may be well supplied with it and yet die of hunger, like the famous Midas in the legend, when owing to the insatiable covetousness of his prayer all the viands served up to him turned into gold.

> It is this art of wealth-getting that is thought to be concerned with money, for money is the first principle and limit of commerce. And these riches, that are derived from this art of wealth-getting, are truly unlimited; for just as the art of medicine is without limit in respect of health, and each of the arts is without limit in respect of its end (for they desire to produce that in the highest degree possible), whereas they are not without limit as regards the means to their end (for with all of them the end is a limit to the means), so also this wealth-getting has no limit in respect of its end, and its end is riches and the acquisition of goods in the commercial sense.

> as therefore the desire for life is unlimited, they also desire without limit the means productive of life.

Now the thing I actually want help with (please don't just tell me "Aristotle distinguishes household management from the art of wealth-getting and thinks the second one is unnatural", I got that far, I want the joints of the argument):

One of the other people in the group came up with a dumb little thought experiment that I have not been able to shake. Suppose a city where the currency can't be saved: every coin stops being valid at midnight, say it's stamped with the date and re-stamping costs more than the coin is worth. Call it perishable scrip. Nobody can accumulate money over time, but grain, tools, land and livestock can of course still be stored, and trade goes on normally during the day.

My questions:

1. Does perishable scrip even count as money on the account Aristotle gives in the second block? His story seems to build money out of two features: something that is itself useful and easy to handle/portable, plus the compact. Is storability part of what's doing the work there (money as a device for getting around the fact that necessaries aren't portable), or is the "mutual compact to give and accept" bit meant to be doing all the work, so anything the city agrees on would count?
2. The bit that really confuses me: he calls money "nothing by nature but entirely a convention" and in the same breath treats the trade-y kind of wealth-getting as *un*natural. If money is purely conventional, what is the unnaturalness supposed to consist in? Is it about the end (money/wealth becoming an end instead of a means, so nothing intrinsic stops the accumulation), or is it about money itself somehow? If it's the latter then the convention bit makes it look circular, and if it's the former then the Midas story and the "money is nonsense" paragraph look like they're doing less work than people assume.
3. The main one: in the perishable-scrip city, would the unlimitedness disappear? He gives two explanations that I can't tell apart. One is about money's own peculiar feature — it's a token whose only use is exchange, so there's no natural point of satiation, and every art "is without limit in respect of its end". The other is about a mistake people make about *life*: because the desire for mere life is unlimited, they want without limit the things that sustain life, and they try to get the good life via bodily enjoyment. If the second explanation is the real one, scrip that expires at midnight changes nothing at all — people would just chase unlimited grain instead, and the household branch and the trade branch would still get confused. If it's the first, then scrip should actually cure the disease, which seems like a weird conclusion and makes me suspect I'm misreading the medicine analogy.

So: walk me through how you'd read these lines to settle as much of this as the text can settle, and be explicit about where the text just underdetermines it and you'd have to pick between two readings. If it helps, say which parts of the shoe example (the "use peculiar to a thing" move) would or wouldn't carry over to money itself.
