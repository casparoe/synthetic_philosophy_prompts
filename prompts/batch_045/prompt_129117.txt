You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and moral philosophy, with real expertise in animal ethics, theories of consciousness, and the moral status of artificial systems. Think carefully and show your work: start by reconstructing the pattern in my verdicts before proposing anything, try out at least two candidate principles rather than settling on the first one that fits, and tell me plainly where you think a verdict of mine is mistaken rather than accommodating it with an ad hoc clause.

I am trying to work out what I actually believe about moral status as a matter of degree, going by gut verdicts on cases rather than by theory. Here they are.

A housefly I am about to swat: small but real status; a hundred fly deaths feel to me like one bad afternoon for a dog. A lobster boiled alive: clearly worse than the fly, and I am less sure than I would like why. A nematode with a few hundred neurons: nonzero, but I would not change any behavior for it, and I resist saying zero because I do not know where to stop the slide. Bacterial chemotaxis and a slime mold navigating a maze: zero, though I am noticeably less confident about the slime mold than about the bacterium. A thermostat: zero. A billion thermostats in a grid, not communicating with each other: still zero; no number of non-subjects makes a subject. A billion flies: that does add up.

A neuron-for-neuron digital simulation of a fly nervous system on a server: same status as the fly; substrate is irrelevant to me. The same functional organization implemented as a giant lookup table over the system's entire input history, with no real internal dynamics: my gut says zero, or much less, and this is the case I keep going back and forth on. A continuous chemical system that realizes the same state transitions smoothly in physical time rather than in discrete steps: my gut also says zero, and I cannot articulate why this differs from the simulation case. That pair is what makes me doubt I have a coherent view at all.

A large perceptual and predictive system that models its environment in extraordinary detail and acts on it, but gives no sign of anything mattering to it: zero, but I hold this uneasily. A hypothetical system with almost no world-model but a single internal state that is intensely bad for it and that it reliably acts to end: substantial status, in my view more than the fly's. So I seem to weight valence far above representation. A bee whose two brain halves are disconnected so that each sustains a complete sensorimotor loop but neither ever exchanges information with the other: I do not know whether to count one subject, two diminished ones, or one diminished one, and my leaning toward two halves totaling about one bee feels like a dodge. A single, still-unified subject with a hundred times the fly's neurons: worth perhaps three flies to me, not a hundred. A being capable of intense suffering but no positive states at all: counts for more than one capable of mild contentment but no suffering.

My tentative principle: moral status requires valence, a minimal self-model in which those valenced states are represented as pertaining to the system, and a unity or integration condition that marks off one subject at a time; given those, status scales with what I will call resolution, the number of distinguishable ways things can be better or worse for that subject, with normal summation across subjects but sharply diminishing returns within one. Test that against my cases, repair it, or replace it. Where must it break - especially on the simulation, lookup-table, and continuous-realization trio, on the split bee, on the slime mold, and on the diminishing-returns verdict - and would a cleaner principle that overturns one of my verdicts be preferable to a messier one that saves them all? State your principle or principles precisely, note which case motivates each clause, and give verdicts on two cases I have not listed: a honeybee colony treated as a single agent that thermoregulates and selects nest sites, and a system with a fly's resolution of valenced states but a human-grade world-model.
