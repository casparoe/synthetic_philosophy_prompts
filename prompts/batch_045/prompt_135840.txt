I'm a retired fluid dynamicist — thirty-odd years of groundwater modelling — and for the past year I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the public library: six retired engineers and one very patient grad student. We've come badly unstuck on the absent-minded driver, and I think its because we never separated the questions. You know the setup: you can exit at the first or the second intersection, exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, driving past both pays 1, and when you're at an intersection you can't tell which one you're at.

Half the group works out the planning answer — exit with probability one third at each intersection, expected value 4/3 — then says the driver should re-open the decision when he actually arrives, and half says he obviously shouldn't, and we go in circles for an hour. So I want a decision tree instead of an argument.

What I'm after: five branch nodes, each one a question somebody could answer without already knowing which view it leads to, routing you from 'I accept the setup and the numbers' down to a named leaf. The leaves I want at the bottom, in plain language with the textbook label if there is one:

- mix at one third, decided once before you set off, expected value 4/3
- the same plan, but held by refusing to re-decide at the intersection (the resolute-choice answer)
- always go straight, take the 1
- exit at every intersection, take the 0 — I suspect nobody lives here and I want to know whether that's because it's silly, incoherent, or just unvisited
- 'one decision, not two': your evidence at the two intersections is identical, so whatever you do at one you do at the other
- the view that your credence of being at the second intersection depends on your own policy, since the more likely you are to continue the more likely you are to arrive there at all

Don't build the tree out of those labels, build it out of the questions and then say where the six land; by the time all five are answered every leaf should be reachable. Flag any pair your tree can't separate — I'd bet the first and the fifth collapse in the basic case, and I want to know which of the cases below pries them apart.

And please hang each node on one of these cases rather than on an abstract claim. Work out whatever numbers you need yourself: no citations, no quotes, the group has no patience for the literature.

- the basic case with 0 / 4 / 1, just for calibration
- three intersections instead of two (0 at the first, 4 at the second and third, 1 for missing all of them): does your answer generalise, and does it generalise smoothly or does something snap?
- a trembling hand: with some small probability your foot slips and you do the opposite of what you intended. Does your recommendation approach your original one as that probability goes to zero, or does it jump?
- before leaving, you can buy a device that tells you which intersection you're at. How much do you pay? I want a number for each branch.
- a bookie at the intersection offers you even odds that this is the second one. Do you take the bet, and does your answer to that change what you do with the wheel?
- one coin flip in your pocket before you set off, consulted at both intersections, versus a fresh flip at each. These come apart and I want to know which leaf cares and why.
- you're only half absent-minded: with probability one half you remember whether you've already passed an intersection. Does your policy move continuously as that probability changes?

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one branch node each, a sentence or two apiece: the question, and where each answer sends you. No preamble and no summing-up at the end. If some combination of answers leaves an empty cell or a pair of leafs that can't be told apart, flag it inside the relevant item rather than in a coda.
