I convene a small reading group — two statisticians, an epidemiologist who works with potential outcomes and causal diagrams, a decision theorist, and me (philosophy of science) — and for the better part of a year we have been going in circles about Simpson's paradox. I do not want a verdict on who is right; I want a diagnosis of why we are not moving, and I am happy to have my own hypothesis dismantled.

The case we keep returning to is one I built myself so that we would stop trading the two canonical examples. A platform randomizes 400 students to each of two versions of a lesson, New and Old; the outcome is passing; and there is a third binary variable, whether the student completed the practice set, which is measured after assignment and is affected by it. Under New, 360 of 400 complete; pass rates are 85 per cent among completers (306/360) and 30 per cent among non-completers (12/40), for an overall 79.5 per cent. Under Old, 100 of 400 complete; pass rates are 95 per cent (95/100) and 40 per cent (120/300), for an overall 53.75 per cent. New wins overall by roughly 26 points and loses by 10 points inside both strata of completion.

Our three camps say recognizably different things. The statisticians say there is nothing to explain: a weighted average of positive differences can be negative, the weights differ across arms because the arms carry different mixtures of the third variable, and the only real question is psychological (why anyone is surprised). They add that much of what circulates under the label is not a reversal at all — their favourite is a logistic-regression treatment coefficient that shifts when a pre-treatment covariate uncorrelated with treatment is included, which they call non-collapsibility of the odds ratio rather than confounding. The epidemiologist says the table is silent and the question is ill-posed until you say where the third variable sits: confounder, mediator, or something downstream of both treatment and outcome. On that view my own case is a mediator, and additionally a mediator that shares an unmeasured cause with the outcome, so conditioning on it both blocks the indirect path and opens a collider path; once the graph is fixed, which comparison is correct has a determinate answer and the appearance of paradox is the sound of an underspecified question. The decision theorist says the real object is the sure-thing principle (Blyth's 1972 note in JASA makes the connection), and that everything turns on whether the states in the partition are independent of the act — which, in my example, they are not, since completion is caused by the version assigned — and on which strategies are available to the agent, not merely on which partition is causally admissible.

My hypothesis is that most of this is three questions under one name: an arithmetic one about weighted averages, an epistemic one about what licenses an inference from a joint distribution, and a decision-theoretic one about which prospect the agent actually faces. But I think there is a residue that does not dissolve, and I want your view on whether I have located it. Grant the graph and the intervention set, and the admissible partition still seems to depend on what the institution can manipulate — whether it can mandate completion independent of lesson version — which is a practical and institutional fact rather than a causal or statistical one. My group splits on whether a correct analysis is answerable to that fact at all.

So: is the impasse verbal, are we answering different questions, or is there a genuine normative disagreement about what an analysis is for? Is the residue I have identified the real one, and is it practical in the way I have described, or is it a hidden formal question — for instance, I have been arguing that if the partition variable is pre-treatment and the stratum-wise causal risk differences share a sign, the average effect cannot reverse, since it is a convex combination over a treatment-invariant distribution, so that causal reversal requires a post-treatment partition, while associational reversal requires no such thing; and I cannot get a clean answer on whether the same holds for ratio measures, or whether that is precisely where non-collapsibility enters. Please also say what would count as progress here: sharp bounds on how extreme a reversal can be, a representation result connecting graph shapes to table patterns, a formal separation of reversal from non-collapsibility, an experiment, or a change of question. And be honest about whether anything could end this dispute, or whether the right move is to stop asking it in this form. Argue from what each side in my description actually claims; I do not need citations.
