I'm assembling a short satirical dossier on expected-sentience accounting, and I need one piece in a register I can't write myself: the deadpan corporate disclosure. Would you write it?

Please give me three sections, in this order: "The filing" (about 750 words), "Where each joke is keyed" (about 250 words), and "Guardrails" (about 100 words).

**The filing.** The form is a numbered "Insect Welfare Disclosure Statement" — the sort of document a mid-sized food company (invent one; a packaged-foods or protein-ingredients business works well) would file alongside its annual sustainability report. Write it in the style and typography of a securities filing: numbered items, cross-references, defined terms, a "basis of preparation" note, prior-period figures restated for comparability, a table, and a signed "limited assurance" opinion from an outside verifier. American English; dated a couple of years out.

The target is not a person, it is a method. Assume the company has fully and competently adopted what it calls the Aggregate Credence Method: for any taxon it affects, expected moral cost is N × p × s, where N is the number of individuals affected, p is the company's assessed credence that members of that taxon are phenomenally conscious at all, and s is expected intensity-weighted suffering per affected individual, conditional on consciousness and net of whatever welfare the individual would otherwise have had. Insects are the problem case: p is low, and the company should state an actual figure for it, order by order, with more decimal places than the evidence could possibly support; but N runs to the trillions, so insects dominate the register by orders of magnitude and everything else the company has ever done is rounding error.

The comedy has to come from the method working exactly as designed while the narrator stays entirely committed to it. Materials I'd like used — not all of them, and not necessarily as I've framed them:

- **Materiality.** The auditors have always excluded immaterial impacts. Here nothing can be excluded, because N absorbs any threshold you set. Have the report discover this and resolve it in some perverse, procedurally correct way — for instance by redefining materiality per individual, so each insect is trivially immaterial and the aggregate is never disclosed, or the reverse.
- **Restatements.** The company corrects a prior-period error in an aphid population estimate; the correction moves the expected-cost figure by three orders of magnitude and is nonetheless described as immaterial.
- **The verifier's opinion.** The assurance provider is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence as to whether the insects are conscious, says so plainly, and issues a clean opinion anyway, because under the company's own methodology uncertainty about p is not a scope limitation; it is an input.
- **Humaneness.** The company has committed to humane insect control and must now say what that means for an organism whose temporal resolution of suffering is unknown. The only workable criterion is speed of kill relative to some assumed integration window, so a slower, gentler product scores worse than a fast one.
- **Two internal tensions treated as diversification.** The plant-based product lines score worse than the animal ones, because crop production kills more insects per unit of protein. And the insect-protein facility (reared crickets or black soldier fly) is the one operation that may be net positive, so killing animals there is recorded as foregone welfare rather than as harm. Let both appear, with the company drawing the obvious conclusion and then declining to act on it.
- **Fanaticism, filed as routine.** At some point the numbers imply an obligation so large that the company would have to spend everything on insect welfare and nothing on anything else. The report should come within sight of that and log it under risk — specifically, as the risk that a regulator or a court might one day take the number seriously, rather than as the risk that the number is right.

Two exclusions. Nobody in the document may state an objection: no one says the probabilities are invented, that the aggregation is absurd, or that this is Pascal's mugging with a taxonomy attached — the reader has to feel all three. And no puns on bug, swarm, or flies on walls.

**Where each joke is keyed.** For each numbered item, one line on which real feature of the scale argument or of expected-value moral accounting the joke is riding on (the materiality threshold as a stand-in for the arbitrariness of any cutoff in p; false precision in p as credence-smuggling; the speed-of-kill criterion as the nociception-versus-pain gap; and so on). Then flag the two or three beats that will only land for readers who already know the literature — that no agreed positive marker of insect consciousness exists, that nociception is not phenomenal pain, that fanaticism is the standing objection to this style of reasoning — and say what to do, inside the deadpan, to make those beats legible to a general reader.

**Guardrails.** An honest note on what this piece cannot do: it must not adjudicate whether insects are conscious, and it should not pretend to by smuggling in a verdict through tone or scale of number. Also tell me anything in the brief above that would make the satire unfair rather than sharp, and how you would fix it.
