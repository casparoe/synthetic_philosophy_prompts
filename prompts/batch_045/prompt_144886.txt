Setup, kept short. Imaginary jurisdiction, but the structure is the kind of thing that actually exists: the Charter of Vereda. Art. 50 is the amending formula — two thirds in both chambers plus a majority in one referendum. Art. 51 entrenches it: Art. 50 may be amended only by three quarters in both chambers plus approval in two referenda held at least two years apart, and Art. 51 says of itself that it is amendable by that same double procedure. Art. 52 is an eternity clause: universal suffrage can't be touched.

The case the chapter is built on. In 2019 the Assembly, using only the simple Art. 50 procedure, purports to enact Amendment N, which repeals Art. 51's double lock. Under Art. 51 as it then stood, N is invalid. In 2022, after an election, a new Assembly uses the full double procedure to enact Amendment M, which says that N is confirmed and shall be taken to have had effect from the day it was purportedly enacted. Between 2019 and 2022 the official statute book printed N, courts applied it in at least two reported cases, a statute was struck down under it, and a referendum was held under the simple formula it introduced.

The thesis I'm defending: there are three legal statuses here, not two — valid, void, and provisionally valid. N was law during the interregum; that status was real, not just a prediction about future ratification and not just a summary of what officials happened to believe; and it was settled only retrospectively, by M. So positivism has to seperate a norm's existence from the settlement of its validity, and the rule of recognition has to be able to return a third value at a time. I lean on an analogy with void vs. voidable in private law (N is like a voidable transaction awaiting ratification), and I take a run at the revenge case: a clause providing that no amendment shall have effect before its own enactment — a clause which is itself amendable, possibly retroactively.

The room: my supervisor, a fairly orthodox Hartian who thinks the rule of recognition is a social practice among officials and who will smell morality being smuggled in; the department's other legal philosopher, a planning-theory type who will want to know what the plan says; a visiting Kelsenian who holds that a norm cannot validate the act that created it and that my retroactivity is a confusion about the hierarchy of norms; a logician visiting for the week who works on self-reference and revenge paradoxes and has written about that self-amending rules game Nomic; a comparative constitutional lawyer who works on unconstitutional amendments and basic-structure review and thinks this is all judicial review with the serial numbers filed off; and a retired judge on the panel who just wants to know what a drafter or a court is supposed to do on Monday morning.
