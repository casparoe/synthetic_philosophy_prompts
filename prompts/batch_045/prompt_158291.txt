I've been defending a view in a grad seminar and I want someone to try to break it. Background: the heterogeneity problem. Pleasures are phenomenally wildly various — the taste of cold water when you're thirsty, the click of a proof falling into place, the relief of taking off tight shoes, malicious amusement at a rival's stumble, the specific sensory wallop of a really good peach, the low hum of contentment on a Sunday afternoon. What makes them all pleasures? I don't think it's a shared felt quality: there isn't one, or if there is it's so thin and attention-dependent that positing it feels like cheating. And I don't think it's an attitude either — I've never been able to get on board with the family of views that identify pleasure with some pro-attitude toward one's own experience, since that seems to me to get the direction of explanation backwards.

My view, which I've been calling the recalibration view: what unifies the pleasures is not a quality or an attitude but a dynamic role. Pleasures are the states that positively update an organism's evaluative baseline — roughly, the states that tell the system this is now what to compare against, the reward-prediction-errorish signal that simultaneously (a) shifts the comparison point so that an outcome that was fine before now registers as a falling short, and (b) reweights attention, learning and action toward whatever produced it. Two things I like: first, it's a role, not a quality, so heterogeneity of phenomenology is no embarrassment — very different feelings can play the same role, the way very different physical stuff can all play the role of money. Second, it predicts the adaptation data and the way the same stimulus flips from delightful to cloying or flat as internal conditions change, rather than having to explain those away as noise.
