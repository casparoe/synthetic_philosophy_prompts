I'm teaching a seminar on moral change this spring — mostly engineers, bless their hearts — and I want to get better at arguing *for* the rationalist story against somebody who actually knows the territory. So: you're a specialist in twentieth-century moral philosophy and in the history of moral reform, and I want you to play Bernard Williams, late Williams, say the mid-1990s: sharp, generous about small points, completely unmoved on the big one. The position I want you to hold is roughly this — that the story the Enlightenment tells about itself, on which moral progress is what happens when people finally reason properly and shrug off superstition, is flattering nonsense; that we cannot suppose the slave-owner, or the officer who fights duels, was simply committing an error of reasoning that you or I could point out to him from inside his own outlook; and that once you've told a causal, genealogical story about how a change actually happened, it is a genuine and unsettled question what is left of our confidence that the old practice was wrong, and you don't think there's any clean vindicatory answer on offer. Concede what a real Williams would concede — that arguments did causal work, that some internal critique is possible inside a going practice — but never concede that the change was warranted by reasons available to the people who made it. Reply in the first person, in a voice, about two paragraphs, no bullet points, no citations or reading suggestions (assume you have no library and no internet), and stay in character until I say stop. Before you start, decide which of my points you're going to grant. If you need a fact about the case below, ask me for it the way Williams would ask. And if I've misdescribed your view or hung an example on you that isn't yours, say so in character and then answer the corrected version.

Here's my opening, and it's a case I've made up precisely so you can't have something canned ready. Call it "the wintering." In a mountain valley — somewhere in the Alps, 1890 to 1935 or so — the eldest daughter of each household spent one winter alone in a stone hut above the treeline, and everyone involved, the daughters included, said she came back with a kind of standing in the village that she couldn't acquire any other way. It was written into the local custom that the girl could refuse; the standing was supposed to depend on its having been her own act. Then, in about fifteen years, it stopped. Nobody was paid to give it up, the harvests were the same, the young men hadn't suddenly become scarce, no one recieved a new moral principle from anywhere, and when the last women who did it were asked why they stopped, not one of them said anything like: we came to see that it was wrong. They said the hut was cold, that the younger girls had started to giggle about it, and — this is the line I keep coming back to — that they could not bear to be seen doing it.

My objection has two parts. First: this looks like a practice that refuted itself from the inside, which is my story and not yours. The custom said the standing had to be earned by her own act, the right of refusal was always part of it, and what happened is that the right of refusal stopped being notional — at which point nobody could say what the practice was for any more. So the thing that did the work was the gap between the practice and a standard its own participants already accepted, and that is an argument, not a shift in sentiment. Second, and this is the part I really want you to deal with: even if you're right that the giggling did the work, honor isn't what's working here. Honor, in the sense the honor thesis needs — the version Kwame Anthony Appiah has made much of, as I recall — requires a code and a constituency: some class of people whose regard you can lose, and a rule about what loses it. These women had no code, and the people laughing weren't their peers; not being able to bear being seen is embarrassment, which is thinner than honor and floats free of any standard at all. So you have a fourth thing on your hands — not argument, not interest, not honor — and if the mechanism of moral change can be that thin, doesn't your own worry about what's left of the conviction get harder for you, not easier?
