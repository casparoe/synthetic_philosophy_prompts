A friend sent me a transcript of a popular philosophy podcast on strong longtermism, and the host's summary goes roughly like this:

"Strong longtermism, the view pushed by the Oxford effective-altruism philosophers, starts from a banal observation: there could be vastly more people in the future than have ever lived so far — trillions upon trillions, if we avoid going extinct and spread beyond this planet. Since every life is worth roughly the same, the value at stake in the far future dwarfs everything at stake today, so nearly all of the value of what we do now comes down to how it shapes the very long run. And there's really only one lever on that: whether our species survives. So the view boils down to a priority — reduce extinction risk, almost regardless of what else you could be doing. The numbers are meant to make rival priorities look silly: saving thousands of lives now is a rounding error next to shifting the chance of extinction by a fraction of a percentage point, which is why these people treat existential risk work, especially AI safety, as overwhelmingly the most important cause, and why critics say they treat everyone alive today as mere fuel for the future. The standard objection is that this only goes through if merely possible future people count exactly as much as real ones, and it also assumes the future will be good rather than, say, full of miserable digital minds."

I've read a little of the actual material since and somthing feels off, but I can't tell which sentences. Two hunches: (1) the move from "the future is astronomically big" to "so reduce extinction risk" needs an extra premise about how much leverage we actually have on the far future, not just about how big it is; and (2) a claim about where most of the value sits doesn't by itself tell you what you ought to do, not unless you're already a straight expected-value maximiser. But I may be inventing problems.

Can you go through it claim by claim — what a careful defender would happily sign onto, what they'd call a simplification or a shift of emphasis, what they'd reject outright — and then say whether the punchline survives the corrections? Two extras I want a ruling on, not a survey: the host said the whole thing collapses into fanaticism, since expected value gets dominated by tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs, so a longtermist owes you that any finite cost is worth paying for a one-in-a-billion shot at steering things; and he brushed off the reply that value might "saturate" (past some astronomical number of good lives, more lives add almost nothing) on the grounds that the numbers are too big for it to matter. Take a definite position on each, with reasons, and tell me how confident you are — don't just lay out what various people say.
