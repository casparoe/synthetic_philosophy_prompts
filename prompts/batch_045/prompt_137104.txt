I am a novelist, and I need help I am not qualified to give myself. My work in progress is set about fifteen years from now and turns on a fictional consumer technology: a soft band at the wrist that reads pulse, skin conductance, respiration and vocal prosody, infers from them a coarse "affect signature" — valence, arousal, and one of perhaps a dozen labels such as grief, relief, envy, dread — and pushes that signature, continuously, to a small circle of the wearer's choosing. The founders, who read Smith in college, have named the company Inmate, after the figure Smith calls the man within the breast. Its features matter to me and I will list them: the transmission runs one way only, since a recipient receives the signature but can send nothing back in kind and the wearer is never told who attended to which moment; the stream is archived, and the archive, not the sharing, turns out to be the thing subscribers pay for, a diary nobody had to write; the signature arrives without a situation, so a recipient is told grief, sustained, and given no cause; broadcasting is possible, and the company's fastest-growing line of business is a brokerage for paid witnesses, strangers who are hired to attend to a subscriber's stream; and the founding claim, repeated in every pitch, is that the device removes the guesswork from fellow-feeling and therefore makes sympathy more accurate, because the sufferer no longer has to perform his distress for an audience that may misread it, and the audience no longer has to imagine what he feels.

My protagonist is a fifty-eight-year-old moral philosopher who has spent her career on The Theory of Moral Sentiments and who, for money she needs, has agreed to write the company's ethics white paper. I want her judgment on the device to be one a serious Smithian could actually defend, and I would like you to reconstruct it: what would Smith, given the machinery of Part I and Part III — sympathy as an imaginative projection that works on the causes and circumstances of a passion rather than on the bare passion itself, the mutual adjustment by which a sufferer brings his emotion down to a pitch the bystander can go along with and the bystander raises his own to meet it, the concord that is its own reward, the propriety that belongs to expression rather than to feeling, and the internal spectator that is built up out of the faces and behavior of the people we actually live among — say about this? I am interested in several things at once. Whether what the band transmits is sympathy in his sense at all, or something that mimics its outward signs while bypassing the mechanism. What an internal spectator would become if it were assembled out of attendances that are one-directional, unacknowledged, and detached from anything the wearer does, as opposed to out of the countenances of people who are watching him act. Whether Smith, who is notoriously clear-eyed about the fact that what most people want from wealth and rank is to be observed, attended to, and noticed with sympathy and approbation, would regard a market in paid attention as a corruption of that desire or as its logical and perhaps even its democratizing fulfillment, and whether his worries elsewhere about what the division of labor does to the mind of the person who performs a single narrowed operation bear on professional witnesses. And how he would take the company's aggregate product, a public index of the emotional state of whole populations, given how little he expects from our concern with multitudes we cannot picture — I am thinking of the notorious thought experiment in which a man in Europe is unmoved by the destruction of an enormous distant population but devastated by the loss of his own little finger.

I also want the strongest Smithian case *for* the device, not only the case against it, because I do not want the scene to be an argument with a strawman: one of my secondary characters is a hospice nurse who uses the stream to sit with dying patients she cannot always be beside, and who says, plausibly, that it has taught her to attend. Tell me what she gets right by Smith's own lights.

Please answer in American English. There is no need for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the argument, not the apparatus. And please be scrupulous about your own epistemic status. Mark the difference between what Smith plainly commits himself to and what is genuinely contested among people who work on his moral philosophy — whether the impartial spectator is irreducibly a social product or can be fully idealized and internalized, whether his project is normative theory or the phenomenology of moral judgment, whether fellow-feeling requires co-presence or only an imagined presence, and how the moral philosophy relates to the later economic work are all places where I gather specialists disagree. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and tell me explicitly where your reconstruction of Smith's verdict rests on a reading a substantial minority of scholars would reject, where you are extrapolating from his text rather than reporting it, and where you are guessing at his psychology. Finally, say where an honest Smithian has to admit that the text simply runs out: I would much rather have my character concede in a scene that Smith offers no guidance on a question than have her mouth a doctrine he never held, and I would like to know which single sub-question the prediction you give me is most fragile with respect to, and what would move it most.
