The question we set was:

(a) State Bradley's regress in your own words and identify the assumption that generates it. (b) Explain why the regress is supposed to threaten the view that a proposition is a complex built out of objects together with a relation. (c) Some philosophers hold that the regress is "benign" rather than "vicious". What would have to be true for it to be benign, and how should someone who thinks it's vicious reply? (d) Suppose the regress can be blocked — for instance by holding that relations are unsaturated and relate their relata as such, without needing to be related to them by something further. Does blocking the regress by itself give you a positive account of what makes a proposition one thing rather than a mere list of its constituents? Why or why not?

ANSWER #14:

Bradley's regress starts from the thought that a proposition like "a loves b" is a complex made of three items: a, b, and the relation of loving. But if the relation is just one more item sitting alongside a and b, then nothing so far binds the three into a single proposition — a list of objects is not a claim. So we need a further relation R1 to relate the loving relation to a and to b. But R1 is itself another item, so we need R2 to tie R1 to loving, a and b, and so on without end. The generating assumption is that relations are entities on the same footing as the terms they relate: a relation is just one more object in the complex. The threat is then clear. If every attempt to bind the constituents requires a further binding, the proposition is never unified — or is unified only by an endless chain of relations — and an ununified plurality of objects is not the sort of thing that can be true or false. Bradley himself concluded that relational thought cannot describe reality as it really is, and that reality is an undifferentiated whole beyond all relations; most people now think that is far too large a conclusion to draw from this argument alone.

On (c): a vicious regress is one that goes on forever and a benign one is one that stops, so the question is whether the chain of relations inside a proposition is finite. The benign theorist therefore has to show that the chain terminates somewhere — that at some level a relation manages to relate its relata directly. The defender of the vicious reading replies that nothing in the set-up ever permits this: at every level the relation is still just another term, so nothing ever gets bound, and the proposition never achieves unity. The real disagreement is thus about whether the regress is infinite or finite.

On (d): yes, I think blocking the regress is all that unity requires. Frege's answer, which Russell later took over in his multiple-relation theory of judgment, is that relations are unsaturated: they relate their relata in virtue of what they are, so no additional relation is needed. Given that, the proposition is unified — its constituents are bound by the relation itself. The unity of the proposition just is the relation's relating its relata, and asking for something further over and above that is simply asking for another object to do the gluing, which is exactly what starts the regress in the first place. So there is nothing left to explain.
