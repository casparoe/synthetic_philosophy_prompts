I am trying to settle on the welfare input to a social welfare function — specifically, the interpersonal comparisons of well-being that a national cost–benefit analysis would need in order to attach distributional weights to gains and losses, and to defend those weights to citizens who disagree with one another about what well-being even consists in. Before I look at any particular proposal, I want the specification that any such account of interpersonal comparison would have to satisfy in order to be usable for that purpose.

Write that specification. For each entry, state the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and sort your entries into three grades: those that follow from the mathematics of utility representation or from the purpose I have fixed; those that are genuine but negotiable; and those that are really preferences of one camp — utilitarian, prioritarian, sufficientarian, capability-based, and so on — presented as though they were constraints on everyone.

The cases I want your list to engage, at minimum:

— a person with a blunted or restricted affective range (chronic pain, a medication that flattens affect) who reports on the same ten-point scale as everyone else, so that an "8" from them may sit near the bottom of a much narrower underlying range;
— two people whose well-being appears to be constituted differently — one contented because her desires are being met, one contented because her experience feels a certain way — where the two accounts of welfare disagree about which of them is better off;
— comparisons of gains rather than levels: the claim that a policy costs A more than twice what it gains B, and what must be in place before a claim of that shape is even well formed;
— a policy that changes which people come to exist, where the comparison seems to require a neutral level, a zero line, that is not merely the bottom of some existing person's scale;
— a population containing members whose welfare-bearing states may not resemble ours and who cannot self-report at all (non-human animals; artificial systems with a very different dynamic range);
— the fact that self-reports drift with mood, question order and recent events, so that any procedure for eliciting comparisons inherits a vulnerability the theory must address rather than stipulate away.

Then I want your judgement on five things. First, whether full comparability of levels is a requirement or an extra stipulation going beyond anything the individual utility functions alone deliver. Second, whether the list is jointly satisfiable: if some subset — invariance under each person's independent positive-affine rescaling, plus enough content to decide between benefiting the worse off and benefiting the better off, plus completeness of the resulting ordering — cannot be had together, say which requirement gives way and why that one. Third, whether the familiar disagreement between aggregating expected utilities before uncertainty resolves and aggregating them afterwards is a dispute about interpersonal comparison at all, or about something else your specification should have settled earlier. Fourth, which single requirement, as you have stated it, excludes the largest share of the accounts currently defended, and why that requirement is not simply a prejudice of your own. Fifth, whether genuine partial comparability — comparable in direction but not in magnitude — can be represented in a form a decision procedure can actually use, without the choice of decision rule quietly converting it back into full comparability.

Where two of your requirements pull against each other, say so explicitly, and say whether the conflict is real or only apparent. A short closing note on how the main families of accounts fare against the list is welcome, but keep it to a small fraction of your answer; the criteria and their motivating cases are what I am after.
