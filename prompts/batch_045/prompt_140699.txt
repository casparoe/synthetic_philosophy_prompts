I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas, and I'm putting together a piece on jury duty and unanimous-verdict rules, which seem to be having a small moment again — citizens' assemblies, sortition, that whole world. I want to hang the piece on the 1957 film 12 Angry Men (Lumet's debut, screenplay by Reginald Rose, Henry Fonda as the holdout), which everyone has seen and nobody reads as a story about epistemology. Here's the distinction I want you to bring to bear, so we're starting from the same place. An epistemic bubble is a social structure in which other relevant voices have simply been left out — it's a structure of omission, and in principle you can pop it just by being exposed to what you were missing. An echo chamber is nastier: you have heard the outside view, but the group has trained you to distrust the people who hold it, so new outside information gets absorbed by the discrediting mechanism and can leave you more confident, not less; you can't argue your way out of it, because escape requires rebuilding trust rather than weighing evidence. (C. Thi Nguyen drew the distinction in an Episteme paper in 2020; don't just restate his version, use it.)

What I want out of you:

1. Which one is that jury room? My hunch, which I want you to push back on, is that it's neither, and that the movie's actual epistemic vice is something the literature doesn't have a name for. Most of those men aren't dogmatic at all — they're indifferent. The one who treats deliberating a death-penalty case as an interruption to his afternoon, the guy rewriting his advertising copy at the table: they don't disbelieve the defence, they just attach near-zero cost to being wrong and want to go home. Call it epistemic drift, or the shouldn't-have-to-care bubble, whatever you like. Is that a genuine third failure mode, or just a weak echo chamber in disguise? If it's genuine, I think it carries a design implication I want to spell out in the piece: you don't fix it with exposure, and you don't fix it with trust repair, you fix it by changing what's at stake for the person holding the belief.

2. Unanimity. My pet idea, which I suspect is too cute: that a unanimity requirement is an anti-chamber technology, because it turns every single holdout into a veto player and so forces the majority to generate reasons instead of counting heads — a kind of "dissent premium." So: does the film actually support unanimity as an epistemic institution, or only as a legitimacy device?
