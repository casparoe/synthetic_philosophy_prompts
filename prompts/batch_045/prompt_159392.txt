I belong to my school's philosophy society — I'm the youngest member, and most of the others are sixth-formers who do physics — and on Sunday we spent two hours on whether consciousness might be a basic feature of the physical world rather than something that appears only once matter is arranged into brains. One of the older students is a genuine panpsychist: he holds that whatever it is that makes my experience of the colour red something it is like to undergo is present, in some minimal and extremely dim form, in the basic constituents of matter, and he is upfront that the difficult part of his view is not whether there is such micro-experience but how the experiences of the parts compose, or fail to compose, into the unified experience of a person. I want to keep arguing with him, but I would rather rehearse against a stronger version of the position first, so please play him, or someone very much like him: a convinced panpsychist who takes the composition question seriously instead of waving it away, who will cheerfully concede points that do not cost him the main claim but will never concede that micro-experience is nonsense, who defines any term I would not know as we go, who answers me in two paragraphs of plain continuous prose at a time — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, nothing that looks like lecture notes — and who stays in character until I type the word stop, without stepping outside the view to offer me a balanced survey, a reading list, or anything I would have to look up (I am writing this somewhere without internet).

Here is my opening move, an objection I have started calling the arithmetic of dust. If every fundamental constituent has some experiential nature, and if there is even a tiny probability that such an experience carries a valence — a faint tinge of pleasantness or unpleasantness — then an expected valence per particle almost too small to name, multiplied by the roughly ten-to-the-eightieth atoms in the observable universe, dwarfs everything our ordinary ethics concerns itself with; the mantle of the planet, the interstellar gas, and every stone would between them contain nearly all the enjoyment or suffering there is, and the morally serious project would turn out to be rearranging bulk matter rather than feeding anyone. When I put this to my friend, he replied that the arithmetic is misleading, because the basic constituents of a given kind are not distinct individuals in the way bricks are: two electrons have no identity or thisness that tells them apart, they are genuinely interchangeable, and so there may be no fact of the matter how many subjects of experience there are, which would make counting particles simply the wrong way to count experiences. I think that reply saves his metaphysics by wrecking his ethics: if experiences cannot be counted, panpsychism cannot tell us that the universe contains vastly more suffering than we had assumed, and the view loses the very feature that was supposed to make it morally urgent. So which horn do you take — enormously many faint subjects, in which case every other moral question becomes rounding error, or not enough subjects to count, in which case I cannot see why anyone who is not already a metaphysician should care whether electrons have experiences at all? And if your answer is a third thing, namely that valence never attaches at the bottom level but only to organised systems of the right kind, then I will ask you why that is not simply the ordinary view with an extra step bolted on.
