I'm not a philosopher at all — I work in warehouse logistics and got dragged into this by an argument with my brother-in-law that we've now been having for something like two years. The original thing: we're driving to the airport, I say "I know the flight's at 8:20, I read the confirmation email," and he goes "you don't know that, pull over and check it — if you miss this flight we're loosing the contract." We both dug in, and eventually it came out that we actually agree about the strange part (how much you're willing to say the word "know" seems to move around depending on what's riding on being right) and completely disagree about why. I've since found out the two camps have names: on one side the people who say the standard that "knows" has to meet shifts with the context of whoever is speaking or evaluating — contextualists — and on the other the people who say the standard is fixed but whether you know depends on what's at stake for you, the person doing the believing — the interest-relative / pragmatic encroachment people. Reading them go at each other has been like watching me and my brother-in-law, so: write the joint statement the two sides could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

What I want, concretely: first a numbered section of claims both could put their name to — my seeds are that the stakes-sensitivity is real and not just politeness, hedging, or nervousness about asserting; that the plain invariantist who says stakes never make a difference to whether someone knows is wrong; that what's at stake doesn't change the facts (the departure time is what it is) or how good my evidence is, so whatever it changes has to be something else; and that knowledge is the thing that's supposed to license both acting and flat-out asserting. Then a disagreements table: for each remaining disputed claim, the sentence as the contextualist would want it worded right next to the sentence as the encroachment person would want it worded, each phrased so its own side would definately accept it and neither is a strawman.

Then the cruxes, and this is the part I care most about: for each one, a concrete case both sides agree actually bears on it, plus what each side predicts and what each would count as evidence that would move them. Please build it around these four cases I've been chewing on, because I think they pry the views apart better than the airport one does. (1) The calm bystander with nothing at stake who says, about me in the car with everything riding on it, "he knows the flight is at 8:20" — and specifically whether his "he knows" and my brother-in-law's "he doesn't know" could both be correct, or whether one of them has to be wrong. (2) The case where the person holding the belief has no idea the stakes are high: nobody told her the group's water is nearly gone and she thinks it's a casual afternoon walk, so she's confident the trail goes left — do the hidden stakes still cost her the knowledge. (3) Stakes that are enormous for the group but negligible for her personally (her being wrong sinks everyone's plan, and she'd personally be fine). (4) Stakes that are high only because of a stupid or nasty goal — she's ruined if the bank is closed because she's there to cash a forged cheque — and whether the encroachment side has to say the worse your aims, the less you know. If it matters, add a fifth about "knows" buried inside an if-then or inside a report of what somebody said, instead of said flat out. And if you conclude that part of their fight was only ever about words, say which part and why.

Format it as numbered agreed claims, then the table, then the cruxes with predictions — no citations, no bibliography, no journal names, since my brother-in-law and I are going to read this out loud at thanksgiving and neither of us can look anything up. Keep the prose plain enough for two non-academics to follow, but don't sand off the disagreement where it's real.
