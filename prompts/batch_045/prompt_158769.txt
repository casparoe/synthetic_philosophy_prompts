I'm a journalist working on a long piece about how a handful of decision-theoretic ideas have migrated out of the circles where they were first formulated and into arguments about institutional design — who should hold a veto, whether a review board should be able to block a line of research, whether a company should be able to ship a powerful model on its own recognisance, and so on. One idea I keep running into is the unilateralist's curse, which I'll state in my own terms so that we aren't relying on anyone's particular formulation: suppose a number of roughly independent agents each has the power to bring about some high-stakes outcome single-handedly, and each is privately uncertain whether the outcome would be good. Then the very fact that you are in a position to be the one who acts is evidence against acting, since the others — with the same opportunity and evidence of the same quality — declined. Conditional on being the one who pulls the trigger, your private case looks weaker than it did before you conditioned, so each agent should require a higher expected value than a lone decision-maker would, and the more potential actors there are, the higher that bar should rise. That much now seems close to commonsense among the people I interview.

What I cannot get straight, and what I would like you to argue for, is a thesis I sketched badly at a dinner with two economists and have been trying to sharpen ever since. Consider the mirror-image institution: the outcome occurs unless somebody blocks it — a unanimity rule, a distributed veto, a chain of sign-offs. Now your veto is pivotal only in the worlds where every other gatekeeper let the proposal through, and if those gatekeepers are even moderately competent, that is evidence in the proposal's favour; so each of them should rationally raise their own bar for blocking. But multiplying veto-holders was supposed to be exactly the device that makes a bad proposal likely to be caught. My thesis has two parts. First, the two effects do not net out the way the naive "many watchdogs" intuition assumes: because each agent's threshold adjusts endogenously to the fact of the others' deference, adding veto-holders buys far less caution than designers expect, and can in the limit buy none at all, or leave the arrangement no more cautious — possibly less cautious — than a single well-informed gatekeeper who did not know that anyone else had already waved the proposal through. Second, and more provocatively for the piece I'm writing: the practical moral people draw from the unilateralist's curse — concentrate the power to act, require broad agreement, build in veto points — is largely self-defeating, because a unanimity requirement is precisely the institutional form in which this pivotality logic bites hardest against blocking, and the defects of unilateral action are not cured by replacing them with the timidity of collective gatekeeping.

So please argue for that position as strongly as you fairly can. I'd like the reasoning built from first principles and small explicit numerical illustrations rather than from any literature: I'm travelling, I have no library access, and I don't want citations or reading suggestions, since I can't follow them up. I also have a half-memory that something in this neighbourhood appears in formal work on jury decision rules — I may be misremembering, so please don't rest anything on it, and don't try to reconstruct it. Where my sketch above is imprecise or where the symmetry between the two cases breaks down, say so and repair it; but stay committed to the overall conclusion that distributed veto power is a weaker safeguard than its advocates suppose, and that the real lesson of the curse concerns the informational structure of the decision — whether gatekeepers can see one another's signs of approval, whether their evidence is correlated, whether a single designated blocker can be consulted in isolation, whether the identity of the consulted veto-holder can be randomised — rather than the mere number of people who must consent.
