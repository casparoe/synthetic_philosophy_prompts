I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on truth and paradox. Sixteen students, one 110-minute session, a room with movable tables, a whiteboard, and index cards. The students have already met, in secondary-source form, the three standard families of response to the Liar: the hierarchical response that splits languages into levels, the three-valued fixed-point response that lets some sentences fall into a gap, and the paraconsistent response that tolerates a sentence being both true and false. Nobody has seen the technical constructions, and most of them still think the Liar is a riddle with a clever answer they have not been told yet.

What I want is a single session-length activity, runnable with cards and a printed score sheet, that makes them feel two things in their hands rather than hear them from me. First: that no matter how they respond to the paradoxical card, there is a mechanical recipe for producing a new card that their response cannot handle, provided their response was stated as a general rule in the same vocabulary the cards use. Second: that the cost of each family of response is not that it is wrong, but that it is paid for somewhere specific — in what they can say, in what they can infer, or in how much of the board they are forced to leave unmarked.

Some constraints on the design.

No lecture before the activity, and no naming of the three families until the debrief. Students should encounter the problem as a scoring problem: each small group receives a shared board of cards, each card bearing a sentence that attributes a truth status to itself or to another card by position, and must return a complete assignment of statuses to every card. The board should include not only the self-referential card that says of itself that it is not true, but also a card that says of itself only that it is true, and short cycles of mutual attribution, so that the tempting move of declaring the problem card meaningless has to be applied consistently to ungrounded cards that look harmless.

The scoring rule should be published in advance and should encode disquotation directly: a group pays a fixed penalty for every card whose assigned status contradicts what that card says about another card's assigned status, and a larger penalty for any card left blank. On top of that I want a transparency tax: any status beyond true and false that a group invents for itself costs a fixed amount per card per round, so that enriching the stock of available statuses is a budgetary decision rather than a free move. There should also be a bonus that rewards marking as many cards as possible true or false, so that the trivial move of declaring everything gappy is available but expensive. Groups should be able to see, in their own running totals, that they are choosing between paying the contradiction penalty and paying the tax.

The second half should be a repair-and-revenge loop. When a group proposes a repair, it must state it as a general rule governing all cards, using only the vocabulary fixed at the start of the session. I then write a fresh card, on the spot, that exploits that rule, and the group must re-score the board with the new card on it. Please specify the fixed vocabulary, the exact form in which a repair must be submitted so that the exploiting card can be generated mechanically rather than by my ingenuity, and how many rounds the loop runs. Give me the fallback cards to have ready for the two or three repairs that students predictably propose, including the one where they try to climb a level and the one where they try to ban self-reference outright.

Then I want a debrief, twenty-five minutes or so, that maps specific moments in the game onto specific features of the three families: which penalty encodes the principle the hierarchical response gives up, which feature of the board the gap response cannot recover, which inference the paraconsistent response has to block elsewhere in order to contain the contradiction. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the group that refuses to state a general rule and just wants to tear up the offending card, the group that reasons in a language richer than the cards are allowed to use, the argument about whether my own scoring instructions are themselves a card on the board. Say how to handle each, and say which of those derailments is worth encouraging rather than preventing.

Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot show. My worry is that a well-run version of this game teaches the wrong lesson: that there is no coherent response at all, when the truth is that coherent responses exist and each one gives something up. The game also cannot reproduce the transfinite character of the hierarchy, the existence proof for a fixed point, or the fact that giving up a classical inference rule has costs across the rest of logic and mathematics that a card game cannot display. Tell me which of these I have to supply myself in the following lecture, and where the game actively misleads.

Give me the materials list, the rules and scoring scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief script, the failure modes, and that closing note on limits.
