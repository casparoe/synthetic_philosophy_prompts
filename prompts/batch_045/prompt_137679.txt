Hi — sorry, this got long. Back in September my brother-in-law and I had a £50 bet on one sentence: "if the RMT strike goes ahead on the 4th, the conference gets moved online". The strike was called off on the 2nd, the conference went ahead in person, and now he says the bet is void — no fact of the matter, nobody pays. I said the conditional came out true and he owes me. I'm not asking you to settle the bet, but the row made me actually read about this stuff, and I've ended up with an argument I don't trust because it seems to prove far too much.

A = strike goes ahead, C = conference moves online.

(1) Ramsey test / Adams thing: my credence in "if A then C" should just be the credence I'd have in C if I supposed A, i.e. cr(if A then C) = P(C|A).
(2) That's meant to be a norm on rational credence, not a quirk of what I happen to know today. So it holds for any credence function I could rationally have, including the one I'd have after learning anything: P(if A then C | E) = P(C | A and E) for any E. Equivalently: supposing and learning commute.
(3) "If A then C" is a proposition, something I can believe, bet on, embed, and put a single number on — and that number obeys the normal calculus, in particular P(if A then C) = P(if A then C | C)·P(C) + P(if A then C | not-C)·P(not-C).
(4) Apply (2) with E = C: P(if A then C | C) = P(C | A and C) = 1.
(5) Apply (2) with E = not-C: P(if A then C | not-C) = P(C | A and not-C) = 0.
(6) So by (3), (4), (5): P(if A then C) = P(C).
(7) So by (1): P(C|A) = P(C), for every A and C — nothing is ever evidence for anything.
(8) That's absurd, so (1), (2) or (3) has to go.

Here's why I'm stuck rather than just nodding along.

First: I tried to save it by banning self-embedding — a typed language where a conditional can contain other conditionals only of a strictly lower type, so you never get "if (if A then C) then ..." and the whole hierarchy is well-founded. Then I looked at steps (4) and (5) and realised they never embed anything. All the work is done by applying (1) to conditionalised credence functions. So either I've misread how these proofs go, or my typed fix is worthless for this version of the argument. Which?

Second, the local version, which is the one that actually gets under my skin. I now know not-A — the strike was called off. So P(A) = 0 for me, and by (1) my credence in the thing I bet on is P(C|A), which is undefined. I'm supposed to have no degree of belief at all. But I plainly do: I'd still say, fairly confidently, that if the strike had gone ahead the organisers would have moved it online. And whatever that attitude is, it survived learning not-A. So (1) looks like it fails in exactly the case my bet lives in — and it fails there even if I never get to steps (4)–(7).

Third, the bit where I think it proves too much: the pattern of (3)–(6) doesn't look like it cares about conditionals specifically. Take any sentence S whose rational credence is fixed by a rule cr(S) = f(P(C)) with f increasing, f(1) = 1, f(0) = 0 — "probably C" is the obvious example. Run the same expansion and I get f(P(C)) = P(C) for every value, i.e. the operator collapses into the identity and "probably" does nothing at all. That seems like more than I bargained for, though I might be messing the general case up.
