I teach a weekly graduate reading group on chance, symmetry, and ignorance. The students have had one course in probability but no measure theory, and I am assembling the two-page handout for the week we spend on Bertrand's chord paradox. I do not want another exposition of the three classic constructions. I want a decision tree the students can walk themselves: something that starts from a commitment everyone in the room already shares and ends at a named position about what the paradox shows and how, if at all, it is to be resolved.

Design constraints:

1. Entry point. Begin from the claim that the three standard constructions — two points chosen independently on the circumference; a radius chosen and then a point chosen along it; a point chosen in the disk as the chord's midpoint — are all defensible readings of the instruction to draw a chord at random, and that they yield three different numbers. The tree must not presuppose any resolution of the dispute, and no branch may be phrased in a way that tips the student toward one.

2. Nodes. Six to eight branch nodes, each phrased as a question in the second person that a student can answer straight off, without knowing which position it leads to, and without needing more than a one-line integral. Among them I want at least:

   a. A node that separates those who think the problem as stated has a unique correct answer from those who think its answer is under-determined or empty — and that then separates the latter into those who think the gap should be closed by more mathematics (a symmetry or invariance requirement) and those who think it should be closed by a fact about the world (the mechanism that would actually generate the chord).

   b. A node forcing a choice about which quantity indifference is to be applied to, with the options including at least the angular position of an endpoint, the distance of the midpoint from the centre, the position of the midpoint within the disk, and the length of the chord itself.

   c. A node about what kind of invariance is being demanded: invariance under rotations of the circle alone, versus invariance under the full group that preserves the problem's description, including translating and rescaling the circle. Add a further node about whether enlarging the group still further narrows the answer or destroys it, if you think that question belongs on the tree.

   d. A node that makes the student commit on a case where the circle's symmetries are absent: the same question asked about chords of a square, of an ellipse, or of an irregular convex region. Use whichever best separates your leaves, and say in a line why you chose it.

   No node may name a position or a person. Names appear only at the leaves.

3. Leaves. Eight to ten of them, each carrying (i) the label you would actually use in a seminar, (ii) one sentence stating the position, and (iii) one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its holder has to swallow. These must be reachable at minimum: the under-specification view, on which the problem as stated is ill-posed and there is no determinate answer; the permissive view, on which many answers are rationally permissible and the paradox shows only that the word random was incomplete; the chance-setup view, on which the number is fixed by whatever physical process would generate the chord and is therefore a matter of fact to be specified or measured; the invariance or maximum-entropy line of reply associated with Jaynes; a view that arrives at the same number by a different route, via the rigid-motion-invariant measure on the space of lines in the plane; and a view that treats indifference over a privileged parameterisation as basic.

4. Coincidences and blind spots — the part I most want done carefully, because it is where I expect to be challenged in the session.

   Several leaves that look different give the same number for the circle. Say explicitly which pairs your tree cannot separate by anything a student says about the circle alone, and supply the one further question or variant case that would separate them. Candidates: the non-circular case from 2(d); a case in which a mean chord length has been measured and can be imposed as a constraint; or the parallel case of a point chosen at random on a sphere and asked how near the equator it lies, where indifference over latitude and indifference over surface area come apart.

   Then mark the cells of the logical space that are available but unoccupied. The one I keep returning to is indifference over the chord's own length, which yields a number none of the three standard constructions gives: I want your diagnosis of why it has no defenders — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Note any other empty cells you find, with the same diagnosis for each.

5. Two walk-throughs. Run the tree twice: once for a student who consistently answers in the direction of no determinate answer, once for a student who consistently answers in the direction of symmetry should decide it. Show the paths and the leaves reached. If both students land on the same leaf, the tree is not sorting and you must repair it rather than note the failure.

6. Key. Close with a short answer key mapping each of the three classic constructions, plus the invariance argument, onto a leaf, and flag any construction that lands on a leaf whose awkward commitment its actual defenders would reject.

Wherever a node turns on a distinction that would normally be stated in the vocabulary of groups, invariant measures, or maximum entropy, gloss it in one sentence for students who lack that vocabulary, and say plainly which distinctions you had to simplify away to make the tree usable in a fifty-minute session. Keep the whole handout to two sides of A4 when printed.
