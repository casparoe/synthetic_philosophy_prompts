Two pragmatisms, one fixed point.

The two classical pragmatist theories of truth are usually presented as variants of a single view, differing in temperament rather than in structure: Peirce is said to give the austere, community-of-inquirers version, James the generous, individual-believer version. I want to argue that once one looks at a certain class of propositions the two views come apart structurally, and that the natural way of repairing Peirce's version turns it into something close to James's. The argument works by replacing a limit conception of truth with a fixed-point conception.

Start with the contrast as usually drawn. Peirce identifies truth, roughly, with the opinion on which inquiry would settle if it were pushed indefinitely far, by improving methods, by a community that never gives up; reality is what such inquiry converges on, and the convergence is supposed to be explained by the independence of the object rather than constituted by the agreement. James identifies truth, roughly, with what it is good to believe: a true belief is one we can assimilate to the stock of beliefs we already hold, act on successfully, and corroborate, where the goodness in question is not momentary convenience but expediency on the whole and in the long run. Both are then charged with the same verificationist consequence, and both are usually defended against that charge in the same spirit: there are propositions about which no inquiry could ever converge because the evidence has been destroyed, and there are propositions about which no belief is more expedient than suspension because nothing hangs on them. I think this shared difficulty is real and I set it aside, because it does not tell us which of the two views to prefer. What I want is a case that separates them.

The separating case is what I call an endogenous proposition: one whose truth value depends, partly or wholly, on what inquirers come to believe about it. Whether a bank survives the week depends on whether enough of its depositors believe it will; whether a currency holds its value, whether a norm is in force, whether a consensus forms around a hypothesis, whether a candidate wins, whether a spelling survives in a language — in each case the object of inquiry is partly constituted by the beliefs of the inquirers about it. Now apply the Peircean definition to such a proposition. It singles out the opinion fated to be ultimately agreed to, but for an endogenous proposition there is no guarantee that there is exactly one such opinion. There may be two or more endpoints, each stable under indefinitely prolonged inquiry from some starting point: if depositors begin confident, inquiry finds the bank solvent and it survives; if they begin alarmed, inquiry finds it insolvent and it fails. Both endpoints are genuinely fated relative to their starting points, and neither is arrived at through accident, prejudice, or methodological error. So the definition does not pick out a truth value. It picks out a set, or it picks out nothing, and the failure is not the familiar one about destroyed evidence. It is a failure of uniqueness, and it appears precisely where the object of inquiry fails to be independent of the inquiry into it.

James's definition, I think, survives this case, and survives it for a structural reason. Because his notion is indexed to a believer with a situation and a stock of beliefs, multiplicity is not a defect in it but a feature of it: the belief that is good in the way of belief for these depositors now is the true one for them, and the rival belief can be true for a differently situated community without any contradiction being generated. Peirce cannot say the corresponding thing, because part of what he is trying to capture with the ideal limit is the independence of the object.

The obvious repair is to reformulate the Peircean view as a fixed-point view rather than a limit view: the truth about an endogenous proposition is the endpoint that ideally conducted inquiry actually reaches from the starting point we are in fact at. This restores uniqueness, at the cost of making truth for this class of propositions path-dependent and therefore historical: had our community begun somewhere else, the proposition would have had the other truth value. And that cost is, I claim, the collapse in question. A path-dependent truth is a truth made true partly by where the believing community happens to begin, which is very close to the Jamesian thought that truth is a property of beliefs relative to the practical situation of believers. Peirce's view can be repaired only by surrendering the feature that was supposed to distinguish it from James's.

If that is right, the interesting question becomes a boundary question. Endogenous propositions want the fixed-point treatment and inquiry-independent propositions want the limit treatment, so the pragmatist needs a principle that sorts propositions into the two classes. But the sorting is itself difficult in a way that I do not think has been noticed: whether a given proposition is endogenous can itself be endogenous. Whether a currency's value depends on what people believe about it depends in part on what people believe about whether it does; whether a norm is in force is itself sensitive to beliefs about whether it is in force. So the boundary cannot be drawn from outside the practice, and the pragmatist faces a second-order version of the very problem the first-order reformulation was introduced to solve.
