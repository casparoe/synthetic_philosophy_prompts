I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am working on a long piece about what the recent generation of machine-learned weather models has done to the way working scientists talk about prediction. Before I draft it, I need to get straight on a distinction that my sources keep sliding past and that I suspect philosophers have a settled view about.

My working vocabulary is that of numerical weather prediction and climate modelling, and I am fluent in it: state vectors and initial conditions, model error versus initial-condition error, data assimilation, parametrisation of unresolved scales, ensemble forecasting and spread, the exponential growth of small errors and the characteristic time on which they double, verification against a baseline, the lead time beyond which a forecast is no better than climatology, and the difference between forecasting the state of the system at a later time and forecasting the statistics of the system over a long run. I have no formal training in philosophy and I do not want a survey of positions, authors, or books.

What I would like you to translate into that vocabulary, one item at a time, is the following cluster of claims:

(i) The thesis that a complete specification of the state of the world at one time, together with the laws, fixes the state at every other time.
(ii) The standard reply that this thesis does not deliver prediction, because sensitive dependence means that any finite-precision grip on the state becomes useless after a while.
(iii) The mirror claim that reliable prediction of statistics is fully compatible with the underlying system not being deterministic.
(iv) The claim that sensitive dependence, or indeterminacy at small scales, makes room for human agency in a way that the thesis in (i) did not.
(v) The claim that a system can be deterministic and yet such that the only way to know its later states is to let it run, or to simulate it step by step, because there is no shortcut that gets you there faster.

Translate each as faithfully as you can using only my vocabulary, and please do not quietly substitute my framework's assumptions for the claims and then present the result as a translation. Where you had to bend a claim to make it fit, say so at the point where you bend it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually need. Which of these have exact counterparts in forecasting vocabulary? Which go through only after distortion? Which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary in either direction? For anything in the last group, give me your view on whether that is a defect in how forecasters talk or a sign that the original claim was doing less work than its wording suggests. I am especially interested in the phrase in principle, as in predictable in principle but not in practice: I cannot find a counterpart for it anywhere in my vocabulary, and I cannot tell whether that is a gap in my side of the conversation or an indication that the phrase has no content.

One more thing, and it is the reason I am asking now rather than last year. The piece I am writing concerns weather models trained directly on decades of reanalysis data, which at some lead times now match or beat physics-based models, and which have no explicitly represented state variables and no equations of motion: they are fitted functions that take one observational field to the next. Please run the residue report a second time with this in mind. Does the distinction between determinism and predictability survive contact with a situation in which the best available model of a system is not a state-plus-law model at all? If a forecaster a decade from now has only models of that kind, is determinism still something she could be asserting or denying about the weather, or has the claim lost its subject matter? Tell me whether you take that to be an interesting finding about determinism or merely a finding about what forecasting has become.

Please keep the whole answer under a thousand words, and give me no reading suggestions, references, or names to look up.
