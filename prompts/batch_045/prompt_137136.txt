My friend Marcos keeps this absurd spreadsheet he calls the meme court — every week someone in our reading group nominates some bit of culture and he rules, yes or no, on whether it's "an actual Darwinian meme" or just "stuff people happen to do." Two years in and the infuriating thing is that he refuses to state his criterion; he says the verdicts come first and the rule gets read off at the end, so all I've got is the track record. As best I remember it: the old "forward this to ten friends or something horrible will happen" chain email, yes; a shampoo jingle that lodges in your head, yes; a rumour that travels because it makes people angry, yes; a ritual where no two practitioners give the same reason and all the stated reasons are false, which persists anyway because it synchronises the group, yes; a fashion that spreads while it's rare and dies the moment it's common, yes; a sound change of the kind where a consonant cluster simplifies and keeps turning up independently in languages with no common ancestor, because each new generation of learners rebuilds it the same way out of articulatory laziness, no; the antiseptic-routine-before-surgery thing, no — his reason being that every hospital's version is slightly different and they each reinvent it from the same mortality data rather than copying a canonical form, so it's people deciding, not selection; and, the one he's firmest on, a keyboard layout that survives purely because everyone else already uses it and retraining is expensive, no. (He's also said he doesn't want to argue about the word "meme" itself, which I think is a cop-out but fine.)

So this week I made up the most annoying case I could and put it to him: a boat-building tradition across a handful of neighbouring islands where nobody could ever copy a whole boat — you learn by sitting beside a relative for years, guessing, trying, keeping what floats and doesn't capsize when the wind reverses — and where over two centuries the boats in the region got measurably better at exactly that, without anyone ever being able to say what the design is. Then a wrinkle: the design that's currently winning isn't the best-performing one, it's the one that can be taught in a single season, and the better design is now held by one eighty-year-old man who won't take apprentices. Two smaller ones in the same batch: the keyboard-layout-style standard where the arrangement is copied perfectly faithfully but never improves and never varies, and a monastery's rule that the copyist must reproduce the text letter for letter and must never correct it. I've got twenty quid on him saying no to the first, and I genuinely can't tell about the other two.

What I want isn't who's right about memes, it's what rule he's secretly running. As far as I can see at least two fit his whole record equally well — something like "you need a real lineage of faithfully copied units, not something each person or hospital regenerates from the same environment," and something like "you need ongoing heritable variation plus differential spread plus cumulative adaptation, i.e. it has to actually build up over generations" — and they come apart on my cases. So: lay out the two or three candidate rules that fit his verdicts so far, say what each one predicts about the boats (including the teachable-but-worse variant), the standard, and the scribal rule, then give me your best guess at his verdicts with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others. And then tell me the single cleanest extra case to put to him next week, phrased as concretely as the ones above, that would force the rules apart no matter which one he's on. Please don't give me a reading list or tell me which famous theorist he sounds most like — I want the rules stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself to a case he hasn't seen, and I'll go find the names later.
