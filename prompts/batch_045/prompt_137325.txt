Please adopt the standpoint of a professional philosopher — a metaphysician and philosopher of language who has actually worked through the unity-of-the-proposition literature rather than only its slogans. Think carefully and in two stages: first a short unstructured brainstorming pass of ten or so lines in which you list every candidate diagnosis you take seriously, including the ones you will reject, and then your considered diagnosis.

I write novels, and I'm blocked on a scene in which a retired analytic philosopher tries to explain to her daughter why a sentence is not merely a heap of the words in it. Reading my way in, I was struck by how little has moved in over a century on Bradley's regress: the Fregean answer that concepts are unsaturated and so no further unifier is needed; the Russellian answer that a relation must be taken as actually relating rather than as one more term, and that the regress this generates is benign; the Tractarian answer that since a proposition is itself a fact, the demand that it be assembled out of parts is confused; recent structured-proposition theories that let the instantiation or satisfaction relation do the unifying work; and the Bradleyan conclusion that the regress is vicious and that unity must be prior to the parts it unifies.

My working hypothesis, which I'd rather you stress-tested than flattered, is that the debate is stalled because unity is doing three different jobs at once — distinguishing a structured entity from the mere set or heap of its constituents, grounding the truth-conditional difference between Brutus-loves-Caesar and Caesar-loves-Beatrice's reversed counterpart, and explaining why a state of affairs is one thing rather than many — with each camp answering a different one of these while believing it has answered all three. A related suspicion: the residual dispute is really about which of relation, structure, or fact may be taken as primitive, and so bottoms out in a clash of starting points nobody can argue for. To sharpen it, imagine a possible language whose sentences are unordered multisets of words, so that no notation marks which word plays which role: if the problem survives there, it is the composition problem; if it dissolves, the real issue was only about order and asymmetry.

Is the deadlock verbal, is it a hidden question about grounding or composition doing the work, are the sides answering different questions under one name, or is it bedrock? Ground your diagnosis in what each position actually claims about the regress itself, tell me which of my three jobs unifies with which, and say what would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction like the ordered-multiset one, a change of question — or whether I should conclude that the honest ending for my scene is the impasse, and why no progress should be expected.
