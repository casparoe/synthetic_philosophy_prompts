I have been going back and forth on an argument that I think proves too much, and I want to know where to cut.

(1) No moral difference without a difference in what is brought about. Whether an agent's contribution to an outcome takes the form of an action or of an omission is not itself a difference in what is brought about.
(2) Take matched pairs: same agent, same victim, same motive, same manner and time of death, same effects on everyone else. One member kills, the other lets die, and that is the only difference. So nothing outcome-relevant differs.
(3) Therefore killing and letting die are morally on a par: equally permissible where permissible, equally serious wrongs where not.
(4) What a third party may do to avert a harm is settled by how serious the harm is and how grave the wrong would be, not by whether the agent's contribution is an action or an omission. Call this mirroring: whatever means are necessary and proportionate against a killer are permissible against someone who lets the same victim die in the same circumstances.
(5) Dana can save Victor's life by pressing a button and declines for a trivial reason. By (3), her refusal is as serious a wrong as killing Victor for an equally trivial reason would be.
(6) The only way to avert Victor's death is to compel Dana's body: seize her and force her finger onto the button. The stakes are Victor's life and no lesser means will work, so mirroring says this is permissible — the same intervention, on the same stakes, that would be permissible against someone about to shoot Victor.
(7) But we think we may shoot the man about to shoot Victor, and we do not think we may drag Dana to the switch. So something in (1) through (6) is false.

I want to keep (3): it is what settles the doctor's case — withdrawing a ventilator at the patient's request versus a lethal injection at the same request — and I think the verdict there is right. I will even grant that Dana's refusal is a grave wrong. What I cannot accept is (6). Which step should I reject, and why did it look safe? The thing I keep circling is this: whatever rescues (3) seems to have to distinguish blocking an agent's agency from conscripting it, and that distinction looks like the doing/allowing distinction turning up again inside the theory of defensive force, where I did not expect it to do any work. Is it the same distinction under another name, or a different one? And if I reject (4), am I committed to there being grave wrongs that no one may prevent by force? If instead I reject (2), what exactly was missing from the matched pair?
