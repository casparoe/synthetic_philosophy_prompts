I sit on the review board for a proposed small-scale field trial of a stratospheric aerosol injection experiment, and in our meetings the case for blocking it keeps being put as an insurance argument: precaution is just buying insurance — the premium is the benefit we forgo by not acting, the payout is the catastrophe avoided, the payout is enormous and the premium modest, so it would be irrational not to buy. I want that analogy audited rather than either accepted or waved away.

Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the insurer, the insured, the premium, the policy period, the payout, the actuarial table — and then where the mapping fails. I care most about four places:

1. Insurance prices risk with tolerably stable frequencies over many roughly independent policies, while precaution is invoked precisely under ambiguity, where there is a spread of defensible models and no agreed probability. Deprived of an actuarial table, does the argument have to fall back on something like maximin, or on an "act only if every credible model permits acting" rule — and does that rule amount to anything once you notice how much work is done by who counts as credible?

2. Insurance works because independent risks pool; the hazards precaution is deployed against are usually unique, correlated and unrepeated — one atmosphere, one biosphere — with no diversification and possibly no bound on the loss. Is the word "insurance" then just a reassuring label on a one-shot gamble against an open-ended downside?

3. Premium and payout land on different people: those who pay for precaution are often identifiable and present, those spared by it statistical and future, while those who gain from the risky activity are present and organised. Ordinary insurance has one insured party who both pays and collects. How much of the argument's normative force rests on that coincidence, and what survives once it is removed?

4. Precaution has a cost structure insurance lacks: delay can destroy the option to learn, and can be irreversible in its own right — the activity blocked today may be the one that would have averted the very catastrophe we were insuring against. Can the analogy even represent insuring against the premium?

The load-bearing question: does the insurance framing support the conclusion it is used for in our meetings — that a large enough possible harm justifies blocking, full stop — or does that conclusion come from importing exactly the features precautionary cases lack: known probabilities, pooling, bounded liability, a single payer who is also the payee? If it cannot carry that, say what a better analogy would be, and whether some reading of it does support a weaker conclusion, for instance a bias toward measures that preserve future options rather than toward inaction.
