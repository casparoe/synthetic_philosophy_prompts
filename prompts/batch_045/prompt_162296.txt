I'm in a small reading group that works through decision-theory puzzles of a particular kind — the kind where an extremely reliable predictor asks you for money — and instead of tracking which theory I'm rooting for, I've been trying to write down what I actually think, case by case, and then see whether any single rule produces those verdicts. In every case below, the predictor (call it Omega) is perfectly truthful and its predictions are as reliable as stated: if it says it predicted your answer, it did, and if it says it would have paid out in a branch that didn't happen, it would have. Assume money is linearly valuable to me over these amounts and that I know all the rules in advance.

(1) Omega flips a coin that comes up tails one time in a thousand. It comes up tails. Omega asks me for $100 and explains: had it come up heads, it would have handed me $10,000, but only if it had predicted that I would hand over the $100 in the tails branch. Nothing I do now can affect the $10,000 — that branch is gone and I know it. My verdict: pay.

(2) Same shape, no prediction machinery: a driver in the desert rescues stranded motorists only when he is confident they will pay him $1,000 once they're in town, and he is never wrong about this. I'm in town. My verdict: pay.

(3) No prediction at all: I have a medical condition that would cost me $100,000 if it became known; a blackmailer who knows about it demands $1,000 and will reveal it if I refuse. My verdict: pay.

(4) I have the same condition with probability one in a thousand. A blackmailer will reveal it unless I pay $1,000, and he is known to send his letter if and only if exactly one of the following is true: I have the condition, or I would pay upon receiving the letter. The letter arrives. My verdict: don't pay. A friend says this is unstable: given that I've settled on not paying, the arrival of the letter is proof that I have the condition, and then paying saves me $100,000 for $1,000, so the reasoning that led me to refuse seems to hand me a reason to pay the moment I act on it. I don't find that persuasive, but I can't say what's wrong with it. (I have the same verdict — one-box even when I can see the million sitting in the transparent box — if that helps you see the shape of what I believe.)

(5) Exactly like (1), except that what plays the role of the coin is a fact that could not have been otherwise: the ten-thousandth binary digit of pi, which I have verified on my laptop and which is, let us say, 1. Omega tells me that had the digit been 0 it would have handed me $10,000 iff it predicted I would pay, and asks for $100. My verdict: I lean toward not paying, and I can't articulate why this differs from (1) — my friend insists the two are the same problem with the same numbers and the same answer.

(6) Again like (1), but Omega doesn't simulate me as I am now: it simulates the person I was at fifteen, before I had ever thought about any of this, and uses that person's answer. My verdict: pay, though shakily. If instead Omega simulates a stranger who merely resembles me on the psychological variables it can measure, my verdict flips to don't pay. I don't know what the difference is supposed to turn on.

(7) I also find that my willingness in (1) fades as Omega's accuracy drops: at 90% reliable I still pay, at 55% I have no idea, and I'd like a rule that tells me where the threshold is rather than my guessing.

My own tentative formulation, which I don't trust: before you learn which situation you're in, choose the policy with the highest expected payoff, and then follow that policy in the situation you turn out to be in, disregarding the information that only tells you which situation that is. Please test this against the cases, tell me where it breaks or where it is indeterminate (I suspect (6) is where it runs out of instructions), repair it or replace it with something precise, state what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — I care much more about keeping (1) and (4) than about keeping (5). Give me at least one new case your principle settles that isn't on my list, and give me the accuracy threshold for (1) as a formula if there is one.

One case I have deliberately not made up my mind about, and which I want you to decide using whatever principle you end up defending: it is (4) again, with the single change that the envelope also contains a certified lab report proving that I have the condition. Conclude your reply with three labelled lines and nothing after them: a line beginning Overturned: naming which of my verdicts by number your principle cannot accommodate, or none; a line beginning Principle: stating the principle in one sentence; and a final line beginning Verdict: followed by either pay or don't pay for the lab-report case.
