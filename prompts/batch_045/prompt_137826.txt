Hi — practical problem that I've slowly realised is a philosophy problem, which is annoying, because my entire formal training here is one survey course I mostly slept though, and my partner is the philosopher in the house, so right now these questions get settled by whoever talks louder over dinner. That is not a scalable way to run a data platform.

Six months ago I inherited ownership of the shared clinical ontology for a network of about eleven hospitals — it's the layer that says what kinds of things exist in our data: patients, encounters, problems, devices, caregivers, and so on. Every couple of weeks somebody proposes a new entity type, and whatever I let in gets coded, billed against, audited, and reused as an inclusion criterion in studies for years; unwinding one costs months and makes us look foolish, so the decisions are small, frequent, and stickier than they look. They go to a data governance group that meets monthly, is mostly clinicians and one compliance person, and will rubber-stamp whatever I put in front of them, which means the actual epistemic decision is mine and I'd like it to be defensible rather than vibes. What makes it hard is that most of what people want to reify is inferred rather than recorded. I've started sorting proposals into three buckets: things that simply weren't collected but could have been; things that could only be measured by a measurement that changes the thing measured (asking someone about their adherence changes their adherence; auditing a ward changes the ward); and things that are inaccessible in principle for us — defined by a counterfactual, or by what something was like for the person living it. And my sense is that the philosophical literature on identifying what there is when some of the information can't be had — the observable/unobservable distinction, the Ramsey-sentence move where you define a term by the role it plays and then existentially generalise it away, the Newman-style worry that a finite relational structure is trivially satisfied by lots of different choices of objects so structure alone never pins down what the objects are, the proxy-function / inscrutability stuff, whatever in that neighbourhood is actually load-bearing — says something sharp about when you've identified a kind of thing versus when you've just carved out a convenient slot in a model and started talking as if the slot had an occupant.

So: write me a decision procedure for admitting a proposed entity type. I want something an analyst with no philosophy can run in under an hour and then defend at a governance meeting. Concretely: the ordered questions; for each, what actually counts as a sufficient answer given that we mostly have claims about recorded stuff and not the world; where the procedure hard-stops with a no; where it hands off to a human because the question is about what we owe patients or what we're willing to assert, not about facts; and — importantly — the tests that carry the philosophical weight, including some version of the shuffle test (if I swapped this candidate's identity conditions for a rival candidate's and nothing downstream changed, I haven't identified an entity, only a slot), a portability check across sites with different collection habits, and a "two different ontologies, same predictions" check, plus a rule for when the honest answer is "add a derived score or a property, not a kind." Be explicit about where the procedure misfires. Then run it on three live cases: medication non-adherence inferred from pharmacy refill gaps (we already reified a version of this years ago and it partly turned out to be tracking our own reminder system rather than the patient); caregiver burden, inferred from free-text notes and partly a matter of how things seem to the caregiver; and 'avoidable admission', which leadership badly wants as a per-admission entity because a regulator is about to levy penalties on it, and which I suspect the procedure will refuse me — if it does, argue that refusal properly and give me something I can offer leadership instead, because " philosophy says no" will not survive that meeting. Please don't pad it with reading suggestions or references, I can't chase them and they won't help me on Thursday. Lead with the checklist itself, then the three applications, then a short paragraph on what the procedure can't do; no more than 1,200 words total, and define any jargon in parentheses the first time you use it.
