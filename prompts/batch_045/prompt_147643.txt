I am working through a chapter on the mind–body problem in a recent popular book on consciousness, and I want to know how much of its treatment of functionalism to trust before I rely on it in an essay I am writing. The passage that matters runs as follows:

"Functionalism says that what makes a mental state the state it is has nothing to do with what it is made of and everything to do with the job it does. Pain is not a particular pattern of neural firing; pain is anything that occupies the pain role — being produced by damage, producing wincing and the desire for it to stop, interacting with belief and attention in the ways pain does. The mind is to the brain as the program is to the computer: the same software will run on any hardware, and so the same mind could in principle run on silicon. This is the doctrine of multiple realizability, and it is why identity theories of mind fail. A type-identity theorist has to say that pain simply is a certain brain state, but since the pain role could be filled by Martian chemistry or by a chip, pain cannot be identical with any brain state. It follows that there is no such thing as the neural correlate of a conscious experience, and that hunting for one with a scanner is a category mistake: the best you can find is the hardware that happens to be running the program.

The doctrine also carries a commitment that is harder to swallow. Consider the bridge cases, in which surgeons rerouted a damaged thalamocortical pathway through a silicon device that reproduces the original pathway's responses exactly, input for input, over every condition the patients could actually encounter in ordinary life. The patients behave normally, discriminate normally, and report no change in how anything feels. Yet the device does nothing remotely like what the tissue did: it is a lookup table, a vast recorded list of input–output pairs, with no plausibly mental-looking internal structure. Functionalism is nevertheless stuck saying that the experience is exactly as it was, because only the causal role matters and the role is unchanged. A list is not an experience, so functionalism is refuted. The same point can be made with inverted qualia: if two systems have the same functional organization, functionalism must say their experience is the same, and so it must deny that your red could be my green — which is not something anyone can seriously deny."

Two things make me hesitant. I have a memory that functionalism is officially a thesis about causal roles rather than about computation, so the software/hardware formulation may be the book's adornment rather than the view's, and the corollary about neural correlates seems stronger than anything multiple realizability could deliver. On the other hand, the bridge cases do impress me: if functionalism really is committed to saying that a lookup table feels just like the tissue it replaced, that strikes me as a genuine cost, and I would like to know whether the commitment is real or whether the book has quietly rigged the case. I have never seen these cases cited anywhere else, so please treat the description as a thought experiment to be granted as stated, including the fact that outside the recorded range the device's outputs diverge wildly from the tissue's — a divergence that only shows up under laboratory conditions the patients never meet.

Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a defender of functionalism would accept, what is merely a popular simplification, and what is actually wrong or misattributed. In particular I want to know:

- Whether the lookup-table verdict is genuinely entailed, or whether it depends on how the functional role is individuated — role versus realizer, relations to other internal states, and whether the role is specified over actual or merely ecological conditions or over the full counterfactual range — and whether the divergence outside the recorded range is enough to say the role was never preserved.
- Whether multiple realizability refutes type identity outright or is better understood as one contested argument among several, and whether it has the corollary about neural correlates that the book draws from it.
- What the inverted-qualia argument actually establishes, and which options a functionalist has besides the flat denial the book attributes to them.
- Which version of functionalism the bridge case damages, if it damages any: a computational or machine-functionalist version, a causal-role version, or the identity of mental states with functional states — and where, if anywhere, the description is doing question-begging work rather than argumentative work.

Do not spend the answer introducing functionalism from scratch, and do not settle any point merely by saying that philosophers disagree; say which of the book's moves a defender has a real answer to and which they would have to concede. Finish with a single last line of exactly one of these forms: Verdict: FAIR, Verdict: MOSTLY FAIR, Verdict: MIXED, or Verdict: UNFAIR.
