I'm in a small reading group on social ontology, and a friend who is considerably more hard-nosed than I am has written an argument on the whiteboard that I cannot find the exit from. I went in a social constructionist about race and I would like to come out one, but her argument seems to show that constructionism about race collapses into something like eliminativism, and that in doing so it pulls the rug out from under exactly the claims about racial inequality that anti-racist politics and disparate-impact law depend on. She adds, cheerfully, that the same reasoning runs on gender with the same result, and that it leaves money untouched only because money is the one case where we are already happy to say yes, we made it, and no, nothing alarming follows — which makes me suspect the argument proves far too much, since I don't see why the economics of money should be in better shape than the sociology of race if her premises are right.

Here is the argument as she wrote it out. (1) A kind K is socially constructed if and only if whether a given thing falls under K depends, at least in part, on human attitudes, practices, or conventions rather than on the thing's intrinsic, non-attitudinal features alone. (2) Money is the paradigm case: a banknote's being legal tender consists in its standing in a system of collective acceptance, and money is both perfectly real and uncontroversially constructed. (3) Folk racial categories shift with time and place: different societies draw the colour line in different places, official census categories get redesigned, and people get reclassified when they migrate. (4) No set of intrinsic biological features, genetic or morphological, is shared by all and only the members of a folk racial category; human genetic variation is clinal, and greater within the folk groups than between them. (5) So race is socially constructed, in the very sense in which money is. (6) If whether someone falls under K depends on attitudes, then there is no attitude-independent fact of the matter about who falls under K: K-membership claims are made true by us, and are true only relative to a local classificatory scheme. (7) So there is no scheme-independent fact about anyone's race. (8) If there is no scheme-independent fact about anyone's race, then the claims that carry all the weight in anti-racist politics and in disparate-impact law — that Black defendants receive longer sentences for comparable offences, that women of colour are paid less for comparable work, that this lending model disadvantages a protected group — are not false but empty; strictly speaking they have no determinate subject matter, or at best they report what local classifiers happen to believe. (9) But those claims are plainly true, and they are the backbone of everything I care about here. (10) So at least one of (1) through (8) is false, and the only step in that range with any give in it is (5): race is not socially constructed after all.

The conclusion I cannot accept is (10), and I also think (8) is a misdescription of what those inequality claims are doing. But I can't locate the break. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I want the diagnosis, an explanation of why the step looked fine when it was written on the board, and an honest price for each of the alternatives — including the possibility that the flaw is in the final move from one of them is false to it is (5), and including the possibility that the argument is valid and (5) really is what I have to surrender. I'd also like to know what each of the standard positions — biological realism about race, eliminativism, constitutive or ameliorative constructionism, response-dependence accounts, and quietism about the whole debate — has to pay to reject the step you've picked. If you think the argument does prove too much rather than proving exactly one thing, say what the generalising version looks like and where it breaks.

One idea of my own, in case it is useful: I've been playing with a distinction between live and inertial attitude-dependence. Money's dependence on acceptance is live — if acceptance collapses, the money goes with it, more or less immediately. Racial membership, I want to say, is inertial: constituted by attitudes and practices, yes, but with those attitudes now sedimented into property records, court records, training data for facial recognition, and housing patterns, so that the kind is attitude-dependent in origin and in constitution while being entirely unresponsive to what anybody now believes, and therefore perfectly able to figure in objective explanations of who gets what. Does that distinction do the work I need it to do, or does it only block a step the argument wasn't actually making? If it fails, I'd like a better formulation rather than just a dismissal.
