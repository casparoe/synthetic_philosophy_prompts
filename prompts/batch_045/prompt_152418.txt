Hey, I'm stuck on something and want to think it out with someone.

Take the standard paradox of fiction: you cry over a character in a novel, you know perfectly well she never existed and that nothing is at stake for anybody, and yet the grief seems real. So either emotions dont actually need a belief in the existence of their object, or you're not really feeling what you think you're feeling. The two usual moves are (a) the thought-ish view: merely imagining or entertaining the proposition is enough for a genuine emotion, no existence belief required; and (b) the make-believe / quasi-emotion view: you don't literally grieve, you quasi-grieve, or you imagine yourself grieving, so there was never a real emotion to explain.

Now the case I actually care about — lets call it the paradox of the rehearsal. A hospital offers patients an immersive simulated film of their own upcoming surgery. Actors play the patient and the surgical team, every stage is staged, nothing is happening to anyone while the film runs. Patients report genuine terror, and it seems obviously reasonable: they really do believe that something very much like this will happen to them, and that it carries real risks. So here you've got full-blooded belief about the future attached to a depiction you know is fictional.

Compare how (a) and (b) handle the rehearsal case. Which of the two does better on it, and what does that tell us about whether the original paradox was ever really about existence-belief in the first place? Also: does the rehearsal case feel paradox-y in the same way, or is the wierdness coming from somewhere else entirely, like the timing of the emotion, or what exactly the emotion is pointed at? Please actually pick a winner and defend it, and say where the losing view goes wrong — don't just tell me both are live options.
