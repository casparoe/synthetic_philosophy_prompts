I'm writing an essay for my ethical theory module on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I think I have a case that settles the question, so I would like to check whether the argument is as decisive as it looks before I build the whole essay around it. Consider a rescue case in which the two candidates for rescue are relevantly identical in every respect: the same distance away, the same prospects if helped, no prior relationship or special obligation to either, and no downstream or third-party consideration that breaks the symmetry. Whatever reason I have to save the first, I therefore have a reason of exactly the same strength to save the second, and since the case is invariant under swapping the two people, it seems no weighing of reasons could come out in favour of either one. So my two obligations are both undefeated: whichever person I save, I fail to do what I ought to with respect to the other, and I am left with inescapable moral failure, which is just what a genuine dilemma is supposed to involve. That seems to leave the anti-dilemma theorist only two options, neither attractive: deny that reasons ever really tie in such cases, or claim that I discharge my duty by satisfying some merely disjunctive obligation to save one or the other. But obligations surely attach to particular people, and a coin toss that arbitrarily selects who is left to drown does not look like the sort of thing that could make an action right. So my questions are these. Is the symmetry argument decisive, and if not, which premise would a defender of the no-dilemma view actually reject, given that the tie itself seems hard to deny? And what follows practically: in such a case, is tossing a coin the rational thing to do, or is there a better way of choosing that does not simply treat the two people as interchangeable units? Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and please lead with your verdict on whether the argument works before going into the details.
