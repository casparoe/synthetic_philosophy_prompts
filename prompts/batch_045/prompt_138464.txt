ok so last night my friend Marcus came over for dinner with his girlfriend Jen and the two of them spent like three hours going at it about the principle of alternative possibilities - you know, you're morally responsible for what you did only if you could have done otherwise - and I tried to referee and honestly just made it worse. Ive been turning it over since and I keep flipping sides. Roughly how it went:

(1) Marcus opens with the classic Frankfurt-style case: Jones decides to kill Smith for his own reasons, and Black is waiting in the wings with a device that would have forced Jones to kill if Jones had shown any sign of wavering, but Jones never wavers and Black never does anything. Marcus says Jones is plainly morally responsible, and equally plainly he couldn't have done otherwise, so the principle is false and the debate should move on.

(2) Jen says that's been answered: for Black's device to work it has to trigger on something, and whatever it triggers on is either a reliable prior sign of the decision Jones is about to make or it isn't. If it's a sign that deterministically fixes the decision, the case just assumes determinism in the actual sequence, so it's useless against someone who thinks determinism is exactly what kills responsibility - it begs the question. If the sign is indeterministic, then Jones could have produced the other sign and then decided otherwise, so he did have an alternative possibility after all. Either horn, no result.

(3) Marcus says fine, he'll fix the case: no sign at all. Black hooks the device to a radioactive atom. If the atom decays in the next hour, the device zaps Jones's brain and he kills Smith immediately, involuntarily, no deliberation. If it doesn't decay, the device sits there doing nothing and Jones deliberates on his own and kills Smith for his own reasons. Actual sequence: no decay, Jones kills on his own. The trigger has nothing to do with Jones's decision, so there's no sign to run the dilemma on, and the randomness means the case doesn't smuggle in determinism either. But there's no scenario in which Jones fails to kill Smith. Responsible, and couldn't have done otherwise.

(4) Jen says look at the branch where the atom decays. There, Jones's body kills Smith but Jones doesn't act at all - it's something that happens to him. So it's not true that he had no alternative: the alternative left over is that he performs no action at all and a killing happens through him. She says this is the general problem with all of these cases: you can make the alternatives as thin as you like but you can't get rid of them entirely, so the real question was never whether he could have done otherwise full stop, but which alternatives are the ones that matter.

(5) Marcus says sure, and that's exactly why the principle has to be given up or rewritten: an alternative like my body gets hijacked by a brain zap isn't the kind of thing that could be what makes me responsible when I act normally, so the principle has to become something like responsible only if you could have done otherwise in a way that was genuinely up to you - and once you add that clause, the bit about alternative possibilities isn't doing any work anymore, it's a control or source condition in a costume.

(6) Jen says that's her whole point and it cuts deeper than Marcus wants: in the random-trigger version he's free to stipulate that Jones's deliberation is perfectly ordinary and deterministic, and Black's machine makes no causal difference to anything. But then take the machine away entirely and nothing about Jones changes - he's responsible or not because of how he actually deliberated, not because of Black. So the Frankfurt case wasnt really an argument at all, it's a way of restating the compatibilist's own account of the actual sequence. An incompatibilist who thinks determinism in the actual sequence is what destroys responsibility will just deny that Jones is responsible in the no-decay branch and walk away, and nothing in the case touches him.

(7) Marcus says that's fine, it's still a real result: it shows the fight was never about alternative possibilities, it's about whether the actual sequence has the right kind of source. The case is only ever meant to kill the principle, not to prove compatibilism, and the incompatibilist now owes an independent argument - about manipulation, or luck, or whatever - instead of leaning on the thought that no alternatives means no responsibility.

(8) Then I stupidly asked whether any of this depends on Jones not knowing about the device, and they both jumped on it and I lost the thread. Jen said if Jones knows he's rigged to kill either way, his situation is completely different and the intuition that he's responsible gets shaky, which she thinks shows responsibility can't just be a fact about the actual sequence. Marcus said that's confused, that whether Jones is responsible depends on how he actually decided and not on what he believes about some machine that never fired.
