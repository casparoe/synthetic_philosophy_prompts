I spent a career in experimental biology, most of it running multi-environment variety trials, and a good deal of the last decade of it arguing about genotype-by-environment interaction with colleagues who wanted a single number for a trait. In retirement I have been reading philosophy of biology, and I find I have acquired a position I would like stress-tested before I inflict it on a seminar group. Call it the Inertia Thesis. It has three parts. First: a heritability coefficient is a ratio of variance components in a specified population, over a specified range of environments and times; it is a description of a state of affairs, not a causal quantity about individuals and not a property of the trait as such. Second: it therefore licenses no inference about the malleability of the population mean under an environmental intervention. A trait can sit near the top of the usual range of heritability estimates and still be almost entirely removable by a dietary change; the phenylketonuria case is the one everyone reaches for, and I think it is representative rather than exotic. Third: it licenses no inference about the cause of a difference between two population means, even when combined with knowledge that the populations differ in allele frequencies.

I do concede what anyone who has worked in breeding knows, that heritability has an honoured predictive use in the breeder's equation, where it forecasts the response to selection in the next generation. My claim is meant to cover interventions that alter what the developing organism encounters or is given, not interventions that alter which genomes are represented. I am not certain that distinction is principled rather than gerrymandered, and that is part of what I want examined.

So: what would count against this? I would like the defeaters ranked, fatal first, and I would like you to separate what would genuinely move a defender of the thesis from what is merely the standard critic's repertoire. Some candidates I can see, which you should feel free to replace or supplement.

One. Suppose an intervention does not introduce a new environment but removes an existing source of environmental variance, by making some condition uniform that was previously heterogeneous. It seems to follow that the residual phenotypic variance should approach the additive genetic variance, so that a high heritability does predict a modest reduction in variance under this kind of intervention. Is that a real prediction the thesis makes and risks? Does it touch my second claim, which is about means, or only a claim about variances that I never made? And does anything like it generalise to the mean?

Two. If heritability earns its keep for selection responses, is carving out environmental interventions ad hoc? What would a principled line look like: interventions on the distribution of genotypes versus interventions on the mapping from genotype to phenotype? Note that some purely environmental changes do alter the mapping, by equalising a nutrient that previously varied, and some selection regimes look like environmental manipulations from the organism's point of view. So where exactly does the line fall, if anywhere?

Three. Suppose molecular methods eventually account for nearly all of the additive variance that twin and pedigree designs estimate for a trait. Does heritability then become informative about intervention, because we would know the variants and their mechanisms? My own answer is no: knowing which variants are involved does not tell you what a dietary or schooling change will do, any more than knowing the wiring tells you what happens when you change the input. But I want the strongest version of the contrary argument.

Four. Measured heritability of cognitive test scores tends to rise across the life course. Does that tell against the stability of these coefficients, or does it show them tracking the accumulation of gene-environment correlation, in which case it supports the thesis? I am genuinely unsure.

Five. Is there any formal result connecting a within-population variance decomposition to the geometry of reaction norms across environments? I suspect not, and if not, I worry my thesis is close to a tautology, true because of how the quantities are defined rather than because of how the world is. Tell me whether that would be a defect in a thesis of this kind or merely a sign that its proper use is to block invalid inferences rather than to license new ones.

Here is what I would find most decisive, and I would like you to say whether it is a fair test. Give a competent scientist heritability estimates for, say, two hundred traits across thirty populations, and nothing else: no marker data, no reaction norms, no prior intervention results. Ask them to predict, for a specified intervention never yet tried, the sign and rough magnitude of the shift in the population mean. If there is a subclass of traits and interventions for which someone so equipped would beat chance, the strong form of my thesis is false. Is there such a subclass, selection aside? If instead the honest answer is that heritability alone never suffices, I want to know whether that insulation is a vice. Because intervention outcomes are only known after the intervention, the thesis may be protected by construction, and I would like your view on whether a claim of this sort can be substantive at all if no possible observation could refute it.

I would also weigh a softer pattern: a systematic tendency, across many traits and many attempted interventions, for high-heritability traits to resist environmental manipulation even where a mechanism was known. That would move me more than any single case, and I would like you to assess whether such a tendency would be evidence or artifact, given that we may preferentially attempt interventions on traits we already suspect are plastic, and that measurement reliability varies with the trait.

Finally, and this matters to me: if the thesis as I have stated it is confused, or collapses into something trivial, or the task as posed cannot be done well, say so plainly rather than complying politely. Please also mark how confident you are in each of your main claims, flag where you are extrapolating beyond anything established, and indicate which of them depend on contested assumptions, such as the equal-environments assumption in twin designs. I would rather have a short, hedged, correct answer than an encyclopaedic one.
