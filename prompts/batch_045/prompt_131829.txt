I'm putting together a handout for the discussion sections of a first-year seminar on free will and responsibility. The last two sessions are the neuroscience unit, and every year the conversation collapses into the same two slogans — the students who say the brain decided first and therefore no one is ever responsible, and the students who say that motor preparation is not a decision — and then stalls, because both camps are answering different questions and nobody notices. The seminar ends with a unit on criminal responsibility and on everyday practices of blame, so I want the instrument to terminate somewhere that has consequences for those practices rather than in the abstract.

What I'd like you to write is a decision tree that takes a student from a single starting commitment — I accept the basic Libet result: in self-paced movement tasks, averaged motor-cortex activity (the readiness potential) begins several hundred milliseconds before the muscle moves, whereas participants' reports of when they first felt the urge or formed the intention to move come only a fraction of a second before the movement — and leads them, by way of a sequence of branch questions, to a named position about what that result does and does not show. To keep the tree from being a list of the usual suspects that a student could just recognise, I want the seminar to grant three further pieces of background as stipulated, regardless of what the actual literature says: first, that a large preregistered replication finds that participants who are instructed to cancel a prepared movement at the last possible moment succeed most of the time and report no antecedent urge on the trials where they succeed; second, that a decoding study predicts which of two buttons will be pressed with modest but reliable accuracy starting about a second and a half before the reported decision, but only when the participant's attention is on the task display, with the effect disappearing when participants are told they may veto the movement at will; and third, that the reliability of subjects' clock-based reports of when they became aware of an urge is contested, with some evidence that such reports are partly reconstructed after the fact. Please treat all three as true for the purposes of the handout.

The discipline I want you to impose on yourself is this: build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Work out which questions genuinely separate the positions, which combinations of answers the questions permit, and only then locate the actual views in that space — including, as leaves, at minimum: the view that conscious intentions are causally inert byproducts of neural activity; the view that the result is an artefact of unreliable introspective timing and tells us nothing; the view that it shows only that motor preparation is unconscious while decisions about whether to act remain unthreatened (the veto view belongs here); a compatibilist view on which unconscious neural antecedents are simply how a reasons-responsive agent's decision is implemented, so nothing about responsibility is in question; and the view that the experiment addresses the wrong question altogether, because it concerns the timing of urges in an arbitrary laboratory task rather than deliberation about what to do. Give each leaf a label — the standard one if there is one, your own descriptive label if there isn't — plus one sentence on what that position implies for holding people responsible and one sentence on the commitment its holder has to bite the bullet on. I need eight to ten branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Every node should be phrased as a question a first-year student can answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to, and wherever possible it should be a concrete case rather than a slogan: a case in which a monitoring device would have forced the movement but never had to, a case in which a student's movement is initiated by direct stimulation of motor cortex with no reported urge, a case in which the same neural antecedents are followed by an action the agent has reasons against, and so on. Explicitly no node may ask the student to self-classify as compatibilist or incompatibilist, or to say whether free will exists. Finally, note any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than pretending the questions divide them, and for any combination of answers that no one occupies, say in a clause whether that combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. No citations or reading list needed — I want the structure, not the bibliography.
