The episode is the GameStop squeeze of January 2021. Assume the rough shape as I remember it, and don't spend time on the chronology: a structurally declining retailer's stock was extremely heavily shorted — short interest was reported at the time to exceed the number of shares outstanding — retail traders organized publicly on a message-board forum to buy and, crucially, to hold; the price went from well under twenty dollars to several hundred dollars within a couple of weeks and then fell back within weeks; at least one prominent short-taking hedge fund took losses in the billions and was recapitalized by outside money; several brokerages restricted customers' ability to buy the stock for a day or two at the peak.

Here is what I want. First, the mapping. State precisely which premise of the standard no-trade result fails here, and how. I can see three candidate diagnoses and I want you to adjudicate rather than list them:

(a) Genuine heterogeneous priors: both sides were looking at the same public information — the same reported short interest, the same financials — and simply drew different conclusions from it, in which case the common-prior assumption fails and the result doesn't apply at all.
(b) Structural endogeneity: the traders were not betting on an allocation whose payoffs are independent of their own trading. Buying forced short covering, covering pushed the price up, and the higher price triggered further margin pressure and further covering. If the asset's payoff to a position depends on the volume and direction of others' trades in a loop like this, then the "no speculative gain to be had" premise fails in a way that is not informational at all, and no amount of common priors or shared information would restore the conclusion.
(c) A non-instrumental motive: at least part of the buy side was not maximizing expected financial return — members were paying for participation in a collective action, for the expressive value of inflicting losses on a visible opponent, for entertainment. On this reading the failing premise is not about beliefs or information but about the assumption that traders are maximizing expected return on the asset.

My own guess is that the interesting content of the case is (b), and that (a) and (c) are both real but secondary — but I'd like you to tell me if that's wrong.

Second, a suggestion I want you to take up or reject explicitly. The overconfidence-and-resale-option style of model says that heterogeneous beliefs plus constraints on short positions produce prices above any trader's own valuation, because the buyer is buying the option to resell to a future optimist. In this episode the constraints that mattered seem to be on the other side: borrowing was scarce and expensive, and the shorts' binding constraint was financing and margin, not conviction. It looks to me like a mirror image — the short side was forced to unwind at prices below their own valuation of the position, and the unwinding itself pushed the price further away from their view. Try to formalize that inversion inside a belief-heterogeneity framework, and tell me whether it is genuinely the same mechanism seen from the other side or a different one that the models don't have room for.
