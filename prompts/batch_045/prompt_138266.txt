QUESTION:
In the Dennett–Caruso exchange, Caruso defends free will skepticism and argues that, because we lack the kind of free will needed for basic desert, retributive punishment is unjustified. He proposes replacing it with a public health-quarantine model that is forward-looking, non-punitive, and focused on prevention, rehabilitation, and social justice. Dennett replies as a compatibilist: he claims that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism, that the sort of desert we actually need is not Caruso's metaphysically loaded basic desert but a naturalized, practice-dependent notion, and that our responsibility practices are justified by the role they play in sustaining a cooperative moral community. Evaluate the following objection to Dennett: if Dennett's desert is just a product of social practices justified by their forward-looking benefits, then his view cannot really justify retributive punishment; it collapses into the same consequentialist rationale as Caruso's quarantine model and thus fails to preserve the backward-looking desert that retributivists care about. So Dennett is either a surreptitious retributivist who needs basic desert after all, or a consequentialist who should just adopt Caruso's model. How might Dennett respond? Then apply both views to the neural tumble case described in section. Would either theorist treat the agent as an appropriate target of blame, punishment, or quarantine? What does your application show about the real crux between Dennett and Caruso?

STUDENT ANSWER:
The objection is forceful but ultimately misses what Dennett is doing. Dennett is a compatibilist, and he agrees with Caruso that there is no libertarian free will or ultimate responsibility. However, he argues that this does not threaten the everyday notion of moral responsibility. On Dennett's view, an agent is morally responsible when her actions flow from her reasons-responsive mechanisms and she is sensitive to moral reasons over the long run. This gives us a real, naturalized form of desert: it is not basic desert in Caruso's sense of an intrinsic, consequences-independent deservingness, but Dennett argues that Caruso's basic desert is a philosophers' fiction that nobody should want. Once we see that, the objection's dilemma is false. Dennett can say that the desert we preserve is enough to underwrite backward-looking practices of blame and even retributive punishment, because to hold someone responsible just is to treat them as a member of the moral community who deserves censure for what they did, not merely as a carrier of disease to be managed. Thus Dennett does secure basic desert in the only sense that matters—an agent deserves blame simply in virtue of having performed the action, apart from any forward-looking benefits—and this is exactly what separates his view from Caruso's consequentialist quarantine model. Dennett is therefore best read as a retributivist who has naturalized the basis of desert.

Turning to the neural tumble case, the two views diverge in interesting ways. For Caruso, the case is a vivid illustration of the general skeptical claim that our actions are always the product of causes beyond our control. Because the agent lacked free will and basic desert, she is not morally responsible at all. But Caruso's public health-quarantine model is purely forward-looking: since the neural tumble was a one-off event and the agent has no history of violence and is not a continuing threat, there is no public health justification for quarantining her. She should be released, perhaps with some monitoring, but not punished or blamed.

Dennett, by contrast, has more nuanced resources. He has famously defended a two-stage model of decision making in which a consideration-generator produces a series of undetermined considerations, and the agent's reasoned selection among them constitutes autonomous action. The neural tumble in the hypothetical is just an especially vivid instance of an indeterministic consideration-generator; the fact that the action was indeterministic is precisely the kind of thing Dennett's model embraces. Since the agent's assault issued from an indeterministic process, Dennett would say that she exercised the relevant kind of autonomous control and is morally responsible. She would thus be an appropriate target of blame and, on Dennett's naturalized retributivism, of punishment proportionate to the wrong.

The crux between the two, then, is not really about determinism versus indeterminism. It is about whether the naturalized, practice-dependent desert Dennett defends is robust enough to do the work retributivists need, or whether, as Caruso argues, once you give up basic desert you are left with only forward-looking quarantine. The neural tumble case helps us see this: Dennett's commitment to the two-stage model forces him to treat even sub-personal indeterministic tumbles as expressions of autonomy, which shows his view is more hospitable to free will than Caruso's skepticism.
