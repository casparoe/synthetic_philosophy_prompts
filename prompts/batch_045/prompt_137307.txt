I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and a few of us at work have been running a lunch reading group on whether the sciences actually carve nature at its joints or whether they just impose convenient filing systems on a continuous world. I came in with a prejudice from my own field: a good abstraction is one where you can swap any implementation in behind the interface without touching anything downstream, and one that keeps earning its keep in contexts you never designed it for. So I've been scoring candidate kinds with two tests and a three-way grading scheme. The swap test: take a setting where one member of the category is doing explanatory or causal work, substitute a different member, and check whether the behaviour of the surrounding system is preserved without further adjustment. The borrow test: the category licenses inferences to properties it wasn't defined by, and keeps licensing them after you change context — new lab, new environment, different substrate, intervention rather than mere observation. My grades: a joint passes both robustly; a working category passes them locally but breaks under context shift or under intervention; a gerrymander passes neither except by luck. What pushed me toward this was an incident at work: we classified endpoints as "slow" using a round-number threshold on p95 latency, and that classification predicted incidents and guided fixes for about two years, then we migrated regions and it predicted nothing at all. Something real had happened during those two years and then stopped happening, and I want a vocabulary for that.

Here is where I actually land, and I'd like to know whether my two tests plus three grades add up to one principle or whether I'm smuggling in something extra that I haven't made explicit. Yes, paradigm case: the element gold, i.e. the stuff with atomic number 79. Yes: water as H₂O, though I don't know what to say about heavy water. No: jade, which turned out to be two unrelated minerals traded under one name. No: fish, which I rejected because the group includes salmon and lungfish but excludes the land vertebrates descended from the same ancestors — though I suspect this verdict is doing more work than my two tests can justify. Yes-ish, and this is the one that won't leave me alone: the succulent "cactus body plan" — water-storing stem, spines, that odd night-time carbon fixation — arrived at independently by cacti in the Americas and by euphorbs in Africa; my gut says this is a genuine kind of organismal solution, and I cannot square that with rejecting fish for not being a clade. No, but I don't want to say "nothing": hypertension, blood pressure above the usual clinical cutoffs (roughly 140 over 90 as I remember them), where the boundary sits on a smooth continuum and has been moved by committees. No, and more firmly: major depressive disorder as a diagnostic category, which looks to me like several different things with different causes and different treatment responses sharing one label. Yes, but not naturally: money — it licenses endless successful inference and yet seems to hold together only because we agree that it does. No stable verdict at all: planet, ever since Pluto.

What I want from you is the principle, not a survey of positions. Assume I can't look anything up, so don't cite or attribute anything and don't give me a reading list: argue from the cases and from whatever distinctions you think I'm missing. And please don't settle this with "it depends what you mean by natural kind" — if the meaning of the phrase is doing the work, tell me which meaning and why that one.
