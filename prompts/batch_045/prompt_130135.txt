I'm a PhD student helping to run a university panel series on historical injustice, and at last week's session I asked a guest — a political theorist who writes on reparations — about a case I use in my own teaching, and got shut down with a line I can't get out of my head: "guilt can't be inherited." The case: in 1889 the Kingdom of Ardan seized roughly a third of the arable land held under customary tenure by a highland minority, the Sarn, abolished their tenure system by statute, and reallocated the parcels to settlers; the taking was legal under Ardan's own law at the time and was enforced by troops. Ardan became a republic in 1921 and later a federation, with continuous legal personality and no break in its land registry, so present titles still trace back to the 1889 grants. No compensation was ever paid. Today the Sarn districts sit at roughly 45 percent of national median household wealth, with about a seven-year gap in life expectancy and a third of the seats they'd be entitled to on population. Meanwhile roughly 40 percent of current citizens descend from people who arrived after 1950, including refugees from a different persecution; some of the most valuable former Sarn land is held by families who bought it in good faith at market prices in the 1960s from the original grantees; and a handful of Sarn families themselves participated in the administrative allocation. The guest's one-liner was doing three jobs at once: it was meant to show that no present-day citizen owes anything, that the state may not apologize in the name of its citizens, and that it would be wrong to feel or express guilt about 1889 at all.

What I want is a disambiguation of that sentence rather than a verdict on the case. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses "guilt can't be inherited" could be asserting, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical, and for each: state it as explicitly as possible, say whether it is a near-conceptual truth, a substantive but defensible claim, a live and contested one, or straightforwardly false, and then say which of the three jobs it would actually do if it were true. I'd like the menu organized along several axes at once, since I suspect the work is being done by unmarked shifts: what kind of thing "guilt" denotes (criminal guilt requiring act and intent; moral blameworthiness; answerability or liability to repair; an undischarged debt; a stigma or status; the emotion; political liability incurred by membership in a polity that acted; something like metaphysical or solidarity guilt), what "inherit" means as a transmission mechanism (biological descent, membership in a collective that persists, holding the proceeds of a wrong, occupying a successor institution's role, or simply being the current bearer of an unsettled claim), and whose guilt is in question (individual persons, the state as a corporate agent with continuous legal personality, or the nation as a historical community). It would help me a lot if you mapped the readings onto the fourfold taxonomy of guilt that's standard in this literature — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — since my sense is that the sentence is obviously true on one of those, plausible on two, and either false or not even truth-apt on the fourth, which would explain why it feels like it settles everything while settling much less than it appears to. For each reading, please also mark how much of the gap between "no one now alive did this" and "nothing is now owed" it would actually bridge.

Two further things I need, because the easy readings are the ones I already know. First, I've tried to make my own distinctions and would like you to test them rather than replace them: call the "clean-hands-by-descent" principle the claim that a person's liabilities cannot outlive their own agency, the "undischarged-debt" principle the claim that an obligation to repair survives the death of both parties whenever the obligation was never satisfied and the wrongdoer's estate persists in some successor form, and the "tainted-asset" principle the claim that whoever presently holds an asset whose title is defective owes something to the person with the better claim, whatever they did or intended. Tell me which of my three principles each menu reading is closest to, where they come apart, and which of them the guest would have to deny to get his three conclusions. Second, please run the readings through five stress cases and tell me which readings survive all five without embarrassment: (i) the late arrival, whose ancestors came in 1955 and who personally gained nothing; (ii) the good-faith purchaser of 1962 who paid market price with no knowledge of the 1889 taking; (iii) the dissolved predecessor, where the wrongdoing state was extinguished and its successor formally disclaimed responsibility while keeping the registry and collecting the taxes; (iv) the mirror-image case, where the Sarn descendants of victims have claims — since if guilt cannot be transmitted across generations, I want to know why claims can be, and what asymmetry (if any) does that work; and (v) the apology case, where a head of state apologizes and a citizen says she was not consulted and is not guilty. I'd especially like to know which reading a person repeating this slogan at a panel is most likely to mean, and whether that reading is the one strong enough for the use it was being put to. A numbered menu with a one-line verdict per entry is what I'll take into the next session, so please keep it structured rather than essayistic.
