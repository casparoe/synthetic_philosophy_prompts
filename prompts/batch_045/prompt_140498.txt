I direct training for a free, anonymous advice service that runs a phone line and a web chat and fields roughly two hundred questions a week, and my sense from reading the logs is that the overwhelming majority are not requests for information at all but requests for a moral verdict: should I tell my sister what I saw her boyfriend do, am I obliged to offer a kidney to my father, is it disloyal to move to another city when my mother is ill, do I have to report a colleague I watched take money from the till. Our volunteers are eighteen to twenty-five, they receive two days of training and then take shifts unsupervised, the exchanges are one-shot and usually over within ten minutes, and nothing follows the questioner afterwards. Over the past year I have grown uneasy about what we are actually doing when we answer. My volunteers come from a narrow slice of one country's middle class, our logged verdicts visibly cluster by volunteer age and background, and when someone writes back it is often to say, in effect, that they had already decided and were hoping we would tell them they were permitted. I keep circling the philosophical question of whether a person can come to hold a moral view on the strength of someone else's say-so, and of what is missing when they do, but I need that question turned into something my volunteers can run rather than something to discuss in training. My current draft triage has three outputs: give a verdict, decline to give one and work through the reasons with the questioner instead, or redirect to someone better placed, meaning either a professional with the relevant non-moral knowledge or a person with the relevant lived experience. I am fairly sure this is too coarse, and that a volunteer with two days of training cannot tell which output is right without some prior questions.

What I want is an ordered procedure: the questions in sequence, and for each one an account of what would count as a sufficient answer in the ninety seconds a volunteer actually has before the chat goes quiet, where the procedure stops with no verdict at all, and where it hands the decision to a person because the matter is one of values or of particulars the volunteer cannot see. It has to be short enough to hold in the head after two days of training, phrased so that a supervisor reading the log the next morning can tell whether it was followed, and it has to cope with at least these recurring shapes of question: the questioner who has already decided and wants permission; the questioner in distress who simply wants to be told what to do; the questioner who asks what some third party should do, or whether a third party wronged them, rather than what they themselves should do; the question whose morally relevant facts are unknown to the volunteer and would have to be guessed at; the question on which the volunteer is, unusually, the person in the room with the relevant experience; and the question that nearly everyone the volunteer can imagine would answer the same way, as against the one on which thoughtful people plainly disagree. Please then run the finished procedure in full on three of our real shapes so I can watch it work: a twenty-year-old asking whether she must report a coworker she saw taking money; a man in his thirties asking whether he is a bad person for refusing to donate a kidney to the father who was violent to him; and a sixteen-year-old writing at two in the morning, apparently frightened, to ask whether it would be wrong to call the police on her mother. My own instinct is that volunteers should almost never issue verdicts and should nearly always do the slower work of reasons instead, so if your procedure tells me that in some of these cases they must issue one anyway, I want that argued rather than asserted, and I would like at least one worked case where the procedure returns an answer I will dislike, so that I can find out whether I am prepared to trust it.

Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do. I take it that no sequence of questions will make a volunteer's verdict correct if their underlying judgment is bad, and that a one-shot exchange can hand someone a sentence without handing them whatever it is that makes the sentence true, but tell me what else is missing and mark the point at which this stops being a procedure and becomes a judgment call that no checklist can remove. Close by saying, in a sentence or two, what would most change your mind about the procedure you have given me, whether that is an objection to routinizing this kind of thing at all or some pattern in our logs that would convince you it is doing harm, and by flagging the failure mode you would expect to surface only after a few months of use rather than in the first week. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to someone who asked you for a favour, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and aim for something in the neighbourhood of fourteen hundred words that a tired volunteer could read once and still remember. Do not cite any literature and do not point me toward further reading; I have no library access and would not follow it up, so I need the distinctions stated in your own words and in terms my volunteers could act on.
