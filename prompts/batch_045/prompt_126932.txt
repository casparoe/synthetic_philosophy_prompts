I sit on the ethics consultation service at a large hospice, and over the past year I have drifted, rather to my own surprise, into something close to Augustine's absolutism about lying. I am now writing a short piece for our chaplaincy newsletter arguing that the old practice of withholding a terminal prognosis at the family's request should be abandoned, and a colleague who read a draft says I have simply rebuilt that practice with extra steps. Before I send the piece anywhere I want to know whether he is right, and I would rather have my own commitments audited than be told what Augustine held. So here is the set as I actually hold it, and I honestly don't know where the fault line runs.

(1) Lying is absolutely prohibited, with no exception clause even for saving an innocent life, and I want to defend that in secular terms rather than by appeal to a divine command. (2) The wrong-making feature is what the lie does to the liar: it is a willing that someone else hold a false belief while you yourself hold the true one, a kind of self-division, and so the wrong survives when the hearer benefits, or consents, or is never deceived at all — I do not locate the wrong in harm to the hearer or in damage to social trust. (3) A lie is asserting what you take to be false with the will that the hearer take it to be true; so a confidently asserted falsehood the speaker believes is not a lie, though it may be culpable negligence. (4) Deliberately letting a false belief form is a different thing from lying and is permitted: when a dying patient asks me straight out whether he is dying, I will not say he isn't and I will not say he is either — I ask what he is afraid of and leave the information with the family — and I think the team is entitled to discuss his scan results in the corridor, in jargon, within his hearing, precisely because he won't parse it. (5) But I also want to say it is wrong to tell a falsehood nobody in the room will believe — the family insisting that a nurse tell a dying man he'll be going home in a few days — and I want to call that one a lie too, even though no one is at risk of being deceived by it. (6) Jokes, fiction and stage magic are not lies at all, since nobody is meant to walk away believing the false content; a theologian friend tells me Augustine eventually abandoned the lenient treatment of joking falsehoods he had earlier toyed with, I haven't checked, and I'm unsure whether it matters, but flag it if it does. (7) The lie told to ruin a life and the lie told to spare one are not equally serious, and the difference between them is not merely a difference in their consequences. (8) A murderer at the door has no claim on the truth from me, and I don't rest the prohibition on anyone's rights — yet it is still wrong to lie to him.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the set and tell me which commitments are load-bearing and which pairs genuinely pull against each other, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad; for each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me, and be explicit if a repair is cheap in theory but expensive at the bedside, since that is where I have to act on Monday morning. Then tell me which pairs only look tense and dissolve once the distinction is drawn properly — I suspect at least one of these is fine and I'd like to know which — and finally which single revision would clean up the most, what kind of absolutism I would be left holding afterwards, and whether what remains is still recognizably the thing that made me give up therapeutic privilege in the first place.
