My younger brother is on one of these community treatment orders — he lives at home, but he's required to show up for his depot injection, and if he misses two appointments the psychiatrist can have him picked up and taken back to the ward for up to 72 hours while they reassess him. They call it a "recall." I've been to two of the tribunal hearings with him and I can't get strait in my head what the system thinks it's actually doing.

Three things bug me.

First, the discharge checklist they work through has a box for "insight." He's supposed to agree that he has an illness and that the medication helps. When he says he doesn't — that the voices started after he lost his job, and the meds just make him sleep fourteen hours a day and he can't hold a conversation — they write that down as lack of insight, and lack of insight is one of the reasons they give for not discharging him. So agreeing gets you out and disagreeing is itself evidence you aren't ready. That feels rigged, but I can't say exactly why.

Second, the same hospital let a man in the next bay refuse an amputation that would have saved his life, and everyone said that was sad but his right. My brother isn't allowed to refuse the injection. Nobody has formally told him he lacks capacity — it's more that his refusal gets treated as part of the thing being treated. Meanwhile he can still vote and sign a tenancy.

Third, when he was first admitted he signed a form saying that if he got unwell again he wanted to be treated even if he was refusing at the time. The tribunal keeps citing it. He says the person who signed it was on so much medication he couldn't feel his own face, and that guy isn't him.

A friend sent me something by this Szasz guy saying mental illness isn't an illness at all and psychiatry is just coercion with a stethoscope, and I half belive it and half think it's tinfoil. What I actually want to know is: what does a practice like this one presuppose — about what counts as a disease, about what makes someone's refusal a real refusal rather than a symptom, about when you're allowed to override one? Does it secretly presuppose something like Szasz's view, or the opposite of it? Is the "insight" bit load-bearing, or could the practice run fine without it? And what would concretely have to change if they dropped it — say they replaced "does he accept the diagnosis" with something like "can he repeat back the hospital's reasons for treating him and give reasons of his own against"? Would that be a real difference or just a rebrand? Same question for the risk-to-others stuff: does the practice need any theory of illness at all, or could it all be justified on danger grounds and drop the medical language?

Please be clear about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on it and which are live fights — don't hand me Szasz as the consensus, and don't write him off as a crank if people are still arguing with him.
