I have come to accept epistemicism about vagueness, in roughly its standard form. That is: I hold that every vague predicate in ordinary empirical vocabulary — heap, bald, red, tall — divides its domain by a perfectly sharp boundary; that bivalence and classical logic hold for borderline statements, so that a borderline heap-claim is determinately true or determinately false; that the penumbra is wholly a matter of ignorance, explained by a margin-for-error (roughly, safety-style) condition on knowledge rather than by indeterminacy in meaning or in the world; and that higher-order vagueness is accommodated by iterating the same explanation, so that the boundary of definitely-F is also sharp, also unknown, and located further out. I also take on the usual externalist backing: the boundary is fixed by facts about our use, even when nothing in our use singles out one candidate boundary as the correct one.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed this position actually is. Please skip the objections I already know I am supposed to dismiss — bare tolerance intuitions, the incredulous stare in its naive form, the fact that rivals exist. I want the considerations a committed epistemicist should concede count against the view, and those ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

The lines of pressure I care about most:

First, the absence of inquiry. Ordinary ignorance comes with the apparatus of inquiry: evidence one could gather, experts one could defer to, a way of being corrected, a way of getting it badly wrong. For borderline applications of heap there appears to be none of this. Suppose a consultant or measuring device classified borderline cases reliably and built a track record; would its testimony give us knowledge of the cutoff, and if not, in what sense is our state ignorance rather than something else? Likewise, suppose speakers jointly stipulate a cutoff and thereafter feel no sense of having discovered anything: is that a change of meaning, a stipulation that failed to track the prior fact, or evidence that the posit was idle?

Second, the burden on semantic externalism. In the paradigm externalist cases the world supplies a joint — a natural kind, a causal chain — that makes the referent eligible. Here the underlying dimension is continuous and our practice is tolerant, so what makes one sharp boundary the right one rather than its near neighbours? Is this a genuine explanatory gap, or a demand the view can meet in principle?

Third, the empirical profile of speaker judgment. Disagreement about cutoffs does not look like error scattered around a hidden true value: it shifts with comparison class, with stakes, with order of presentation, and speakers do not treat one another as having made a mistake. Does that count against the view, can it be absorbed as context-sensitivity, and if absorbed, has epistemicism collapsed into contextualism or supervaluationism in every respect that matters?

Fourth, costs elsewhere: does the margin-for-error machinery overgeneralize into skepticism about perceptual, memorial, or legal knowledge? Does iterating definitely generate an infinite hierarchy of sharp boundaries whose tidy nesting looks like a coincidence, and does the view predict anything — anything at all — that its rivals do not?

Finally, an honest verdict on insulation. My worry is that the central claim has been qualified into something no possible observation could touch, and that the unknowability of the boundary follows from the machinery rather than being discovered. If that is right, say so, and say whether it is fatal for a view of this kind or merely the price of holding bivalence. For what it is worth, the consideration that moves me most personally is the first one — the missing apparatus of inquiry — so tell me whether that is a real defeater or the incredulous stare in better clothing. Argue from the structure of the position; I do not need a survey of who has said what, and I have no interest in a reading list.
