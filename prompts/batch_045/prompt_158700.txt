I spent something over thirty years working in radiation protection — latterly on the committee that signed off the local rules at a large teaching hospital — and only in retirement have I begun reading moral philosophy in any seriousness. I have recently come up against the objection to utilitarianism that turns on the separateness of persons: roughly, that classical utilitarianism extends to a plurality of people a principle of rational choice that makes sense only for a single person, summing gains and losses across lives as though the boundary between one life and another carried no moral weight. What struck me at once is that I had spent my working life inside a regulatory system that appears to do exactly what that objection condemns — and then, in the very next clause, to refuse to do it. I should like to know what it presupposes.

The practice is the international system of radiological protection, as implemented in Britain. Its structure, as I recall it, has three parts. First, justification: a practice involving exposure must, on balance, do more good than harm. Second, limitation: every individual must be protected against risks that are too great, by the application of a per-person dose ceiling, set much lower for members of the public than for occupational exposure. Third, optimisation: below those ceilings, exposures are to be reduced so far as is reasonably achievable, taking economic and societal factors into account, and both the number of people exposed and the magnitude of each person's dose are to be pushed down. In Britain the test is not a flat cost–benefit calculation; since a 1949 case about a colliery, the requirement has been that a risk must be averted unless the sacrifice involved is in gross disproportion to the benefit, which loads the dice in favour of protection.

The instrument by which optimisation is actually done is the collective dose: the sum of individual doses over the exposed group, expressed in person-sieverts, to which regulators in several jurisdictions attach a monetary value so that rival protection options can be set against each other. Three features of it seem to me to carry the philosophical weight. Additions of this kind presuppose the linear no-threshold model, on which risk is proportional to dose with no safe lower bound; on the Commission's own figures, one person exposed to a sievert faces a few per cent chance of a fatal cancer, and on the same model a thousand people each exposed to a millisievert face the same expected number of fatal cancers, so that protecting one person against a large dose counts for exactly as much as protecting a thousand against a small one. The people who bear the cost of a protection measure are generally not the people whose dose is averted: the hospital's capital budget, or the ratepayer, on one side; patients, staff and neighbours on the other. And the same body that recommends the quantity warns that it is an instrument for optimisation and comparison, not a tool for risk projection, that estimating cancer deaths from trivial exposures spread over very large populations is unreasonable, and that low individual doses should not be aggregated over wide geographical regions or extended periods — even as assessments for the geological disposal of long-lived waste report a single collective dose figure committed over periods far longer than any institution has existed, to people who do not yet exist and cannot be identified.

So my questions. Reconstruct the implicit moral view: does deciding by summed dose presuppose that burdens and benefits falling on distinct persons can be added and traded off against one another, and is that the very commitment the separateness objection attacks, or is it a weaker and different one, given that what is summed is a physical proxy for expected harm rather than welfare or a good life? Tell me what the system strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with — I can see readings of it as prioritarian, as egalitarian about risk, and even as a contractualist device, and I want to know whether the practice rules any of them out.

Then tell me where the objection actually bites. Is the equivalence of a sievert to one person and a millisievert to a thousand something the objector must reject? Does that equivalence follow from the no-threshold model alone, or does it require an additional moral premiss, and if so, which one? Does the objection reach the aggregation of risks before the fact, or only the aggregation of harms actually suffered — and does it reach, as it were, the aggregation of money spent against dose averted, where the losers and gainers are different sets of people entirely?

Then the part I find hardest. The system aggregates, but it also refuses to aggregate: hard per-person ceilings, a test weighted against gross disproportion, and a standing instruction not to sum trivial doses over huge populations and long times. Are the ceilings and the caveat the framework's way of taking the separateness of persons seriously — side-constraints on an otherwise aggregative procedure? Is the combination coherent as constrained aggregation, or does it equivocate between two incompatible views? Which part is doing the real moral work? And would a consistent aggregator have to abandon the ceilings — with what consequence, say, for trading a cleaner's or a radiographer's dose against the convenience of patients, or the public's dose against the electricity bill?

Finally, what would have to change if the objection were accepted? Could one keep the no-threshold model and discard the summing? What would replace optimisation by collective dose — a ceiling on individual risk alone, a maximin rule, lexical thresholds — and what would be lost: the capacity to decide anything at all, or specifically the capacity to justify protecting large populations against diffuse low-level exposure? And does the intergenerational case, where the summed dose is borne by unidentifiable future strangers, make the objection easier or harder to press?

I should like a verdict at the end: whether the practice I worked under for thirty years is a counterexample to the separateness objection, a specimen of what it condemns, or simply silent on it. Please be disciplined about the mapping — what quantity is being summed, whose dose, what counts as the benefit — and say plainly where the framework is silent rather than making it speak. And spare me references and reading lists; I can find those myself.
