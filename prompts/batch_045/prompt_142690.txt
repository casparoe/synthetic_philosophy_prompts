I'm a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I'm about two hundred pages into a book set in Elea a generation or so after Zeno — my narrator is a rope-maker's daughter who walks the length of the rope-walk all day twisting and measuring strands, sharp as anything but entirely self-taught. There's a dinner scene I keep rewritting where a visiting sophist condescends to her with the standard paradox — you can't get across the yard, because before the whole distance you do half, before the half a quarter, and so on without end — and I want her to answer him with a puzzle of her own that he *cannot* dissolve in one line, while still being the kind of thing a clever but not omniscient person could actually pose at a table. So I need a thought experiment built to specifications, not an illustration.

The specs, roughly in order of how much I care: (1) Reverse the usual shape. I want infinitely many tasks with *no first member* but with a determinate last one, all packed into a finite stretch of ordinary time, with the times and the distances both converging nicely — so that the "but the infinite series sums to a finite quantity" line is fully available to him, and my case has to survive that line rather than dodge it by diverging or by needing infinitely much time. (2) No lamp-and-switch style indeterminacy: the terminal state has to be fixed by the setup, so he can't reply that the question simply has no answer. (3) The move I actually want to break is the other standard one — there doesn't have to be a first step, you just begin, the order is dense and there is no first half to cross — and I want it to break for a structural reason, not because my narrator stipulates that it does. My own idea was to link the tasks by dependence instead of by their being nested halves of each other: each task can only be performed once its immediate predecessor has been performed, so the chain has no anchor anywhere, even though it has an end. If that's the best device, use it; if you have something better, use that. (4) Constraints on the furniture: no gods, demons, omniscient storytellers, infinitely fast messengers, or infinitely precise measuring gear; it has to be stageable on a rope-walk or a ferry deck with rope, lamps, water clocks, sand, runners; speakable aloud in one paragraph, because a guest who talks for two minutes loses the room; and it shouldn't lean on set theory or physics that his friends would sneer at as anachronistic. (5) It can't beg the question — the puzzle has to fall out of the structure of the case, not out of her announcing that therefore motion is impossible.

After the scenario, please show me the machinery: the sequence and its order type; exactly which convergence facts I'm granting him; why the "no first member needed" reply does and doesn't get a grip here; what the Benacerraf-flavoured complaint that no individual sub-task explains the final state does with my case; which single feature is doing the real work; what happens if I remove it (give the sequence a first task? make the tasks independent of one another?); and the strongest thing the sophist could say back, phrased so a reader would feel it land — I'd much rather he score a point than have my narrator win by authorial fiat. Also tell me honestly if two of my specs can't be met together, which one you'd drop, and what I'd lose in the scene if I dropped it. No reading lists or citations, please: I'm writing this in a cottage with no internet and a stack of books I keep meaning to open, so I just want the argument. And flag where you're least confident — which step of your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and what would convince you the case doesn't work.
