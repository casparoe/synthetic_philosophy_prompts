hey — you're a philosopher of history who's also read a ton of actual historiography: equally at home with longue-durée, quantitative, climate-and-grain-prices stuff and with microhistory of the sort where one illiterate miller's trial record is made to carry a whole book. Please think this through carefully — do a bit of unstructured brainstorming first, sketch two or three candidate readings, then commit to one and say where it's weakest. Don't just list schools of thought; I want you to actually reconstruct an implicit theory and stress-test it.

Here's what I keep coming back to. There's a very consistent asymmetry in how professional history actually gets done, and I want to know what it presupposes.

When historians write about elites — a king, a first minister, a pope, a general — the defualt is intentional, narrative, source-close explanation: read the correspondence, recover the reasons, weigh the road not taken, write the biography. And historians working that way are scathing about anything aggregative: you cannot explain a cabinet decision with a regression, a structural account "leaves out the people who actually decided."

When they write about everyone else — weavers, peasants, enlisted soldiers, enslaved people, women in a village — the defualt flips: structural and material explanation (price series, harvests, demographic cycles, "mentalities"), serial evidence out of tax rolls, court registers, parish books. And a single vivid anecdote is an anecdote: something to be a little embarrassed about, or to be framed as illustrative of a pattern that's been established quantitatively elsewhere.

Same discipline, same journals, same departments, two quite different epistemic styles — and each style's declared vice is roughly the other style's declared method. So:

1. What does this asymmetry presuppose? Does it only make sense under the view that agency and deliberative reason really are concentrated at the top of a society — that ordinary people's reasoning isn't the kind of thing history is *about*? Or is it fully accounted for by a boring source-survival story (elites wrote letters, poor people got counted) plus a bit of division of labour? Be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it's underdetermined, i.e. where several quite different philosophies of history would license exactly the same behaviour.

2. Where does the practice contradict itself? I'm most interested in the seams where the two styles have to touch: a grain riot (is it a price series, or a set of intentions?), a soldier's letter home, a petition, a court deposition, a slave narrative — cases where the archive hands you one ordinary person's own words. Does the discipline's handling of those cases betray a commitment it won't avow?

3. The part I most want you to work on, and where I think you'll have to do real thinking rather than reach for a stock answer. Imagine a national research council adopts what I'll call the survivorship rule: grant panels are instructed to treat the fact that a topic is richly documented as a point against funding it, on the grounds that the archive is itself an artifact of past power, and to give priority to projects about people who left no records in their own voice. Never mind whether it's a good idea; I want the implicit theory. What view of historical significance does it presuppose — must significance be independent of source-survival, and can it be, given that we mostly know about the illiterate through literate intermediaries? What view of historical *knowledge* — is recovering "voice" a way of knowing the past, or a way of being *related* to it, and is the rule secretly a reparative-political principle wearing an epistemic costume? And what view of the historian: if it's committed to the thought that proximity to power distorts the record, why isn't the history of power the most distorted history of all?

4. Finally, concretely: what would have to change in practice — graduate training, what counts as evidence, what a monograph looks like, what gets reviewed where — if a department genuinely ran one method for kings and weavers? Say which of the two styles would have to give up more, and say what would be lost. I'm not sure its even possible, so if you think it isn't, say what exactly blocks it.
