Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (formal epistemology adjacent) and I've been asked to run one session of our department's Thursday reading group on Dutch book arguments. 14 people, mostly first- and second-year grads plus a couple of advanced undergrads, 75 minutes, room with a whiteboard and movable chairs. I've got index cards, a deck of cards, two opaque bags, and poker chips. No real money, and I'd definately rather not have to invent a currency either — points tracked on the board that count toward nothing except a leaderboard nobody will remember next week.

What I want is a game, not a walkthrough. I want people to get sure-lossed: to hand over chips across a set of bets that they themselves priced and each of which looked fine to them individually, and then to watch the ledger show they're down no matter how the world turns out. That's the bit I want them to feel — not "incoherence is irrational" as a slogan, but the specific nausea of having named your own prices and still losing for sure.

Two more things I want built in, because last time someone just assigned the usual encyclopedia article on it and everyone nodded and nothing landed:

1. A short diachronic round. Evidence arrives mid-game, people state in advance how they'll revise, and anyone whose update rule isn't conditionalization gets bitten by a bet placed now plus a bet that only executes if the evidence shows up. Assume at least one participant will plan to update in an intuitive-but-wrong way (setting the posterior to the probability of the evidence given the hypothesis, or sliding every hypothesis by the same amount) — that happens every time, and I want the payoff structure to punish exactly that.

2. A last round whose point is that the argument runs out. Two or three people will refuse to name one two-sided fair price and will instead give a buy price and a lower sell price; and when the ambiguous bag comes out, their prices will look "incoherent" in a way you can't actually exploit. I want them to find out for themselves that they can be booked only if they'll trade both ways at a single price, and that some patterns you'd want to call irrational just aren't Dutch-bookable. So the game should end with the sure-loss trick failing, not working.

Give me: the setup and materials, the exact rules and payoff scheme, the timeline as a compact table (75 minutes, minute marks), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the argument. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm assuming at least one person tries to be the bookie and at least one refuses to bet on principle — and how to handle each. And be honest about which feature of the Dutch book argument the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself; I'd much rather read "you have to supply this bit" than get a debrief that pretends the game did all the work.
