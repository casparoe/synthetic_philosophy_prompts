I teach an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology — 16 students, one 90-minute session, a room with a whiteboard and no reliable projector. The students are comfortable with Bayes' theorem, partitions, and expected value, and most have met strictly proper scoring rules, but none of them has seen the update rule I have in mind and I do not want the name used until the last fifteen minutes.

I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel, rather than be told, the difference between updating on evidence that arrives with certainty and updating on evidence that arrives uncertain. The formal target is this. Let H be a hypothesis partition and let E and F be two cross-cutting partitions. Ordinary conditionalization on E and then on F gives the same result as the reverse order: both give the distribution conditional on the intersection. But if the evidence comes as a direct shift of confidence on the E partition and then on the F partition — the operation where the conditional distribution of H within each cell of the shifted partition is held fixed and only the cell's total probability is moved to a new value — then the two orders generally disagree. My own toy check with a 2×2×2 table produced a gap of only a few percentage points, which I think is too small for anyone to care about; I want numbers where the gap is large enough to change what a rational agent would do.

Please design the activity and give me everything I need to run it:

1. Setup and rules. A neutral cover story that rests on stipulation only, with no empirical background the students would have to look up — my preference is a fictional remote-diagnostics vignette, but use whatever keeps the numbers self-contained. Split the room into two halves that believe they are doing the same task. Both receive identical priors and the same two evening readings; the only difference is the order in which the handout tells them to process the readings, and they should not be told that the orders differ. Each half works in pairs and reports a final probability distribution over the hypotheses plus one recommended action.

2. Materials. Priors and the joint distribution given as integer counts out of a round total, so that every update is a rescaling of counts and the fixed-conditional constraint is visible as "the mix inside each column is untouched, only the column total changes." Include the actual handout wording for the two readings — phrased as shifts of confidence on an evidence partition, not as likelihoods or sensor accuracies — and an answer key. Build in one wrinkle: the second channel's reliability should depend on which hypothesis is true, so that holding the within-cell mix fixed is substantively doubtful rather than a harmless technical step, and flag where that shows up in the numbers.

3. Payoff scheme. Small stakes, no money. I would like a strictly proper scoring rule applied to the final forecasts, with the truth drawn from a key, plus a decision threshold the students set themselves before they compute. Please also tell me honestly whether the scoring can discriminate between the two orders — my expectation is that a single draw cannot, and that even over repeated rounds the difference in expected score will be small; if that is right, say how many rounds it would take and whether running more rounds would spoil the point rather than sharpen it.

4. A minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, including a short opening round in which both readings arrive with certainty and the two halves get identical answers. The agreement in that round is what should make the divergence in the second round land.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments onto formal points: why certainty makes the order irrelevant, exactly what the second update holds fixed and why it is applied to a distribution the first update has already distorted, why a student who instead re-fixes the original within-E mix gets a third answer, and where the hypothesis-dependent reliability undermines the whole procedure. The debrief should also stage the objection I expect from the strongest student — that a reading from an instrument should come in as a likelihood ratio on the evidence partition rather than as a posterior probability on it — by having the room redo the numbers that way and discover that commutativity returns. Facilitator framing for that contrast, and for when to name it, please.

6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the mistakes, the objections that will derail the arithmetic, the pair that finishes early and contaminates the reveal, and how I handle each.

7. An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself — in particular, that it can show the order-dependence but not whether holding the within-cell mix fixed is the right constraint, nor why one should update at all rather than merely score well here.
