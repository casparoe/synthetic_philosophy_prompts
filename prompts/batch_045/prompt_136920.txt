I'm a journalist working on a long feature about how appellate judges actually resolve statutory cases, and I've hit a problem I keep failing to solve on my own. Every example anyone sends me of textualism and purposivism genuinely pulling apart is either a centenarian chestnut from the casebooks — a statute barring the importation of contract laborers read not to cover a minister, a firearm traded for narcotics read as one "used" in a drug transaction — or a case where one side is being deliberately obtuse, which my interviewees correctly point out proves nothing. My most useful source, a friend who clerks on a federal court of appeals and describes herself without embarrassment as a Scalia-style textualist, says the honest cases are the ones where neither method is cheating and they still disagree; I'd like to put one of those in the piece, and I'd rather construct it than hunt for it, because a hypothetical lets me hold everything else fixed. So I need a case built to order.

Here is what I need it to satisfy. First, the statute: one paragraph of realistic text in a real regulatory area — hospital billing, tenant protections, groundwater permitting, nursing-home staffing, whatever you like — and it must contain its own purpose clause or findings section, so that the purpose is available from the enacted text itself. No committee reports, no floor statements, no sponsor's letter: both methods argue from the same materials. Second, and this is where my own drafts died, the case must not be propped up by any of the usual escape hatches. My friend killed my first attempt in one line ("scrivener's error, next") and my second in two (the result was absurd enough that the absurdity canon resolved it before any purpose-talk was needed). So: no drafting mistakes, no results absurd on their face, no constitutional-avoidance route, no bare vagueness — please don't build the case on words like "reasonable," "appropriate," or "significant," whose whole function is to underdetermine — and no manufacturing the divergence by having the textualist read words with their nineteenth-century meaning while everyone else reads them with today's. Third, the divergence has to be generated by one structural feature: something that changed between enactment and application without anyone amending the disputed words. A later-enacted statute in a different title, a cross-referenced regulatory schedule that has since been revised, a definition that behaves differently after a subsequent amendment, a term whose ordinary meaning drifted. And it has to survive the textualist's own toolkit: I want you to show that a textualist who reads the whole act, the whole code, and the later enactment too — who applies the canons rather than being forbidden from them — still reaches the reading the purpose clause resists. If your case works only by barring the textualist from looking at the later material, it's rigged, and I can't use it. Both readings should be ones a competent lawyer could sign a brief to; I don't want a straw textualist any more than I want a straw purposivist.

After the statute and a short, ordinary factual scenario — real parties, real stakes, nothing fanciful — I'd like the analysis in four labeled parts. (1) The verdict under each method, naming which party wins: what the textualist does with the disputed words and which canons get applied in reaching that result, and what the purposivist does with the purpose clause, including what happens when the purpose is stated at a different level of generality. (2) The load-bearing feature, named explicitly, plus a demonstration that removing it collapses the case back into agreement — say what each method says about the stripped-down version. (3) The strongest objection a textualist would make to your own construction: I'd guess it's something like the purpose clause doing legislative history's work in disguise, or the claim that you've only exhibited an ambiguity that canons resolve, or the complaint that the real dispute is about the level of generality and not about method at all — but tell me which one actually bites hardest here, and how much it costs the case. (4) One line on what you'd expect a real appellate panel to do with it and why, and one line on whether it makes the point for my piece better than the chestnuts do — whether it isolates something about the two methods rather than something about a drafting accident.
