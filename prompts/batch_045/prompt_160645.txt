Hey — I'm TAing a second-year philosophy of language seminar next term, about 18 students, one 80-minute slot, and I want to run a session on rule-following skepticism that isn't me talking at them for 80 minutes. They'll have had one lecture by then, so they've seen a plus/quus-style example, but I want them to run into the problem themselves before anything gets named out loud.

Here's the bit I can't get to work. I want them to feel that there being no fact of the matter about what they meant isn't just "the data underdetermines the hypothesis, lol" — that's the failure mode I keep hitting whenever I sketch this. Roughly half the class is maths and compsci students, and they'll just say "sure, curve fitting, get more data" and leave feeling like they already knew it. I want something where the underdetermination bites on them as agents who meant something, not as theorists squinting at a data series. If that distinction isn't the right one to be aiming at, tell me.

So: design me a runnable thing — rules, materials, timeline, debrief. Some constraints and half-ideas:

- An early phase where each person privately commits to having meant a specific rule, with real but small stakes. I can do chocolate or a tiny bit of participation credit, not cash.
- Somewhere in the middle I want a mechanic that makes their own appeal to "what I obviously meant" come apart in their hands. Two ideas I've been toying with: (a) I collect their phase-one answers, keep them, and later hand each person a sheet of anonymised earlier answers — actually their own — and ask them to judge whether that person meant the same rule they did; (b) I quietly give different people slightly different training sequences, all consistent up to the test point and diverging after, so each person is convinced the others are the ones who broke the pattern. Tell me which of these (or something else) does the actual work, and be honest if both are gimmicks that only look clever.
- A later phase where the group has to decide, with no appeal to what anybody "meant," who counts as having gone on correctly — i.e. make them invent some kind of communal check because nothing else is on the table. I'd like them to produce the shape of the skeptical solution themselves before I name it.

For the debrief: map particular moments in the activity onto the actual moves of the argument — the finitude of examples, the gap between meaning something and what you're disposed to do, the move from truth conditions to assertability conditions and agreement within a community. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run: the maths students' shortcut, and the opposite failure where someone just goes "fine, so meaning isn't real, whatever." Say which step people usually dig in at, and what it costs them to dig in there.

And be frank about what the activity can't reproduce. My worry is that a game can only ever stage underdetermination by evidence, while the real worry is about whether there's any fact of the matter about what you meant at all — and that isn't obviously the sort of thing you can engineer someone into feeling. If that's right, say so, and tell me what I have to just say out loud instead, and where in the session.

If you think the whole thing is a bad idea as posed — wrong target, or the collapse into plain underdetermination is unavoidable and I should teach it a different way — say that straight rather than handing me a lesson plan that runs smoothly. I'd definately rather have the objection.
