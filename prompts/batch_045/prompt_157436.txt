I spent a little over forty years as an experimental physicist, most of it working on phase transitions and the behavior of matter in the solid state, and in retirement I have begun reading metaphysics systematically for the first time. I find that I cannot get clear about a piece of my own former scientific practice, and I would be grateful for a careful reconstruction of what that practice presupposes — and of whether its parts presuppose consistent things.

The practice is this. Working scientists routinely rank theories, and the entities and laws those theories posit, along a scale of fundamentality, and the ranking is not idle: it organizes departments, funding lines, prestige, and what counts as an explanation. Several features seem to be doing the work. First, fundamentality appears to track scale: the physics of small things and short distances is called more fundamental than the physics of composites, and a derivation running upward from the smaller theory to the larger one is treated as an explanatory achievement. Second, fundamentality appears to track generality: a theory with a wider, less hedged domain of application is called more fundamental than one that holds only here, or only over this range of energies. Third, the asymmetry is real and one-directional in scientific reward: recovering the behavior of a macroscopic system from a microscopic model wins admiration, while a standalone macroscopic model — however accurate — is patronized as phenomenology, as though it had described the phenomena without explaining them. Fourth, and in tension with all of this, the higher-level vocabulary is indispensable in practice and is treated as reporting discoveries rather than conveniences: new phases of matter, quasiparticles, universality classes and the like are announced as things found in nature, they are multiply realized by utterly different microscopic constitutions, and the same higher-level law is what lets us predict across that variation. Fifth, everyone involved cheerfully concedes that our best microscopic theory is not the last word but an effective description, expected to be superseded in the way its predecessors were.

I want to know what conception of grounding and fundamentality this bundle of habits commits its practitioners to — and here are two invented cases I have been using to test it, since I do not trust my intuitions about the actual cases anymore.

Call the first the *basementless tower*. Suppose the sequence of ever-more-microscopic theories never terminates: every layer turns out on closer inspection to be an effective description of a deeper layer, indefinitely, with no final theory and no smallest constituents. Suppose further that at some layer we find two empirically equivalent but ontologically incompatible microscopic descriptions of the same phenomena — each as deep as the other, neither reducible to the other. Does the scientific practice of ranking still make sense in that world? Does it strictly presuppose that grounding relations terminate in a base, and does it strictly presuppose that the fundamental description is unique? Or would the practice survive essentially unchanged, which would suggest that what scientists track is something weaker than metaphysical fundamentality?

Call the second the *inverted hierarchy*. Suppose we discovered that explanatory dependence ran downward in scale rather than upward: that the most general, most restrictive, most informative description available to us is stated at cosmological or thermodynamic scale, and that the small-scale laws take the form they do because of constraints imposed from above, so that the small is derivable from the large and not the reverse. Would my former colleagues, on discovering this, say that the large-scale theory is the fundamental one — or would they go on calling the small-scale theory fundamental while conceding that it is explained by something above it? I genuinely cannot tell, and the answer seems to me to separate two quite different things that the word fundamental may be conflating.

So please reconstruct the implicit theory: the view of grounding, dependence, and fundamentality that this practice only makes sense under, and the rival views it quietly rules out. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits well with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined — where several competing accounts would license the same scientific behavior, so that my practice gives no evidence at all for one over another. Where you diagnose an inconsistency among the five features I listed, say which limb ought to be given up and what would concretely change if it were: in the vocabulary scientists use, in the prestige asymmetry between derivation and phenomenological modeling, in whether entities like quasiparticles are described as real, and in how a research enterprise is justified when there is no bottom to reach. Where your answer turns on a distinction I am unlikely to have met, define the term as you go.

Please write in American English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and aim for something in the neighborhood of fifteen hundred words. I would rather have a committed reconstruction with the weak joints marked than a survey of options, though I do want to be told plainly which parts of this are genuinely unsettled.
