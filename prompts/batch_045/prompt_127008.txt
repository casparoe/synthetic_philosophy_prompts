I'm a PhD student in atmospheric science, and this term I've been sitting in on an interdisciplinary reading group — two relativity grad students, one philosopher, and me — that works through a chapter a week of a trade-press book on the philosophy of space and time. I'd rather not name the book, because I'm not trying to review it; I just want to know how much of last week's chapter on the hole argument to believe. The two relativity students came away disagreeing with each other about whether the chapter was basically fine, which is why I'm asking here rather than just accepting whichever of them sounded more confident.

As close as I can manage to the chapter's own wording, it goes like this: 'Take a region of spacetime containing no matter — the hole — and hold fixed everything outside it, including the entire distribution of matter and fields. Because general relativity is generally covariant, you are free to spread the metric and the other fields differently across the points inside the hole while leaving them untouched outside: you can, in effect, drag every event in the hole over to a different point. What results is a second model that satisfies Einstein's field equations just as well as the first and agrees with it everywhere outside the hole. So the equations determine nothing at all about the interior of the hole: given a complete specification of the world outside it and before it, infinitely many different futures for the hole are permitted, and there is no fact of the matter about which one occurs. General relativity is therefore indeterministic, and because the rival futures differ only over which points have which properties, the argument also refutes the idea that spacetime is a thing with an identity of its own, over and above matter and fields — points, unlike material objects, cannot be told apart, so there is nothing for them to be. Physicists drew the obvious moral: the two models are not two possibilities but a single physical situation described twice, since the difference between them is a diffeomorphism, that is, pure gauge. With that, determinism is restored and the paradox evaporates. Einstein himself hit on this argument while still building the theory, which is why he briefly abandoned generally covariant field equations before returning to them; the episode is usually told as a cautionary tale about how even he mistook a freedom in the mathematics for a freedom in the world. And the moral generalizes: wherever a theory's equations admit a symmetry, the differences that symmetry generates are unreal — the author dubs this the redundancy rule — and the same argument can be run against Newton's absolute space, which is part of why nobody holds that view any more.'

My own instinct, such as it is, is that the determinism scare is being oversold: surely the theory still tells you exactly what an observer who falls through the hole would record, and it isn't indeterministic about that. But I can't tell whether that is the standard reply or whether I've simply failed to see what the fuss is about, and I have no way to check the history on my own. So please audit the passage clause by clause: which parts would a philosopher of physics simply accept, which are defensible simplifications of something more careful, and which are real misrepresentations — a technical term used in an everyday sense, two claims conflated that the literature keeps apart, a contested interpretive stance presented as settled, a standard argument presented as a refutation? Then tell me whether the chapter's later claims follow from the accurate part of the summary: does the gauge reading really restore determinism without residue, does the redundancy rule hold in the generality it is stated in, and does the same argument really run against Newtonian absolute space in anything like the same form?
