I am a novelist, and I need an essay I can think with rather than one I can cite.

The novel I am finishing turns on two decisions, and both scenes must be written as deliberation rather than as swoon. In the first, my protagonist — who has never wanted children, has never spent a night under the same roof as an infant, and is wary of the standard reassurance that one simply adapts — decides to try to conceive. In the second, a secondary character consents to an irreversible neurological procedure that will restructure her aesthetic sensibility: she is told, reliably, that music, which presently leaves her cold, will become the organizing passion of her life, and that some of her current attachments will thereafter strike her as provincial. She is told this but not told which ones.

Both are transformative in the sense made current by L. A. Paul's Transformative Experience (2014): choosing them alters the chooser in a way that cannot be assessed in advance from the standpoint she occupies when she chooses. What I want from you is not a survey of that literature but an adjudication of a live disagreement within it. One side treats the trouble as informational: the agent lacks knowledge of what the experience is like, and were that gap closed — by imagination, by proxy experience, by testimony — ordinary expected-utility reasoning would simply apply, the residual difficulty being practical rather than normative. The other side treats the trouble as constitutive: what changes is not the agent's information but the valuer herself, so no quantity of information could settle the question, and the transformed woman's retrospective endorsement is a verdict issued from a rival standpoint rather than evidence the earlier self could use. A third option rejects the framing outright and holds that rationality here is not maximization at all but exploration, the relevant virtue being something like authorship of a self rather than choice-worthiness of an option.

I have my own distinctions, and I want you to tell me which of them, if any, is actually load-bearing rather than decorative. First, scaled proxies versus inversive proxies: some transformations admit a rehearsal that differs from the target only in duration or intensity — a month of caring for a friend's baby, a half-dose of the drug — while others admit none, either because rehearsing them just is undergoing them or because the rehearsal's hedonic character is inverted relative to the outcome, the first hour of the new sensibility being nausea rather than music. Second, the surviving-stakes test: which of the agent's present ends can she confidently predict will still be hers afterwards — that she will still want to read, still want her brother's regard — and whether a choice can be arbitrated by appeal to those invariants alone. Third, the peculiar shape of the information my second character is given: she receives not a sample of the experience but a warning about the standpoint change itself, and I do not know whether information of that shape helps the informational view or quietly destroys it.

So: write me an essay of roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words that argues for one verdict and defends it. It should say which framing it endorses and why the others fail; say which distinction — mine or one you substitute — does the real work in separating tractable transformative choices from untractable ones; apply the verdict to both of my characters' decisions, marking explicitly where the procedure differs from the pregnancy in a way the standard framing misses; and tell me what my verdict implies for the craft question, namely whether the deliberative chapter is writable as deliberation at all, and whether that implication is itself a reason to reject the verdict. Do not hedge into a non-committal summary, and do not lean on citations or bibliographies — argue from the structure of the problem. I would also ask, in the body of the essay and not only in the summary below, that you state plainly what would change your mind: the single consideration that would flip the verdict, and the place where you think the reasoning is most likely to break.

After the essay, append a single JSON object with exactly these keys:

- `verdict` (string): one sentence stating the conclusion.
- `framing` (string): the framing endorsed — informational, constitutive, exploratory, or one you name yourself.
- `load_bearing_distinction` (string): the distinction that does the work, and one sentence on why.
- `case_one_verdict` (object): with keys `tractable` (string: yes, no, or qualified) and `reason` (string, two sentences maximum) for the pregnancy decision.
- `case_two_verdict` (object): same two keys for the procedure decision.
- `scene_implication` (string): whether the chapter can be written as deliberation, and why.
- `strongest_opposing_argument` (string).
- `would_change_my_mind` (string): the single argument or consideration that would reverse the verdict.
- `weakest_link` (string): where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
- `confidence_percent` (integer): 0 to 100.

Aim for the register of a serious essay rather than a blog post, but remember that the prose will be read by someone who has to put it into a character's mouth afterwards.
