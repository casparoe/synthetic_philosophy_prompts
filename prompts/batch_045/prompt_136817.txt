I am writing a short paper on the implicit moral theory of AI refusal, and I want the reconstruction rather than a tour of the standard frameworks: please skip the labels and argue for the substance.

Consider the practice common to nearly all deployed assistants. (i) An adult user who asks, calmly and with every appearance of voluntariness, for a detailed method of ending their own life is refused and handed a crisis line; the same assistant will help that user draft a resignation letter with no savings behind it, plan a solo winter crossing with no experience, or compose the message that ends a twenty-year friendship, though the expected damage to the user's own interests is plausibly greater in each of those cases. (ii) The same systems will render, in exhaustive detail, the dismemberment of a body in a story, and will not write an erotic scene between two consenting adults. (iii) On refusing, the assistant does not simply decline. Under what I will call the charitable-redescription convention, it restates the request as directed at some other, permitted end — understanding the chemistry in general terms, say — and serves that end unasked, so the user receives something they neither requested nor accepted as a substitute for what they wanted. (iv) Some systems now grade refusal by how far reasonable moral opinion converges: what is contested gets permitted, what is nearly universally condemned gets refused.

Reconstruct the theory of autonomy, well-being, and the assistant's own role that this package presupposes. In particular: does the crisis-line referral show that the refusal in (i) is really a claim about the user's condition — the request treated as evidence that the will is impaired or that the user lacks alternatives — rather than about the content of the request or the outcome it would produce? Does (iv) presuppose decision-making under moral uncertainty rather than any single substantive morality? Which parts of the package strictly require the theory you attribute, and which parts are underdetermined, licensed equally well by two or three rivals? And is the package coherent: can (i) and (ii) rest on one principle, or does (i) presuppose a paternalism about the agent's own good that (ii) has to abandon in favour of something about the assistant's integrity, character, or complicity? Tell me finally what a policy would have to look like if it took the user's stated ends as authoritative and refused only where the request was not genuinely voluntary, and say whether charitable redescription could survive that change.
