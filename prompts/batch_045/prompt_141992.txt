You are a historian of historiography who also works as an analytic epistemologist: someone equally at home with fifth-century Greek logographers, early-medieval Latin diplomatics, and current work on the epistemology of testimony and of evidence. Please work carefully and in stages. Start with a brief, deliberately unstructured pass in which you simply inventory the moving parts of the problem below — what the candidate sources are, what the candidate objects of inquiry are, which tests look applicable, and where you expect the trouble to be — and only then produce the ordered, structured answer I ask for at the end. Where a claim of yours depends on an assumption, name the assumption. Where the theory simply has nothing to say about the case, say that plainly rather than forcing it to speak.

The episode to analyze. Here is the case as I understand it; if you think some detail is wrong, flag it in a single line and move on, and do not let your analysis rest on that detail. The Donation of Constantine is a Latin document, transmitted under the title Constitutum domini Constantini imperatoris, that purports to be an imperial decree by which Constantine I hands to Pope Sylvester I (pope 314–335) and his successors the Lateran Palace, imperial insignia including the tiara, primacy over the sees of Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem and Constantinople, and authority over Rome, Italy and the western provinces of the empire — presented as gratitude for Sylvester's cure of the emperor's leprosy, a story built on the fifth-century Acts of Sylvester. It was almost certainly composed in the eighth century, perhaps in a Roman or Frankish clerical setting, was later folded into the Pseudo-Isidorean decretals, and was cited in earnest by Pope Leo IX in 1054 against the Patriarch of Constantinople. Its authenticity was not universally granted even before the fifteenth century — the imperial chancery under Otto III rejected it — but the demolition that stuck came from Lorenzo Valla, who wrote his declamation De falso credita et ementita Constantini Donatione in 1439–1440 while in the service of Alfonso V of Aragon, then in conflict with the Papal States, and who was subsequently called before the Inquisition. Valla worked almost entirely from internal evidence: the vocabulary is anachronistic (he singles out the word satrap as something no Roman emperor of Constantine's day would have used), the Latin is bad in ways an imperial chancery would not have produced, Constantine could not legally have alienated what the document makes him alienate, and no contemporary source records an administrative change of that magnitude. A century and a half later, in 1576, Gregory XIII — himself a believer in the Donation — had Valla's tomb and epitaph destroyed during building works at the Lateran.

The lens. Source criticism as it runs from Herodotus and Thucydides to modern diplomatics: external criticism, which asks about provenance, chain of custody, date, authorship and the material carrier; internal criticism, which asks about the competence, access, interest and plausibility of the source, its internal consistency, and its fit with what else is known; and the derived devices of modern practice — multiple attestation (independent sources are worth more than the sum of their parts), the criterion of embarrassment (a report that is inconvenient or humiliating for the party that would have had reason to invent it is more likely to be true), and the argument from silence (absence of attestation where attestation would be expected counts against).

Deliver the following, in this order.

1. A disciplined mapping. Identify, explicitly: what the source-objects are (the decree as an artifact, the copy-chain, the text as reconstructed), what hypotheses are in play, and which of the tests above bear on which hypothesis. Be careful to distinguish the question whether the document is what it claims to be from the question whether its content is true, and from the question what we may legitimately infer from it.

2. The epistemic structure of Valla's argument. Reconstruct it, without enthusiasm, as an evidential argument: say what the likelihoods and base rates are that have to be in place for an anachronism in vocabulary to be strong evidence against authenticity, and note how much of the argument's force comes from internal criticism alone, with a broken chain of custody behind it and no rival manuscript in front of it.

3. A device I want you to use: the three clocks. Any source can be dated in three distinct ways — the time of the events it purports to report, the time of its actual composition, and the time of the earliest surviving witness — and a question addressed to the wrong clock yields nonsense. Apply the three clocks to the Donation, and say which of the three the standard tests are actually sensitive to, and which they are powerless about. Then say what happens to the analysis when the composition date and the report date come apart by four centuries while the surviving witness is later than both.

4. A knot I want untied, which I call the embarrassment inversion. Valla's argument is in part that Constantine could not plausibly have done what the document says: the grant is legally impossible, politically self-humbling, and unrecorded. But the criterion of embarrassment says that a report which is inconvenient for the party with reason to invent it is a mark of authenticity. Whose interest is supposed to be the relevant one — that of the putative author, of the beneficiary, of the copying institution, or of the modern inquirer — and on the correct answer to that, does the criterion cut for or against the Donation's authenticity? Say clearly whether the criterion is well defined at all when the author is a fiction of the document.

5. What a forgery is a source for. Once the Donation is known to be an eighth-century composition, it stops being evidence about the fourth century and becomes evidence about something else. Say precisely what, how good that evidence is, and what the general principle is for converting a source's target of inquiry after a test has failed. Say also whether this conversion is available for every failed source or only for some.

6. The inquirer's own position. Valla had an employer with a territorial quarrel, and the popes went on using the document after him. Say what this does to the standing of source criticism: whether a test whose results are institutionally unwelcome is still a test, whether the method can be detached from the motives of whoever applies it, and what the 1576 episode shows about the difference between settling the epistemic question and settling the social one.

7. A counterfactual stress test I call the Valla trap. Suppose a forger with present-day tools — large digitized corpora of dated Latin, statistical stylometry, period-correct materials — produces a charter that passes every anachronism, stylistic and material test we have, and plants it with a plausible but unverifiable provenance. Ask what is left of source criticism once the internal tests have been saturated: which tests survive, whether multiple attestation survives given that purportedly independent sources can descend from a common origin or copy one another, and whether the argument from silence gets stronger or weaker as the corpus of surviving documents shrinks. Answer for that hypothetical case, not for the historical one.

8. Finally, run the case back against the theory. Set Herodotus's practice of preserving variant accounts and withholding adjudication against Thucydides's practice of reconstructing a single authoritative account from his own observation and from the reports of others, and say which posture the modern source critic is actually in, and which posture the Donation case vindicates — remembering that a source can fail the test of authenticity and still carry information. Then close with a short verdict: is the exposure of the Donation a confirmation of source criticism, an anomaly for it, or a case the apparatus was never designed for?
