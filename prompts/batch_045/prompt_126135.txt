I have been going back and forth with a friend for weeks about whether laws of nature govern the events that fall under them or merely summarize them. Last night he produced an argument that I have not been able to shake, and my difficulty is not that I doubt the conclusion. It is that the argument seems to deliver a conclusion neither of us can accept, including him, and I cannot locate the step where it goes wrong.

Set the vocabulary first, so we mean the same things. Call the Humean the one who says laws supervene on the totality of particular matters of fact — the mosaic — so that what the laws are is fixed by which generalizations earn a place in the best overall systematization of everything that happens, best meaning some optimum of simplicity and strength. Call the anti-Humean the one who says laws are something over and above the mosaic: necessary connections among universals, or primitive governing relations, so that the mosaic could have been exactly as it is while the laws were otherwise.

Now the case. Consider a world whose mosaic splits into two regions that never interact and never will. Call them the Vault and the Drift. The Vault is small and orderly: a few million objects tracing out an elegant, exceptionless, highly informative pattern P. The Drift is larger by many orders of magnitude and almost entirely disorderly, but buried somewhere in it is one fantastically long and intricately hedged regularity R — the sort of thing that costs a great deal of simplicity to state but buys an enormous amount of information if you state it.

(1) On the Humean account, whether R is a law of this world is settled by whether the systematization of the total mosaic that includes R beats, in combined simplicity and strength, every systematization that leaves it out.
(2) The competition is scored over the total mosaic. So whether R is a law depends on the composition of the Drift: how many instances of the messy pattern there are, whether some rival regularity there fits them better, whether the hedges pay for themselves.
(3) Nothing in the Drift makes any difference to anything in the Vault, and no inquirer confined to the Vault could detect the Drift even in principle.
(4) So whether R is a law — a status that, if R has it, licenses the Vault's scientists to project it, to cite it in explanation, and to reason counterfactually with it — is settled partly by facts they could never detect and that are irrelevant to everything in their region.
(5) Lawhood is not that kind of thing. It is supposed to be a feature of the world that inquiry can track and that underwrites local inference; a status that flips with undetectable, causally inert goings-on in a region one can never observe is a bookkeeping fact about a description, not a law.
(6) So Humeanism is false, and laws must be independent governors rather than features of the mosaic.

That much I followed. Here is where it ran away from us.

(7) The same reasoning seems to reach the anti-Humean as well. On her view the laws hold unrestrictedly: if it is a law that F things are G, then every F anywhere is G, and a single stray F that is not G anywhere in the Drift means that the Vault's elegant P was never a law at all. So her lawhood is also sensitive to undetectable, causally irrelevant goings-on in the Drift — one particle there can strip P of lawhood.
(8) So the constraint in (5), taken seriously, appears to force lawhood to be wholly local: whether a regularity in the Vault is a law must depend only on what goes on in the Vault.
(9) But if lawhood is wholly local, then a single world can have different laws in different places, and what our science calls the law of gravitation is really a patchwork of regional laws. Worse, almost any local accident begins to qualify — every coin in this room is heads-up, every gold sphere in this vault is under a mile across — because confining a pattern to a small region makes it trivially cheap to state and maximally strong there.
(10) So there are no laws of nature full stop, only local patterns of varying local elegance. Neither of us believes that, and my friend least of all.

His answer to (7) was that on his view the Drift does not make it the case that the Vault's pattern is a law; it only reveals that it never was one — whereas the Humean makes the Drift partly constitute the law. I could not tell whether that distinction was carrying the weight he put on it or whether he had simply relabeled the problem, and in any case it does not seem to help with (8) through (10).

Tell me which step you would reject, explain why that step looked compulsory in the first place, and price the alternatives: what does rejecting it cost, and what would it cost someone who instead reached for one or two of the neighbouring steps? I care much more about there being laws of nature full stop than about which of the two official accounts is correct, so I am prepared to pay a great deal anywhere from (5) onward — I just want to know exactly what each option buys me and whether anything recognisable as lawhood survives the payment.
