I've been going back and forth on the sorites paradox and I'd like to know whether I'm making an error or just reinventing a standard position under a new name.

Call a predicate *territory-sharp* if, for every candidate object, there is a determinate fact of the matter whether the predicate applies: no gaps in extension, no degrees of truth, just an exact boundary somewhere in the space of cases. My argument is short. The map/territory distinction does all the work, and the territory contains no vague objects. Any pile of grains has perfectly precise physical properties — exact count, exact packing geometry, exact static stability — and every respect in which we might judge heap-hood is like that. Apparent vagueness is a property of our words and our conversational standards, not of the grains. Now, heap-hood is a package deal over several such precise dimensions: how many grains, how tightly they cluster, how the pile behaves under jostling, and how much any of that matters for the purposes in play. But if each dimension is precise, the trade-off between them must also trace out some exact, if monstrously complicated, surface in case-space; our word simply doesn't tell us where that surface sits. So every predicate is territory-sharp, vagueness is nothing but ignorance of a boundary that is really there, and the whole logic-of-vagueness program — supervaluations, degrees of truth, truth-value gaps — looks like reifying a feature of our language into a feature of the world.

Where does this go wrong? And if it doesn't, what exactly is the residual disagreement between epistemicism and its rivals even about?
