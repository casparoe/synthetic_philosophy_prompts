I convene a small reading group on language acquisition — three people who work on formal syntax, three who do distributional and usage-based modelling — and we have now spent two years on the argument from the poverty of the stimulus without anyone's mind being changed by a single exchange. I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it, and I would like the diagnosis to earn its keep against a concrete case. Please do not give me a survey of the literature; give me a structural account with reasons.

Here is where each side actually stands. The argument as our nativists run it: children converge, quickly and uniformly, on grammatical properties that are underdetermined by the speech they hear; the target property is not entailed by any finite sample of positive evidence; children receive no systematic information about which strings are ungrammatical (corrections are sporadic, unreliable, and in any case directed at truth and politeness rather than form); and the classic learnability results about identification in the limit from positive evidence alone suggest that a learner whose hypothesis space is unconstrained cannot be guaranteed to arrive. So the space of candidate grammars must be constrained in advance, and that constraint is what "innate" is doing the work of naming.

The replies as our other half runs them: (i) "impoverished" is a rhetorical label, not a finding — what has been shown is that the relevant distinctions are absent from child-directed speech *in the form the nativist's idealized description of the grammar requires*, not that they are absent in any form a statistical learner could exploit, and estimates of how much information the input carries keep rising as methods improve; (ii) the argument is structurally unfalsifiable, because each time a learning model succeeds the target is quietly redrawn — the property is redescribed at a higher level of abstraction, or an auxiliary assumption about which input counts is added — until the model fails again; (iii) the conclusion is not well specified, since "innate" slides between "constrained by the architecture of the learning mechanism" (which nobody denies), "domain-specific" (which is a claim about the mind's faculty structure, not about genes), and "genetically encoded as symbolic knowledge" (for which no one has offered a developmental mechanism).

Each of these moves is known to the other side in advance, and each has a stock reply to its stock reply. That is what makes me think the disagreement is not where it appears to be. My own hypothesis, offered as one: the real dispute concerns what would count as a learning-theoretic explanation. One side wants a *guarantee*-style result — a specification of mechanism plus input from which the target property follows, ideally with a proof — and reads the absence of such a result as evidence for innate structure. The other is content with an *existence*-style demonstration — a learner without language-specific symbolic priors that in fact acquires the property from realistic data — and reads the demand for guarantees as a standard no empirical science could meet. If this is right, then "is it innate?" functions as a proxy for a quarrel about evidential standards, and the genuinely empirical question — how much information about the target property is actually present in child-directed speech, and in what form — keeps being conscripted into a dispute it cannot settle.

Before you accept or reject that, test it against the following case. I have invented the findings; please take them as real.

A documentation project on Meridian, a sign language of a small community, reports: (1) Meridian has a constraint C on long-distance dependencies — extraction may cross arbitrarily many clause boundaries, except out of a clause headed by a small, phonologically unmarked class of verbs (call them m-verbs), where it is ungrammatical. (2) Several hundred hours of child-directed signing contain no clause in which an m-verb and a long-distance dependency co-occur, i.e. no configuration in which C is visible; m-verbs are frequent and long-distance extraction is frequent, they simply do not appear together in speech to children. They do occasionally co-occur in adult-to-adult signing, and adults reject the result — but children are not reliably present, and nobody corrects anyone. (3) At four years old, Meridian-signing children reject precisely the sentences C rules out, at adult rates, including novel sentences with novel m-verbs, so the effect is not memorized lexically. (4) A group trains a generic sequence model — no language-specific symbolic priors, standard architecture, effective training exposure matched to a four-year-old Meridian signer — on the child-directed corpus. It reaches human-level next-unit prediction on held-out corpus data, but on the novel-sentence test it accepts C-violating sentences about half the time. (5) A second group adds one inductive bias the nativists had independently predicted, a bias to represent dependencies hierarchically rather than by linear distance, and this model acquires C.

Tell me: would findings (1)–(5) move anyone, and what exactly would each camp say about them? Then give me your diagnosis — verbal, about standards of evidence, about a hidden empirical question, about two different questions run together under one label, or ill-posed — and, given that diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a measurement, a change of question, or nothing. If you think the question should be replaced rather than answered, say what with and why. Keep the prose under about 800 words, and do not spend it restating my summary.

Finish with a table, one row per candidate diagnosis, including at least: verbal dispute about "innate"; disagreement about standards of evidence; hidden empirical question about the input; two different questions under one label; the question is ill-posed. Use exactly these columns:

- "diagnosis" — one sentence stating it
- "commitments" — the implicit premise each of the two camps would have to accept for this diagnosis to be correct
- "explains" — which stock move in the exchange above this diagnosis renders rational rather than merely stubborn
- "meridian" — whether this diagnosis predicts that findings (1)–(5) would move the debate, and in which direction
- "credence" — your rough probability that this diagnosis captures the core of the disagreement, with the numbers in the column summing to about 100
