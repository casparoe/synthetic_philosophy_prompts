I am applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship in philosophy and need a two-page, roughly 1,000-word proposal plus a short abstract. The topic is the nature and value of privacy. I will supply the citations myself, so please do not spend words on a literature review or bibliography; I want the argument, not the map of the literature.

Some personal context, because it determines what I want the project to be able to do. Eighteen months ago my mother was diagnosed with young-onset dementia. Among other things, I am now the person who reads her email, keeps location history switched on for her phone, stays on the line during calls she can no longer reliably follow, and decides what her friends are told about her. She cannot exercise control over any of it, and she could not have anticipated any of it in advance. What I have noticed is that the parts of this I flinch at are not the parts I expected. It is not primarily that her friend now knows she is confused. It is that he knows it without her having said so, and that I am the one who arranged for him to find out. That unevenness — some of my monitoring strikes me as a violation and some as mere care, and I cannot get the standard accounts to sort them the way my own reactions do — is what I want to build on.

My half-formed idea is that the familiar standoff between control-based accounts and access- or knowledge-based accounts persists because both sides locate the wrong of a privacy violation in the same place: in something that happens to an individual, roughly at the moment of the violation. I want to relocate it. Two distinctions I have been playing with, and I suspect there is room for only one in a dissertation chapter:

First, an interest I have been calling authorial privacy: not an interest in whether anyone knows that p about you, but in whether your telling is what makes them know — in being the source of the knowledge about you rather than merely its subject. On this view a loss can occur with no loss of informational privacy at all: the relevant parties may all have known already, and the wrong consists in their having found out from a chart, a log, or a third party rather than from you. Its interest for my case is that it seems to survive the capacity to authorize. Someone who can no longer consent may still be owed what I would call authorship-custody, so that a surrogate's duty is not merely one of confidentiality or of acting in the patient's best interests but something more like narrating on her behalf.

Second, an interest in not being re-visitable: the difference between someone's knowing a coarse-grained fact about you and their holding a record fine-grained enough that they can, at will and in your absence, re-attend to you and reconstruct the texture of a stretch of your life without your cooperation. The wrong is not knowledge but being made available to someone's attention on demand. The objection I cannot yet answer: a lover's or a parent's memory does something structurally similar, and we do not treat a parent's remembering your childhood as a violation; if the view cannot say what is different about a location history, it is dead.

Please turn this into a proposal. I need: (i) a precise statement of one question that has a determinate answer — I would rather have a narrow decidable question than an important vague one, so say explicitly what would settle it and what evidence or argument would count as settling it; (ii) why the existing families of position — control accounts, access accounts, and the contextual or norm-based views — leave that question open, rather than merely disagreeing about it; (iii) the method, month by month across nine months, for a single author with no empirical component and no archival work; (iv) what a completed result would look like, including what the project would be able to say to someone actually designing a monitoring system for dementia care, or writing the policy on one; (v) an honest failure section: what would count as the project having failed, and specifically how I would know if authorial privacy had quietly collapsed into a claim about trust or about being treated as a person rather than as a data source, with no independent work left to do; and (vi) a candid scope assessment, including what I should cut if this is really only a nine-month project.

Please be committed rather than even-handed. Choose which of my two distinctions is worth the chapter and which I should drop, and say why; choose whether the dementia-care case should be the load-bearing central case or only the motivating illustration, given that a project whose only convincing cases involve relationships of dependency will have trouble generalizing to surveillance by states and firms, which is a large part of what makes the topic fundable; and take a view on whether the memory objection is fatal to the re-visitability idea or merely difficult. Where you are making a judgement that is really mine to make, or where your confidence is substantially lower than it sounds, mark it as such rather than smoothing it over.
