I am in my final year of secondary school and I run the philosophy club here — around fifteen members, ages 15 to 18, meeting for a single 75-minute lunch block. Most of the group plays games; four or five don't and came for the arguments. We have one classroom, a whiteboard, a projector and school laptops we are not allowed to install anything on, so whatever we do has to work with paper, cards, dice and people standing up. Please don't point me toward things to read: I want something I could actually run on Tuesday.

Next term I want a session on whether what happens to characters in video games can matter morally — whether hurting a non-player character can be something you ought not to do, and not merely something that reflects badly on your character or risks leaking into how you treat real people. I don't want to hand them that framing in advance. I want them to walk into it.

The thing I keep getting stuck on is the move people reach for when they say game-world violence obviously cannot matter: you can always reload. The harm, if there was any, is undone; nothing is left over; so there was never anything to be wrong about. I think that move is weaker than it looks, for two reasons I want the session to expose.

First, in most games a reload is not an erasure but a branch. The outcome you brought about still happens, in a timeline you then abandon. If you shoot a shopkeeper, take the stock and reload, the shopkeeper you shot doesn't get un-shot — a version of them is dead in the branch you walked away from. The reload doesn't cancel the action; it only relocates you to a branch in which you didn't perform it. Whether that difference makes any moral difference is what I want the group to argue about, and I'd like them to feel both sides rather than be told them.

Second — and this is a stipulation I've invented rather than a fact about any real game — imagine a mechanic I'll call a persistent ledger: the characters keep their memories across reloads. Every time you hurt one of them and rewind, they remember it. Nothing else in the world state carries over; only what they remember. I'm interested in what that does to the reload defence, and in the asymmetry it opens up: the player's memory of the event and the character's memory of it come apart in a way they never do in ordinary life, where hurting someone leaves them with the injury and you with the guilt.

What I'm asking for is a runnable activity for this group, in this room, in 75 minutes.

Give me a game the students actually play, in groups of three or four, using only paper, cards and dice, taking about 30 minutes, with a scoring scheme that creates small real stakes — ideally not money; something like the winning group choosing next term's topics would be fine. The mechanics should be arranged so that they run into the reload question on their own: they should find themselves wanting to rewind, discover that they can, start using it, and only later discover what the rewind did and did not undo — including, at the reveal, that the ledger kept the entries they had assumed were wiped. No lecture before the play, and the phrase moral status shouldn't be spoken before the debrief; ideally the group produces the problem in their own words first. The activity shouldn't depend on gaming fluency, because a quarter of the room has none.

Then give me a 30-minute debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto specific claims: the reload defence; the branch version of it; the thought that it's only code and so there is no one there to be harmed; the character-and-virtue line about what playing does to the player; and the thought that the responsibility really belongs to whoever built the world and made the character killable rather than to the player. For each, say what in the activity actually put pressure on it and what still has to be said out loud.

Be honest about what will predictably go wrong: the group treating it as a party game and playing for points; the it's only pretend shrug deflating the whole thing; someone refusing to hurt the character at all; someone who finds it genuinely upsetting; groups finishing at wildly different speeds; and the deception in the reveal souring their trust in the club.

And be blunt about what the activity cannot reproduce — what playing a real game feels like, the sheer number of characters involved, and above all the metaphysical question of whether a simulated character is the kind of thing that can be harmed at all. Tell me what the facilitator has to supply in words because no game can show it. I'd rather the session ended with a question the room can't settle than with a conclusion.
