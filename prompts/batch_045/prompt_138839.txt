Here's the half-formed idea I keep going in circles on. The standard setup: you have first-order evidence for p, and then you get "higher-order" evidence suggesting that your handling of that evidence was unreliable — a peer disagrees, or you learn you were reasoning while impaired, or you learn your judgment in this domain is badly calibrated. The debate then runs between roughly three camps: the conciliationists (split the difference, move toward the peer, defer to the higher-order stuff), the steadfast types (your first-order evidence can rationally survive, even while you hold the higher-order belief that you're unreliable here), and the people who think there's no distinctively higher-order evidence at all — evidence of evidence is just more evidence, full stop. And everyone worries about the same thing: the "akrasia" of believing p while also believing that your belief in p is untrustworthy.

What I want to argue is that this framing lumps together at least three different things, and most of the puzzle comes from the lumping. Roughly: (a) additive cases, where the new evidence is independent evidence about the same subject matter and aggregation looks fine; (b) diagnostic cases, where what's impugned is the very update you already performed, so you can't just add it in — you already used the compromised process, and re-running it doesn't help because you'd use the same process again (think: you compute your taxes with software, then learn the software had a bug affecting an unidentifiable subset of returns, and you can't inspect the code); (c) self-consuming cases, where the evidence impugns the faculty you'd have to use to evaluate that very evidence. My suspicion is that only type (a) really calls for conciliation, that type (b) is best handled by distinguishing *holding* p from *using* p as a premise in further reasoning (a transmission failure rather than a defeat), and that type (c) may not be rationally respondable to at all rather than being a case where you should do something specific.

So the proposal needs: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate-ish answer; why the three existing positions leave it open; the approach — what I'd actually do week by week over roughly thirteen weeks, including what the first draft would be of and when the taxonomy gets tested against cases; the two or three running example cases I'd use, at least one of which is not a variation on the split restaurant bill or the drunk physician; the possible conclusions and what considerations would decide between them; and what would count as the project failing.
