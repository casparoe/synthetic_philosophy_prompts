I do a column and the occasional click-through interactive for a science magazine, and I want to build one on a question my editors keep telling me is too inside-baseball: does what you believe, expect, want or fear change what you actually see, or does it only change what you say about what you saw — and does it get in at the level of the sensory response itself, or only at some later stage where you're recognising objects or deciding what to call them? I've decided the way to make it work is to stop asking readers which side they're on. Instead: show them a study, walk them through some questions about that study, and drop them on a verdict about what the study showed. Sorting findings, not people. Readers get defensive about which camp their in, but they'll argue all afternoon about whether a particular experiment proved anything.

So I need a decision tree. Rules:

- No more than seven branch nodes and eight leaves.
- Every node has to be a question someone can answer from a one-paragraph description of an experiment. No node that turns on what "perception" really means, no node that asks whether something is "top-down", and no node whose answer depends on already knowing the literature. If a node can't be answered without a measurement the original paper probably didn't report, say so and tell me what it would cost to get it.
- Attention gets handled as a concrete question (did the manipulation change where or how widely people sampled the display, and does the effect survive when that's held fixed?) rather than as a shrug — "it's just attention" is the thing people say to end arguments and I want readers to see what it would take to earn it.
- No naming papers, authors, schools or -isms. Plain leaf labels with a one-line gloss each, and one sentence on the most awkward thing you have to swallow if you land there.

The leaves I want covered, roughly (rename as you like, but don't drop any): the effect is real and it reaches the sensory response itself; it's real but only downstream, at object recognition or categorisation; attention or sampling is doing all the work and the experience never changed; the experience never changed and only the report moved, because of a shifted response criterion or plain demand; the effect is real but it's recalibration — the last few minutes of exposure rewrote the mapping, not a belief you're entertaining right now; there's no fact of the matter, the stimulus was too degraded or ambiguous for "what the experience was like" to have an answer; the question is misposed, because expectation-shaped inference is just what perception is and there's no line where the debate put one; and the artifact leaf, where the cleanest reading is that the difference isn't there.

Here are five studies I've collected, anonymised and slightly compressed — run each one down and tell me which leaf it hits:

1. Hungry and just-fed subjects get the same food-ish picture flashed for 80 ms and have to name it. Hungry subjects name the calorie-dense option more often. At 500 ms the gap is gone.
2. Twenty minutes in prism glasses that shift the world ten degrees, and people's judgements of straight-ahead move. The shift survives a few minutes after the glasses come off and doesn't care whether you tell them the glasses are off.
3. Subjects are told a blurred shape is a duck or a rabbit, truthfully or not. At extreme blur, report follows the label. At mild blur, everyone gets it right regardless of what they were told.
4. People told a tone will be paired with a shock rate a neutral tone as louder. Their ability to tell two tones apart doesn't budge; the share of "yes, louder" responses does.
5. Radiologists and non-radiologists get a chest image for 200 ms. When told the patient is symptomatic, the radiologists report a nodule that isn't there more often — and their eye movements also differ from the non-radiologists' in a way that nobody controlled for.

What I want back: the tree; a reader-facing version of each node question in under twenty words with no jargon; where each of the five lands; at least one of them getting stuck between two leaves, with the extra experiment that would unstick it; the answer-combinations your tree allows that nobody occupies, with your read on whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely untried; any pair of leaves the tree can't separate even in principle, flagged rather than fudged; and which single node is carrying the most weight. Build it from the space of questions first and drop the positions into it, not the other way round.
