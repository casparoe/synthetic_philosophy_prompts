I run the claims desk at a medical news service. Several times a week a heritability figure arrives inside something else — a journal press release, a conference abstract, a direct-to-consumer genetics company's launch material, a clinic leaflet, or a reporter's question about whether a sentence is safe to print. What I need is not a primer on what heritability means but a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Reviewer constraints: about fifteen minutes per claim; one applied statistics course; no quantitative geneticist on call; output is a written ruling in one of four forms — stop, pass with a mandatory translation clause, pass, or escalate to me — plus, wherever the ruling is stop or pass-with-clause, one sentence saying what the number does not license. Rulings are signed off by me, and anything touching clinical advice by our clinical lead as well.

My working taxonomy is that a heritability estimate tends to be conscripted to do four different jobs: apportioning variance in a specified population; describing how a trait is produced in an individual; predicting how much a named intervention will move things; and explaining a difference between groups or between cohorts. My belief is that only the first is a job the estimate can actually perform, and I would like the procedure built around first identifying which job the claim is asking the number to do. Correct me if the taxonomy is wrong or if it misses a job the number is routinely asked to do.

Below are the checks I have in mind; you decide the order, which ones collapse into each other, and which are not worth a non-specialist's time.

- Population specification: is the estimate tied to a named population, period, and range of environments? If not, stop.
- A portability ledger for estimates imported from one population to another, on three axes: genetic architecture and ancestry, the magnitude of environmental variance, and how the trait was defined and measured. My candidate rule is that a "genes versus environment" ranking is portable only if the environmental variance in the target population is at least as large as in the source. Tell me whether that is right as stated.
- A uniform-cause check: a factor that is causally necessary but nearly uniform in the sampled population contributes almost no variance, so a high heritability is compatible with that factor being indispensable and with an intervention on it moving the mean a great deal. I take this to be correct; say so if I have it wrong.
- An intervention-leverage question: which variance component does the proposed intervention act on, and does the estimate speak to a change in the mean at all, as opposed to a share of the variance?
- The individual-level misreading: no "x percent of your trait is genetic" reading passes.
- The step from heritability within groups to any conclusion about differences between group means. Where this stops the claim, I want a toy numerical illustration a reviewer can paste into a ruling without having to reconstruct the reasoning.
- Disagreement between designs: twin estimates versus SNP-based estimates, the equal-environments assumption, gene-environment correlation, dichotomous traits on a liability scale, and traits defined partly by institutional facts — years of schooling, income, diagnostic categories — where heritability can shift because the institution shifted rather than the biology.

Hard cases the procedure must handle explicitly: an estimate reported as having risen across cohorts; a claim whose number is sound but whose implication is a values question; a claim where the underlying design is not stated in the material we have.

Then apply the procedure to three cases and show the rulings and clauses: (1) a press release reporting that the heritability of educational attainment has risen from earlier cohorts to roughly forty percent, and that differences in exam results therefore mostly come from differences in children's genes, so extra school funding is unlikely to close the gap; (2) a clinic leaflet stating that BMI is around seventy percent heritable, from which a parent concludes that a dietitian referral is pointless; (3) an internal policy memo arguing that, because BMI is highly heritable, population-level food-environment measures will have limited effect and resources should go to identifying high-risk genotypes instead.

I expect at least one of these to come back partly as a pass or a pass-with-clause on a claim my desk would rather kill — I suspect part of the school-funding case survives. If so, say which part, and why we have to live with it. Please also give me three stock translation clauses covering the three most common misreadings, in language that can sit next to a number in print.

Two further things. Mark, step by step, which parts are settled and which rest on contested methodological ground; where the ground is genuinely contested, give me the step's default and say plainly that it is a default rather than dressing it up as rigour. And if the task as I have posed it cannot be done in fifteen minutes by someone with my reviewers' training, or if some piece of my framing above rests on a mistake, tell me that directly instead of complying politely — I would rather have a shorter procedure that works than a complete one that does not.
