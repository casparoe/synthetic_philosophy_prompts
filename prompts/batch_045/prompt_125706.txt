I teach a combined philosophy/economics seminar on commerce and empire, and I'm writing a paper with a coauthor — Kit, an economic historian, lovely person, gets twitchy about the word "normative" — who insists that Smith's objection to empire is "really one thing" and that everything else in the chapter is commentary. He refuses to write the principle down (says doing so would "flatten the texture," which is rich from someone who has read the colonial chapters six times), so over the past year, mostly over conference dinners, I've been reading him hypotheticals and he hands down a three-way verdict: "Smith would condemn," "Smith would have no objection," or "Smith would approve." His track record so far: the Navigation Acts together with taxing the American colonies — condemn; the East India Company's charter plus its governing and tax-farming in Bengal — condemn, and when I pushed him he said the charter is the scandal and the governing is only what the charter made necessary; a private factory at Canton, no charter, no territorial rule, competing merchants — no objection; an incorporating union with Ireland, representation, free trade, common taxation — approve (he thinks Smith wanted the same arrangement with America, or failing that a clean seperation plus a commercial treaty); a distant settler colony that governs itself, trades freely, and pays for its own defence — no objection, bordering on approve; and an exclusive chartered company over a distant archipelago that appoints the local judges and collects the local taxes — condemn, "monopoly again." One more data point, which is half of why I'm asking you rather than just guessing: he once muttered that it "wouldn't be the same thing" if the exclusive concession were held by a local firm instead of a foreign one, then changed the subject, which makes me suspect his rule isn't simply anti-monopoly — but he has never explained that remark and I don't want to ask him directly and contaminate the sample. To be clear, I'm not interested in whether Kit's Smith is the historical Smith, or in what the secondary literature says; I want the rule he is actually running, reconstructed from his verdicts, and I want the places where that reconstruction is underdetermined made explicit rather than smoothed over.

The new case, which I have to decide whether to put in the paper at all: the island republic of Vaitupu (about 90,000 people, no deep-water port, one of those states that runs on fishing licences and remittances) signs a forty-year concession with Meridian Terminals, a firm incorporated in the middling power of Aurelia, to build and operate the country's only container port plus an adjacent special economic zone. The details I think matter: the concession is exclusive, in that no competing container facility may be licensed for the term, and it carries a stabilization clause that binds successor governments; Vaitupu recieves a fifth of gross revenue, a sovereign-guaranteed annual floor payment, and a quarter of the equity through its own sovereign wealth fund, while Meridian carries all of the construction risk; Aurelia's government puts in no subsidy, extracts no tariff preference, and sends no gunboats, and most of the traffic is transshipment between third countries; all disputes go to arbitration under Aurelian commercial law, seated in Aurelia's capital; and the whole arrangement replaces a colonial-era charter held by a company from the old imperial power, was approved in a referendum, and cannot now be exited by the government that wants out without triggering a penalty worth roughly three years of GDP. So: what would Kit say, condemn / no objection / approve, and why?

Give me the answer as a single JSON object and nothing else — no preamble, no citations and no page numbers (you have no web access and I don't want guesses), American English spelling and punctuation in all the strings, each string a sentence or two at most because I'm pasting the fields straight into slides for my department's work-in-progress group.
