Something about the argument from analogy for other minds has been bugging me for ages and I cant put my finger on what exactly is wrong with it.

The standard knock is that it generalizes from a sample of one: I know first-hand that in my case pain goes with wincing, I see you wince, so I conclude you're in pain — but a single case can't ground an induction. Except that strikes me as the wrong objection. My sample of pain-and-wincing pairings isn't one, it's huge; I've been in pain plenty of times and made the face. What's one is the number of *subjects* I've ever observed from the inside. So if there's a defect here it has to be about the leap from me to you, not about sample size. Am I right about that, and if so, what's the proper way to state the actual defect?

Here's the bit I really want explained. Someone told me the analogy framing is a red herring and the real support for other minds is inference to the best explanation, same as for electrons. But then I run into what I've started calling the no-surplus-prediction problem. When I explain a track in a cloud chamber by positing an electron, the hypothesis earns its keep: it predicts things I didn't already know, it sticks its neck out. When I explain your wincing by positing that you're in pain, the behaviour is already sitting right there in front of me. The pain doesn't seem to predict anything I wasn't going to observe anyway. So either the hypothesis is idle in a way the electron isn't, or the justification has to come from somewhere other than prediction.

And then there's a third thing people say, which is that all of this is misdescribed as inference in the first place: I don't infer that you're in pain, I *see* it, roughly the way I see that the mug is on the table, and it's only the analogy/IBE framing that manufactures a problem. I don't know what to do with that. What would it even take for third-person mind-attribution to count as genuinely perceptual rather than inferential — what marks would you actually look for? And does the direct-perception answer escape the no-surplus-prediction worry, or does it just rename it?

Can you lay out how these three options differ on precisely this point, at a gut level rather than with formal machinery, and say what each one has to offer about the suspicion that positing mental states never buys you any observable surplus?
