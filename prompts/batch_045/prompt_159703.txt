I spent forty years in experimental condensed-matter physics and now spend Thursday afternoons with a reading group of retired scientists — mostly physicists, one control engineer, one crystallographer — working through philosophy we missed the first time round. We have reached a standstill on free will, and I suspect the standstill is diagnostic rather than merely stubborn: different members of the group are answering different questions, and each thinks the others have simply got the answer wrong. I should therefore be grateful for a decision tree that sorts a person by what they say about cases, and only then tells them which position they have landed in.

The starting point should be a premise the whole group already accepts, so that the tree does not waste branches on it. Grant, for the sake of argument, that the fundamental dynamics is deterministic at the level on which the special sciences supervene; that macroscopic behaviour is in practice unpredictable because sensitive dependence on initial conditions amplifies what we cannot measure; and that such indeterminacy as quantum mechanics supplies is noise of the sort no one has ever seriously wanted to identify with agency. Begin the tree there, not at "is determinism true?".

What I want specifically:

A tree of ten branch questions, each phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim about a case, and each answerable by someone who has never heard the labels — a control engineer should be able to answer every node, and no node may simply ask whether the person is a compatibilist. Build it from the logical space rather than from a roster of names: set out the questions, note which combinations of answers are jointly coherent, then locate the named positions in that space as leaves, and mark the cells that turn out to be unoccupied with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Among the cases the tree must use, please include: a Frankfurt-style arrangement in which a monitor would have overridden the agent's decision but never does, and the agent's own deliberative process is deterministic; a manipulation case in which an agent's deepest values are installed by a process nobody chose, after which the agent reasons and acts impeccably by their own lights; a case I have been pushing on the group, which I do not think appears in the literature — a trained artificial controller whose policy was shaped entirely by gradient descent on an objective function nobody explicitly endorsed, which nevertheless passes every test of sensitivity to reasons we care to apply, in an environment it navigates successfully; a case that separates determinism from predictability, so that my colleagues cannot conflate them; a case in which the indeterminism sits in the motor system, so that the agent's arm might have moved otherwise for no reason at all; and a case in which an agent acts for reasons they wholeheartedly endorse, while their capacities to form such reasons are entirely the product of upbringing and reinforcement no one selected.

Two further requirements, which are the ones I care about most. First, the group's disagreements seem to turn on what is supposed to be at stake, so at each leaf state which of three things survives on that view: whether the action can still be attributed to the agent as revealing what they are like; whether the agent remains someone who can be asked to justify the action and reasoned with; and whether the agent can deserve blame or punishment in a backward-looking sense. Second, I have a proposal of my own that I want placed rather than praised: what matters, I have argued, is whether the actual causal process producing the action was counterfactually sensitive to the agent's reasons in the right way, regardless of whether any alternative sequence of events was genuinely available. Tell me whether this already has a name, where it sits in your tree, and — honestly — whether it is genuinely distinct from the leaf it lands on or merely a redescription of it.

Please also flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate by cases at all, rather than inventing a branch to disguise the fact; I would rather know where the instrument is blunt. Give eight or nine leaves, each with its standard name where one exists (or "unnamed" where it does not), and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

Two practical notes. Several of us read offline and have no library access, so the answer must be self-contained: no citations, bibliographies or reading lists, and no appeal to what some author argues at length — positions should be characterised by their content, even if you name them. And please write the whole thing in British English. I imagine something in the region of 1,200 words, though I would rather have it right than have it short.
