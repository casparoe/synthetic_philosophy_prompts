I was at a bbq last night where two friends got properly stuck into an argument about a puzzle one of them had read about: a sculptor takes a lump of clay that's been sitting in her studio for years and shapes it into a statue. Is the statue and the lump of clay one object or two? They were each making points that sounded good to me and I honestly couldnt tell who was getting the better of it, and by the end I suspected they were talking past eachother a bit.

(1) Bea: one object. Same matter, same place, same shape, same weight. If there were literally two things on the pedestal then the pedestal would be holding up 20kg instead of 10, and the sculptor would have conjured a brand new object into existence out of nothing, since nothing was added to the world — the matter was only rearranged.

(2) Owen: two objects. Squash it into a ball and the clay survives while the statue doesn't, so there's something one of them can survive that the other can't; things that are identical can't differ like that, so they aren't identical. Besides, the clay was sitting in the studio long before there was any statue, so they don't even have the same history.

(3) Bea: both of those confuse the object with the phase it's in. "Statue" works like "boy" — the thing that's now a statue was a shapeless lump before and can go on existing as a ball afterwards, in the way the boy existed before the man did. So nothing new comes into existence when you rearrange matter, and Leibniz's law never gets a grip, because the difference in what-can-survive-what is a difference between phases, not between objects.

(4) Owen: the boy/man comparison only works because one sortal is in charge of persistence across the whole career, and here there isn't one. The lump survives being squashed but not having all its clay swapped out; the statue survives having its matter swapped out but not being squashed. Each one is behaving like the sortal that settles what persists, so it's two objects that happen to coincide. And the 20kg point is a dud — two objects sharing all of their matter don't weigh double, because weight tracks matter, not how many objects there are.

Can you go through it move by move and say whether each of the four is right, roughly right, or wrong, and why — and then say what both of them are missing? I'd like exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each: one per move, plus a fifth on what neither of them noticed. No intro and no summing-up sentence at the end.
