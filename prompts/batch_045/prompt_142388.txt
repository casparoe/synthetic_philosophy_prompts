I chair the weekly signal-triage meeting at a regional public health agency. Every Monday me and five others (two epidemiologists, a lab lead, a comms person, and a rotating field officer) spend 45 minutes deciding whether each flagged signal — a cluster of lab results, a spike in school absenteeism, a handful of GP calls — gets sent to field investigation or gets closed. We get eight or so a week and investigations are expensive, so most get closed. I sign off on the closes myself; anything above a certain cost goes to the director. About six months later we usually find out whether we were right, so I can score last year's calls.

I can tell we herd. I speak first because I'm the chair and I'v read the briefing, and by the third or fourth signal everyone is just saying "agree". What I want is a procedure I can actually run in the room: questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values question (what a false alarm costs us vs what a missed outbreak costs) rather than a facts question, and when a case should go up to the director undecided instead of being settled in the room. It has to work at about two minutes a signal, so tell me which step is the cheap screen that clears most of them, and which step only runs on the one or two near the threshold.

Three things about the room that I think break the textbook story, and I want the procedure to deal with them rather than assume them away: we already do an anonymous written poll before the meeting that I read out at the start, and I genuinely don't know whether that helps or just starts the cascade earlier; we have a standing devil's advocate whose job is to argue against whatever I say, so her dissent carries no information any more; and two of my members look at the same dashboard I do, so when they "agree" with me it's often not an independent check at all, while the field officer usually has something nobody else has. Tell me roughly how much to discount an agreement from someone whose information overlaps mine — I don't need a formula, but "count it as nothing" and "count it as half" are very different instructions.

Please also keep seperate three things I keep conflating: a real cascade, honest convergence because everyone saw the same genuinely strong evidence, and a value disagreement pretending to be a factual one. Say what I should do differently in each.

Then run it on three cases. (1) The pre-meeting poll split 4–2 to investigate, I opened by saying I thought it was noise, everyone then said close, and the field officer said nothing but mentioned afterwards that she'd heard from a local pharmacist about a second school. (2) The same confirmed strain in three patients with no link between them — I want the procedure to tell me not to bother with whatever anti-cascade ritual you invent, because I can't waste the meeting on unanimous cases; show me the 30-second screen that clears it. (3) The one I'll hate: I'm personally about 90% sure a signal is a real outbreak, I've seen the pattern before, and the room is lukewarm. Show the procedure telling me that my gut isn't independent evidence and that I either shut up or lose the vote — and then tell me honestly whether that's right or whether the procedure is just bullying me.

Finally, flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say plainly what a procedure like this cannot do for me. Don't point me at papers or reading lists — I want something I can run on Monday.
