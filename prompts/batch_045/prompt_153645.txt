I'm in an undergraduate seminar on just war theory, and last week we discussed the "supreme emergency" exemption: the idea that a community facing imminent and catastrophic defeat may do what the in bello rules otherwise forbid, with the area bombing of German cities in 1940–42, when Britain stood effectively alone, as the stock example. In the middle of that discussion, one student defended the bombing by saying, "well, all's fair in love and war," and our TA replied that the proverb hides several quite different claims and that at most one of them could do the work its user needed. Could you lay those readings out as a menu — for instance, that in bello rules are mere conventions that lapse under enough pressure; that military necessity simply overrides them; that the acts in question are not permitted but excused, the "dirty hands" reading; that "fair" means symmetric rather than unlimited, i.e. that combatants on both sides have the same permissions whatever the justice of their cause; or that in extremis the combatant/non-combatant distinction itself gives way — and for each say whether it is a platitude, defensible but contentious, or straightforwardly false, and whether it actually gets you to the conclusion it was being used for, namely that deliberately targeting civilians in that case was permissible rather than merely excusable or tragic? I'd like to know which reading is doing the real argumentative work and which ones are only gesturing at it.
