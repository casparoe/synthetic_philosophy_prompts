I'm in the final year of a joint maths-and-philosophy programme, and this term I'm taking a small seminar on probabilistic reasoning where the assessment is a paper of about 7000 words, due at the end of a twelve-week term, preceded by a proposal of roughly 900 words that my supervisor has to sign off on. Last year I tried to write something on the principle of indifference and got the comment that I had managed to write about everything and settle nothing, so this time I want a question narrow enough to have a determinate answer. I'd like you to draft the proposal.

Here is the half-formed thing I keep turning over. There is a very common style of argument that estimates a probability, a count, or an asymptotic frequency by treating a family of events as independent when nobody has shown that they are: a heuristic argument in that sense. Philosophically, the presumption of independence seems to get handled as a binary — either independence is a default that holds until defeated, or it is an ordinary empirical hypothesis to be confirmed or refuted — and neither option tells you what I actually want to know, namely how much of the conclusion is carried by the presumption and which way the error would go if the presumption is mildly wrong. My idea is to replace the binary with a parameterised family: let the joint distribution of the events be a mixture that with weight one minus theta is the fully independent product and with weight theta is a maximally coupled or comonotone alternative, so that with probability theta a single latent driver settles all of them together. Every heuristic conclusion then becomes a function of theta, and there is a critical value theta-star at which the conclusion reverses or crosses whatever threshold matters; call that the fragility index of the argument. Two things make this more than bookkeeping. First, the direction of the error for small theta seems to be fixed in advance by whether the conclusion is an increasing or a decreasing function of the events, so that a union-type conclusion such as at least one of these happening is pushed one way by any positive coupling and an intersection-type conclusion such as all of these happening is pushed the other way, which suggests a short monotonicity lemma instead of a case-by-case slog. Second, coupling comes in at least two shapes: local, where each event interacts mainly with its neighbours, and global, where one common driver fixes the whole family at once. My suspicion, which is the part I most want the proposal to sharpen, is that heuristic arguments fail in characteristic and different ways depending on which shape they involve, because local coupling can often be absorbed into a correction factor that leaves the architecture of the argument intact, whereas global coupling cannot be absorbed at all and instead silently converts many events into effectively one.

The three cases I want the proposal to build around are a Cramér-style random model for the primes and why its naive version needs correction factors for divisibility by small primes, the molecular chaos or Stosszahlansatz assumption in Boltzmann's H-theorem where independence is presumed repeatedly and then has to be re-earned in the face of correlations the dynamics itself creates, and the many-universe move in fine-tuning arguments where the probability that at least one universe is life-permitting is computed as if the constants were drawn independently when the natural alternative is that one theory fixes all of them together. I may be garbling some of the history and the technical details, so please do not rest anything on my sketch of them and flag it if a case looks unusable. What I need from the document is: the question stated precisely enough that a reader could tell which answer the paper reached; why the existing ways of handling the presumption of independence leave it open; the method, laid out month by month across the twelve weeks, and why this mixture-parameter approach rather than the obvious alternatives of doing sensitivity analysis over the whole space of joint distributions or just treating independence as an ordinary testable empirical assumption; the two or three possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; what the deliverable would be beyond prose, which I am imagining as fragility indices computed for the three cases plus a short checklist someone could apply to a new heuristic argument; and an honest statement of what would count as failure. I take failure to include at least these: if theta-star comes out at essentially zero or essentially one for every real case, so the index carries no information; if the local versus global distinction cannot be made precise without smuggling in a causal assumption I would then have to defend; and if the monotonicity lemma needs conditions the actual cases do not satisfy. Please also give an honest assessment of what could make this too big for one term, write it in formal prose with headings if that helps, and do not include any bibliography or literature review by citation, since I will fill those in myself.
