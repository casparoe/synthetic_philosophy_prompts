I am reworking a graduate seminar session on cooperation in one-shot games, and before I put the following reconstruction in front of students I would like it checked, because I suspect that one or two steps in it are doing more work than they can bear.

My reconstruction is this. Hofstadter's superrationality settles the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. The superrational player notices that any two rational agents faced with the same problem and the same information must arrive at the same conclusion; so, in effect, her choice is everyone's choice. Comparing the two symmetric outcomes — everyone defects versus everyone cooperates — she sees that the latter is better for her, and she cooperates. As I read it, this is nothing more than evidential decision theory applied to the case where the other player is a copy of me: the entire weight of the argument falls on whether the two decisions are correlated, and since a copy and I are physically identical, microphysics guarantees that they are. Causal decision theorists, who defect in the one-shot game against a copy, are therefore not offering a rival conception of rationality; they are simply failing to notice a correlation that is there, and leaving utility on the table. If that is right, my plan is to teach causal decision theory as refuted, present superrationality — or its later development as functional decision theory — as the correct theory, and tell the seminar that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma the rational answer is cooperation, full stop, and against any opponent, since nothing in the argument mentions the opponent's character.

There are two extensions of this that I also intend to assert, and I would like them checked at the same time. First, a colleague's gloss that I am inclined to repeat: superrationality is just Kant's categorical imperative fitted with a payoff matrix, so the real content of the idea is not new to moral philosophy at all, and the decision-theory literature has been reinventing an old wheel. Second, the variant in which only one of the players' moves is actually implemented — say twenty players, a prize awarded to the one player who acts, and awarded only if no one else acts — seems to me to have exactly the same structure: what does the work is the correlation between the players' reasoning, not whether every move is played out. So I cannot see why any randomized strategy would have a role in that variant, and I mean to tell the seminar that the suggestion that one does is a confusion.

Please tell me, carefully and without granting my reconstruction, where it goes wrong and what the corrected version looks like. I would rather be corrected now than in front of thirty graduate students. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I want the argument itself.
