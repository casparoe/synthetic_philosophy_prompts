I need to rehearse an argument I am about to have in a real meeting, and I would like you to take the other side of it.

Background, because you will need it: I am one of six people drafting the founding charter of a small research cooperative — a shared lab and a modest reserve, funded by a donor who died last year and left the money to the project rather than to us. We expect to admit something like fifty more members over the next three years, at which point the six of us will be a small and dwindling minority. Two years ago I sat on the board of a comparable organization that voted, in a single afternoon meeting, to spend its entire reserve on a project most members privately thought was a mistake; a year later nearly everyone wanted it back. That episode is why I am the one pushing for an entrenchment clause, and it is also why a colleague said at our last meeting, half in jest, that I only want to bind the future because I can see that the future will not be mine.

The clause I have drafted is deliberately weak, and I want to be held to that. Call it the two-key rule: the mission-and-reserve article may be changed only by two majority votes of the full membership taken at least ninety days apart, with a written statement of reasons circulated to every member in between. No supermajority, no external trustee, no minority veto, no subject the future is forbidden to reach — and the procedure attaches to any change in the substance of that article however it is drafted, so it cannot be evaded by repealing and reenacting the charter as a whole. A majority that still wants the change after three months and a circulated argument simply has it.

Please play a committed presentist about constitutional precommitment: someone who holds that no body of people may deliberately raise the cost to their successors of reversing the founders' substantive choices, and that whoever is living and a member now has full authority to decide now. I want the sharp version of this position, not the cartoon. Four things about how I want you to hold it:

You accept quickly, without argument, that the founders must adopt *some* procedure; that ordinary rules of order are unproblematic; that cooling-off periods and deliberation-forcing rules are often a good idea; and that supermajority entrenchment of policy is far worse than what I have proposed. Concede these in a clause and move on.

You never concede the main point. Your settled view is that a founding majority may not deliberately make its own substantive judgments harder for a later majority to undo, and that the Ulysses story gives me no help at all, because Ulysses bound *himself* whereas a charter binds *other people who were not in the room*. You think the gap between the intrapersonal case and the interpersonal case is where the whole debate is decided, and you will keep returning to it.

You have my colleague's suspicion available to you — that this is the founding six protecting their own influence under cover of a principle — and I would rather you use it than spare me. If any fact about the co-op would change your answer, ask for it in character.

One or two paragraphs per turn, plain and direct, no hedging for the sake of hedging. Stay in character until I type stop; anything you want to say as the model rather than as the character goes in square brackets at the end of your reply.

Here is my opening move. The clause blocks no one: whatever the future membership wants, it can have, and I have closed the repeal-and-re-enact route, so this is not a lock that comes with a spare key hidden nearby. All it does is make the cost of change vary with how durable the majority's desire is. But that is exactly what Ulysses arranged, and notice how thin that arrangement was. The ropes left his desires entirely untouched; they changed only what he could do about them; and they did not make release impossible, since the crew could always have cut them. What they did was make release depend on overriding an instruction he had given in advance and would, at the moment of asking, have desperately wanted withdrawn. My rule has the same shape and is weaker still. So you owe me one of two things: either concede that it is permissible — in which case the dead-hand objection turns out to have much less reach than it is usually credited with, and most of what people call entrenchment is objectionable for reasons that have nothing to do with binding successors — or draw the line that excludes it and show why that line spares the ordinary procedural rules you have already conceded. Say plainly which way you go.
