Hey — I run a film-and-philosophy night at the public library, maybe twenty people, almost none of them philosophy readers, though there are two retired engineers who will happily derail the whole discussion if I leave any gap. Next month is Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve one; a few of the group have read the Chiang novella it's based on, most haven't, so please stick to the film as film). I want to run it on future contingents rather than the usual free-will-and-foreknowledge thing, and I want to know whether that actually works or whether I'm just smuggling in the same topic with a fancier name.

Here's the thing I keep coming back too. Everyone talks about this movie as if its question is: if you could see your future, would you try to change it? But Aristotle's worry in the sea-battle chapter of On Interpretation isn't about what anyone can do with foreknowledge — it's about whether there is already, today, a fact of the matter about a battle that nothing present settles. Those feel like different problems to me, and I suspect the film only looks like it addresses the second because it's so good at staging the first. Am I wrong to separate them, and if I'm right, does the film have anything real to say about the truth-value question at all, or does it just borrow its atmosphere?

The wrinkle I want to put to you, since I haven't see anyone make it: Louise's foreknowledge isn't inferential. She isn't weighing evidence and extrapolating. She learns the alien language and afterwards her awareness of her own future has the shape of memory — it arrives with the phenomenology of something recalled rather than something deduced, including stuff she'd have no way of reasoning to. Call it anamnetic foreknowledge. Why I think that matters: on the views that say the open future has no determinate truth-value yet, prediction can at least be modeled as a guess or a hope about something unsettled. But memory of a future that isn't settled is a much stranger object — it seems to need either a block universe where the later event is just there to be accessed, or some new category of quasi-memory that doesn't require the 'remembered' event to have happened, or be going to happen, at all. So: does anamnetic foreknowledge push the film into a stronger metaphysical commitment than the standard compatibilist reading gives it credit for, or can an open-future theorist quietly absorb it? And does the emotional payoff — Louise embracing having her daughter anyway — secretly depend on the future being settled in a way the film never argues for?

Then the practical bit: which two or three scenes would you make the group sit with, and where does the film resist this reading? I'd rather you told me now where my hobbyhorse is confused than let me walk in with it and get taken apart by the engineers.
