I help run a community energy co-op in a village — about 140 households. We bulk-buy wood pellets and heating oil together, we own a village hall with a knackered oil boiler, and every year the same argument eats a whole evening of the AGM. Half the room says some version of "my household's bit makes no difference, the truck comes either way, the hall's emissions are a rounding error next to anything real." The other half says some version of "that's not the point — it's about whether you're part of it." I've noticed I personally keep saying the second thing and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a position or just a mood.

So I want to make an A3 flowchart poster for the AGM, with a decision tree that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which slogan they like, and then tells each of them, honestly, what their answers commit them to about the three things we're actually voting on: (1) replace the hall boiler now vs. run it into the ground, (2) merge our pellet order with the next valley's co-op so we clear the supplier's 12-tonne minimum in one lorry instead of two, (3) whether the co-op should buy offsets for the hall.

What I want from you: build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of yes/no answers they permit — and only then drop the actual named positions into the cells that are occupied, labelling any empty cell and saying in a line whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to defend it. Max 10 branch questions, max 7 leaves. Every node has to be phrased so someone who has never read a word of philosophy can answer it — concrete, about our lorry or our boiler or the hall, no theorist's names and no labels like "expected value" or "complicity" in the questions themselves.

Some cases I want used, because they're the ones that actually come up and they don't feel like they should all get the same answer:

- the ordinary case: the lorry comes, the pallets are rounded to whole pallets, our household's tonne changes nothing at all this year;
- a genuine threshold: the 12-tonne minimum — if we drop below it the supplier won't come at all, and our village alone is hovering right at it;
- a threshold that isn't one: the supplier have told us their delivery schedule is set off a rolling three-year average of our orders, so if we skip this year no truck is cancelled and no price changes, it just nudges next year's schedule by some fraction of a pallet, and less than a pallet, and so on;
- a lockstep case: the shared lorry only runs if both valleys order, so my household matters only together with a stranger's decision;
- an overdetermined case: the hall boiler is going to be replaced anyway by a grant that's already approved, and my "yes" vote is the 90th of 140 in favour;
- a case where the whole point is that nothing causal happens: imagine you find out for certain, from the supplier and the council both, that your not buying changes no emission, no price, and nobody's behaviour — I want a node that separates the people for whom that settles it from the people for whom it doesn't even touch the sides.

Leaves should include at least: the view that there's genuinely no duty in the no-threshold cases; the view that a tiny chance of crossing some threshold is enough to make the act worth doing when the stakes are big; the view that duties only bite where there's an actual trigger or rounding point; some non-difference-making view about being a participant in a practice the group is running; and — if it survives the tree — whatever it is I'm gesturing at when I say it's about the kind of household you are. Where two leaves keep collapsing into the same answer on all six cases, say so and tell me which extra case would pry them apart, or admit that nothing would.

Last thing: each leaf gets one line on the most awkward thing it commits a person to saying out loud at our AGM. If my own mood turns out to be a leaf, don't be gentle with it.
