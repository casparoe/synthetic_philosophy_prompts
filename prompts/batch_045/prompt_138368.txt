Someone at my philosophy society closed a discussion of free will the other week with: "Chaos theory settled all this — a butterfly flapping its wings can bring about a tornado weeks later, so the universe isn't the clockwork Laplace described, and determinism is dead." I want to take the one-liner apart rather than the argument. It seems to me it could be claiming several quite different things: that tiny causes sometimes have very large effects; that nearby states of a system separate at an exponential rate, so that no achievable precision of measurement allows prediction beyond a finite horizon; that a wholly deterministic process can nonetheless be statistically indistinguishable from a random one; or merely that some systems are unpredictable in practice although their equations single out exactly one future. Please set out, as a list, the distinct precise claims the line might be expressing — including, if it belongs on the list, a reading on which the amplification is restricted to perturbations along particular directions in the state space rather than to every minute difference, and a reading on which what is fragile is the model itself rather than the initial data. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or straightforwardly false, and whether it concerns determinism or only prediction. Then tell me which of those readings would have to be true for my interlocutor's conclusion to follow, whether anything on the list really threatens determinism rather than forecastability, and why the line is so often heard as a claim about the one when it is at best a claim about the other.
