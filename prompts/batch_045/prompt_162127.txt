Working on a short piece about everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking — and about whether a visitor has to play by the local version. I want to compare two accounts of where that obligation comes from, and I want the comparison at the level of the mechanism, not just the verdict.

Call the first one scheme-membership: a queue or a tipping practice is a local distributive scheme; you enter it by joining the line or sitting down at the table; and you thereby owe your assigned share — your turn in line, the 15 percent — to the other participants. On this account the obligation tracks participation rather than the merits of the convention, so it looks like it survives even when you think the convention is silly.

Call the second expectation-dependence: what you owe is settled by what the people around you can reasonably count on you doing, so the obligation can fade out when nobody is relying on you, and can be undercut when the expectations themselves are objectionable.

Two wrinkles I want the comparison to earn it's keep on. First, the transient asymmetry: a visitor can always leave, and while she's there she dosen't have to live with the convention's long run effects — she doesn't get a say in how it evolves and won't be the one stuck with it. Does either account make her less bound than a resident, or more bound, and is whichever it is a feature or a bug? Second, a fork I can't get past: a lot of these conventions aren't neutral coordination devices but mildly extractive ones — the tip is really a wage subsidy — so complying marginally sustains an arrangement you think is bad, while defecting on your own just takes money off one particular server and changes nothing about the arrangement. Say which account handles that structure better and what exactly stops the other one from handling it.

Also: where do the two actually come apart in verdict, not just in style? If they mostly converge, say so and tell me which one is doing the real explanatory work. And there's a signalling bit that seems to fit neither — the same non-compliance reads as protest coming from a resident and as contempt coming from a visitor — tell me whether that's a third consideration both accounts have to make room for, or something one of them should absorb and the other can safely ignore.
