I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-sized magazine, and an ever-larger chunk of what I do is blaming people in print: a journal that sat on a retraction for fourteen months, a university that quietly buried a study its funder disliked, a research centre that took money from an industry body and left it out of the disclosures. My editor and I end up having the same argument roughly every week, and it always has the same shape: someone in the letters page or the comments says "you're one to talk" — our own corrections policy is a shambles, we run sponsored explainers paid for by companies whose research I criticise, I have taken the free flight to the conference — and we have no principled way of telling when that tu quoque actually bites and when it is just noise. I've skimmed a bit of the philosophy-of-blame stuff myself (standing, hypocrisy, the idea that you can forfeit the right to blame by being implicated in the very thing you're blaming), but nobody else on staff has, I can't send my editor to a seminar, and please don't send me a reading list or footnotes — I need something I can use on a Wednesday afternoon.

So: write me a decision procedure my editor and I can actually run, in fifteen or twenty minutes, on a piece that is about to blame somebody. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure simply stops with a no, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. My working heuristic up to now has been a "mirror test" — if the target could run my sentence back at me with the names swapped and it would sting, I don't have standing — and my editor's is that a "full disclosure: we've been guilty of this too" note at the top fixes everything. I want the procedure to say what's wrong with both of those, and where they're actually not bad. It has to handle: blame laundered through a quoted source ("Dr X called the delay indefensible") — does quoting preserve standing, destroy it, or count as endorsing it, and when does the quoting stop being reporting and start being blaming; complicity-by-benefit (my salary, the flights, the owner's portfolio) versus complicity-by-agency (I personally did the thing); unsigned institutional blame versus my own byline; blaming for a structural or aggregate harm where no single person's individual act is wrong; and the second-order case where the thing I'm blaming is itself hypocrisy. Also whether it matters if the blame targets a practice rather than a person, and whether "you are a hypocrite" is a different moral act from "that was wrong."

Then run it on three pieces I have on the go: one calling out a university press and its oversight committee for slow, opaque retractions when my own outlet has quietly rewritten a piece after a complaint without noting it and has never once run a correction; one on a climate research centre taking money from an energy trade group, when my magazine's parent company puts on a "future of energy" conference series with oil and gas sponsors (I did once take a junket flight, and I did disclose it, which may or may not help); and one on a medical journal's conflicts-of-interest failures where every harsh phrase is inside quotation marks from clinicians I interviewed and our health coverage is underwritten by a foundation that holds hospital stock. At least one of those, I suspect, is going to come back with an answer I don't like, and I'd rather find out from you than from the letters page. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and end by saying what the procedure can't do at all.
