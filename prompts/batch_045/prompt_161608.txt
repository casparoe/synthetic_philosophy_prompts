I work in platform reliability, and I am building a small internal tool that scores candidate infrastructure changes so we can rank them before committing engineering time. Right now it scores every option by expected value in a single currency (pick whichever fits your case: expected dollars of revenue at risk over a quarter, or expected hours of customer-visible downtime). I am considering adding a probability-weighting layer: instead of weighting each outcome by its probability, weight it by differences of a risk function applied to tail probabilities. Concretely, for an option whose distinct outcomes are ordered worst to best as x1 < x2 < ... < xn with probabilities p1, ..., pn, let T_i be the probability of getting something at least as good as x_i and U_i the probability of getting something strictly better than x_i; the weight on x_i is then r(T_i) − r(U_i), with r(p) = p^k for one fixed k > 1 applied to every option. Call the resulting score REU. I have no formal background in philosophy or decision theory; if I have written that formula wrong, correct it, say what the correction is, and use the corrected version.

Before I ship the layer, I want one worked case showing that it does work that the metrics my colleague already trusts cannot do. His position is that a worst-case number plus a tail average is enough to capture anything a pessimist could care about, and I want a case aimed squarely at that claim.

Please construct a decision problem meeting all of the following specifications.

1. Setting: an ordinary infrastructure decision a real team could face — two competing options, e.g. two rollout or migration strategies, two incident-response postures, two capacity plans. One paragraph for the scenario itself; no science fiction, no adversarial predictors, no artificial entities. The numbers should be ones a team could plausibly put in a design doc.
2. Exactly two options, each with exactly three distinct outcomes, with every value and probability stated explicitly. Probabilities must be multiples of 0.05, and none smaller than 0.05.
3. Utility is the identity function on the chosen currency, u(x) = x. None of the divergence below may come from curvature in utility; all of it must come from the probability weighting.
4. Plain expected value ranks the two options strictly, in one direction.
5. REU with r(p) = p^2 ranks them strictly in the other direction. Write out the three weights for each option and the resulting arithmetic, at the level of detail I could re-check in a spreadsheet.
6. Both options must have the same worst outcome value, the same probability of the worst outcome, and the same best outcome value. This is what makes the case answer my colleague: worst-case reasoning and a tail average taken at that worst-outcome probability must come out exactly indifferent between the two options, so tail metrics have nothing to say here.
7. The option REU prefers must be the one with the lower probability of the best outcome. I want the flip driven by where the non-tail probability mass sits and how good those middle outcomes are, not by one option having a scarier tail — otherwise the case begs the question I am trying to settle.
8. Neither option may first-order stochastically dominate the other, and you should show the dominance check rather than assert it.
9. Give me the critical exponent: the smallest k > 1, to two decimal places, at which the ranking reverses, together with the equation you solved for it. Also say whether the reversal is monotone in k beyond that point or whether the ranking flips back at some larger k.

After the scenario and the computations, I want four things.

First, name the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work, and show that removing it collapses the case: for instance, what happens to the ranking if the two middle outcomes are made equal, or if the best-outcome probabilities are matched while everything else is held fixed?

Second, tell me honestly whether a plain variance-aversion rule of the form mean minus lambda times variance would rank the options the same way REU does. If it would, say whether the case can be adjusted so that it would not, or explain why it cannot.

Third, give the strongest attack available to someone who wants to keep scoring by expected value: what does he say is wrong with the case or with the weighting layer, and is there a standard argument (a sure-thing or separability objection, a dominance or money-pump style argument, an argument about what happens when the same choice is faced repeatedly) that he can press here?

Fourth, one check I care about: would an inverse-S shaped weighting function — small probabilities overweighted, moderate and large ones underweighted, rather than the uniformly convex p^k — reproduce the same flip? If it ranks the options differently, explain why the shape of the weighting function matters for this case.

For the avoidance of doubt, the case fails its purpose if the flip traces back to the options differing in worst-case severity, in the probability of the worst outcome, in the best outcome value, or in a dominance relation, or if it depends on a curved utility function. I am not asking you to stipulate verdicts; I am asking for a case whose numbers generate them.
