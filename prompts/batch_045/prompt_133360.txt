Hi — I'm on a small team at a philanthropic fund, and most of what we do rests on very long-run quantitative forecasts. Things like "by 2050, N hundred million people will be living in water-stressed basins" or "within a generation, antimicrobial resistance makes routine surgery impossible" or "in the 2040s, water stress becomes a first-order driver of violent conflict in the Sahel." We commit real money against these, we've cited some of them publicly, and every three years we're supposed to go back and look at them. Every single time the same argument breaks out. The forecast isn't tracking. One person says, it's a 2050 call, it's 2026, you can't call it yet. Another says, the thing it's supposed to be driven by has already happened and the effect hasn't showed up, so the mechanism is broken. A third says the whole exercise is unfalsifiable with a number stapled to it. I want to stop having that argument from scratch every cycle.

Can you write us an actual procedure for it? Something a three-person committee can run in an afternoon on one forecast, with no statistician in the room and no web access, that outputs a verdict we can file: not yet testable / on track but delayed / falsified / or kept alive only by repairs that eat its content.

Where I think the real work is, and what it has to handle:

Most of these aren't really calendar predictions, they're threshold predictions — not "by 2050" but "once X gets past level L." A prediction can be late without being wrong if the trigger hasn't been reached yet. But if you let yourself convert a deadline into a trigger after the deadline has passed, nothing can ever fail. So I want a rule that says when that conversion is legitimate and what the person proposing it has to show (that the theory said so in advance, presumably), plus what to do with the fact that most of our archive has no such statement — a 2004 memo that just says "by 2030" and nothing else.

Related: a lot of these mechanisms are supposed to be feedbacks, not trapdoors. The pressure is meant to show up in intermediate quantities before it shows up in the headline catastrophe — prices, wages, age at marriage, how much land is under cultivation, whatever the theory's own version of that is. So "did the disaster happen" is often the wrong check, and "did the pressure show up" is the right one. But a theory that only ever predicts the catastrophe and predicts no observable trace of itself beforehand is a different animal. I want the procedure to tell the difference and treat them differently.

And I want every repair to cost something. If someone says "the mechanism was real but this other thing switched it off," fine — but then the domain should shrink, or the remaining shelf life should shrink, or they should owe us a specific pre-committed test with a date on it. If a rescue would be equally available for any outcome at any date, it isn't a rescue, it's a way of never being wrong. There's a philosophy-of-science word for this that I half remember, but please keep jargon out of the document — my colleagues have to run this, and one of them will rebel at anything that looks like theory.

Also: if we file something as "not yet testable," I want the procedure to force a date at which it becomes testable, and a cap on how many times the same forecast can be deferred before it defaults to a miss. And some of these are really families of claims — a headline catastrophe, a mechanism, a trend — where the mechanism might be basically fine while the headline is dead, or vice versa. I'd rather grade the parts separately and have a rule for what that does to the grant, than give one verdict to the whole thing.

Deliverable, concretely: the ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it hands the question to a human because it's a values call and not a facts call. A half-page verdict template at the end (we have to show these to the board). Be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

Then run it on three, please.

One: Malthus, since he's the case everyone reaches for. Population outrunning subsistence, positive and preventive checks, the whole thing. Is he "right but early," falsified and then rescued, or just dead? I genuinely don't know which, and I'd like the procedure to be the thing that decides it rather than us arguing. Whatever it says, spell out the consequence for how we should treat a structurally similar mechanism today.

Two: a stylized version of something in our own archive from the late 1990s — "within twenty-five years, antibiotic resistance will make routine surgery and chemotherapy impossible in most hospitals." That didn't happen at anything like that scale. Resistance is unambiguously worse now, though. I want to see whether the procedure calls that delayed or whether it missed which part of the mechanism does the work.

Three, and this is the one I'm dreading: our flagship, the one the current strategy is built on, which is the water-stress-and-conflict-in-the-Sahel one for the 2040s. Run it straight and give me the honest verdict even if it's the bad one. If it comes out degenerating, don't tell me to abandon the strategy — I can't, and that's not what I asked. Tell me what a repaired version of the *forecast* would have to look like to earn a live verdict again, and tell me what we should stop saying out loud while it's in whatever state it's in.

One constraint I should flag, because it's the reason this is hard rather than easy: we have donors who gave against these specific numbers, and there's real pressure to file everything as "too early to tell." So the procedure needs a step that catches that pressure rather than being steamrolled by it. If the only honest answer is "there's no procedure that can do this, here's the structural reason," I'd rather have that than a fake flowchart — but I'd want the reason, and I'd want to know which parts of what I asked for are still salvageable. Sorry, this got long. Thanks.
