Please act as a philosopher of rational choice who works specifically on decision-making under risk and uncertainty — someone equally fluent in expected utility theory as a normative benchmark and in the family of views that try to amend it (risk-weighted expected utility, ambiguity-sensitive views, lexical and threshold views, and the standard reductions of apparent risk-aversion to curvature in the utility function). Take your time and think carefully before answering: I would rather have a careful reconstruction that leaves the complications in place than a clean verdict that smooths them away. Before you answer, sketch briefly the several different things that could in principle be doing the work in the practice I am about to describe — nonlinearity in the probabilities, nonlinearity in the value of the outcomes, attitudes to model uncertainty, side constraints, option value — and then tell me which of them the practice actually requires.

I sit on the grants committee of a mid-sized philanthropic foundation, and I have become bothered by an asymmetry in how we handle small probabilities. On the upside we are aggressively expected-value: we deliberately fund long-shot, high-upside work — a neglected vaccine, a policy campaign with perhaps a 3 percent chance of landing, an early-stage research programme — and we openly rebuke colleagues who get cold feet about low probabilities, on the grounds that a 1 percent chance of a thousandfold payoff is worth ten times a guaranteed tenfold one. On the downside we refuse to reason that way at all. Any proposal carrying a non-trivial chance of what our risk register calls an unacceptable outcome — catastrophic loss of life, a laboratory release with pandemic potential, an irreversible ecological collapse — is vetoed outright; the expected value of the upside is not permitted into the discussion, and, strikingly, the stated probability of the catastrophe seems to make very little difference to the conversation, so that a one-in-a-thousand chance and a one-in-twenty chance of the same catastrophe get treated almost alike. A second feature of the pattern: we are happy to multiply when the numbers come from a well-measured base rate, such as vaccine efficacy or the historical frequency of a given kind of regulatory win, but our confidence collapses when the numbers come out of a model whose own parameters are themselves estimates — and this happens even when the model's uncertainty is roughly symmetric and we have no particular reason to think it is biased up rather than down. A third: the veto applies to harms we might bring about, but we do not treat declining to fund a catastrophe-prevention project as anywhere near as serious, even though on our own accounting the unspent money and the risky project have the same expected cost in lives; when I raised this, one trustee said that is just the act/omission distinction and has nothing to do with probability, and another said our downside is bounded by the endowment so the two cases really are not symmetric. I do not know what to make of either reply.

What I want from you is the implicit theory of rational choice under risk that this pattern presupposes. I have seen the label risk-weighted expected utility used for views that keep the broad shape of expected utility but let a risk function transform the probabilities, so that very small chances of disaster are not diluted in proportion to their probability — but I am not sure that is what we are doing, and there are other candidates in the neighbourhood: plain expected utility combined with the thought that extinction or collapse is not merely a very large number of deaths, a lexical or threshold constraint on losses, genuine ambiguity aversion in the model-uncertainty cases, or something about irreversibility and the value of keeping options open. Do not just survey these views; I can read about them on my own. Instead: reconstruct the theory our practice commits us to; separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with; tell me where the deviation from expected utility is located in our pattern — in how we weight probabilities, in how we value outcomes, or somewhere else entirely; say which parts of the pattern are consistent with several theories at once and therefore do not discriminate between them; and say what would concretely have to change in how we make grants if we adopted, first, straightforward expected utility with no weighting, and then, explicitly, a risk-weighted view. If some part of the pattern is simply incoherent — including the upside/downside asymmetry or the act/omission asymmetry — say so and say which part of the practice would have to give.
