I help run, unpaid, an internal forecasting pool at the mid-sized manufacturing company where I work in procurement. About forty of us log in once a week and make small bets with each other — five to twenty-five dollars, settled by me out of a kitty — on internal questions: whether a product will ship by its announced date, which band next quarter's defect rate will land in, whether a particular supplier will deliver on time. Management reads the average of our numbers as a rough forecast, and the pool is meant to be both mildly accurate for them and harmless fun for us, so I have two jobs that sometimes pull apart: I set position limits and decide whether a proposed trade goes through at the size requested, and I also trade myself. I look at somewhere between ten and thirty proposed trades a week, I have about five minutes for each, almost none of us has any training in this, and a couple of members have quietly lost more than they can shrug off. I have no economics background at all, but I half-remember reading something online — some result from game theory or finance, I think — saying that if two people start from the same basic assumptions about the world and each knows the other is reasoning carefully, they should not be able to end up disagreeing once everything they each know is out in the open, and so, paradoxically, there should be no trade between them at all: every real trade is supposed to come from somewhere else, because one side knows something the other doesn't, or one side is shedding a risk the other is happy to hold, or one side simply isn't thinking straight. There was also a joke attached to it about not wanting to join any club that would accept you as a member.

I would like you to turn whatever is actually true and usable in that idea into a procedure I can run on a proposed trade, and run it on three or four cases. The backbone I have in mind is this: before I allow a trade, and before I take a side myself, I should have to write down, in one sentence, what the other party knows or wants that makes their side of the bet sensible for them — and if I cannot fill that sentence in, the presumption should be that I am the sucker and the trade should be shrunk or refused. Tell me whether that is the right spine, and what has to be added around it: how to tell a genuine informational advantage from a difference in prior judgment, what to do when the disagreement closes or fails to close after the two parties exchange their reasons, how to weigh the fact that one of them works directly on the thing being bet on and the other only reads memos about it, how to treat the explanations that make trade legitimate rather than suspect (someone hedging an exposure, someone with a different appetite for risk, someone who just wants the action), and how to size a trade once I have decided it is allowed. I need the questions in a fixed order, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the points where the procedure just stops and says no, the points where it has to hand the decision to me because the remaining question is one of values or of office politics rather than of fact, and — importantly — what to do at a step where I simply cannot get an answer, which in my setting will be often, since people cannot always say why they think what they think and cannot always disclose what they know. Please also be blunt about which steps are really judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing. And please do not send me a reading list or point me at papers: I have no library access and I need something I can run on a Tuesday afternoon, not something I can study.

The cases I want worked are these. First: a production supervisor who has just come out of a tooling meeting wants to bet the maximum that a launch will slip, against an engineer who is equally confident it will not, and when I ask him why, all he will say is that he has seen something he is not allowed to talk about; my instinct is that this is exactly the kind of trade the theory should bless, and also exactly the kind that will make the pool feel rigged. Second: two people in finance, neither of whom has any special line on the product and both of whom read the same internal demand memo, have disagreed every week for a month about whether next quarter's order numbers will beat plan, and now want to double their usual size. Third: me, wanting to take the other side of a colleague who is famously overconfident and has been wrong three weeks running, fairly confident I would be taking his money — if your procedure says I should not do that, say so plainly and tell me what I should do instead, because it is the case where I am least likely to listen. Fourth: someone whose bonus is tied to on-time delivery wants to bet heavily that the delivery will slip, which looks like legitimate risk-sharing but also gives him a reason to make the outcome happen, and I would like the procedure to say what to do when the standard benign explanation for a trade collides with the fact that the trade itself changes the incentives around the event. Please finish by saying, in a few sentences, what such a procedure cannot do — where it will misfire in a setting like mine. Please write all of this as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long email to someone with no formal training in the subject, and keep it under about fifteen hundred words.
