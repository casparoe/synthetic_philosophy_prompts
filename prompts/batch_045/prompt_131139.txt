so I'm writing a novel — two protagonists on opposite sides of the same fight, starting with something small and local (a school curriculum meeting that gets ugly) and spiralling outward — and while doing research for it I keep bumping into a thing I don't have a word for. I dont want a word to drop into dialogue, I want to understand the machinery well enough that neither of them is a caricature, so bear with the rambling setup.

My character Dana is not in a bubble. She reads the other side constantly. She can recite, from memory, six infuriating things people on their side have said this year; she follows several of their writers on purpose, specifically to get angry. But nothing she reads was picked by the other side for the other side. It was picked by her own side, screenshotted, and circulated because it was enraging or ridiculous — last week her group chat passed around a clip of some guy with a few hundred followers saying something genuinely unhinged, and everyone in it treated him as what the other side really thinks. So her sample of them is basically a gallery of their worst moments, assembled by her own allies, and the boring, reasonable majority of what the other side says never makes it into the gallery because nobody on her side has any reason to pass it around. The part I can't get right is what happens next: when Dana actually meets one of them at a meeting and he's mild, concedes a point, makes an argument she half agrees with, it doesn't move her at all. She files him as an exception, or as someone being polite in mixed company, and goes back to the gallery.

What I keep wanting to call it is a reverse echo chamber — not too little contact with the other side but a kind of adversarial curation, where more contact makes you surer they argue in bad faith rather than less. Everything I've run into feels adjacent but wrong. The usual echo chamber / filter bubble stuff is about information being kept out, whereas here information is being pushed in, by her own teammates. There's something I half remember called the "hostile media effect" (I may well be mangling the name) about people perceiving neutral coverage as biased against them, but the material Dana reacts to really is nasty, so she isn't misperceiving anything. And there's work on how we overestimate how extreme the other side is, which isn't it either: if you asked Dana to guess what fraction of them believe X, I think she'd be roughly right. Her problem isn't a false belief about the distribution of their opinions — it's what her sample is made of, who assembled it and why, and the fact that meeting them face to face doesn't fix it. (Also: there's that exercise where you have to state the other side's position well enough that they'd sign off on it. Dana would be great at it. I don't think it would help her at all, and that's a detail I'd love to get right.)

So: is there an established concept for this, in political science or social epistemology or media studies or anywhere else, and if so what exactly does it cover and what is it used to explain? If there isn't one, give me a coined name and a definition — but more importantly give me the edges. Where does this stop being its own thing and become just ordinary mockery of your opponents, which is ancient and probably mostly fine? Does more exposure ever help rather than hurt here, and is the difference the amount of contact or the kind of contact? And what would the honest version of the fix even be, because my instinct is that "go read the other side" is definately the wrong advice for someone like Dana, and I want to know whether that's insight or just my own cynicism.
