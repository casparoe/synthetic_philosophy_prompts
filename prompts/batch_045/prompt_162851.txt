I'm a control engineer — mostly industrial process control, some work on adaptive and robust control — and I've recently been pulled onto a procurement committee that has to write acceptance criteria for so-called "general-purpose" autonomous systems. The committee keeps talking past itself about what intelligence actually is, and the popular writing I tried to read on the subject was either neuron diagrams or hand-waving about "general intelligence," so I'd like to get it straight in vocabulary I actually trust: plant, controller, cost functions and LQR, reference tracking, state estimation and Kalman filtering, disturbance rejection, robustness and stability margins, online identification and adaptation, closed-loop versus open-loop behavior, the internal model principle, sample efficiency, and compute and bandwidth budgets. I have no philosophy background beyond the usual engineer's hobbies, so please don't assume I know the literature, and please don't send me off to look things up — no reading lists or citations, just the translation.

Here are the four positions as I've managed to extract them from my colleagues over the last few months; I may be caricaturing some of them, so say so if a position as I've stated it is a strawman. (1) The single-capacity view: intelligence is one broadly unitary, resource-bounded capacity to achieve goals across a wide range of environments; it varies as a roughly scalar quantity between systems and between people, and standard test batteries measure it imperfectly but genuinely. (2) The situated view: intelligence is not a property of the agent alone but of an agent-environment pair, including the tools and scaffolding around it; remove the structure of the environment or the artifacts the agent leans on and you don't have the same intelligence, you have a different and lesser one. (3) The pluralist view: "intelligence" names a loose family of only partly correlated capacities — learning speed, abstraction, transfer, planning depth, error monitoring — and there is no deep fact about how much intelligence something has, only profiles. (4) The reasons-and-understanding view: however good a system's behavior is, it isn't intelligent in the full sense unless it does what it does for reasons it in some sense grasps; reliable success without understanding, whether in a thermostat or in a gigantic lookup table, is the wrong kind of thing.

Translate each of the four into control vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and don't smuggle into the translation any conclusion the position itself doesn't carry: if two positions collapse into one another once restated, say so, and say whether the collapse is an artifact of my vocabulary or a real implication of what they claim. Then give me the residue report. Which claims map cleanly onto things I already have words for? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly gets bent in the bending? Which cannot be expressed in control vocabulary at all? My own guesses, for what they're worth, are that (2) will translate almost too easily, since I already know that performance is a property of the closed loop rather than of the controller, and that (4) is nearly all residue, nothing in my vocabulary distinguishing a system that "grasps why" from one that merely reduces prediction error. Then the part I actually care about: for each piece of residue, tell me whether it's a genuine blind spot in control-theoretic vocabulary — something real I should want a richer framework to talk about — or a sign that the philosophical disagreement is less clear or less substantive than it sounds, the way an argument about "the best controller" dissolves once you notice nobody specified a plant.

To pin this down, here is the case the committee is stuck on. System A is trained offline against a large, well-characterized distribution of environments; inside that distribution it is excellent — fast, low regret, stable — but off-distribution it degrades badly and sometimes unrecoverably. System B is considerably worse inside that distribution: slower, higher variance, needs more data to reach competence at all; but it identifies the plant online and stays competent, if clumsy, on environments neither designer enumerated or could have enumerated. On the single-capacity view, which of the two is more intelligent? I think the honest answer is that it depends on the distribution of environments you score against, and that this is a version of the no-free-lunch point, but my colleagues think that observation dodges the question rather than answering it. Settle it. Is that dependence on a chosen distribution a defect of the question "which is more intelligent?", or is there a way of specifying generality that survives once you grant that you always need some distribution — a meta-level criterion, say, about how cheaply a system acquires competence on a newly encountered plant? And whatever you conclude, close by telling me concretely what I should write into the acceptance criteria and what I should refuse to write into them.
