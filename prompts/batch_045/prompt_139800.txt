Our group (vertebrate functional morphology) keeps a standing verdict list for every trait we publish on. Our four categories, as we use them: adaptation — the trait was shaped by selection for the effect it now has; exaptation — it now matters to fitness but was not built by selection for that role, having been co-opted from something else; spandrel — a structural consequence of selection on something else, with no role of its own; unresolved. We have also occasionally used aptation for an exaptation subsequently modified by selection for its new role, without ever fixing when the promotion is earned.

Seven precedents, all agreed, with the reasoning my supervisor recorded:

1. The male nipple — spandrel. No current role; a consequence of the early developmental program shared with females.
2. The human chin — spandrel. Buttressing and speech stories rejected; the chin is what is left over when the tooth-bearing part of the mandible retracts faster than the basal bone.
3. The antlers of the giant deer — spandrel, over one dissent. Current utility for display and combat, extraordinary complexity, but size tracks body size across the clade with no residual once size is controlled, and the combat story predicts a shape they do not have. The note attached to the ruling says explicitly that current utility plus elaborate form is not enough.
4. Feathers — exaptation. Insulation and display before flight. The ruling recorded, and explicitly declined to resolve, whether the flight-specific refinements (the asymmetric vane, the locking barbules) convert part of the complex into an adaptation.
5. The radial sesamoid that makes up the panda's false thumb — exaptation. It strips bamboo now; the design is workmanlike rather than good; at origin it was an ordinary wrist element, and we found no evidence of later selection refining it specifically for stripping.
6. The pharyngeal jaw apparatus of the cichlid radiations — adaptation. Repeated independent modification tracking diet, a derived state that outperforms the ancestral one at the same task, and no allometric or developmental alternative on offer.
7. The lens crystallins that several vertebrate lineages have recruited from ordinary metabolic enzymes to serve as structural lens proteins — exaptation, with the note that at this level the verdict attaches to a use of the molecule rather than to the molecule.

The new case. *Fossoriscincus* is a clade of fourteen head-first burrowing skinks. In all of them a bony flange roofs the orbit and anchors a sheet of connective tissue that transmits force from the jaw adductors to the snout; cutting that sheet, flange left intact, abolishes the force gain, and strain gauges show that at a given head width, species with longer flanges bite harder while burrowing. The flange's internal trabeculae are aligned with the measured principal strain directions, in the way trabecular bone elsewhere aligns with load. In field enclosures, removing the flange lowers burrowing rate and eight-week survival by a margin that is significant but modest.

Developmentally and phylogenetically, though, the flange is what you get when an enlarged nasal capsule crowds the orbit: outgroup species with the largest capsules have a flange but no connective sheet, and no species has a long flange on a small capsule. The enlarged capsule is itself an adaptation, for chemosensory navigation underground. The sheet's attachment to the flange is a derived state at the base of the clade. A genome scan finds a recent selective sweep at a locus affecting flange projection; capsule volume and flange length are strongly genetically correlated (r_g about 0.8), although at least two loci move flange length independently of capsule volume. Whether the sweep predates or postdates the clade's shift into compacted soils is not resolved; the estimate straddles it.
