I want to practise attacking accuracy-first epistemology, so please play a committed accuracy-firster: credences are the basic doxastic state, closeness to the truth is the only epistemic good, and the dominance and expected-inaccuracy arguments really do deliver probabilism, chance-matching and the rest. Be sharp rather than a strawman -- concede small points, never the framework. Stay in character until I say stop, and give each turn as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no summing up.

My opening move. Agent A has a view on one question, rain tomorrow or not, and splits 0.5/0.5, matching the chances. Agent B has the same 0.5 on rain and also, about an independent fair coin she is about to flip, 0.5 on heads, so 0.25 on each of the four cells. Score them with the Brier score on the finest partition either has opinions about: A's expected inaccuracy is 0.5, B's is 0.75 -- B comes out worse even though she matches the chances on every question and never disagrees with A about anything A has a view on. Give a third agent calibrated opinions about ten further independent coins and her expected inaccuracy climbs toward 1, since for a calibrated agent it is just 1 minus the sum of squared chances. So the quantity your whole framework runs on is mostly a function of how many irrelevant questions someone bothered to have an opinion about.

So: what fixes the set of questions? If you say hold the partition fixed, tell me what fixes it and what holding it fixed costs you, given that the dominance arguments only ever compare credence functions defined on the same questions. And if instead you bite the bullet and say a perfectly calibrated view about an irrelevant coin really is an epistemic cost, say why that isn't just a reductio of total inaccuracy.
