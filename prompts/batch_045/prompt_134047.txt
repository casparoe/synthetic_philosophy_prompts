My grandmother died in February, and in October her department is putting her name on a graduate fellowship — small ceremony, podium, a plaque, her three surviving co-authors, a dean, a donor, me, my brother, my cousins (14 and 19), and her sister who has spent fifty years being fairly sure economics is what made her cold. I've been asked to speak for five minutes. I have no idea how to do it, and the reason is a problem she definately would have enjoyed.

She was one of the last genuine revealed-preference purists: for her, "utility" was just whatever the choices revealed, full stop — it never meant happiness, it never meant wellbeing, and "she really wanted X but chose Y" was, in her mouth, not a subtle hypothesis but a sentence with no content. I use to argue with her about that at Sunday lunch. I lost every time.

So here's what I need. A five-minute dedication speech — it does have to actually dedicate the thing (say what the fellowship covers: tuition plus a summer stipend, no teaching the first year) — in which the philosophy does the work structurally rather than as decoration:

- Not one mental-state attribution about her. No "she wanted," "she believed," "she hoped," "she valued," "she loved," "she'd be so proud," and above all no "she would have wanted this fellowship to go to someone who..." Every single claim about her has to be a claim about a choice she actually made, what it cost her, and what else was available at the time — I want the economists unable to catch you inferring a preference from a single act or forgetting the feasible set.
- She drove me to school every Tuesday for two years and complained about the drive the entire way, both directions, without ever once saying a good thing about it. You can't use that. On her own view the complaining is a choice too, so it can't be evidence that she didn't want to be there. Whatever you do with the drive, it can't rest on that move.
- The word "utility" appears exactly once, fairly late, and it has to be defined her way in a sentence a 14-year-old gets, without using the words happy, happiness, wellbeing, or satisfied.
- One place where the speech openly says the hard thing: the choice data supports two descriptions of her that her sister and I have been arguing about for decades (generosity, versus not being able to say no), and on her account there is no further fact that settles it. It has to land as tenderness toward her, not as a debunking, and not as a shrug — and it must not imply the two descriptions predict anything different, because they don't and one of the co-authors will say so out loud afterwards.
- At least one line that gets a laugh from the whole room, economists and cousins alike.

Don't psychologize, don't smooth her into a consistent agent, and don't turn it into a lecture on microeconomics. It should still be a speech someone cries at. Write it, then add a short note on which line was hardest to write under these rules and why.
