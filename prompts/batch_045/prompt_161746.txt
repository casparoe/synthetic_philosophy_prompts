I write a weekly column for a science-and-ideas magazine, about 800 words, pitched Monday and filed Wednesday, and an editor who is very smart but not a philosopher has to sign off on the headline. I recieve maybe one paper a month from the cross-cultural "experimental philosophy" literature — give people a vignette in two or more countries and see whether they answer differently: Gettier cases, the chairman-and-the-environment style of case, trolley stuff. These pieces always do numbers and always get over-read. I publish "people in X answer differently" and by the end of the week it's being passed around as "culture determines whether you think Y." I want to stop guessing at this.

So: give me a procedure I can run in about half an hour, on a PDF before my Monday pitch meeting, to decide (a) whether there is a story, (b) how strong a claim I'm allowed to make in the headline and lede, and (c) when I have to kill it. Ordered questions; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and — importantly — where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's not a factual question at all, e.g. how much risk of over-reading is worth a good story.

The hard cases it has to handle, because these are the ways I've been burned: the paper says "Brazilians vs Japanese" but it tested undergraduates in two cities; the load-bearing word ("knows," "intentionally," "responsible") may not map cleanly onto the other language, and I get one email to the corresponding author with 24 hours to reply; the samples differ in years of education and in how willing they are to use the top of the scale; the effect is significant but tiny, or it's a shift in the middle of the distribution rather than a flip in which answer is modal; it's a null result being pitched as a failed replication; and the difference might be about the pragmatics of the vignette — what the subject thinks the experimenter is getting at — rather than about the concept at all. Also: my readers want to know whether any of this changes a philosophical conclusion, and there's that standing objection that folk intuitions from undergraduates were never evidence for anything in the first place. The procedure should tell me when that objection kills the story and when it's simply irrelevant to the only claim I'm making.

I'd like an explicit claim-strength ladder: for each grade of evidence, the strongest sentance I'm allowed to write, down to "we can't say culture at all, only these two samples." Plus a rule that every such piece carries one sentence saying what would have to be true for this not to be a cultural difference.

Then run it on three. (1) Around 150 students each in Toronto and Seoul on a Knobe-style intentionality case: the asymmetry shows up in both samples, but one sample is much less willing to say "intentionally" across the board, including for the good side effect. (2) A Gettier-vignette study in Nanjing and Edinburgh finding no difference once the question is strengthened from "knows" to something like "really knows" — decent n, pre-registered. (3) The one I've already half-pitched to my editor and really want to write: a large, careful study where the between-sample difference is real and robust, but every measured covariate on which the samples differ (English fluency, years of university, urban vs rural) also tracks the dependent variable — and my proposed headline is "Why [culture] doesn't believe in [thing]."

Commit. Give me actual numbers where numbers are the right kind of thing — effect-size floors, n, what counts as a passed comprehension check — take a position on each of the three (write it, write it downgraded, or spike it), and tell me where your own procedure is likely to misfire. Don't hand me a menu of considerations to weigh myself; I already do that and I usually get it wrong.
