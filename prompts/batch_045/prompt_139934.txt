I'm a science journalist working on a piece about where medicine's remit ends — what counts as treatment rather than enhancement, accommodation, or mere alteration — and I've found that my own verdicts on cases don't match any slogan I can say out loud. My working principle is that a condition is a disease just when it impairs a species-typical function, but I keep having to bolt clauses onto it.

Sickle-cell anemia: a disease. Sickle-cell trait: not one, even though it can cause trouble under extreme oxygen deprivation. A valley town where a century of smoke has left nearly everyone below species-typical lung capacity: a disease, though it's the local statistical norm. A variant that lets someone thrive on four hours of sleep with no physiological cost, leaving them awake and alone for hours each night: not a disease. But a variant that sets the body clock to a roughly 26-hour cycle, so the person is chronically sleep-deprived in a 24-hour world: a disease — even though in both cases what fails is the fit between a body that works and a social schedule, not the body itself. An extra finger: a difference, not a disease. Congenital inability to feel pain: a disease, without hesitation.

Please test my principle against these verdicts and repair it rather than just swapping in a theory of your own. Then apply whatever you end up with to the case I can't settle: a symptom-free trait that does nothing but raise the probability of later illness — say, mildly elevated blood pressure in someone who feels entirely well.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion, covering: (1) the repaired principle stated in one sentence; (2) the clause each of my verdicts forced you to add or drop; (3) which of my verdicts you would overturn, if any; (4) your principle's verdict on the blood-pressure case; and (5) which of your claims people who work on this topic would treat as settled and which are genuinely live disputes. In the last point, do not present a contested view as the consensus.
