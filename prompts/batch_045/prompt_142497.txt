so I'm on a short contract with the secretariat of our union's competence chamber — 14 member states, roughly 180m people, the chamber reviews whether the union-level legislature has overstepped and can send measures back for reconsideration. I've been reading about subsidiarity for fun (the EU version, the way Swiss cantons hoard everything they can, the German setup where the Länder end up administering most federal law, the American anti-commandeering stuff) and I've started trying to reverse-engineer the rule the chamber is actually running rather than the one it recites, since the recital is just the usual union-may-act-only-where-member-states-cannot-achieve-the-objective-sufficiently formula and obviously doesn't decide anything by itself. I put our recent decisions into a spreadsheet and can't get one clean rule out of them: we upheld a cap on sulphur in fuel for barges on the river system that runs through six member states; we upheld a rule forcing member states to recognise each other's vocational licences so an electrician qualified in one can take jobs in another; we upheld a cap on roaming charges for mobile calls, reasoning that no member state can regulate a call that originates in a different one; we struck down a directive requiring member states to criminalise denial of a listed set of historical atrocities with a minimum prison sentence; we struck down a union-wide ban on selling raw-milk cheese aged under sixty days; we upheld a rule requiring member states to reimburse residents who get treated in another member state's hospitals; we upheld, seven votes to six, a directive guaranteeing every worker twenty days of paid annual leave, with the majority saying member states would otherwise undercut each other competing for investment and the dissent saying that's a political judgement dressed up as a competence test; we struck down maximum waiting-time guarantees for hospital treatment even though the union produced mortality data; and we upheld a requirement that hospitals publish comparable data on infection rates. The two axes I keep coming back to are whether the harm or the decision's costs cross a border versus whether the failure mode is a coordination problem (everyone better off if everyone moves together) or a competition problem (each state individually wants to be the low-standard one) — and then there's a third variable that seems to matter to them more than the stated test does, namely whether the instrument is disclosure, a binding minimum, or full harmonisation.

Next month's case is a directive requiring member states to impose a binding minimum staffing ratio in care homes for the elderly — one staff member per six residents on night shifts — plus a floor under care workers' hourly pay. The stated justification is that member states compete to attract private care-home chains, that any state holding standards loses investment and ends up with worse care overall, and that the resulting wage floor also depresses the care systems of neighbouring states whose workers get recruited away. The chamber has the usual paperwork: regional parliaments were consulted, there's a forty-page reasoned opinion, and the union's own impact assessment concedes that residents basically never cross a border and that care labour is overwhelmingly local, though it notes that about a seventh of care workers are posted in from other member states.

What I want is not really the bottom line. I want: the two or three rules that fit our precedents about equally well and what each of them predicts here (I can think of at least a narrow transboundary-effects rule, a race-to-the-bottom rule, a core-domain carve-out for criminal law and health and social care, a process-only rule where we just check the reasoning and the consultation, and something like a least-intrusive-instrument ladder); which one you'd actually bet on and why, including whether the annual-leave decision or the waiting-times decision is the real tell; one further case that would separate the surviving candidates cleanly, ideally something I could get discussed as a hypothetical — I was thinking the same care-home directive but drafted as a transparency requirement instead of a binding ratio, though maybe there's a better probe; and finally, if I'm the one drafting the opinion and want it to cohere with what we've already done, what should we decide, which precedent is the closest analog, and what exactly are we committing ourselves to either way. My inside view is that they strike the binding ratio and drop a heavy hint that a disclosure version would have survived, but the outside view is that chambers like this defer whenever the paperwork is in order, and I can't tell how much of my hunch is just me pattern-matching on the cheese case.
