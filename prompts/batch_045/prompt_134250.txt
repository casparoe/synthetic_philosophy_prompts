I have no academic background — I left school at eighteen and have driven delivery vans ever since — but for the last few years I have been reading philosophy on my own in the evenings, and I recently worked through Tolstoy's book on art in an old paperback translation. What I cannot get out of my head is his claim that a real work of art infects other people with a feeling the artist actually lived through, and that the counterfeit is made by producing the outward signs of feeling through technique while the feeling itself is absent. I want the fourteen adults who come to my Saturday-morning group to feel the pull of that distinction rather than hear me explain it.

The group meets for two hours in a room above the public library in a small town. Ages run from the late twenties to the mid-sixties; nobody has a degree in philosophy or in anything artistic; two or three are far more practised at drawing or writing than the rest. We have paper, pencils, index cards, envelopes, a bell, and about thirty identical glass counters — no projector, no internet, and no money changing hands.

Please design a runnable activity for this group in which participants make small things under different conditions and then have to respond to each other's things, and in doing so run into two failures that I think sit at the centre of Tolstoy's view. The first is what I call mute sincerity: someone makes something out of a feeling they genuinely had, and it simply fails to land on anyone else. The second is what I call the successful forgery: someone makes something by assembling intensities borrowed from works they already love — a chord progression, a way of describing weather, a camera angle — with no corresponding feeling behind it, and it moves people more reliably than the honest piece did. I want the group to encounter both, and to notice that they cannot tell the two apart from the outside, because the difference is supposed to be a private fact about the maker's history while the test Tolstoy appeals to is a public fact about the audience. Please build the activity so that this gap becomes something they feel rather than something I announce.

Two constraints matter to me. First, I do not want anyone pushed into disclosing real grief or anything else that would leave them exposed in front of the other thirteen; if the "made from a real feeling" condition needs genuine material, tell me how to get it safely. Second, craft has to be neutralised somehow, since two of my members would otherwise outshine everybody and the exercise would just measure who can draw — I had been thinking of forcing every piece into one deliberately impoverished form, such as six marks plus a fixed number of words, but I would rather have your judgement on that.

I would also like the reporting of responses to be non-verbal where possible. In my experience people say a thing moved them when they only mean it was well made, and they stay silent when they are embarrassed. I had considered having them spend physical counters, or simply step toward the piece, and I would like the mechanism to make polite verdicts difficult.

Please give me the rules and any roles, the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular parts of the idea — including where the participants' own behaviour during the activity is the evidence. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure in the room. And be frank about which feature of Tolstoy's position the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will simply have to say myself at the end.

Nobody should hear Tolstoy's name, or any of this vocabulary of mine, until after the last exercise is over.
