I am a PhD student in political theory, working on the conditions under which a private person may kill a ruler in the Thomistic tradition. My supervisor is the closest thing we have to a specialist on Aquinas on political authority and licit killing, and over the past year she has given me firm verdicts on a series of cases. I have a supervision with her on Tuesday and I want to know which way she will jump on a case I have built, and — more importantly — what principle she is really running, because I have come to think her verdicts so far are jointly consistent with several different rules that come apart on my case.

Her verdicts, as I have them:

1. Ehud killing the occupying king Eglon, privately and by stealth, and clearly intending his death: licit, and praiseworthy.
2. A private citizen killing a usurper who seized power by violence, holds no title, and oppresses the city: licit, on the ground that such a man is a public enemy rather than a ruler.
3. A private citizen killing a hereditary king, lawfully crowned, who now rules oppressively: illicit; the remedy belongs to the community assembled or to a superior, not to a private person.
4. A traveler killing a man who attacks him on the road, where he defends himself with proportionate force and the death is foreseen but not what he is after: licit.
5. A private citizen killing a murderer whom the courts have failed to punish and whom no one can otherwise restrain, where the community is plainly safer as a result: illicit; punishment belongs to public authority, and the good outcome does not license the act.
6. A magistrate, acting with full public authority, killing an innocent citizen to placate a besieging army and thereby save the city: illicit; public authority does not reach the direct killing of the innocent.

What I cannot work out is which feature of these cases is doing the work. On the face of it, verdicts 2 and 3 are explained by a rule about the target's title, verdicts 3 and 5 by a rule about whether the agent acts with the community's authority or as a private person, verdicts 1 and 4 by a rule about averting an imminent grave harm, verdict 5 by a rule that serving the common good is not by itself sufficient, and verdict 6 by a rule that even public authority has limits. All of these fit what she has said so far.

Here is the case I want to put to her. A hereditary king, lawfully crowned and long accepted, has concluded a secret agreement to surrender the city's citadel to a foreign army the day after tomorrow; the massacre and enslavement that follow are certain, not speculative. There is no superior with appellate jurisdiction — the overlordship that once existed has lapsed — and the great council that could in principle depose him cannot lawfully assemble: its members are dispersed, and the king has forbidden them to meet on pain of death. There is no realistic prospect of a lawful deposition before the surrender. One of the king's own household knights holds no office of any kind and acts entirely on his own initiative, but he can kill the king tonight, alone, in his chamber, with certainty, killing no one else, and doing so will prevent the surrender. He cannot describe the death as a foreseen side effect of defending himself, since the king poses no threat to him personally; he must intend it.

So: do you expect her to say the killing is licit or illicit? Tell me which rule you think she is running, what each of the other candidate rules would predict about this case, and why you think the one you favour is the one she is actually applying rather than merely one she could apply. I am especially interested in whether, on her view, an agent counts as a private person in the relevant sense even when every route to public authorization has collapsed, or whether the absence of any functioning public authority reclassifies someone like the knight. If you think her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say what principle she would be committing herself to with each answer.
