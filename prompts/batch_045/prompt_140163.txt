Doing a pragmatics module this term and I knocked together an argument for my supervision essay that I'm now pretty sure can't be right, because it seems to prove way too much. We did Grice on speaker meaning and conversational implicature, and then the lying vs merely-misleading distinction, and I think my argument wrecks both. Here it is:

(1) On Grice's account, S means q only if S intends the audience to end up believing q *by recognising that very intention* — the reflexive recognition condition.
(2) Implicata belong to what the speaker means, not to what is said.
(3) Call something a "dead-letter implicature": the hearer derives q, the speaker foresees that uptake and wants the hearer to end up with it, but the speaker intends the hearer *not* to recognise the intention — the nod-and-wink press briefing, the official who wants a conclusion drawn without ever being on record as having drawn it, the hint that is engineered to be deniable.
(4) Dead-letter cases pass the usual diagnostics — cancellable, non-detachable, calculable from the Cooperative Principle and maxims — so I can't just declare that they aren't implicatures.
(5) By (1) and (3), the speaker does not mean q. Yet q is what gets across, and it is what the speaker gets blamed for.
(6) So communicated content is not speaker meaning, and implicature is at bottom a hearer-side phenomenon.
(7) Then we should fix content by what a reasonable audience would infer from the presumption of cooperativeness.
(8) But then there is no stopping point: a speaker is answerable for everything a reasonable audience could work out, "what I meant" stops having a determinate answer, and the lying/misleading distinction goes too, since you'd be lying about anything an audience infers.
(9) That's definately worse than where I started, so one of the earlier steps has to go.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (7), and I'd rather say dead-letter cases are cases of meaning after all than give up the intention-based picture — but I can't see how to run that, and I'm not even sure (3) is coherent.
