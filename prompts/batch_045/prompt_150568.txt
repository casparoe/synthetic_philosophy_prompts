I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether the classic philosophical case against believing in miracles rests on anything more than the known unreliability of witnesses. I need your take on a scenario that the author of the most famous version of that argument, David Hume, could not have anticipated.

Suppose that in 2031 tens of thousands of people across several hundred cities simultaneously watch a granite obelisk in a public park rise two meters, hover for ninety seconds, and settle back down. Every recording carries tamper-proof cryptographic provenance, so editing or fabrication is excluded as thoroughly as anything short of being there; hundreds of calibrated instruments register the displacement and no propulsive force; no religious or political movement claims the event, no one profits from reporting it, and two years later no mechanism has been found and nothing similar has recurred.

In roughly 500 to 600 words, write me the verdict Hume would give, in the voice of a sympathetic but rigorous Humean. Make it a verdict rather than a survey of interpretations: commit to a definite position on two questions, and defend it with reasons. First, given how he defines a miracle, does an event like this even count as one? Second, does his argument still block belief once the witness psychology he catalogues — credulity, love of wonder, religious zeal, the rival claims of competing faiths — has been engineered out, or should a well-attested anomaly of this kind instead force a revision of the law it appears to violate? Close by naming the single assumption your verdict leans on hardest, so I can flag it in the column.
