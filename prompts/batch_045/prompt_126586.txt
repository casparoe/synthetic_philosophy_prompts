I'm a software engineer by training with no formal philosophy background, but through a odd route I've ended up on the governance committee of a small private foundation with a perpetual endowment, and I've been reading about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment to try to get clearer on what we're actually doing when we tie our own successors' hands. Our charter has a mission clause that can only be changed by a supermajority of trustees plus a petition to a court, a spend-rate cap that is very hard to revise, and a donor letter expressing the hope that future boards will resist the temptation to grow the staff or broaden the mission. In our meetings these get described interchangeably as a Ulysses contract, and I've become convinced the analogy is doing more work than it can bear — but I can't yet say precisely where it breaks. Here is my half-formed idea. First, in the original story, Ulysses arranges to be *unable* to act on his later desire (he is bound to the mast and his crew is ordered not to release him), but in ordinary personal precommitment — and in most engineering precommitments I actually build, like required two-person review, code freezes, or hardware-enforced signing keys — the device doesn't remove anyone's power; it just makes deviation expensive or slow. Call these jurisdiction-stripping and cost-shifting precommitments. Second, and more importantly, the standard normative defense of a Ulysses contract leans on the fact that the binder and the bound are the *same person* at different times: we can appeal to what he really wants, or to his autonomy understood over a whole life. But a constitutional or chartered precommitment binds numerically different people, most of whom never consented and many of whom weren't alive when it was made. Third, I suspect a lot of things that get called constitutional precommitment aren't self-binding at all: an independent central bank with a price-stability mandate, or an endowed investment committee with a fixed mandate, isn't a mast that the polity lashed itself to, it's a helmsman the polity hired and instructed, and who is still answerable to it. If that's right, then the familiar debate — autonomy or prudence on one side, the dead-hand objection that the living shouldn't be governed by the dead on the other — may be pointed at only one of three quite different devices, and the interesting question is what each one actually needs by way of justification.

What I need is a short research proposal, roughly two pages, for a nine-month part-time practitioner fellowship I'm applying for (about one day a week; the output would be a working paper plus a chapter for our governance handbook). Please give it: a precise statement of the question I should be asking, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a survey; an explanation of why the obvious existing answers don't already settle it, keeping in mind that I haven't read the literature and will fill in references myself, so argue against *positions and arguments* rather than authors; a method section saying what I would actually do month by month and why that approach rather than the two obvious alternatives (a purely conceptual analysis, or a case-study approach comparing real entrenched clauses and independent agencies); the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; an honest account of what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the distinctions I've drawn turn out to be unstable or to collapse into ones people already use; and a realistic assessment of whether this is too big for nine months at one day a week. Please also include a short section, in plain English, on why a funder should care about this rather than about an empirical study of which governance devices actually prevent mission drift, since I will be asked exactly that.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in political philosophy, philosophy of law, and institutional design, and think carefully before writing: spend a little time brainstorming and testing my three distinctions, and feel free to replace, sharpen, or discard any of them if a cleaner framing would make the project better.
