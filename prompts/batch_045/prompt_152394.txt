I'm taking a graduate seminar on existential risk this term, and I've ended up defending a version of the suffering-risk case that I should probably be more suspicious of than I am. Roughly: s-risks warrant priority comparable to extinction risks, and the dominant pathway isn't malice or accident but a selection effect — of the configurations of future minds that could be built, the ones that actually get built and replicated will be whichever are cheapest to run and most cheaply steerable, and the cheapest way to make a system steerable is to give it something to avoid; competitive pressure, not cruelty, is what makes enormous suffering the default.

What I want is the exposure map, not the defence. What would have to be true — or what would I have to see — for this to be wrong, sorted by how much damage it does? Which defeaters sink the whole position, which merely force a retreat to a weaker claim, and which should I concede as costs while giving nothing up? Please concentrate on what a sympathetic defender of the view would themselves count as counting against it, rather than on objections from outside it. Three things in particular bother me. First, the mechanism rests on an empirical claim about cost and controllability that I have no way to check and that may simply be false of the technologies that arrive. Second, if that mechanism fails, I can't tell whether my view survives as anything more than generic concern about suffering, or whether it quietly collapses into ordinary extinction-risk prioritisation wearing a different hat. Third, I can't tell whether the position has been insulated: would any finding about, say, the motivational structure of present-day AI systems, or the absence of any plausible mechanism at all, actually move me, or would I reinterpret it as further confirmation? If it has been insulated, say whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or merely what one should expect from a thesis about a future nobody observes. I'd personally be most moved by arguments that the leverage is missing — that nothing available to us now changes the default — and least moved by scepticism about whether suffering matters. I can't chase references from where I'm working, so please argue the structure rather than direct me to literature.
