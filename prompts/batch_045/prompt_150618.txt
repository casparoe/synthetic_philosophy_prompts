I've been reading rationalist blogs since like 2009 — LessWrong back in the Sequence days, SSC/ACX after that, and I still lurk the comment sections — and this term I'm helping write the final for a 3rd/4th-year seminar that's half philosophy department, half stats, on causation and statistical evidence. Every single person in that room has absorbed Simpson's paradox as "always disaggregate, coarse data lies", and I want a question that breaks that heuristic without just collapsing into "lol correlation isn't causation". I'd of written it myself but I keep producing tables whose arithmetic doesn't come out.

So: one multi-part question, 25 marks total, meant to be answerable in about 60 minutes as one section of a take-home final. It has to be self-contained — the students get the numbers on the page, no reading, no papers, no citations — and it must be impossible to answer well by reciting the definition or retelling the kidney-stone-style story.

Here's the shape I want, though improve it if you see a better crux:

(a) A worked reversal. Here's a table I sketched: drug A vs drug B, patients split into mild and severe. Mild: A recovers 90/100, B recovers 76/80. Severe: A recovers 30/100, B recovers 56/160. Pooled: A 120/200, B 132/240. Check my arithmetic, replace the numbers if mine are broken, and make the students compute the stratum-specific and pooled rates/risks and state the reversal precisely (in percentage points, for both strata and the aggregate).

(b) The same table, two causal graphs. Ask for one story in which severity is a pre-treatment variable that influences which drug the doctor picks, and one in which "severity" is a post-treatment state caused by the drug (a mediator), and have them show that the identical numbers support opposite recommendations in the two cases. The point is that the table alone doesn't tell you whether to read the stratified or the pooled comparison, and "slice finer" isn't an answer.

(c) Two things that people conflate. First, a derivation: show that if treatment assignment is independent of the stratifier within strata, the pooled risk difference is a convex combination of the stratum-specific risk differences (give the decomposition of the crude difference into an effect term plus a composition/selection term, and evaluate both terms numerically on the part (a) table), and conclude that no sign reversal is possible for the risk difference, risk ratio, or odds ratio in that case — so an observed reversal is itself evidence about the assignment mechanism. Second, a separate randomised table you construct yourself: treatment randomised 50/50 inside each stratum, both strata showing the same odds ratio (I was aiming for about 4.0 in each, with very different baseline risks across strata), where the pooled odds ratio comes out well below 4.0 even though nothing is confounded. Have them compute it and then explain why that gap is not Simpson's paradox, and which of the numbers is the right one to report for which decision.

(d) A short bit on subgroup fishing: a researcher slices a randomised trial thirty ways and reports the one stratum where the sign flips. Ask why the machinery from (b) and (c) doesn't license that, and what would have to be true for a subgroup claim to be credible.

Deliverables: the question as it would appear on the exam paper, a model answer of roughly 700–900 words, and a marking scheme with marks per part adding to 25 that seperates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each has to make, plus a one-line note per part on how students typically get it wrong.
