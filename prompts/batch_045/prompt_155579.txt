My thesis and my argument. The case is Newton's bucket: a bucket of water hanging from a twisted rope; at first the bucket spins and the water's surface stays flat; later the water spins with the bucket and climbs the sides. At the end the bucket and the water are at rest relative to one another, exactly as they are when the bucket hangs still; the relations among the material bodies in the set-up are the same, and the surface is not. The usual moral is that the difference must be explained by rotation with respect to something other than those bodies — absolute space, or, in the modern setting, spacetime — and hence that substantivalism wins and relationism loses.

I want to argue that this moral is too quick, and to offer a relationist-friendly alternative together with an argument for it that I have not seen anywhere. Two parts.

First, a distinction I have been working with. Call kinematical relationism the view that all facts about motion, acceleration and rotation are facts about relations among material things. Call dynamical relationism the further view that the correct laws of motion must be formulable using only those relational quantities. My claim is that the bucket refutes at most dynamical relationism, and even then only given an unargued restriction of the admissible relata to the bodies explicitly mentioned in the set-up. The relationist is free to hold that the inertial structure itself — whatever it is that marks some trajectories as force-free and others as not — is a material structure: contingent, present in varying degrees, able to act and be acted upon, much as the electromagnetic field is. I have been calling this the inertial-field version of relationism. On that view the water climbs because it is accelerating with respect to a real, local, material structure, and the bucket argument gives the substantivalist nothing.

Second, the part I am most pleased with, and the part I expect to be attacked. Replace the single bucket with a case I have not seen discussed: two identical buckets in an otherwise empty universe, set counter-rotating at a fixed rate relative to one another, ω. On substantivalism, I claim, an asymmetric outcome must be permitted — one bucket genuinely at rest with a flat surface, the other doing all the rotating with a concave surface corresponding to the full rate ω. On relationism the case is symmetric by construction, so the surfaces must match, each bucket rotating at half the relative rate. Since the depth of the paraboloid goes as the square of the rotation rate, the symmetric outcome gives each surface a quarter of the depth of the single concave surface in the asymmetric outcome, and that is a difference you can read off the water. So, I argue, the substantivalism/relationism dispute is not the empirically empty stand-off it is often said to be: the two views diverge over a two-body world, in a quantity that is in principle measurable there. But equally, the single bucket by itself does not hand the case to the substantivalist, because once the inertial field is admitted as a relatum the standard argument loses its asymmetry.
