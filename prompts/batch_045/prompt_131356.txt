I've been reading Hofstadter on Aunt Hillary and I've basically converted: I now think a mature ant colony literally has beliefs and desires, and that my having beliefs is the same trick one level down, a brain being a colony of neurons none of which believes anything. Map the exposure for me: what would count against colony-level mentality, ranked fatal to merely awkward, and which of those would a defender of the view concede rather than shout down? The cases I keep going in circles on are the fission case, where a landslide or a flooded gully cuts the trail network in two and both halves carry on, so the view owes me an answer about whether Aunt Hillary has become two half-remembering people or was never one person to begin with, and the boundary problem in general, since neighbouring colonies sometimes feed the same trunk trail, so the states that are supposed to be her beliefs are partly laid down by outsiders, which on a literal reading gives you either one mind smeared across two nests or a swarm of overlapping partial minds. I'd also like to know how much weight to give two other worries: that if a stale trail decaying counts as forgetting, then any slowly-changing field starts to look like a mind, and that the colony seems to have no way of representing, or doing anything about, its own death. Where I expect to be most movable is a prediction about what a colony should be able to do that a brain can't, given that only brains have anything like a bottleneck or a global workspace. And if it turns out that everything awkward can always be reclassified as "a different kind of mind", tell me whether that insulation is itself the fatal defect or whether a view like this one is entitled to it.
