I'm presenting to our hospital's ethics committee next week and could use a Q&A prep sheet. The room is two transplant surgeons, a neurointensivist, two palliative-care nurses, a risk-management lawyer, a Catholic chaplain, and one community member whose sister was declared brain dead last year and who still isn't sure it was handled right.

I'm defending what I've been calling a one-way ratchet for death determination: the default stays the current dual standard (neurological or circulatory), but any patient or surrogate can opt into a stricter protocol than the default — mandatory ancillary testing on top of the clinical brain-death exam, longer observation windows, a no-touch period for circulatory determination measured in tens of minutes rather than a few — while nobody can opt for anything looser than the default. Sketch of the argument: what does the moral work here isn't locating the true border between life and death but treating people as agents whose own framework for what counts as a serious boundary gets respected; the two error directions aren't symmetric, since declaring someone dead who isn't (or cutting short a dying they wanted to be allowed to complete) is a different order of wrong from failing to recover some transplantable organs; and the ratchet should actually lower refusal rates, because families who distrust us get something concrete to say yes to instead of just being told the protocol is settled science.

Give me the objections I'll actually get from this room, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase it, with how much damage each one does, a one-to-two-minute reply, and where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front and which question I'm hoping nobody asks.
