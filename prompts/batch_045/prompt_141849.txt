I write novels — four published so far, realistic ones, mostly set in and around the post-industrial towns of the English Midlands — and I care about sentences in a way my friends find slightly embarrassing. Several of them are academics, and at dinner last week we got into an argument I have been turning over since. The question was what George Orwell would have made of the way prose is produced now: predictive text that offers to finish your clause before you have decided what you think, assistants that will rewrite a paragraph into any register on request, and — the part that actually got to me — the quiet convergence of everything toward one flat, competent, faintly transatlantic house style, because my British and American copyeditors are both steadily sanding my characters' dialogue into something nobody in Stoke would say and nobody in New York would notice. Everyone at the table said the same thing: Orwell would have loathed all of it. I think that answer is too cheap, and I want the extrapolation done properly.

What I am asking for is an argument from the machinery of his actual position, not from his reputation. I have read 'Politics and the English Language' and the appendix on Newspeak in Nineteen Eighty-Four and I don't need either of them summarised back to me; take them as known. Two features of his view make me doubt the obvious answer. First, the mechanism he describes is one of scarcity: a language deliberately made smaller, edition by edition, so that the heretical thought has no words left to be thought in. Our situation looks like its mirror image — an enormous vocabulary, statistically averaged, in which every sentence has fifty available phrasings, all interchangeable, and the range of thought narrows not because words were removed but because the most probable ones keep winning. In my own notes I have started calling this 'thesaurus Newspeak', and my hypothesis is that he would recognise it as the same disease at a different stage, reached by surplus rather than by deletion. But I would like to know whether that is right, or whether his account really does depend on scarcity, and would simply not fire on a language this large. Second, he held that decayed prose is a symptom with political and economic causes, and that no amount of verbal hygiene repairs it while those causes stand. If that is the structure of his thinking, he should be far less interested in the software than in who owns it, what it is optimised for, and what it does to the person doing the writing — which would make him an odd ally for people who want the tools banned or their output policed.

So I would like you to write the piece he would write, staying inside his own logic and telling me where it leads, including where it leads somewhere uncomfortable. Concretely: what would he isolate as the tell — at what level of the sentence would he locate the damage, given that his usual suspects are the dying metaphor, the prefabricated phrase, the abstract noun and the passive, whereas the new failure may be something he had no name for, like uniform sentence rhythm, symmetrical hedging, or the absence of anything a writer had to pay for? Where would he draw the line for a working novelist between help and authorship — correction of spelling and fact, correction of sentences, generation of sentences, shaping of structure — and would he draw it where I think he would, which is much further toward the sentence than most of my colleagues would accept? What would he say to me, knowing I am tempted because it is fast, and to the publishers who will soon be able to produce a serviceable book without me? And would his anti-censorship instincts and his suspicion of any intellectual orthodoxy ever put him on the wrong side of his own cause — defending speech he despised because the remedy on offer was worse? Ground every claim in the arguments he actually made and in how those arguments are built; don't invent quotations from him or attribute lines to him you cannot stand behind, and don't send me to look anything up. And please don't turn this into a culture-war sermon about campuses or about Big Tech. I want the diagnosis from inside his mind, including the places where it refuses to say what the people at my dinner table wanted it to say.
