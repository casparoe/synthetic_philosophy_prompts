I'm in a final-year seminar on distributive justice, and our week on desert and luck has left me with a pile of verdicts I can't get into any order. I'd like you to help me find the principle behind them. Please don't spend the answer pointing me at readings or reconstructing what various theorists have said; I have plenty of that, and what I need is the shape of the view I apparently already hold, stated precisely enough that I can argue with it.

Here are the cases, with my gut verdicts.

1. Two students turn in final essays of the same quality, and collaboration was allowed, but one worked steadily for three weeks and the other wrote hers from natural facility the night before. I think they deserve the same mark, and I also think the first deserves something the second doesn't — credit, or admiration, or something I can't name. Both verdicts feel firm.

2. Desmond reads everything, comes to every office hour, writes in good faith, and produces a badly argued essay that fails. I don't think he deserves a pass. But I do think he deserves the discretionary participation mark, and I don't feel I'm being charitable in giving it to him — I feel he has a claim on it.

3. Priya is very gifted, skips half the reading, writes a first-rate essay in an evening, and gets the top mark. I think she deserves the mark. I also feel, without being able to justify it, that she doesn't quite deserve the praise that goes with it, and that the mark overstates what she actually accomplished.

4. A student copies a proof from a solutions manual but had already solved it correctly on his own. He deserves the mark for that problem, and he deserves to be punished, and the punishment should not be a zero on the problem. A student who copies a proof he couldn't have produced deserves neither the mark nor much sympathy.

5. Two engineers of equal skill and equal diligence; an unanticipated change in industry standards makes one of their specialisations far more valuable. I think neither of them deserves the resulting pay gap, and I also think it would be wrong to tax the difference away. I can't explain why those two aren't inconsistent.

6. She gets the job because her father is a major donor. She is competent and does the job well. I think she didn't deserve to get it, and I also think it would be wrong to fire her now in order to give it to the candidate who deserved it.

7. My department allocates the eight places in the honours lab by what it calls an effort-weighted lottery: one ticket per applicant plus one extra ticket per logged hour of preparation, capped at ten. I think this is fairer than a flat lottery and worse than ranking applicants by a diagnostic, and I think a student who wins with two tickets over someone with ten does not deserve his place but should keep it.

8. A student has a language model produce a first draft and then rewrites it so heavily that the final essay is, I think, genuinely his. He deserves the mark. A student who prompts the model well and edits lightly produces an equally good essay and, in my view, deserves nothing — even though the output is the same.

The pair I can't reconcile is 1 and 8: equal output, unequal effort, and in one case the low-effort student keeps the mark while in the other he loses it. The other wobble: when I'm told that Desmond's capacity for sustained work is largely the product of an unusual upbringing, my verdict on 2 wavers, but my verdict on 3 doesn't, even though Priya's facility is equally unearned. That seems backwards.

The principle I keep reaching for is that there are two currencies — credit for what you put in, entitlement to what you produce — and that both are discounted but not cancelled by how much of the input or the output traces to unchosen luck, except that I don't apply the discount to Priya. Test that against the cases and repair it. Then tell me what the repaired account says about two cases I haven't listed: the student who works hard only because a visa condition forces him to, and the student whose essay is poor because she is working two jobs, judged against a well-resourced classmate whose essay is just as poor. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts above the account cannot accommodate, and whether that verdict or the account should go.
