I have no academic background — I'm a retired contracts manager who has spent the last eighteen months reading about pain on my own, mostly philosophy of mind and the occasional neurology paper I can half follow — and what I need from you is help planning one session, not the material for it.

Every second Sunday I run a discussion group: eight or nine adults, a whiteboard, no slides, seventy-five minutes. One is a retired nurse who has watched morphine do its work and has administered pain scales for years. One is a physics teacher who will want a definition before we go anywhere and will keep asking whether the brain imaging settles the question. One does ten-day silent meditation retreats and will tell us within ten minutes that pain is entirely optional once you stop resisting it. None of them has studied philosophy.

The question I want them to leave with is not whether pain is bad but something narrower: what is it about being in pain that is supposed to be bad — the feeling itself, or something that normally but only normally accompanies the feeling? The case I keep encountering is the one in which people with certain kinds of brain injury still report pain: they locate it, describe it as stabbing or burning, rate its intensity as high as anyone would, but do not appear to mind it in the least — they do not pull away, they sometimes laugh, and they are in genuine danger of injuring themselves further because nothing about the pain moves them to stop.

Because that case seems to me to cut only one way, I have invented a companion case, and I want to know whether it is coherent and whether it earns its place. In it the pain feels exactly as it always did — same place, same quality, same modest intensity — but it has acquired what the man in the case describes as an unbearable urgency: he cannot think about anything else, he cannot be talked out of doing something about it, and when pressed he says that it is not that it hurts more, it is that it will not stop demanding that he act. My suspicion is that the usual two-way split, between the sensory-discriminative aspect and the affective-motivational aspect, is too coarse, and that what people call the badness of pain really has two separately detachable parts: how much it hurts, and how much it insists. If that is right, the first case on its own will let the group stop too early, at the thought that the badness lives in the affective part, and will also leave them with the comfortable moral that suffering is voluntary and that a person who has trained themselves out of it has lost nothing.

Please give me a plan for the session in the sections below, at roughly the lengths indicated.

**What I have got wrong** — about 120 words. Whether my second case makes sense at all, whether the three-way decomposition is worth the trouble at this level, and anything in the way I have framed the question that will cause trouble later in the hour.

**The order of ideas** — about 250 words. What has to be in place before what, and specifically what breaks if two of the pieces are introduced in the wrong order.

**The example that carries the session** — about 200 words. Which case, mine or a better one, and what it must have and must lack in order to do the work.

**Misconceptions, and where each one gets defused** — about 300 words. Include at least these four: that the answer will be delivered by neuroimaging; that this is just the claim that pain is really two things, a neutral sensation plus a separate emotion, like seeing a snake and then being afraid of it; that the meditator's first-person report settles it; and that someone who does not mind their pain is not thereby any worse off. Place each at the point in the sequence where it will actually arise.

**The question I will ask at the end** — about 100 words. One question whose answer I could not get from a participant who is merely parroting back that pain has two components.

**What I should refuse to go into** — about 120 words.
