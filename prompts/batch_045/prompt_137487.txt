Two people in a reading group I sit in on spent the last session arguing about whether you can acquire justified aesthetic beliefs from what others tell you, and they asked me to adjudicate. I could not. Compressed, their exchange went like this.

(1) Nadia: Not really. A friend whose taste I trust tells me a record I have never heard is exhilarating, and I can come to believe it. But the belief is second-hand in a way my belief about the record's running time is not. The point of an aesthetic judgment is to register how the thing strikes you, and I have outsourced the one part I was supposed to do myself. It is belief on a ground of the wrong kind.

(2) Owen: That is a purity claim, not an epistemological one. The process is reliable: trusted critics' verdicts predict what you will later experience, and you rely on them constantly to allocate attention you cannot spare. If the process is reliable, the belief is justified, and that closes the epistemic question.

(3) Nadia: I grant the reliability. That is why my claim concerns the kind of ground, not the track record. A perfectly reliable indicator can still be the wrong thing on which to base a given kind of judgment: form the belief that a joke is funny because you were told it is funny, or choose a partner on the strength of a matching algorithm's score, and the mechanism works while the resulting attitude still seems defective.

(4) Owen: Those two examples are carrying your argument, and neither is analogous. Being told a joke is funny fails because amusement just is a response, and you cannot have a response at second hand. But aesthetic judgment is not a response in that sense; being told the record is exhilarating is more like being told the soup is salty. You check later, and you keep or drop the belief.

(5) Nadia: Saltiness is precisely the disanalogy, since you can check it without the soup's having to strike you as anything. Here is the case I think settles it. An installation runs forty hours; no single visitor can experience all of it; a critic who has done so tells you that it turns monotonous around hour thirty, in a way you would only register by being there through hour thirty. You trust her, you believe it, you will never be able to confirm it, and you reorganise your plans around it. If that belief is justified, testimony transmits aesthetic verdicts wholesale and my worry in (1) evaporates. If it is not, then reliability was never doing the work in (2), since she is as reliable as testimony gets.

(6) Owen: I will take the case head on: justified, provisionally, revisable on further experience, but justified. And now you have overreached. Critics predict my responses better than I do, and they know I will end up loving the thing I am currently prejudiced against. So on your view I may justifiably believe, on testimony, that I would love it, while being barred from believing that it is good. Those cannot come apart, because my evidence for the first is simply that it is good in the way I respond to.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the six, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Where a move is ambiguous between a stronger and a weaker reading, say which reading it needs in order to work.
