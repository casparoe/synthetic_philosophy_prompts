My reading group (we do a slow read of old confirmation-theory papers, with a lot of LessWrong-adjacent arguing around the edges) got stuck on Hempel's raven thing again last week. Someone made the standard move: bayesian resolution, the green apple does confirm but only by a vanishingly small amount, the reason we go and look at ravens is efficiency rather than relevance, topic closed, let's move on. I pushed back and said I thought you could cook up a situation where the ordering flips, and now I owe the group a case and I'm honestly not sure one exists, hence this ask.

So: construct an ordinary, non-science-fiction scenario — a warehouse stocktake, a museum collection inventory, a bird checklist for a small reserve, a photo archive, whatever works — in which a single observation of something that is neither a raven nor black confirms 'all ravens are black' MORE strongly than a single observation of a black raven does. That's the reverse of the usual verdict, and I want it to fall out of the setup rather than be stipulated. Constraints: the population has to be finite and small enough that you could in principle enumerate it; the counts and the priors have to be readable off facts you actually put in the story (an inventory printout, a packing list, the caretaker's notebook) rather than numbers you assert because they deliver the answer; and the asymmetry should be a fact about the world in the story — non-black objects being rare in the reference class, or the sampling procedure not being uniform over the population, or whatever you pick — and not a fact about anyone's psychology. If you use uniform random sampling over the whole population, say so explicitly instead of leaving it implicit.

Then do the actual work. State the hypothesis and its contrapositive, state the prior over the ways the population could be, and compute (or at least argue very carefully) the posterior after each of the two observations, so I can see the two confirmation verdicts come apart in the direction I asked for. Check the equivalence condition out loud: the two sentences are logically equivalent, so any Bayesian has to treat them identically — so where does the intuitive difference between 'go check the ravens' and 'go check the non-black things' actually live, if it isn't in the hypothesis? And then the diagnostics I really want: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the verdict if I flip just that feature (make non-black things abundant and ravens rare), where I expect it to collapse back onto the standard 'of course you go look at ravens' answer and want to watch it do so; and how someone who is still a stubborn Nicodian — holding that only ravens can be evidence about ravens — would attack the case. Don't just announce the verdicts, derive them; if it needs arithmetic, do the arithmetic; and please be straight with me if you think the spec can only be met by smuggling something in.
