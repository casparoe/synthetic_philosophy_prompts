The problem is that I can't tell whether she's a textualist or a purposivist, and it matters for both cases. Here's what I've got from her opinions over the last few years, badly abbreviated:

1. Security deposit statute: "a landlord shall return the deposit within 21 days of the tenant vacating the premises." Tenant moved all her stuff out and handed over the keys 30 days before the lease ended but kept paying rent; landlord mailed the deposit 24 days after the lease ended. Held: no violation — "vacating the premises" means end of the tenancy, and she got there by looking at how the rest of the landlord-tenant chapter uses "vacate" and "surrender." (I thought the tenant had the better textual argument honestly, and the point of a quick-return rule is that you shouldn't have to wait when you've allready left.)

2. Wage statute: "an employer may not require an employee to bear the cost of a uniform the employer requires." Employer made employees rent their uniforms from it at six bucks a week, deducted from payroll. Held: violation. Rental is a cost.

3. Meal-break statute: "an employer shall provide a meal period of at least 30 minutes for any shift longer than five hours." Employer gave the 30 minutes but required people to stay on site and answer the phone; one guy got interrupted twice. Held: complied. The rest-break section right next to it says "duty-free," this one doesn't, and she said the omission has to mean something.

4. Financial responsibility statute: "no person may operate a motor vehicle on a public highway unless the vehicle is covered by liability insurance." That title's definition section: "motor vehicle means any self-propelled device not operated on rails." A different title defines motor vehicle to exclude "electric personal assistive mobility devices," but says "as used in this title." Guy on a stand-up e-scooter, 20 mph, no insurance, hits a pedestrian. Held: motor vehicle. She also refused to look at the committee report saying the chapter was about cars and trucks — there's a line somewhere to the effect that she doesn't consult floor statements and that when the words are ambiguous she asks how the code uses them elsewhere.

5. Insurance cancellation: statute says notice has to be "mailed to the insured's last known address." Insurer emailed the cancellation notice; the insured had been emailing with them for years and admitted he got it. Held: adequate. She leaned hard on a general provision in the code letting written notice go electronically to someone who's consented in writing.

6. Abandonment: "a tenant who abandons the premises before the end of the term remains liable for rent for the remainder of the term." Tenant left after the city condemned the building following a fire she didn't start. Held: not liable — she invoked absurdity and said she wouldn't read a statute to make someone pay rent for a unit the city had ordered nobody to occupy.

7. Worker classification: statute has an enacted "findings" section about the harms of misclassification, then a presumption of employment unless the hiring entity proves three things. She quoted the findings and read the three conditions hard against the platform. Worker is an employee.

So: is she running "text, read against the whole code, no unenacted history, absurdity as a narrow escape hatch," or "read it for what the legislature was trying to do and stretch the words when they undershoot"?
