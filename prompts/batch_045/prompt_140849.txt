I host a tiny podcast called *Unforced Errors* (mostly ethics and aesthetics for people who already have opinions), and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who's just published a book on what Bernard Williams's enthusiasm for opera has to do with the rest of his ethics. I've read the manuscript and I want the episode to actually press on it, so help me draft 9 or 10 questions.

Here's what she argues, so you have the targets.

Her central claim: Williams spent a lot of energy on the thought that ethical conflict leaves a *remainder* — an agent who does the right thing may still have reason to regret what she did, and the morality system (obligation, blame, the demand that every conflict be resolvable by a decision procedure) has no room to represent that residue. Her book's thesis is that opera is the one art form that has developed techniques for representing remainder, and that this is why it matters philosophically rather than just being enjoyable. Her term for the mechanism is the *ensemble condition*: in a big ensemble, four characters sing four mutually incompatible resolves at the same time, so you don't get the roads not taken narrated sequentially as counterfactuals — you hear them as simultaneously live. She calls this the compresence of alternatives, and says it does something first-personal deliberation structurally can't do: hold both options open without settling between them.

Three places she gets specific, and three places I want to push:

1. She uses this to answer the old charge that opera plots are ridiculous. Her line is that the objection is a category mistake: it treats the libretto as a failed piece of spoken drama and judges it by whether the characters' beliefs and motives are propositionally believable, when in fact the music gives you a kind of uptake that isn't belief-shaped at all. So the apparent silliness is what you get when you apply a test the form was never answerable to.

2. She has a chapter on surtitles and translation. She insists she's not anti-accessibility — she's explicitly for them — but she also argues that reading a translation above the stage turns the performance into something closer to illustrated text and thereby encourages exactly the propositional misreading from point 1. This looks to me like the softest spot in the book, and I want to get at it without being cheap about it: she can't just be told that deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences exist, she knows that and has an answer.

3. On production: she argues that director's-theater stagings which rewrite an ending so that the heroine survives, or gets what she wants, are committing what she names narrative consequentialism — treating the work as an outcome-delivery device and maximising the net balance of satisfaction, which is precisely the utilitarian-shaped error Williams spent his career attacking. Whenever anyone raises this she says, more or less, that's a staging decision, not an objection.

Some other things to work in if they fit:

- Williams's Gauguin case, where the justification is essentially retrospective and can fail. She extends this to performance: a singer's justification for the life she's led depends on whether the performance comes off, and it might not, on any given night. I'd like a question on whether aesthetic value can be luck-dependent in the way she needs here, or whether she's just borrowing the structure of the argument.
- The one thought too many. She claims that people who love watching a character suffer on stage don't owe a justification for that, and that philosophy of art has been trying to extract one for too long. But her own framework makes audiences articulate why the compresence matters. Is she handing them one thought too many?
- Her general escape hatch: whenever a question gets too empirical she says the music isn't in the business of making claims. I want a run of questions that makes that move progressively less available, and I want the last one to be about what evidence could actually count against the view — some finding about how ordinary listeners process ensembles, or about whether the effect survives when you don't know the plot.

Format I want: for each question, one follow-up written for the answer she's most likely to give, not for a strawman. Assume she's read all the Williams, knows the secondary literature cold, and is very good at this. Don't give me any what drew you to this material openers, and don't let the questions turn into requests to summarise the book.
