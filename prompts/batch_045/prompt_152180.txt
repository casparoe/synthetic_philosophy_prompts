I have been hanging around rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years now — Less Wrong in its heyday, then Slate Star Codex, then whatever it renamed itself to — and one habit I picked up there has turned out to be my actual professional obligation: whenever someone proposes a fix for a bias, I want to know what observation would tell us the fix worked, and I want that specified before the fix is adopted rather than after. I am now the token philosopher of science on the Research Practices Committee at my institute, a nine-person body (two PIs from different departments, a statistician, the veterinary director, a postdoc rep, a grad student rep, our compliance officer, a senior ethologist, and me) that adjudicates methods disputes on animal-behaviour studies and issues recommendations that get attached to annual renewal reviews. We can recommend and we can attach conditions; we cannot shut a lab down. We have one meeting, in eleven days, to resolve a case that has split us almost exactly in half, and I am drafting the document I will circulate beforehand. I would like you to draft it for me.

The case is this. A lab is testing whether an anxiolytic compound increases affiliative behaviour in group-housed mice. Nothing about the design is exotic: treated and control animals are filmed in the home cage, and a rotating group of undergraduates and one postdoc score the video frame by frame for events the lab cares about — social approach, rearing, grooming bouts, and what the lab calls agonistic contact, which is the outcome variable. The coders are not blinded, and the lab says they cannot be: the treated animals carry a shaved patch for injections, and the cage cards are visible at the edge of the frame. Inter-rater agreement between two coders is high by the lab's own numbers. The effect is large and in the predicted direction. Four options are on the table: require genuine blinding (re-filming, cropping, digitally obfuscating the patch and the cage card, and using coders who have not been told the hypothesis); replace human scoring with off-the-shelf pose-estimation software that detects body-part keypoints and derives the events automatically; keep the current pipeline but add more training, calibration sessions, and periodic reliability audits; or leave the humans in place but force hard pre-registered decision rules plus a held-out set of reference clips scored by consensus to serve as a ground truth. Roughly half the committee thinks automation dissolves the problem entirely, since the computer has no hypothesis; the other half, including me, thinks that pushes the problem upstream, because such systems are trained on human-labelled frames and the labels carry whatever the humans brought with them. Underneath the disagreement is a question I recognise from philosophy of perception, and which I think, unlike most of what we argue about, actually determines which policy is right: whether a coder's belief about which condition they are watching can change the content of what they visually experience — a two-hundred-millisecond contact between two animals — or whether it can only change where they look and for how long, or only what they say afterwards once the seeing is over. Those three loci, it seems to me, call for three different interventions, and the interventions are not equally expensive. If the experience itself is shaped by the expectation, then agreement between two coders who share the expectation is not evidence of accuracy, training will not fix it, and the only remedies are removing the expectation or removing the human. If it is only attention that is steered, then forcing full-frame review, minimum dwell times, and enforced coverage of the whole clip may be enough. If it is only downstream judgement and reporting, then hard rules and reference sets are the right tool, and blinding is gold-plating that costs a postdoc a year. I am not confident which it is, and I want the memo to be honest about that rather than pretending to a diagnosis the literature does not support — but I also want it to land somewhere actionable, because abstaining is itself a decision that leaves the current practice in place.

Please work as a professional philosopher of perception and general philosophy of science who has served on methods and integrity committees before, and who is comfortable writing documents that non-philosophers have to act on. Before you write the memo, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming and listing: the candidate diagnoses (experience, attention, and report, and any intermediate case you think is real); what each one would predict about the observable pattern in this lab's data, including the pattern you would expect if the effect were partly at one locus and partly at another; which policy follows from each; and where you are genuinely uncertain. Then write the memo itself, roughly eight hundred to a thousand words, in American English, addressed to the committee. It should open with a concrete recommendation with a phase-in time frame — not a menu of options — and it should be specific enough that the lab could comply with it by a date. It should then give the reasoning in terms the statistician and the ethologist can follow, defining any technical term in a line or two, since only two of us are philosophers. It should include a serious, steelmanned statement of the strongest argument for whichever option you are rejecting — I would rather lose an argument that has been stated in its best form than win one that has been stated in a weak one — and it should close with a short section, under a heading like What would change our mind, that specifies in operational terms the observation or result that should make us revisit the policy, ideally something the lab could actually produce, such as rescoring the same clips under two differently framed hypotheses. Do not include citations, references, or a bibliography: nobody on this committee will check them and half of them will be distracted by them, so the argument has to stand on its own. And please do not give me a general essay on objectivity in science; I want a document with a bottom line that nine people can vote on.
