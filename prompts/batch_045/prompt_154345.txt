I'm in a final-year seminar on nationalism, and I want to frame my term paper around a genuine disagreement rather than around my own opinion, so I'm trying to work out how our seminar leader will rule on a case he has never discussed. The course has been built around two clusters of reading: the invented-tradition literature — the collection Hobsbawm edited with Terence Ranger, and the definition offered in its introduction, roughly: practices of a ritual or symbolic kind, governed by overtly or tacitly accepted rules, which aim to inculcate values and norms by repetition and which thereby imply continuity with the past — and Anderson's treatment of the nation as an imagined community, imagined as finite, as sovereign, and as a deep horizontal comradeship, held together by a shared standardized public language and a print-mediated public sphere rather than by face-to-face acquaintance.

For weeks he has been handing down yes-or-no verdicts on individual cases without ever stating the test he is applying. On nationhood:

- Scots: a nation. Union of 1707, devolved parliament, an active independence movement, a literary vernacular with an eighteenth-century canon, and the whole kilt-and-tartan apparatus.
- Catalans: a nation. Their own language of public life, autonomy, a mass sovereignty movement.
- Québécois: a nation. Provincial institutions, sovereignty referendums, no state.
- Kurds: a nation. Stateless, dialect-divided, a standardized written language only relatively recently, a strong movement.
- Americans: a nation. A settler nation, English-speaking, no single descent group, but a canonical print-public, a civic creed, and founding myths.
- Swiss: not a nation — he called it a state, not a nation: four public languages, no single vernacular readership, no national movement, despite strong civic institutions and a founding myth of its own.
- Yugoslavs: not a nation, notwithstanding a state, a state-sponsored official nationalism, and a substantial number of people who did declare themselves Yugoslav in the census; his reason was that it had no uptake from below.

On traditions:

- The Highland kilt-and-tartan clan pageantry: an invented tradition.
- The ceremonial a colonial administration staged for its African subjects, devised in the first half of the twentieth century and taken to be immemorial within a generation: an invented tradition.
- A village harvest festival with continuous observance traceable to the sixteenth century, recently given a fixed date and an organizing committee: not an invented tradition — the continuity is real, only the formalization is new.
- A firm's annual founders' day, openly celebrating the company's 1911 founding with a formal ceremony: not an invented tradition — he said it is a commemoration rather than a tradition because it claims nothing beyond its own date.

The case I want to write about is the Esperantist community, Esperantujo. It has a fully standardized planned language, created by one man and published in 1887, which the community dates and celebrates openly rather than disguising. It has a substantial literature and periodical press from early on, and today a public sphere that is largely online: forums, podcasts, a very large encyclopedia, video channels. It has a flag with a green star, an anthem, an annual world congress held in a different country each year since the early 1900s, a holiday on its founder's birthday, a ritual vocabulary of its own, and a network of members who host travelling speakers in their homes. Nobody is born into it; it has no territory, though there have been periodic proposals, mostly tongue-in-cheek, to acquire one; it makes no claim to statehood and its self-conception is explicitly universalist — its origin story is a cure for Babel rather than a descent from it. Members imagine and feel solidarity with thousands of others they will never meet, and the community plainly has outer boundaries; but the solidarity is voluntary, achieved, and elective rather than inherited.

Give me your best prediction of his verdict, with the reasons for preferring it, and be explicit about which element of the imagined-community characterization — boundedness, sovereignty, horizontal comradeship — is doing the work in your prediction, and whether a print-capitalist mechanism is supposed to generalize to an online public sphere.
3. If his track record genuinely underdetermines the case, say what he would be committing himself to with each verdict.
4. Name the single further case you would put to him that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and say why.

Please argue from the cases and from the concepts rather than sending me to further reading — I have no access to the library database from home, so citations would be no use to me anyway.
