Question: Most people think that the fact that you promised is itself a reason to do what you promised — not merely a prediction about your behavior, and not just a shorthand for the bad consequences of backing out. Explain two accounts of why that is: (i) promising as a social practice or convention, whose whole point is to make it possible for people to count on each other, and (ii) the view that the obligation comes from what you do to the promisee in promising — you give them an assurance, and breaking it wrongs them specifically. For each, say what it gets right and what it has trouble with, using these cases: a promise nobody ever relies on, a promise nobody would ever find out about, and a promise extracted by a credible threat.

Here is the answer I'm grading:

Both views have to explain the same datum: that the fact that you promised is itself a reason to act, and specifically a reason to do the thing promised rather than merely a reason to bring about whatever good the promise was aimed at.

Hume's practice view says promising is an artificial virtue — a convention we invented because of how useful it is to be able to count on people, and a convention that only holds up if people mostly keep their promises. Its motive is the general usefulness of the practice, which is why the obligation is artificial rather than natural. Since usefulness is the whole point, the obligation bottoms out in consequences: you should keep a promise when and because keeping it produces more good overall than breaking it, and the convention is mostly just a reliable proxy for that. That's why it's fine to break a trivial promise in order to do something more important.

The alternative, Scanlon's principle of fidelity, starts from the promisee instead. When you promise, you give someone an assurance and lead them to expect that you'll act; they then rely on it, and the wrong of breaking the promise is the setback to their interests that this reliance produces. So the obligation is owed to that person in particular, not to the practice. It follows that the strength of the obligation scales with how much the promisee actually stands to lose, and that a promise nobody relies on and nobody is disappointed by is barely an obligation at all.

A third option worth mentioning is the power view: promising is a normative power over your own will, by which you hand the promisee a claim on you. This handles the secret promise fine, since the transfer happens whether or not anyone notices or relies on it. And it explains why duress isn't a defense: you still chose to say the words, so a promise extracted by a credible threat binds just as much as any other.

Both Hume and Scanlon struggle somewhere. The practice view explains why the practice is worth having but not why I have to keep this promise when no one would ever find out. Scanlon's view has trouble explaining why promises bind at all, if the obligation is really just a function of the interests at stake.
