hey — I'm a journalist, I cover tech and ideas, and I keep hitting the same move in interviews and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear.

The move: "a virtual world is just a game of make-believe, like kids playing house or like reading a novel. Nothing real happens to anyone in there." I got it again last month from a studio exec I was interviewing about a game they're sunsetting — players are going to lose inventories they've spent years and in some cases thousands of dollars assembling — and he used it to argue that (a) wiping that stuff isn't destroying anyone's property any more than burning a copy of a novel murders a character, and (b) stuff like griefing, or the VR groping cases, isn't a real harm because nobody is physically touched. Then a couple of weeks later I talked to a VR researcher who used the same make-believe framing to argue the opposite: that because it's all staged, platforms have *more* responsibility, the way a theater company does, not less. Same analogy, opposite conclusions, which makes me think the analogy is doing all the work and nobody's checking it.

So: audit it. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the props, the rules of the game, the players, the fictional truths, the pretense itself — and then where the mapping breaks. I especially want the breaks that matter for the two conclusions above, not the ones that are just philosophical scenery.

Things I can't get straight on my own and want you to sort out:

1. Where the causal work lives. My hunch: in make-believe, the pretend object's causal powers are loaned to it by the participants' imaginations, and they evaporate the moment everyone stops playing. In a virtual world they're loaned to it by code running on somebody else's machine, and the thing keeps its state after I log off. Is that a difference in kind or just in how long the loan lasts? And does it matter that the lender is a company that can revoke it?

2. Money and labor. Real cash changes hands, and real servers and real devs' hours hold the thing up. Does the analogy quietly import "nothing of value is created or destroyed here" from the fiction case, where that's roughly true, into the virtual case, where it isn't?

3. The immersive version. Does the analogy's verdict shift if we go from a text-only MUD to full-body haptics? Because if it says "no real harm" in both, I think that's a reductio of the analogy rather than a finding about the cases — but if the fans of the analogy should just bite the bullet there, I want to hear the version of that which isn't obviously stupid.

4. Which harms are even in question: harm to the person (fear, distress, the body's stress response), harm to a social fact (a convention of ownership backed by payment and by other people's recognition), harm to the object itself. Tell me which of these the make-believe framing can even express and which it can't describe at all.

And at the end: does either conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? If there's a better comparison — a theater, a club with house rules, a bank ledger, a shared document, whatever — I'd like it; and if the honest answer is that virtual worlds are a genuinely new kind of thing and we should stop reaching for analogies, say that too. I'd definately rather have that than a tidy answer.
