I'm on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and we keep hitting the same wall whenever a case involves someone doing something good that nobody, strictly speaking, had to do. Every definition we invent in practice either swallows the phenomenon — suddenly everything good is optional and no one is ever criticised for doing nothing — or empties it, so that anything worth doing becomes something you're required to do and the category we care about disappears. I'd rather stop guessing and get my own reactions into some order, so here are my gut verdicts on a batch of cases, including a few we made up because the standard ones don't separate the things I want separated.

(a) A passer-by wades into a cold canal to pull out a child who fell in: knee-deep water, miserable, small risk of hypothermia, no real danger to life. Praiseworthy; I don't blame someone who walks on. (b) The same rescue, performed by an off-duty lifeguard who happens to be there and could do it at essentially no risk to herself: still praiseworthy, but less so than (a), and her walking on feels like a failing in a way (a)'s doesn't. (c) Donating a kidney to a stranger: hugely praiseworthy, and I recoil from saying it's required. (d) Donating a kidney to your own brother, same cost, same surgery: I still don't want to call it required, but declining feels much worse to me than declining in (c), and I can't say why. (e) The second shift: someone gives up Saturday to help a neighbour move, is asked to give up Sunday too, and says no. No blame at all — less than if he'd refused the first request — yet saying yes would impress me more than saying yes to the first request did. That pair is the one I can't fit into any single account. (f) Cheap heroism: an unusually strong swimmer does (a) at negligible cost and rather enjoys the attention. Praiseworthy, but barely; it strikes me as a bit like showing off. (g) Preemptive: registering as an organ donor now, while it's voluntary, shortly before a new policy makes it the default. Faintly praiseworthy now, and I think it stops being praiseworthy the moment it's expected. (h) Borrowed generosity: giving a thousand dollars to famine relief while owing a thousand to a friend who needs it back. Not praiseworthy to me at all — perhaps worse than not giving — even though the recipient is benefited exactly as much as in a clean donation. (i) A case my teammates and I invented, which we call shadow supererogation: you decline the good act knowing someone else will step in, and they do, so nobody is worse off. Not praiseworthy, not blameworthy either, and yet there is something slightly off about you that I can't put my finger on. My verdicts also seem torn between two ways of sizing up credit: by what the act cost the agent, which is why the lifeguard gets less, or by how much good it did, which is why the kidney donor outranks the guy who gave up a Saturday.

So: is there one principle that captures this pattern — the acts that are good and optional and whose omission isn't blameworthy, plus whatever clause is needed for cost, for relationships, for doing it second, and for doing it with other people's money — or does the pattern force me to drop one of my verdicts? Please state the principle precisely and tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: an optional good act performed incompetently, leaving the person you meant to help worse off than if you'd never intervened. Keep your whole reply under 450 words, and structure it as follows: one sentence stating the principle up front, then one short line per clause naming the case that motivates it, then your verdict on the unlisted case, then an honest note on which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn.
