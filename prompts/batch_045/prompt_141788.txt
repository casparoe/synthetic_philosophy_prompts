I run a small reading group — six philosophy grad students, a cosmologist, and a friend from machine learning — and we have spent two sessions stuck on what we have started calling the problem of ontology identification under inaccessible information. I have boiled our disagreement down to three cases, and in each one I want you to stipulate the following: the two candidate descriptions assign exactly the same probability to every possible observation that any agent in that world could ever make, they share all the same laws, and neither is more parsimonious at the level of laws — they differ only in what exists, and the difference is confined to information that is in principle inaccessible. Case 1, the Sealed Annex: given accelerated expansion there are regions of our universe from which no signal can ever reach us; hypothesis 1a says the statistical distribution of matter out there continues roughly as it does in our patch, galaxies and planets and perhaps minded creatures, while 1b says that at some fixed distance beyond our horizon matter density drops abruptly to zero and stays zero forever, the difference being only in which outcome of a genuinely chancy early-universe process was realized outside our patch. Case 2, the Long Recurrence: our final physics is a deterministic automaton with a finite state space, so every trajectory is ultimately periodic, and the two hypotheses differ about whether the cycle the universe eventually settles into contains any token of some ordinary natural kind, say an organism, where deciding the matter would require more computational steps than any physical process inside the universe can perform before heat death; note that here the discriminating fact is a perfectly well-defined mathematical fact about the trajectory and is merely computationally out of reach. Case 3, the Traceless Interval: a mature psychology holds that during dreamless sleep subjects undergo a long sequence of rich conscious states, and its variant holds that they undergo none, where the two theories assign identical probabilities to every observation — no memory, no report, no difference in later behavior, mood, or learning, and no difference in any measurable neural state, the brain's physical trajectory being numerically the same on both. Call a case determinate when there is a fact of the matter about which of its two descriptions is correct, one true and the other false, and not merely a choice of description.

I want a number on this: your credence that at least two of my three cases are determinate, plus your separate credence for each of the three. My own headline number is about 0.6 and my machine-learning friend puts hers at about 0.15, and we cannot locate where the gap between us actually lives. So give me the decomposition, not just the number. I have been toying with two principles that seem to pull in opposite directions, and I would like each to get its own probability in your tree: the accessibility filter, on which a difference in what exists is a genuine difference only if it leaves some difference in accessible information; and the continuity principle, on which whatever settles ontology for the accessible parts settles it for the inaccessible parts continuous with them, since there is no metaphysical magic at the horizon or at the edge of memory. Tell me how you handle the fact that those two cannot both be right, and include whatever other terms you think belong — whether realism about the unobservable is true at all, whether reference to theoretical items in inaccessible regions is determinate or is threatened by model-theoretic indeterminacy arguments, whether each of my pairs is a genuine difference rather than a notational or gauge-style one, whether the trace condition matters more than accessibility as such — with a decimal probability and one line of justification for each, then show how they combine and say explicitly which terms screen off which rather than multiplying them as if they were independent.

Then the diagnostics. Which of the three cases is your headline number most sensitive to, and why does that case do more work than the others? Which single sub-question, if it went the other way, would move you furthest, and which term are you least confident in? Given that by hypothesis no observation could ever settle any of this, say what would even count as evidence or as resolution here, and what kind of argument would move you by a substantial amount. Tell me which of my three cases, if any, is not really the same shape as the other two, or which of my stipulations you think is incoherent rather than merely far-fetched, and how your number changes if that case is dropped or the stipulation repaired. Finally, tell me where you think the disagreement between me at 0.6 and my friend at 0.15 most likely lives, and what each of us would have to concede to close it. Please answer in American English, give every probability as a decimal, and commit to numbers rather than to the claim that it depends.
