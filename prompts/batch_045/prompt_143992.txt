I write long-form features on science and ideas for a national magazine, and the piece I am finishing now is about why human beings find consciousness so puzzling — not about whether the puzzlement is justified, but about what in our cognitive makeup generates it in the first place, the question that has come to be called the meta-problem of consciousness. The section I am stuck on is the one where I have to say which of the competing psychological explanations of that puzzlement is in the best shape. The published surveys all hedge, so I would rather run a stress test: I want you to rank the candidate accounts against a stipulated evidence pattern, not to report what the field thinks.

Here are three findings I am stipulating. I am not claiming any of them has been observed; I take them to be empirically coherent, and I have tried to design them so that they pull in different directions.

1. In a pre-registered, double-blind study, a compound that lowers the precision of interoceptive and metacognitive signalling — while leaving first-order perception and reasoning measurably intact — substantially reduces subjects' agreement with the claim that the physical facts cannot settle what experience is like. The effect scales with dose, and it is not accompanied by any change in what subjects can discriminate or report about their own experience.

2. The intuition is largely insensitive to first-person richness. People with lifelong absence of visual imagery, and people who report no inner speech at all, endorse the "physics leaves something out" claim at essentially the same rate as controls.

3. The intuition generalises. The same conviction — that no amount of third-person description could capture the thing itself, and that reassurance to the contrary misses the point — shows up for knowledge of one's own agency, for the felt directedness of one's thoughts, and for the unity of the self over time. It is markedly attenuated in patients with focal damage to midline self-monitoring networks who are otherwise unimpaired on the corresponding first-order tasks.

Here are the accounts, in my own words, plus one that I have not been able to find in print:

A. Two-routes. We can single out a state introspectively and we can single it out by its physical or functional role, and nothing in our command of the first route entails that it coincides with the second. The apparent gap is a gap between routes of reference, not a gap in nature.

B. A posteriori identity. The gap is genuinely epistemic but unsurprising: some truths are necessary yet knowable only empirically, and our puzzlement is a failure to recognise that a posteriori necessities feel contingent. The error, on this account, lies in inferring metaphysical conclusions from conceivability.

C. Illusionism. The introspective judgement that experience has an ineffable qualitative character misreports what it tracks. There is no such character to explain; the whole job is to explain why the misreport is so persuasive.

D. Self-model opacity. The machinery that monitors our own states cannot represent its own mode of operation, so it presents experience as brute and intrinsic. The puzzlement is a system bumping against the limits of its own self-representation.

E. Developmental by-product. We are built to treat minds as detachable from bodies, and we bring an essentialist bias to any kind we care about. Philosophical training overlays this rather than replacing it, and the intuition carries no evidential weight about consciousness itself.

F. Structural insight. The intuition is partly veridical. Physical description specifies only relational structure, introspection is our only access to the intrinsic nature that instantiates that structure, and the puzzlement registers a real feature of our epistemic situation rather than a malfunction.

G. Control calibration — this is the one a researcher proposed to me and that I cannot source. Introspective reports are not descriptions of what experience is composed of; they are control signals, specifying how a state is to be used downstream — attended to, acted on, remembered, reported. The puzzlement is what happens when a channel whose format is optimised for regulation is interrogated as if it were a channel optimised for constitution: we ask "what is this made of?" of a signal that only ever answered "what is this for?"

Rank all seven from strongest to weakest on a single dimension: how much of the stipulated pattern each account accommodates without bolting on ad hoc stipulations, where an account gets credit both for predicting a finding in advance and for absorbing it gracefully after the fact. Put them in tiers rather than a bare list — the accounts you would expect to have predicted this pattern, the ones that survive it with one added stipulation, and the ones that need rescue — and give each a score from 0 to 100 for how well it comes out of the whole exercise. One sentence per account on why it lands where it does.

I need commitment rather than balance: the piece will contain exactly one sentence of the form "On the evidence as stipulated, the account that should come out ahead is ___," so give me your own ordering and defend it, rather than telling me which specialists would vote which way. Along with the ranking, tell me which of the three findings did the most work in producing it, and flag the one adjacent pair in your ordering that you would most expect to have backwards.
