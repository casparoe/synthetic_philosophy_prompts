After a long argument with a friend last night I went back to the primary text, and found we had been disputing a sentence neither of us had read carefully. It comes from Franz Boas's introduction to the first volume of the Handbook of American Indian Languages (1911): It does not seem likely [...] that there is any direct relation between the culture of a tribe and the language they speak, except in so far as the form of the language will be moulded by the state of the culture, but not in so far as a certain state of the culture is conditioned by the morphological traits of the language.

My friend reads this as a flat denial, a generation early, of what later got called the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis. I read it as an asymmetry: influence conceded in one direction (culture shapes the form of a language), refused in the other (a language's morphological traits do not determine a culture). In the argument I was also relying on my recollection that Boas argues elsewhere in the same introduction that each language's obligatory grammatical categories compel its speakers to attend to distinctions that other languages leave unexpressed — which, if right, opens a hole in the asymmetry: culture could shape attention at one remove, by first shaping the categories that then shape attention, and that is merely an indirect relation, while Boas denies only the direct one.

Please help me with three things: whether the sentence really bears the asymmetry I read into it, or whether the restriction to morphological traits and the contrast between moulded by and conditioned by make it narrower than I think; whether my two-step reading is consistent with the passage or would have struck Boas as itself refuting it; and what he would count as moulding — if it amounts only to vocabulary growing to fit a people's practices, my reading collapses into trivia. Say how confident you are in each part of what you tell me, and single out the one claim you would be least willing to defend.
