ok so my friend (econ phd student, insufferable about it) and I spent like two hours last night arguing about whether game theory shows that rational people can't really disagree, and afterwards he sent me a summary of what he says the results actually are. I want to know how much of it to trust, because most of it sounded plausible but I have a feeling he's overselling it. Here's his summary, more or less his words:

'There are two classic Aumann results and between them they basically settle the question. The first is agreeing to disagree: if two people are both rational and they start from the same prior, they cannot disagree — formally their posteriors for any event have to be identical — so any disagreement that survives between two intelligent people has to come either from one of them being irrational or from their having started with different priors. The second is that common knowledge of rationality delivers backward induction: in any game of perfect information, if it's common knowledge that everyone is rational, you get the backward-induction outcome, full stop. That's why a rational player grabs at the very first node of the centipede game. And you don't need the infinite hierarchy literally — after five or six levels of "I know that you know that I know", it makes no difference. Notice also that "rational" here just means maximizing expected utility given your beliefs, not being selfish, so none of this assumes people are greedy. Putting the two together: common knowledge of rationality pins a game down to its correlated equilibrium, since everyone is best-responding to what everyone knows about everyone's beliefs. Which is why communication changes everything: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, if the two players are allowed to send each other a cheap non-binding message first, then it's common knowledge that both are rational, each knows the other will cooperate because both prefer (C,C) to (D,D), and you get cooperation.'
