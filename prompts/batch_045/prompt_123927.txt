You are a decision theorist and formal epistemologist with the St. Petersburg literature, expected-utility theory, and the whole family of infinite-expectation puzzles at your fingertips. Please think carefully and show your work. In your first pass, brainstorm as many candidate defeaters as you can, including ones you will eventually discard; only then filter, sort, and rank. I would rather watch you decide which candidates fail than receive a tidy list of survivors.

The position I want stress-tested is one I hold fairly confidently: the St. Petersburg paradox is not a genuine normative puzzle. On this view the "paradox" is an artifact of scoring gambles by expected monetary value. Score them by expected utility with a concave — and, strictly speaking, bounded — utility function instead, and the game has a small finite expected utility, the reluctance people report is roughly what the theory predicts, and nothing is left over for decision theory to explain. On this view, the finite-casino-bankroll reply, appeals to probability weighting or to the neglect of tiny probabilities, and the thought that infinite expectations are simply pathological, are all beside the point: at best they are psychological stories about how subjects produce numbers, at worst they are confusions, and in neither case do they touch the normative claim.

Give me an honest map of how exposed that position is, and organize your answer as a ranking from what would be fatal down to what is merely awkward. For each entry, say whether a defender of my view should concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as not engaging the view at all, and flag which defeaters are already live in the discussion as it stands and which are merely possible.

Take the payoff schedule to be the usual one: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and k tosses pays 2^{k-1} units, so the payoffs are 1, 2, 4, 8, … with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … Please work through, at minimum, the following.

First, the fit that embarrasses me slightly. As I work it out, logarithmic utility over this schedule yields a certainty equivalent in the neighborhood of two units, which is suspiciously close to the small sums people actually report paying. Is that a real success of the dissolution, or is it coincidence, overfitting, or an artifact of the fact that a very wide family of concave functions will deliver some small number here? Tell me whether I should trust the fit or discount it.

Second, the discriminating tests I can construct. Because log utility is scale-covariant up to an additive constant, I take it to predict that multiplying every payoff by a hundred should multiply the certainty equivalent by roughly a hundred, whereas a bounded utility function should predict compression toward the bound. So a flat willingness-to-pay across a hundredfold rescaling would, I think, kill the logarithmic version but not the bounded version. Relatedly: for a game capped at N tosses the expected value is about N/2 dollars, but as I compute it the expected log utility is essentially saturated once N is even moderately large, so the view seems to predict that willingness-to-pay should be nearly insensitive to where the cap is set. Tell me whether that is a genuinely risky prediction the view could fail, or whether the residual class of admissible utility functions is flexible enough that any observed pattern can be fitted — and if it is that flexible, say so directly, because that is the unfalsifiability worry I most want addressed.

Third, the variant I have been turning over myself, and the one I find most persuasive against me. Run the same game in a currency that is not money but something we have independent reason to value without diminishing returns: years of healthy life, say, or lives, distributed over identifiable other people, with the sure fee paid in the same currency and borne by other people too. Diminishing marginal utility of a personal consumption good now has no grip, since the payoffs are already denominated in the thing utility was supposed to track. The defender must either say that total welfare itself has diminishing marginal value, which is a substantive claim about interpersonal aggregation rather than a fact about anyone's risk attitudes, or say that value is bounded above, which as I see it commits one to something like near-indifference between very large and astronomically large numbers of lives. Is this the strongest available defeater, or am I simply smuggling in a rival theory of value and then complaining that the dissolution does not answer it? Say which, and hold me to account either way.

Fourth, the variants in which the expected-utility series is only conditionally convergent, so that expected utility comes out undefined or order-dependent rather than merely infinite. If those work, the diagnosis cannot be diminishing marginal utility at all, and the real issue is something about infinite series in general. How much of my position survives if I concede that?

Finally, two framing questions. What fraction of the exposure you identify is really exposure of expected-utility theory as a whole rather than of my dissolution in particular, and does my view inherit that risk or is it entitled to treat it as someone else's problem? And give me your honest verdict on whether the position, as I have stated it, has been qualified into something that cannot lose — and if it has, whether that is a defect here, given what kind of claim it is.
