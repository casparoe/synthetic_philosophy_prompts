Had a massive argument with a mate last night about what heritability actually means, and I've stupidly agreed to make a decision tree for our reading group about it (mostly biology undergrads, plus two economists who keep saying 'so you're saying its genetic then'). Can you build it?

Start point: I accept that h² comes out at 0.6 for grip strength in a cohort of British 60-year-olds. I don't care whether that number is right — it's fixed for the sake of the tree. What I want is for the tree to take someone from that starting commitment to a named position on what heritability does and doesn't tell you.

Seven branch nodes, four to six leaves. Every node should be labelled with the single claim that splits it from its siblings, and phrased as a yes/no or a small concrete case that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf gets the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody really holds it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually seperate rather than pretending it can.

The angle I care about is what the number licences you to do: whether it says anything about what an intervention would achieve, about the cause of a difference between two groups, about whether the trait is malleable. I don't want any branch about how h² is estimated — no twin-vs-SNP, no A/C/E decomposition, no missing heritability.

Three things from the row have to show up as cases, because they're what actually split us:

- He said a trait can be 100% heritable and still be completely preventable by an environmental fix, and equally that a trait can be 0% heritable and still be impossible to change with anything we can actually do. I think he's right about both but I couldn't explain why in a way that convinced him.
- He kept going on about whether how much an intervention helps you is itself heritable — a training programme with a decent average effect might work great for some genotypes and do nothing for others. I'd never thought about it. Does that belong on the tree, or is it a different question wearing heritability clothes?
- And he insisted you can't even ask what proportion of the gap between two groups is genetic, that the question isn't defined. I said surely you can at least estimate it. Where does that sit on the tree, and who's closer to right?

One more: h² being higher in older cohorts than younger ones came up a lot. Is that its own branch or just a consequence of one of the others?

If some combination of answers has nobody living in it, say so, and tell me whether it's empty because it's incoherent or just unattractive.
