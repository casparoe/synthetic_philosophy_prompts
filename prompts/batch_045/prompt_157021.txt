I teach a seminar on other minds, and this term I also got roped onto our university's animal research ethics committee, which is revising its rules, and the two things have collided in a way I can't untangle. Our current rules track the statute: a "protected animal" is any living vertebrate plus any living cephalopod, with mammal, bird and reptile foetuses covered only from two-thirds of gestation, cephalopod embryos not at all, and a power to extend the definition to other invertebrates that nobody has used. A separate sentience law from a couple of years ago defines "animal" to include decapod crustaceans as well, and sets up a committee to look at whether government policy has all due regard to the welfare of animals as sentient beings. So crabs and lobsters are sentient in one statute and not protected animals in the other; octopuses are protected in the lab and, as far as I can work out, get nothing at all at the point of killing, and nothing stops a kitchen from boiling a lobster alive; insects fall outside animal welfare law entirely even though there are now industrial-scale insect farms; and rats poisoned as pests get no review while rats in a protocol get the full harm-benefit apparatus. Every time I describe this to my students I announce that it's philosophically indefensible, and then I can't tell them what a defensible version would even have to look like.

The reason I'm writing is a proposal from one of the vet school people on the committee. She wants to throw out teh taxon list and score organisms on three behavioral markers instead: whether their response to injury is specifically reduced by analgesia rather than just being sedated or slowed down; whether they flexibly trade off a noxious stimulus against a competing motivation in what looks like one integrated decision rather than a reflex cascade; and whether a bad experience in one context carries over into something like a persisting negative state in a different one. Organisms get sorted into tiers, tiers get fractions of the protections a rat would get, anything thin on evidence is listed provisionally and re-reviewed on a schedule, and where we're simply clueless we default to protection.

So here's what I want you to reconstruct. First, the theory of consciousness behind the list we actually have: does the practice only make sense if consciousness is a sharp property that whole taxa either have or lack, or is the list just an evidence-based proxy — in which case what it presupposes is a view about how to tell, given that we can't observe other minds directly, and not a view about what consciousness is? Does cephalopods-in / insects-out commit us to anything about centralized brains, integrated representation, or neuron counts, or is that a heuristic several rival theories could happily share? Second, is the decapod split genuinely incoherent, or does it presuppose a distinction I'm failing to see — between being sentient and having standing, say, or between having interests and having interests that this or that statute owes duties to — and which rival views does the list quietly rule out? Third, and this is the bit I keep going in circles on: how much of this does the practice strictly presuppose as opposed to merely fit with? Could someone who thinks consciousness comes in degrees, or who is fairly skeptical about other minds, or who thinks moral status is relational rather than intrinsic, sign off on exactly the same table for completely different reasons? And if we did adopt the tier scheme, what would we be taking on that we aren't taking on now — does fractional standing even make sense if what's graded is our evidence rather than the state itself, and does a graded scheme with a precautionary default end up collapsing back into a threshold in practice anyway? Give me your own reconstruction rather than citations or things to read, and please write it in American English.
