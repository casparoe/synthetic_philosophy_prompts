I would be grateful for your help with an argument that was presented in a reading group I attend. Its author uses a spectrum construction to conclude that all-things-considered betterness among options is not transitive. My difficulty is that the argument, stated as she stated it, seems to me to establish something considerably stronger than that, and stronger than she wants — but I cannot locate the step that generates the extra strength, and I cannot tell what giving that step up would cost me.

The case. Consider, for one person, lives specified along two independent dimensions: the number of years lived, and the level of well-being in each year, on a scale on which 100 marks an excellent life and 1 a life barely worth having. Nothing at all is assumed about how the two dimensions aggregate into an overall value; the argument is meant to rest only on intuitive pairwise comparisons. Let A be a life of 80 years at level 100, and B a life of 476 years at level 1. Between them lies a sequence of 199 options, S0 through S198, with S0 = A and S198 = B, each Sk+1 obtained from Sk by adding two years of life and subtracting 0.5 from the annual level.

The argument:

(1) Tolerance. For any option x, a sufficiently small net gain along one dimension, bought at the cost of a sufficiently small loss along the other, yields an option better than x. Nothing in this principle favours one of the two dimensions over the other.

(2) Each step of the sequence is a trade of exactly that kind: two additional years of life for half a unit of annual well-being.

(3) So each option in the sequence is better than its predecessor.

(4) If betterness is transitive, then B is better than A.

(5) But A — eighty excellent years — is clearly better than B — 476 years each of them barely worth having.

(6) Therefore betterness is not transitive. (And if what each step really establishes is only that its successor is not worse than it, then even the weak relation, "at least as good as," fails to be transitive.)

My worry is that (1) is stated symmetrically in the two dimensions, so the construction runs in reverse as well. Begin at B and trade the other way — surrender two years of life for a half-unit gain in annual well-being, 198 times — and one arrives back at A, with every step licensed by the very same principle. Run forward and backward together with transitivity in place, and the result is not a failure of transitivity but a failure of asymmetry: A better than B and B better than A, for a pair of options that are plainly not equally good. I take asymmetry to be non-negotiable, and so, I think, does she: a relation that can hold in both directions between two options cannot tell an agent what to do, and it is unavailable to someone who wants intransitive betterness to remain a coherent, choice-guiding phenomenon. Moreover, the argument seems to use nothing peculiar to value. Substitute "is the better treatment option" (length of life against severity of side effects) or "is better supported by the total evidence" (fit with the data against simplicity), supply an analogous two-dimensional spectrum in each case, and the same machine appears to grind out the same result. She is happy to accept intransitivity for prudential betterness but not for evidential support, and I cannot see what in (1)–(6) would justify treating the two differently.

So what the argument appears to establish is a global scepticism about tolerant, multi-dimensional comparative judgment, with her favoured local thesis about betterness as only one of several available outlets — chosen, as far as I can tell, because she likes it. I want to know where the machine overreaches.

The claims I am most reluctant to surrender are (5); asymmetry; and the denial that there is a determinate step in the sequence at which the trades stop being improvements, a step whose location we merely fail to know. Given those three, something else must go. Is it (1) itself? The assumption that one fixed trade size can do duty at every point of the sequence, rather than some possibly different, possibly ever-smaller trade at each? The assumption that the 199 pairwise verdicts are all verdicts of one and the same relation, rather than of judgments each made against a standard its own pair sets? Or the assumption that betterness relates options at all, as opposed to relating them relative to a menu, a comparison class, or a question?

Please tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked innocuous enough to pass unnoticed, and what rejecting it commits me to — including what I would then have to say to my friend about why intransitivity rather than the collapse of asymmetry is or is not the right conclusion, and about what an agent who accepted her conclusion should actually do when faced with a choice. If the correct diagnosis leaves us with a transitive ordering that has no maximal element, I would also like to know whether that is a stable position or merely the same problem relocated.
