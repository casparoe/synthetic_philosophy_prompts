I sit on the disciplinary tribunal of a national civil service, and we are about to issue findings in a case that has split us. The respondent managed a unit that ran automated eligibility reviews for a benefits programme. After a rule change was encoded into the triage system, her unit's thresholds began terminating payments to a cohort the change was never meant to reach. A junior analyst flagged the anomaly within weeks; the respondent ran spot checks, satisfied herself the error rate was low and that appeals would catch most of it, and chose not to halt the run or escalate to her director, partly to protect her unit's processing targets and partly because a halt would have required her to put her doubts in writing. Roughly three thousand households lost income for months; a number were evicted before reinstatement. She expressed no hostility toward the affected group at any point, privately disliked the policy's political framing, and on her own time helped some of the same clients file appeals.

Our sanctions framework treats demonstrated hostility toward an affected group as aggravating and absence of personal animus as mitigating, and neither factor was drafted with this kind of case in mind. Half the panel argues that she is precisely the bureaucratic perpetrator: the failing is not malice but a failure to think from the standpoint of the people her decisions landed on, and treating absence of animus as mitigation inverts the point. The other half argues that mitigation must track culpability; she neither intended nor clearly foresaw these harms, and an expressive sanction for a failure of imagination punishes a character trait rather than a chosen act. A further complication: the individual terminations were produced by a system whose parameters her superiors mandated, so it is unclear how much of the decision was hers.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate: a recommended finding on the charges before us, a recommended sanction with a stated range, and a recommendation about what the published findings should say about responsibility, since they will be read as the service's official account. One colleague keeps invoking Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial — evil as thoughtlessness rather than wickedness — and thinks it settles the case; another thinks that is a philosopher's category that will not survive being written into a disciplinary finding. Take up that dispute directly, including whether the category even fits someone who did notice and weighed the notice against throughput. Give your strongest version of the argument you are rejecting rather than dismissing it. Finally, separate what is settled on the facts and on our existing precedent from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and flag explicitly anywhere your reasoning depends on a contestable reading of Arendt or on a contested view of how algorithmic workflows distribute agency.
