I got nerd-sniped by a welfare label on a bag of frozen prawns and ended up reading through aquaculture welfare standards for fun. Please don't send me links, papers or a reading list — I've read plenty of the standards themselves, what I want is the conceptual stuff sorted out.

Here's the pattern I can't make consistent. As best I understand it:

- For farmed finfish the rules are almost entirely about the last few seconds. The fish is supposed to be stunned before it's bled or gutted, and the standards specify how fast it has to become insensible, checked with pretty basic behavioural tests — does it right itself, does it react to being rolled over or prodded, have the gill covers started moving rhythmically again.
- Shrimp and prawns are usually just chilled in ice slurry, and the defence you read is that they go torpid quickly. But the same method looks defensible for warm-water fish like sea bream and much worse for cold-adapted species, where chilling can stretch the time to death out to over an hour. So whether a method counts as humane turns on the animal's own thermal biology.
- Welfare gets scored at the level of the pen or the crop, not the individual: density in kilos per cubic metre, mortality as a percentage, fin and eye condition sampled across a group. A crop can certify while some slice of the animals is in visibly bad shape.
- The pre-slaughter fast — they stop feeding for days so the gut empties before harvest — is filed under food safety and meat quality. It doesn't show up in welfare scoring at all.
- Meanwhile eyestalk ablation (crushing or cutting the eyestalk of female broodstock to make them spawn) is prohibited in European organic production, some retailers have committed to phasing it out, and UK law now recognises lobsters and octopuses as sentient. So its not as if nobody thinks anything can go badly for these animals during their lives.

What I'd like:

What theory of welfare does this package presuppose? To me it looks like it locates basically the whole moral problem in the process of dying, and in acute pain specifically, while being nearly silent on the months of life before that and on how many individuals are suffering at a given moment. Does it presuppose that death isn't a harm to the animal if the killing is quick, that only suffering matters, and that welfare is an aggregate property of a population rather than something had by an individual — or are some of those just what it happens to fit well with?

Where is it actually inconsistent? The eyestalk stuff and the sentience recognition seem to presuppose that what happens over an animal's life matters, and the crop-level scoring seems to presuppose the opposite of individual-level concern. Is there one theory that makes the whole package coherent, or is it several theories plus this is what's cheap and auditable when you're processing tens of thousands of animals an hour?

The behavioural tests bother me most. Unresponsiveness is being used as a stand-in for insensibility, but for a pig we wouldn't treat a sedated or paralysed animal that doesn't move as therefore fine. Does the practice quietly presuppose a behaviourist or operationalist epistemology of animal consciousness, or is it compatible with a perfectly non-behaviourist one given what can be measured on a processing line?

And what would actually change under a different theory? Under a desire-based view, or under a deprivation view where death is the central harm because it takes away the rest of the animal's life, my sense is that you don't get a reformed industry, you get no industry — is that a real implication or am I over-reading it? And if you did take individual welfare across the whole production cycle seriously, what would a standard even look like with tens of thousands of animals and essentially no individual monitoring — or does that collapse into a position that isn't really about welfare standards at all?
