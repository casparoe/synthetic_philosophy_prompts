**The question as set**

*Question 4.* In Part Three of *Reasons and Persons*, Parfit asks us to imagine a teletransporter: a machine that records the states of all one's cells, destroys one's body, and transmits the record to Mars, where an exact replica is created. Parfit argues that teletransportation would be about as good as ordinary survival, and that this shows identity is not what matters in survival.

(a) Explain the distinction Parfit draws between identity and what matters, and explain why he thinks the fission case — in which each hemisphere of your brain is transplanted into a different body — supports his conclusion. Be sure to state what Parfit means by reductionism and by the further fact view, and what Relation R is.

(b) Now consider the Relay Case. You travel to Mars in two stages — Earth to a lunar station, then lunar station to Mars — each stage an ordinary scan-and-replicate. The person at the lunar station lives for six months before being scanned; over those six months she forms new memories, loses many of the ones she arrived with, and her character shifts somewhat. The person who finally steps out on Mars therefore shares relatively few of your present memories and traits, although each link in the chain is fully psychologically continuous with the one before it. On Parfit's account, does the Mars person's relation to you contain what matters? What does your answer show about his claim that what matters, unlike identity, comes in degrees?

Throughout, distinguish clearly between claims about identity and claims about what matters.

**Candidate 0427's answer**

Parfit's teletransporter is designed to prise apart two questions that we ordinarily run together: the question whether some future person will be me, and the question whether my relation to that person contains everything that matters in survival. His claim is that these questions can come apart, and that where they come apart it is the second that should govern what I have reason to want.

The background is a dispute about what identity consists in. Parfit's reductionism holds that a person's identity over time just consists in the holding of more particular facts — facts about physical and psychological continuity — and that there is no fact of my identity over and above these. The further fact view denies this. Since an immaterial soul is the only candidate anyone has ever proposed for such a further fact, non-reductionists are committed to substance dualism, which is why Parfit thinks their view is so unattractive and why he can afford to spend relatively little time on it.

Parfit then argues that even when the question whether she will be me has a clear answer, it is not the question that should concern me. What matters in survival, he claims, is Relation R: psychological continuity and/or connectedness, with any cause. Two features of R drive the argument. First, identity is one-to-one and all-or-nothing, whereas R holds in degrees and can fade. Second, R need not be uniquely instantiated, whereas identity must be. The teletransporter on its own does not settle much, since someone can always dig in and say that the Replica on Mars is not me and that is the end of it. Fission is what does the work. If each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body, I cannot be identical with both of the resulting people, and there is no non-arbitrary way of making me one of them; yet my relation to each contains exactly as much of what matters as ordinary survival would. So the non-reductionist who insists that identity is what matters is left claiming that something of enormous importance to me turns on a bare further fact — a fact that makes no difference to any of the experiences, memories, plans or character traits involved. Parfit finds that incredible, and concludes that in fission the question which of the two resulting people is me is an empty question: there is no further fact to settle it, and since identity is not what matters, nothing of importance hangs on its being settled.

The Relay Case brings out the first of the two features. What matters is preserved here: R is defined over overlapping chains of strong connectedness, and the chain from me to the person who steps out on Mars is unbroken at every link, so her relation to me contains what matters even though she shares few of my present memories and traits. But it contains less of it than an ordinary night's sleep would, because the direct connectedness between us is thin; relay travel is therefore worse for me than an ordinary journey, though not catastrophically so. This is what it means to say that what matters, unlike identity, comes in degrees — or rather, on Parfit's view, identity itself is a matter of degree as well, and the person on Mars is me to just the reduced extent to which R survives the trip.

Two qualifications are worth adding. First, the thesis is restricted: it is a claim about what matters in survival, not about everything we might care about in our dealings with others. Second, reductionism is not a denial that people persist — the claim is only that persistence is not a further fact. That is why the reductionist can say that in the ordinary unbranched case there is a perfectly determinate answer to the question whether some future person is me. It is also worth seeing how radical the denial of a further fact really is: once the physical and psychological facts are fixed there is nothing left to settle, and so even the question whether the person who wakes up in my bed tomorrow is me is, strictly speaking, an empty question — just as it is in fission, and, in a different way, in the Relay Case.
