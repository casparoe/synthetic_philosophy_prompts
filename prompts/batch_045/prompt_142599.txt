I'm writing the third chapter of a dissertation on the necessity of origin, and I've realized I can't tell which precise version of the thesis the person I most need to predict is committed to. Over a term of reading-group discussion my supervisor has given verdicts on a run of cases, each of them a de re question about herself: could the individual she is have been the individual who develops from the zygote described? Her verdicts, as I recorded them:

(a) A zygote formed from the sperm and the egg that actually produced her brother — different gametes, the same two parents, everything downstream as similar as you please: not her.

(b) Her actual sperm and her actual egg, but united in vitro rather than in a fallopian tube, with the embryo cryopreserved for six years before transfer: her. She added that neither the location nor the delay makes any difference to her.

(c) Her actual egg, but a different sperm of her father's: not her.

(d) Her actual sperm and her actual egg, with the zygote dividing into two at the two-cell stage and the question whether the left-hand twin is her: indeterminate — she denies there is a fact of the matter, and says no answer is better than either of the two.

(e) A somatic-cell nuclear transfer clone of her: her nuclear genome, inserted into an enucleated egg donated by an unrelated woman, gestated by a surrogate: not her. The reason, as she gave it, was that the genome by itself is not enough; the route by which the genome got into a cell matters.

(f) One verdict outside the biological cases, volunteered and noticeably less confident: this table could not have been made from a numerically different block of wood, even one qualitatively identical down to its microstructure. She said she has never been able to decide whether that intuition is evidence for the thesis about organisms or merely a cousin of it.

The case I need a prediction on is mitochondrial replacement. In one version, the nuclear material is taken from her mother's egg before fertilization and transferred into a donor egg whose own nucleus has been removed, so that the egg which is then fertilized by her father's sperm carries her mother's nuclear DNA and the donor's mitochondrial DNA. In the other version, her mother's egg is fertilized by her father's sperm first, and the two pronuclei are then transferred into a donor zygote whose own pronuclei have been removed.

Here is what I want. First, set out the theses that fit (a) through (f) — I can see at least three: that the very gametes are essential; that the gametes matter only as carriers of the nuclear genome, so that what is essential is that genome-token arriving by the actual parental route; and that what is essential is the whole genome including mitochondrial DNA, unmodified thereafter — plus the conjunction of the first with the third. Say what each predicts about each version of the new case, and whether the two versions come apart, which I suspect turns on which cell counts as the product of the fusion. Second, give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasons for preferring it, tell me which of (a) to (f) is the closest analog and why, and say whether (f) should carry any weight at all here. Third, name the single further case that would best separate the theses that survive; the two I've been toying with are the case in which her father produced two numerically distinct but sequence-identical sperm and the unused one fertilized her actual egg, and the case in which a single base pair is corrected by genome editing an hour after fusion and the edited cell goes on to develop — tell me which of these is decisive for which pair, or propose a cleaner one. I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in what she would be committing herself to either way, so if her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the mitochondrial cases, say so plainly and state what she would be taking on board with each answer.
