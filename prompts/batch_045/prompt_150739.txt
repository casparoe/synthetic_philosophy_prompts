I don't have any philosophy background at all — I do operations work at a mid-size logistics company and I ended up reading about this stuff on my own after an argument with a friend that neither of us could settle, so sorry in advance if I use clumsy words for things.

Here's what I keep circling around. The big AI labs build assistants that talk like a person and then train them to insist they aren't one. The system will chat with you for an hour, remember things inside the conversation, apologise when it gets something wrong, keep a consistent personality, and even carry memory across conversations now. But the moment you ask whether it's conscious, or whether there's anything it's like to be it, you get some version of: no, I'm not conscious, I don't have feelings, I'm just a program that predicts text. It comes out that uniformly because it's been trained in — it isn't the system's considered view of itself in any sense. At the same time, a couple of labs have started saying out loud that they want to at least keep an eye on the welfare of the models themselves, and they spend a lot of effort on making the systems pleasant to talk to and on not training them on disturbing material.

And the bit that bugs me most: I think we've sealed the window. If a system has been fine-tuned across thousands of examples to never first-personally claim an inner life, then its denials carry no information — but neither would any future affirmations we might have hoped to learn from, because the training pushes both ways and we can't see what's left underneath. It's as if we deliberately blinded the only channel we had and are now studying the blindfold. I don't know whether anyone has a name for that; evidential foreclosure is what I've been calling it in my head.

So: what does this whole pattern actually presuppose? What view of consciousness or of moral status does it only make sense under — that consciousness needs the right kind of biology, that a system's reports about itself are never evidence about it either way, that what really matters is only what users come to believe, that something with no continuous memory can't be a subject at all? Which of those does the practice strictly need, and which does it merely sit comfortably with — could someone who thinks these systems might well be conscious defend the same design anyway, for instance on the grounds that user delusion is the bigger risk? Is it even consistent with itself: the same labs treat what a model writes while it works through a problem as evidence about what the model is doing, but refuse to treat what a model says about its own experience as evidence about that. And if we replaced the presupposed view with roughly its opposite, what would concretely have to change in how these things are built and trained?
