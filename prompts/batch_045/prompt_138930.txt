I'm writing up my position on truth and the paradoxes for a reading group whose whole purpose is to attack it, and I'd like the audit done before I hand it over rather than during. The package as I currently hold it is below.

(1) Modest dialetheism. Some contradictions are true. The Liar, in both its falsity and its untruth versions, is both true and false, and I am attracted to extending the same treatment to the set-theoretic and other paradoxes of self-reference — though there I am relying on model-theoretic existence claims I have not verified myself. I am not a trivialist: not everything is true.

(2) Transparency. The truth predicate is fully transparent: the T-scheme holds without restriction and with no hierarchy, and 'it is true that A' and A are intersubstitutable in every extensional context, self-referential ones included. This, rather than any independent argument, is what generates the gluts in (1).

(3) Semantics and consequence. The right semantics for a language of this kind is three-valued, LP-style: besides the two classical values there is a both-true-and-false value, that value is designated, and validity is preservation of designatedness — an argument is valid iff every interpretation on which the premises are at least true is one on which the conclusion is at least true. Explosion is invalid, and a glutty but non-trivial theory is possible. I also expect consequence to be safe in the following sense: valid inference applied to premises that are straightforwardly true — true and not false — should never deliver a conclusion that is straightforwardly false.

(4) Conservatism about inference. I am not willing to surrender the inferences ordinary reasoning and ordinary mathematics actually run on: modus ponens, disjunctive syllogism, proof by cases, conditional proof, reductio ad absurdum. I have been assuming I get them back by classical recapture — where the sentences in play can be taken to be consistent, classical logic applies — and I have treated this as a local piece of bookkeeping rather than a philosophical cost.

(5) Recapture should be internal. I want to state, in the same language and with no vocabulary beyond negation, conjunction and disjunction, that the sentences of some delimited batch are consistent — to write the consistency of A as not-(A and not-A) — and to have that claim, asserted as a premise, do the work of licensing classical reasoning about A. I have simply been helping myself to this.

(6) Cut. Consequence is transitive. If A entails B and B entails C then A entails C; whatever else goes, I want to be able to chain proofs, since a relation that only licenses single steps is not, as far as I can see, a consequence relation at all.

(7) A methodological preference I am unsure belongs on the same list: I take inconsistency to be a defect in a theory. Of two theories otherwise equal I prefer the one entailing no contradiction, and I count this as a reason — perhaps an outweighable one — against any theory that entails one. I hold this alongside (1), and I have never been able to tell whether that is hypocrisy or bookkeeping.

Someone in the group has already floated a different definition of validity, on which the premises of an argument must be true-and-not-false while the conclusion need only be true. I don't see why anyone would adopt it: it looks like changing the subject in order to save the inferences, and I'm told it also breaks chaining, which I take to be decisive against it.

So: which of (1)–(7) are genuinely in tension, and which only look like it? I want the tensions ranked by severity rather than presented as one undifferentiated list, and for each of them I want to know exactly where the pressure is, what I would have to give up, and what giving it up would cost me — including the cases where the cost is a virtue I hadn't noticed I was buying. I do not know which commitment I would abandon and I would rather not be told that it all depends on my other commitments; tell me which single revision buys back the most consistency. If any item is incoherent as I have stated it, say so plainly.
