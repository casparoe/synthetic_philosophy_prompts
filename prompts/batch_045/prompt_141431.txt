I'm 68, did most of my career in protein crystallography, and I've gone back to uni part-time for an MA in philosophy, which in practice means I sit in a pub with four people half my age and argue. Last week's session was on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and the PhD student who runs the group made a move I can't shake. She said the objection only looks forceful because the interpersonal case feels exotic while the intrapersonal one is familiar, but that really distributing welfare across persons is just like distributing it across the decades of a single life: time slices of one person are 'separate' from each other in all the ways that are supposed to matter (the twenty-something who pays the cost and the sixty-something who collects the benefit never share a moment, and one can be in pain while the other isn't), and yet everyone thinks prudence is obviously in order. So, she concluded, if you accept trading a smaller pain at t1 for a bigger pleasure at t2, you've already conceded everything the utilitarian needs, and the separateness of persons is a distinction without a difference. When my mate Dave (ex-theoretical physicist, same course) objected that within a life there's one continuing subject who anticipates the cost, consents to the plan at the time of choosing, and later literally becomes the beneficiary, she had a reply ready: if continuity of the subject is what's doing the work, then anyone undergoing radical psychological discontinuity — dense amnesia, late-stage dementia — has a weakened claim to prudential concern, and that, she said, is absurd. Dave said that just shows continuity isn't sufficient either, not that it's irrelevant. Neither budged, and my hearing aid was packing up so I lost the last ten minutes.

I'd like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on utilitarianism itself. Can you lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the time slice, the prudent planner, the discount rate, death, anticipation, consent, and the fixed lifetime budget on each side — then separate the places the mapping breaks from the places it doesn't, and say which of those breaks actually bear on the conclusion she wanted? Two things I keep turning over that I don't think either of them addressed. First: how much of the felt difference is really about the *number of subjects*, and how much about the *leakiness of the transfer*? Cross-time transfers inside one life aren't frictionless either — there's discounting, there's the chance you die before the payoff, and a year of joy at seventy may just not be the same item as a year of joy at twenty — so maybe the intrapersonal case is nearer the interpersonal one than Dave allows, and the leakiness is doing more of the argumentative work than separateness is. Second, I tried to build a test case: stipulate a single stream of consciousness that gets migrated from one body into another mid-life, so that the subject who makes the sacrifice at t1 is numerically the same subject who collects at t2; and then its mirror image, one stream that gets divided into two. If the objection is about the separateness of subjects, the first case should look like plain prudence and the second should turn prudence into utilitarianism without anything else changing — which suggests the whole dispute piggybacks on a theory of personal identity, and I want to know whether that's a cost it can absorb or whether it gives up the game.

And if the lifetime analogy can't carry her conclusion, what would a better one look like — or is additivity just something you have to argue for in its own terms, wich I suspect is what Dave was reaching for and couldn't quite say?
