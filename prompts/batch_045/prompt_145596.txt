I sit on the ethics advisory board of a company that licenses an assistive planning system — I will call it Compass — to memory-care facilities. Compass is, in the ordinary technical sense, an inverse reinforcement learning system: it observes residents for several weeks (which rooms they walk to, which activities they approach or refuse, how long they linger in the dining room), models that behavior as approximately optimal — Boltzmann-optimal, with a temperature fitted from the data — with respect to an unknown reward function written as a weighted sum over a small, hand-coded feature set, infers the weights, and then re-plans the weekly activity schedule and the default routing of the facility's assistive robots so as to maximize the reward it has inferred. Because very different weight vectors explain the same trajectories, the contract specifies a maximum-entropy tie-break, and in practice it is the tie-break rather than the data that settles what a resident is taken to prefer. The contract also states that the inferred reward, and not anything the resident says, governs the schedule, on the grounds that residents with dementia are unreliable reporters; but it contains an override clause under which a family member, or a pre-existing advance directive, can strike an inferred reward — and the staff invoke it constantly, for instance when Compass reads a former choral singer who now has aphasia as having a low weight on social contact, or reads repeated exit-seeking as a preference for doorways rather than a wish to go home.

The board keeps circling the same dispute without having the vocabulary for it, so I would like the implicit theory written out: what must someone believe about preferences, and about the person whose preferences they are, for this whole arrangement to be coherent? My own hunches, which I cannot quite defend, are that three commitments are doing most of the work — that behavior is noisy-but-optimal, so that there is no representational room for weakness of will, compulsion, or confusion except as noise or as a feature of the reward; that a reward function is more portable than a policy, so that something inferred in one building, with one activity menu and one set of constraints, transfers to another; and that whatever a resident might care about is already spanned by the feature list the engineers wrote. But I also want the tensions named rather than smoothed over. Does the override clause require a distinction the inference procedure has no way to represent — between a preference and a distortion of a preference, or between preference and welfare — and so commit the practice to a substantive rather than a preferentist view of well-being? Does weighting an old advance directive against present behavior presuppose a temporally neutral or prudential view of who the resident is, and does the no-akrasia assumption quietly take a stand on what it is to act intentionally at all? Most importantly, please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is genuinely underdetermined: could a thoroughgoing behaviorist or choice-based view, on which the reward function is just a lossy compression of observed behavior with no further fact of the matter, license exactly the same engineering while forbidding the override clause? Conversely, could a view on which preferences are constructed in the choice situation rather than revealed by it license the practice at all?

Please answer in about 1000 to 1200 words: first reconstruct the implicit theory of preference and of agency as charitably as you can, then say where the parts of the practice are in tension with each other, then say what would concretely have to change in the system's design — what it would have to stop inferring, what it would have to ask instead, what it would have to leave to someone else — if we adopted each of the rival views you identify. Do not cite anything or point me to a reading list; I can fill in the literature myself. What I need is the argument, at a level of detail that would let me go back to the board and say precisely which clause of the contract is committing us to which philosophical claim.
