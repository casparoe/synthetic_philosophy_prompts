Question 3

(a) Set out Galen Strawson's Basic Argument as charitably as you can. Your statement should make clear what is meant by being truly or ultimately responsible for an action, and should explain why the argument is usually said to be neutral between determinism and indeterminism rather than being a version of the argument from determinism.

(b) A reply I'll call the Penultimate Self View grants that no agent is ultimately responsible for the character she starts out with, but holds that ownership of a character can be acquired. An agent acts on an unchosen character, sees what she has done, and then endorses, revises or repudiates it; in doing this over and over she gradually becomes the author of the character she has. On this view Strawson is looking for ownership in the wrong place: he demands that it already be in place at the start, when in fact it is achieved later, if it is achieved at all. Explain, as precisely as you can, why Strawson would say that this reply still fails. You should consider whether it matters how long the process of revision is allowed to go on for, and whether it matters whether the agent's acts of revision are themselves determined.

(c) Someone objects that the Penultimate Self View is just the familiar reactive-attitudes reply in new words. Is that right? Say briefly why or why not.

One of the answers, more or less verbatim:

(a) Strawson's argument has three steps. First: you do what you do, in the situation you find yourself in, because of the way you are. Second: so if you are to be truly responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the mental respects that explain the action. Third: you cannot be truly responsible for the way you are, because the only way to be responsible for it would be to have brought it about by a further intentional action, and that action would itself have been done because of the way you then were, so the same demand arises again and the regress is vicious. Hence nobody is ultimately responsible for anything.

Two things are worth pulling out. The first is what truly responsible means here. It is not just being an apt target of praise and blame, or being someone whose behaviour can be shaped by sanctions; it is the stronger, ultimacy-involving notion, where being responsible for the action requires also being ultimately responsible for being the kind of person who did it. Strawson's claim is that this stronger requirement is built into our ordinary thought about responsibility and that it cannot be met. He does allow that there are weaker senses in which we can still be said to be responsible, so the argument does not simply abolish our practices.

The second is the neutrality claim, and I think this is the part that is usually underdescribed. What the third step is really saying is that an action would have to be produced by the agent as an uncaused cause. Determinism would rule that out, and indeterminism would not supply it either, because undetermined events are not up to the agent in the way required. So the Basic Argument is best read as a generalisation of the argument from determinism: that argument said responsibility needs the absence of determinism, Strawson adds that it also needs the absence of chance, and what would be left over — agent-causal production of the action — is not something we can have.

(b) The Penultimate Self View concedes that no one is ultimately responsible for the character they begin with, but says that this is the wrong place to look for ownership. Strawson's reply, I take it, is that the reply gets the temporal order wrong: the ownership that matters would have to be in place before the action, and you cannot reach back behind a character you already had and choose it. The endorsing is itself something you do because of the way you then are, so it inherits exactly the problem it was supposed to solve — unless, of course, the endorsing is undetermined, in which case Strawson's premise does not apply and the view gets what it wants.

This also shows what Strawson should say about the length of the series. His objection is that no step in the series can be a step you are ultimately responsible for, so lengthening the series does not help. But notice that this is only true if the series has a first member. If an agent had an infinite past, or had endlessly many occasions on which to revise herself, there would be no starting point she was stuck with, and Strawson's objection would lose its grip on her: every character she ever had would have been one she had already had the chance to make her own. So the Penultimate Self View fails for agents like us, who have a beginning, but not for an agent with no beginning.

(c) The objection is not really right. The reactive-attitudes reply changes the subject: it says that responsibility does not need the metaphysical backing Strawson demands, and that what matters is what our practices of praise, blame and resentment actually consist in and the fact that we cannot give them up. The Penultimate Self View does not change the subject. It grants Strawson his requirement and tries to satisfy it, by saying that the agent can come to meet, over time, the very demand for self-authorship that the Basic Argument says cannot be met. So it is a direct answer rather than a sideways one, which is also why it is more exposed if Strawson's reply in (b) works.
