I teach one discussion section of a first-year survey, "Science and Society," at a large university. Nearly all of the students are non-majors; they have sat through a single lecture in which the lecturer passed without pause from "the Middle Ages" to "the Renaissance" to "the Scientific Revolution," and my section is fifty minutes long, with no reading assigned and, ideally, no slides. I want to spend it on one transferable idea rather than on the history itself: how to determine what work a periodization is actually doing, taking the Scientific Revolution as the worked case. Please do not explain the historiography to me and do not hand me a reading list or a set of sources; I want a plan for the explanation.

The apparatus I have been working with may be wrong, so repair it as part of your answer. I distinguish three jobs a period label can perform: sorting-work, where it merely picks out a stretch of time so that we can talk about it; explanatory-work, where it claims that the stretch is a unit with respect to some cause, so that naming the period does explanatory labor; and discontinuity-work, where it claims that some variable's behavior changes at the boundary. Correspondingly I distinguish three ways of anchoring a period: on a datable event, on a change in the rate of some activity, or on a change in the rules governing what would settle a dispute. Two tests then seem to do most of the work. The shove test: displace both boundaries by two generations and ask which of the claims you care about survives. The ancestor problem: a periodization drawn from downstream tends to collect the ancestors of what we now value rather than what was distinctive at the time, and can therefore be genuinely informative about our lineage while badly misleading about the past. Tell me whether this apparatus, cleaned up or replaced, is the right thing to put in front of first-years in fifty minutes.

Then the parts I most need.

First, the order: what has to be in place before the question "was the Scientific Revolution real?" can even be heard as a substantive question, and what breaks if the order is inverted? I assume the students cannot get traction on "real or invented" until something about the jobs distinction is in place, but I am unsure what exactly must precede what, or whether the apparatus should be introduced abstractly or extracted from a case they already half-believe.

Second, the carrying case: give me the one example that should bear the whole session, state what it must have and what it must lack, and defend it against the candidates I keep returning to. I have in mind a practice that was intellectually serious on both sides of the proposed boundary and that later writers quietly wrote out of the story: either the appeal to a named eyewitness as grounds for a claim about nature, or an activity now classed as occult that continued unchanged across the line. Which of these is better, what would each have to be like to work, and is there a case that does the job more cleanly than either?

Third, the misconceptions, in the order in which they will actually arise, each with the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off. I expect at least these: that a label coined long afterwards is therefore a fiction; that finding medieval antecedents refutes any discontinuity claim; that saying "it's a construct" settles something; that because the dispute looks verbal on the surface it is verbal all the way down; and that periods must tile the timeline exhaustively and without overlap, so that any overlap is an error. Correct and extend that list, and say which of these are worth inviting rather than pre-empting.

Finally, three short things. Give me one question for a closing one-minute paper that would distinguish a student who has the jobs distinction from one who has merely learned to say "social construct," and say what a good and a bad answer to it look like. Tell me what using these terms responsibly requires, since I would rather not produce twenty cynics who conclude that periodizations are just stories we tell: say how the last two minutes should be spent so that the students leave able to use the phrase rather than only to sneer at it. And tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level, with what I should say instead when a student presses me on it.
