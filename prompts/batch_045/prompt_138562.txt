been arguing with people about who started the Cold War for like the tenth time and I'm now fairly convinced the question itself is doing us a disservice. So before I pick a side again I want the spec, not the answer: what does an account of blame for a large historical outcome have to get right? Fix the purpose as blame in a thin, non-legal sense — who is answerable for the fact that things went cold rather than settling into something dull — where the point of assigning it is partly to know what to do differently, not just to scold dead guys.

Things I think I want, you tell me which are real desiderata and which are just my temperament: it has to say in advance what evidence would move the verdict (a verdict that can't be revised when archives open isn't a finding, it's a mood); it has to work from the 1946 information set with no archives, not only with hindsight; it has to keep "did the most to cause it" separate from "is most to blame" and both of those separate from "was most wrong on the merits"; and it has to say whether blame is zero-sum, i.e. whether "both sides" is a substantive answer or just a polite way of declining to answer.

The case that keeps breaking my attempts: everyone more or less agrees that by 1948 the line in Europe was fixed, and that after that most of what each side did was pretty forced by the other side's last move. My pet idea is "lock-in blame" — blame goes to whoever first made the bad equilibrium the only survivable one, even if the other guy made the last hostile move. Is that a genuine requirement on a theory of blame, or is it consequentialism smuggled in under a new name?

The two bits I actually care about: which pair of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most work knocking out the usual stories (the traditional one, the revisionists, the post-revisionists). Table is fine, keep it under a page, skip a full scorecard.
