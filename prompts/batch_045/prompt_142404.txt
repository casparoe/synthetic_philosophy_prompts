I am a novelist, and I'm two years into a book that follows one family across roughly two hundred years — five generations, each section narrated by a different member, no single protagonist spanning the whole thing. I keep finding that the shape of the thing is quietly making an argument I haven't decided whether I believe, and I recently described the problem at dinner to two friends — a historian of colonial public health and a philosopher who works on explanation in the social sciences — who then spent an hour arguing with each other and left me no clearer than before. I'd be very grateful for a verdict on the arguments as actually made, because what I decide here determines what form my book takes.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) The historian opened with the trend lines: by the usual measures the last two centuries have moved in one direction across a remarkable range of things at once — child mortality from something like four in ten children to under five in a hundred, most of humanity lifted out of extreme poverty, chattel slavery made illegal nearly everywhere for the first time in recorded history, mass literacy, women's enfranchisement, deaths in war per head of population down. Coincidence, she said, should be rejected: a random walk does not produce that many correlated directional changes at once, so there must be something with a direction in it, and once you grant that, the ordinary name for it is that history bends. (2) The philosopher said he grants the numbers and denies the inference. Two centuries is a blink; the sample has been chosen so as to capture the one period in which the direction looks encouraging; and the trend is driven by a single non-moral engine — cheap energy plus growing state capacity — which is contingent and could be reversed within a decade by climate breakdown or a war. Worse, the moral gains are outputs of the same engine as the horrors: abolitionism and the slave trade, welfare states and colonial extraction, public health and the administrative machinery of the camps. The curve rises; we narrate the rise as justice because justice is what we happen to care about, but nothing in the process is aimed at it. (3) The historian replied that the window objection is symmetric — pick the fourteenth century and you get the opposite story — so the real question is not the length of the window but whether there is any mechanism with a direction in it, and there is: a ratchet. A practice that is written down, funded, taught to children and defended by an institution is harder to lose than to gain, so each generation starts from a higher floor. Abolition after the nineteenth century was not a mood; it was treaties, naval patrols, courts and a bureaucracy, and restoring it now would require active demolition rather than mere drift. Call it a ratchet rather than a telos: no endpoint, no guarantee, but a real asymmetry between gaining and losing — which, she said, is all that a defensible progressivism needs. (4) The philosopher answered that the ratchet is real but ratchets capacity rather than justice, locking in surveillance and bureaucratic killing at least as efficiently as hygiene and suffrage; and that when a gain depends on continuing contingent conditions, calling it locked in is simply false — especially when the energy pulse driving the gains is the same thing destabilising the climate. He then made the charge that has stuck with me most: that the whole picture is Christian eschatology with the agent deleted — the fall, the redemption, the consummation, every element retained, only God removed — and that it persists because it supplies meaning, which is a reason for wanting it to be true and no reason at all for thinking it is. (5) The historian conceded that the genealogy was worth taking seriously but said a genealogy is not a refutation: showing where a belief came from does not show it false. Then she made the move I have been turning over most: suppose the arc is false and the ratchet is all there is. The ratchet still has to be maintained, maintenance needs a motive, and the belief that history is going somewhere may be the most reliable motive available. (6) He replied that this is the illusion revealing its function — a useful falsehood whose motivating power collapses exactly when it is most needed, which is what happened to European liberal optimism after 1914 — and that the honest replacement is not despair but stewardship without a script.

So: who is right about what, move by move? I'd like each of the six moves rated from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, rather than a single overall verdict. Four things in particular I want settled. First, is the ratchet a genuine third position between teleology and pure contingency, or teleology with the ending filed off — and can it survive the objection that capacity ratchets in both moral directions? Second, is the secularised-eschatology charge a refutation of the arc thesis, or does it leave the thesis's truth-value entirely untouched? Third, is the pragmatic argument in (5) — believe it because believing it maintains the machinery — a sound reply, or does it collapse into exactly the fragility he alleges? Fourth, and most useful to me: what are both of them missing? I have a suspicion the archive is doing work neither of them acknowledged — that literacy, record-keeping and the keeping of archives co-vary with the very improvements being measured, so that the surviving record is selected in favour of gains, and the peoples who lost out under progress are disproportionately the ones who never got to write the record. If that is right it seems to cut against both of them at once, and I cannot tell how hard it cuts.

The reason I am asking rather than reading my way through it is that I need to know what my book's form commits me to. A novel has an ending, five sections have an order, and a family is a unit that persists — and I suspect those three facts alone smuggle in a telos whether I mean them to or not, and that every structural choice available to me is a vote on the question they were arguing about: whose death closes the book, whether the last section resolves anything, whether the generations converge on something or merely accumulate, whether the final chapter explains the previous four or refuses to. If you think that is right, I would value concrete structural advice: what would a two-hundred-year novel look like if it declined to be a theodicy, and which of the devices I have inherited — the closing death, the convergent ending, the explanatory last chapter, the heir who understands everything at last — are the ones doing the smuggling?
