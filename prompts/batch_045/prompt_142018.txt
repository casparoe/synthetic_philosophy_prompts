I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences were being posted, and there is one argument I have now watched go around the same loop perhaps six times, in comment sections and in person at meetups: the mugger who threatens to create and torture an absurd number of people — 3↑↑↑3 of them, say — unless you hand over five dollars. As far as I can tell nobody has moved anybody in well over a decade. Every attempt to justify not paying gets patched: bound the utility function, or let the prior fall off with the description length of the claim so the probability sinks faster than the payoff grows, or apply a leverage penalty to any hypothesis that puts you in a position to affect that many people, or insist the mugger's utterance is not evidence and that for every mugger there is an equally cheap counter-mugger; and every patch then gets its own counterexample, usually a version where the number is larger, or where the mugger is more credible, or where the same move would tell you to ignore some perfectly ordinary risk. On the other side there is a smaller camp that simply bites the bullet and hands over the five dollars, and then has to explain why this isn't a reductio. I care more than is reasonable about this partly for its own sake and partly because the same shape recurs in arguments about how much to spend reducing existential risk, where the stakes are comparably enormous and the probabilities are disputed rather than stipulated.

My hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that this is not really a fight inside decision theory at all, but a disagreement about what counts as data: one side takes the considered verdict that a sane agent does not pay as a fixed point any acceptable theory has to reproduce and treats the axioms as negotiable, while the other takes the axioms — expected value, unbounded stakes, dominance — as the fixed point and treats the intuitive verdict as something to be explained away. If that is right, the two sides are answering different questions under one name, and the fact that both produce internally consistent theories is not evidence that anyone has got anywhere. Two alternatives I can't rule out: that a hidden formal question is doing the real work, about whether any pair of a prior and a utility function can simultaneously satisfy what each side demands, or whether all the avoidance strategies turn out to be re-descriptions of one another; and that the setup is an intuition pump whose answer depends entirely on how it is filled in, so that fifteen years of non-movement is evidence the question was ill-posed rather than hard.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey: what kind of stall this is, what each side is actually committed to such that it cannot concede, and what, if anything, would end it. I'd also like the diagnosis tested against three variants I've been turning over, on the principle that a real diagnosis should predict where the disagreement reopens and where it collapses: a ladder of muggers, each naming a larger number than the last, arriving one per day; a second figure who turns up and offers to cancel the first one's threat for the same five dollars, so that paying starts to look like an ordinary protection racket rather than a metaphysical gesture; and a mugger who first demonstrates, in front of you, a small working simulation in which a being visibly suffers, and only then makes the threat, so that the move about the claim being free-floating testimony is no longer available. Say which of these your diagnosis predicts the two camps will agree about, which it predicts will split them, and which it predicts should change nothing, and say plainly if your answer is that no progress should be expected and why. Please give me exactly five numbered points, two or three sentences each, with no preamble and no concluding summary.
