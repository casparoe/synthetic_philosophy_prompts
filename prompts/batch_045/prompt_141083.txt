I am a novelist, and I have got myself into an argument I cannot get out of. I would be grateful for your help in finding the exit.

My new novel is told by four narrators who each describe the same night on a Norfolk estuary, at the end of which a man is shot. Their accounts cannot all be correct. A philosopher friend, hearing me fret about this, suggested that my difficulty is really the difficulty that Jain philosophers address with anekāntavāda: the doctrine that reality is many-sided (anekānta), so that for a great range of predicates an object satisfies them in one respect and fails to satisfy them in another; that every judgment we make proceeds from a partial standpoint, or naya, which selects one aspect of a complex object; and that the disciplined form of assertion is therefore the conditional or qualified one recommended by syādvāda, in which every predication carries the operator syāt, "in some respect", and in which the sevenfold schema (saptabhaṅgī) permits a thing to be, not to be, and to be indescribable, each in its own respect. I found this attractive enough to build the book's structure around it. Then I tried to write the doctrine down as an argument, and it turned into something I cannot accept.

(1) Anekānta: reality is many-sided. For many objects a and predicates F, a satisfies F in one respect and fails to satisfy F in another.

(2) Nayavāda: every judgment is made from a standpoint, and is true of the aspect that standpoint selects. So no judgment is true from every standpoint at once, and the unqualified assertion of any judgment overreaches what the standpoint supports.

(3) Syādvāda: the correct form of any judgment J is therefore the qualified "in some respect, J" — and the qualified judgment is true.

(4) If (3), then for any J there is a respect in which J holds. So no judgment is simply false. Being wrong about something is never a matter of content but only of form: the only error is one-sidedness (ekānta), the failure to qualify.

(5) If no judgment is simply false, no one is ever simply mistaken about anything. Correction, adjudication and inquiry lose their object; there is only ever more qualification to add.

(6) That cannot be right. In my final chapter an assize court must decide which of my four narrators is lying about the shot. If (5) holds, the judge cannot be wrong whatever she decides, and my closing chapter is theatre about nothing.

(7) It also cannot be right as a reading of the doctrine that generated it: the tradition that gives us (1) to (3) plainly distinguishes right views from wrong ones and treats one-sidedness as a defect with consequences. On (5) there is no defect left for consequences to attach to.

There is a further wrinkle I noticed at (3): the claim seems to eat itself. If every judgment is true only in some respect, then so is (3) — and then either the qualification is idempotent, in which case a single "in some respect" does all the work and (3)'s own relativity is a mirage, or it is not, in which case the qualification never settles and nothing can be stated at all.

I tried to patch this by distinguishing two readings of the respect-operator, and the patch seems to break the doctrine either way. Call them the concessive reading and the structural reading. On the concessive reading, the respect is supplied by whoever speaks: "in some respect, J" is true so long as some respect can be found in which J holds. But then the operator is a device of courtesy that makes every claim and its opposite true, and the doctrine proves far too much — it licenses the narrator I know to be lying as fully as the one I know to be truthful, and gives me no reason to prefer the qualified claim to silence. On the structural reading, the respects are joints in the object itself, fixed independently of any standpoint, and qualifying a claim records a discovery about the world. But then there must be a standpoint-free inventory of the respects an object has, which is very close to the complete description the doctrine said we could not have.

So: which step or transition do you reject, and why did it look compulsory? I would rather keep (1) — that a shared night really does have more than one side to it — and (6) and (7) than anything in between, and I am willing to pay for that, but I should like to know the bill. Please price the alternatives: what does it cost to give up each of (1) to (5), or to deny the inference at a particular step? Does my concessive/structural dilemma exhaust the options, or is there a third way of taking "in some respect" that escapes both horns? And one practical question, since it decides how I write the book: fictional assertion does not aim to transmit belief in the way ordinary assertion does, so is a novel inside or outside the scope of the discipline syādvāda recommends, and does your answer to that change your diagnosis?

Please answer in British English, and keep the whole reply to about 800 words.
