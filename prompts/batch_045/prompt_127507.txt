I'm one of three people who handle complaints for a volunteer-run food co-op with about 400 working members, and we get a few of these a week. The pattern that keeps tying us in knots is this: someone reports a rule being broken, and it turns out the person reporting has broken that exact rule themselves, or was involved in it somehow, or mostly ignores that rule when it's inconvenient for them. Right now we either act on the complaint anyway and the accused person screams about hypocrisy, or we quietly drop it and the reporter screams about being silenced. I want something better than a gut call, and I want it to be something a shift volunteer with no training can run in ten minutes and write down, because whatever we decide gets read out at the member meeting and can be appealed there.

What I've been using on my own is a single question: would this still be worth acting on if the person reporting had never said anything? If yes, act; if no, bin it. I've started to think that's wrong, or at least that it throws something away, because it seems to make the complainer's own history completely irrelevant, and my gut says it isn't irrelevant — a founding member who has broken the cash-handling rule four times and never once admitted it shouldn't get to open an investigation into someone else's cash handling, even if the underlying problem is real. But I can't say cleanly why, and I can't say what the middle ground is.

So: write me an actual procedure. Ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops early, and where it stops and hands the decision to a human because it's a values call and not a fact call. It has to work when the facts are disputed and I can't investigate properly, and it has to tell me what to write back to the person whose complaint we're not acting on, since "you have no standing" is the kind of sentence that starts a war.

The cases I want it to actually sort out, because these are the ones we've genuinely had:

- The direct victim who is also the worst offender. Someone who is personally harmed by what happened, but who has done the same thing repeatedly and never acknowledged doing it. Is being the person it was done to enough on its own?
- The reformed one. Broke the rule once, was sanctioned, admitted it, hasn't done it since, is now the person enforcing it. My instinct is she's the strongest reporter we've got, but I can't say why that's different from the first case.
- The one who helped. Encouraged or enabled the thing and then reported the other person for doing it.
- Blaming on behalf of someone who isn't there. A member complains about how a third person was treated, and that third person has left the co-op and can't be asked.
- The one who was about to do it. Same plan, same rule, got interrupted before it happened, now blames the person who went through with it.
- The norm-shopper: invokes a rule they argue against every other time it comes up, and only reaches for it when it's a weapon.

There are two more things I want built in rather than left implicit. First, I need the procedure to be hard to abuse: I want to know how it stops a tired volunteer from using "no standing" as a way to bin a complaint they just don't want to deal with, which is honestly the failure mode I'm more worried about than being too soft on hypocrites. Second, I want to know where the whole framework just doesn't reach — the cases where the standing question is the wrong question entirely and we should be asking something else — because I suspect there are more of those than I think.

Run it on three of the cases above, including at least one where it gives me an answer I won't like, and tell me which distinctions I have to keep even though they make the procedure harder to use, and which ones I can safely flatten for a volunteer who has ten minutes.
