I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and this autumn I have been asked to run a ninety-minute workshop at a science-and-ideas festival: roughly twenty adults in a room with five round tables, no assumed background in philosophy, one book token as the only stake, and an audience that will include at least one person who has read about cosmology and will want to talk about infinite universes. I have written about the presumptuous philosopher argument and the doomsday argument before, and I keep coming back to them because they seem to me to expose something real about how thin our evidence is when the evidence is just that one is here; but my last attempt to do this as a talk failed in two predictable ways. Half the room decided it was all just a coin toss dressed up, and the other half went straight to Boltzmann brains, and when someone asked what group we were supposed to be sampling ourselves from, I had nothing to hand that did not stall the session. So I want a game rather than a lecture: something runnable, with rules, materials and a timeline, in which participants are sorted without knowing it into observers in one of two possible worlds, a small one and a large one, and must commit in advance — in teams, before they learn anything about which observer they are — to a policy for how to bet. The point should be in the mechanics and not in anything I say afterwards: I would like them to discover for themselves that there are two ways of keeping score, one that counts worlds and one that counts observers, and that the two diverge, and that whichever scoreboard they find themselves trusting is doing serious philosophical work. They should not hear any of the labels until the debrief.

There are three variations I have been turning over and would like built into the session rather than bolted on. First, a capped round: suppose the number of people who actually get to bet is fixed in advance by the organisers regardless of which world is real, so that in the large world most observers never reach a betting slip; my sense is that this makes the anthropic argument evaporate, but I want the participants to hit that result rather than be told it. Second, a change in what a win pays: in some rounds each winning bettor gets a fixed prize, in others a single prize is split among everyone in that bettor's world, and I suspect the two payout rules pull in opposite directions about what it is rational to do. Third, a round in which participants are told only that at least one observer exists and are given no index at all, so that whatever they were updating on has been reduced to its barest form. Please give me the rules, the payoff scheme, the sequences of rounds and the two scoreboards, plus a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the distinction between the self-sampling and the self-indication assumptions, that says which objection each variation is designed to provoke, and that tells me how to handle the person who arrives with infinite populations and the person who asks why we should count people rather than moments or minds or species. Be frank about what the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by hand afterwards, and about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including how to keep it from collapsing into a shouting match about priors. I would also like to know whether the design scales down to forty-five minutes with eight colleagues in a meeting room, and I would rather you did not send me off to chase up papers, since I will not have time to read them before the festival. Please write the whole thing in British English.
