I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book (working title The Second-Best Sperm — my agent hates it) about a woman who finds out at 39 that the fertility clinic used the wrong sample in 1986, and that the man she thought was her father isn't. There's a subplot with her identical twin brother who died at birth, and the characters keep having the same argument in different rooms: whether she would simply not exist if a different sperm had got there first, or whether there'd still be a her, just a slightly different-looking one, complaining about a different father. My writing group (a midwife, a backend dev, a retired history teacher, and one guy who did a philosophy minor and won't shut up about it) fights about this every month, and I want to settle it with an instrument rather than another bottle of wine: a "which theory of your own origins are you actually carrying around?" quiz I can put in my newsletter. Nine or ten questions, each a concrete case with three answer options, and a scoring key that sorts people by what they say about the cases, never by which label they'd claim — nobody in the group has heard of origin essentialism, and I don't want a single question like "could you have had different parents?", because by question two they will have spotted the pattern and started answering as their favourite character instead of as themselves.

Build it from the logical space first, then hang the real positions off it. The branch questions I care about, and you can re-cut them however makes the tree cleaner: a different sperm and egg carrying wholly different DNA; a numerically different sperm that happens to carry a base-pair-for-base-pair identical genome (I want a case that separates people who think the gametes matter in themselves from people who only care about the genome they carry); the same sperm and egg but a different conception event — say the clinic implants the backup frozen embryo from the same batch, or fertilisation happens in a dish instead of a Fallopian tube forty minutes after the fact; an egg whose nucleus is moved into donor cytoplasm, so the mitochondria and early cytoplasm come from someone else; the zygote splitting into twins (are you both of them, one at random, or neither?); a slow sorites where one base pair at a time is swapped in the sperm so nobody can point to where it tips; gametes rebuilt years later out of the woman's own skin cells, so the sperm and egg that make her are made of her; and whether the gametes are parts of her or merely causes of her. Leaves I definitely want on the tree: full gamete essentialism, genome-essentialism, the "same gametes, timing and location irrelevant" view, the organism/animalist leaf, plain anti-essentialism, and a deflationary one where the question is just vague or has no fact of the matter.

Give me the quiz with the scoring key underneath, then the same material as a taxonomy whose branches are labelled with the separating question, and then two short notes to go with it. First: which cells in the space nobody seems to occupy and why — my hunch is the "sperm matters, egg doesn't" box is empty, though the egg contributes the mitochondria and all the early cytoplasm, which feels asymmetric in a way that might make someone bite that bullet; and I suspect the "genome identical but timing definately essential" combination is incoherent rather than merely unpopular, but tell me which is which. Second: which pairs of leaves the instrument can't actually separate — I suspect genome- and gamete-essentialism will collapse for most readers unless the duplicate-genome case is doing real work, and I want you to be honest if it isn't. Also, two or three of my group will produce answer patterns that land on no leaf at all; tell me whether to treat that as a position I've left out or as noise, and what question would flush it out. Around 1200 words, and no citations or reading suggestions please — half the fun of the newsletter is that none of us can look any of it up.
