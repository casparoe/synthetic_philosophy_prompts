I'm in my final year of school and I've been reading Marx on my own for about a year now, mostly the material on machinery and automation, because that is where every argument my friends and I have about AI eventually ends up. I think I've arrived at a set of things I believe that I can state in one go, and what I want is a consistency check rather than an introduction: I'd be grateful if you could argue from the ideas themselves instead of telling me which text settles the question, since I have no access to a university library and a list of things to go and read would be useless to me. Here is where I've landed, roughly in the order I came to it.

(1) The productive forces are what drive history: they develop, and when the existing relations of production begin to hold that development back, those relations get overthrown. That is the shape of the move from feudalism to capitalism, and it is why capitalism is replaceable rather than final.
(2) Marx is not a Luddite, and I don't think the answer is to break machines. The machine as such shortens and lightens work; what turns it into a threat is capital's use of it — deskilling, intensifying, timing and supervising work, breaking the bargaining power of skilled workers, and holding a standing pool of unemployed people over the rest in order to keep wages down. Same hardware, different relations, different result.
(3) But I also resist the flat claim that technology is neutral. The machinery we actually have was designed under capital, to make labour cheap, interchangeable and controllable, and I don't think a post-capitalist society could simply inherit the whole apparatus and flip a switch; a good deal of it would have to be redesigned.
(4) On the economics: competition forces each capitalist to replace workers with machines, and on Marx's account only living labour creates new value, so the more thoroughly mechanization succeeds the more capital squeezes the source of its own profit. I take this to be why a heavily automated capitalism is unstable — and, as I've been putting it to my friends, why a fully automated economy would be fantastically rich in useful things while producing almost no new value and therefore hardly any profit. That is one fact seen from two sides, not two facts. (I have only read about the so-called "Fragment on Machines" second hand, so I'm relying on the idea rather than on any particular wording.)
(5) I hold a version of the immiseration claim: automation under capitalism tends to make workers worse off, and the proportion of the workforce actually needed to produce things keeps shrinking.
(6) I also seem to hold the opposite: mechanization cheapens the goods workers buy, which shortens the part of the working day that goes to reproducing their wages and lengthens the part that goes to profit — so the same process is compatible with more people in work, higher real wages, and deeper exploitation all at once.
(7) And relatedly, I want to say both that automation produces a permanent surplus of people capital has no use for, and that machinery introduced in one industry creates jobs in others — in the industries that build the machines and supply the materials — so that the "reserve army" swells and shrinks with the cycle rather than growing without limit.
(8) Finally, whatever the productive forces make possible, the working class has to emancipate itself; nobody can do it on its behalf, and nothing guarantees that it happens.

What I'd like from you is an audit of that set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how badly — ranked, please, rather than all listed as equally serious? For each real tension, say what exactly would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me, since some of these feel much more load-bearing than others. I also suspect at least one of the apparent clashes dissolves on closer inspection and I'd like to know which, and why it only looks bad. And if you had to pick the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost, which would it be? For what it's worth, the thing that worries me most is a case rather than a principle: if AI really does hollow out the very class that (8) says has to do the emancipating, at exactly the moment (1) and (4) say the conditions are finally ripening, then (8) seems to lose the subject it needs — but I may be misdescribing what (8) requires, so tell me if that worry is misplaced too.
