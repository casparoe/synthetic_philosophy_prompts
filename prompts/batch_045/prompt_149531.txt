Hi there — I'm new to this whole area so apologies if I'm asking the wrong kind of question, but I've got stuck on something and I don't have a word for it.

I'm retired, spent my working life in protein chemistry, and for the last couple of years I've been reading history and philosophy of science properly for the first time since I was an undergraduate. I keep running into the same pattern in the multiple-discovery literature — Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, and I may be misremembering but I think something like it happened with oxygen in the 1770s — and I've seen it close up myself. Twice in my own career the same result turned up in two labs about six weeks apart, and it was pure accident who got the paper out first: one group's postdoc went sick, the other group's postdoc was moving institution and had nothing to lose by writing it up fast.

Here's the thing I noticed. How much credit we think someone deserves for a discovery seems to swing on facts they had no way of knowing and that made no difference at all to what they actually did — namely how close somebody else was to the same result, and how long it would have taken them. If there was a rival six weeks behind, the discovery gets described as inevitable, the field gets described as ripe, and both people get quietly demoted: it would have turned up anyway. If nobody else was anywhere near, the same discovery gets treated as a stroke of individual genius. But the demotion always stops at the person. Nobody ever says the *result* is less of an achievement because it would have turned up anyway, or that the field deserves less money for it. The discount gets applied to the discoverer and then just... stops.

And there's a second layer that bothers me more. Whether we *know* there was a rival depends on whether the rival left something datable behind — a letter, a paper read to a society, anything in a language the historians read. So credit seems hostage to accidents of record-keeping, not just to facts about the world. My guess is there are cases where a second discoverer existed and left no trace, and we treat them as single-discovery cases forever. I don't want to hang anything on a specific example because I can't check one, but I'd be surprised if it hasn't happened.

I've heard of the psychological thing where people rewrite the past so it looks like it was predictable all along, and I don't think that's quite it: that's about how confident people get about an outcome they already know, whereas what I keep seeing is a judgement about how much *credit* is owed, and it tracks what other people were doing rather than what the discoverer did. I also don't think it's the same as the famous-get-more-credit effect, which is about reputation doing the work; here it's the presence or absence of a near-miss rival, including rivals nobody has heard of.

I've been calling it 'rival luck' to myself, which is probably hopeless — it suggests the luck is the rival, when the rival is the thing that's fixed and the luck is whether the record survives. I don't just want a name, though; I want to know what exactly the thing is, what it isn't, and where it stops.

One more thought, which may be a different problem or may be the same one: it strikes me that the wrong question is being asked. Everybody argues about whether the discovery would have happened anyway, but that question is nearly always yes and so it never discriminates. The interesting question looks like how much *later* it would have happened, and whether that delay would have mattered — lives not saved, a field not opened for twenty years. Maybe credit should track counterfactual delay rather than counterfactual occurrence. But I can't tell whether that dissolves what I noticed or just moves it somewhere else.
