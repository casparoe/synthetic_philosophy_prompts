You're a professional philosopher who works on epistemology, and you know the Wittgensteinian literature on so-called hinge commitments — the material from his last notebooks, published after he died as On Certainty, written partly against Moore's business about knowing you have hands. Please take your time with this: before you answer properly, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming loosely about what exactly the practice I'm describing consists in and which distinctions are going to do the real work, then give me your considered answer. Slow and careful beats quick and tidy here.

Background on me: I'm retired, spent about 35 years as an isotope geochemist (U-Pb and Ar-Ar mostly), and only got into philosophy a couple of years ago through a reading group. So please don't assume I know the vocabulary, and do tell me if I'm using a term wrong.

Here's the practice I keep chewing on. In the lab, when a measurement came out strange, we never once considered that the physics had changed. If a zircon gave an age that didn't fit the stratigraphy, we suspected the sample — inheritance, lead loss, contamination — or the instrument, and we ran it again. Nobody ever wrote down: maybe the decay constant is different in this grain. That wasn't treated as a hypothesis at all, not even as a wildly improbable one; it wasn't the sort of thing you could test or attach a number to. Same with an assay plate that read high: you blame the plate. And yet the very same people, me included, would have said if asked — and did say, loudly, at conferences — that every scientific claim is revisable in principle and that science has no dogmas. It's not as if we were shy about overturning well-established results when the evidence was good.

Second thing, which is what really got me: we calibrate instruments using theory (conservation laws, decay constants, reference standards), and then we use those instruments to test theory. When I tried to trace one of those chains back as far as I could, it seemed to loop — the constant is measured with instruments, the instruments are calibrated against standards whose ages were determined using the constant. Nobody in the lab was remotely worried about this, and I couldnt tell whether that was complacency or good sense.

So, my questions:

1. What view of knowledge or evidence does this practice presuppose? My rough reconstruction is that it treats some propositions as standing fast — as rules for describing and checking rather than as things checked — so that a clash between one of them and a datum is always resolved against the datum. Is that right, is it too strong, and what does the practice strictly need as opposed to what it merely sits comfortably with?

2. Where is the practice underdetermined? Would a plain Bayesian or coherentist story — everything is credence, everything revisable, anomalies just get absorbed as small updates — license exactly the same behaviour at the bench? If so, what observation would ever distinguish the two pictures?

3. Is the combination coherent? We said everything is revisable in principle while treating a few things as not even candidates for testing. Is the phrase in principle doing quiet work here, or is there a genuine tension?

4. Are the standing-fast propositions supposed to be beliefs at all — can they be true, or be known? Someone in my group said they aren't the kind of thing that can be true or false, which struck me as a dodge, but I couldn't say why.

5. Last part, which I've made up myself because I couldn't find anyone discussing it. Suppose a metrology institute adopted two rules. Rule A, call it the no-bootstrap rule: no calibration chain may terminate in a proposition that is supported only by measurements made with instruments calibrated via that same chain. Rule B: any paper reporting an anomaly must list, ranked, the background propositions the authors would give up before concluding their instrument was at fault. Under a hinge view, is Rule A even satisfiable, or is it a confused demand — would trying to comply just move the stopping point somewhere else? Would Rule B do any real epistemic work or is it pure theatre? And if the lab were run instead on thoroughgoing probabilism, no exempt propositions, every anomaly nudging every credence, what would actually have to change in day-to-day practice? I half suspect the answer to that is nothing, which makes me think the whole dispute might be verbal — tell me, definately, whether I'm wrong about that and why.
