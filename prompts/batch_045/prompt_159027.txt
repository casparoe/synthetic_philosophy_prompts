I am a graduate student in philosophy, working on whether a broadly computational or functionalist criterion could underwrite attributions of moral patienthood to artificial systems. I keep encountering the same shape of difficulty in three places, and I want to know whether it is one thing or several, whether it already has a name and a settled treatment, and if not, how it should be carved.

The first place is redundancy. Some candidate mental computation is running in lockstep on a hundred physically separate machines, never diverging, with no causal contact after initialization. There is a reading on which one mind is running and a reading on which a hundred are. The moral arithmetic differs accordingly: either duplicating the hardware multiplies whatever matters a hundredfold, or it does not. Neither answer seems to turn on anything that could matter morally, and the question of when the count changes — if the copies later diverge, or if one is deleted without any difference to the internal dynamics of the remaining ninety-nine — looks equally stipulative.

The second is scatter. Consider a system in which the relevant state-transition structure is spread across nodes that exchange only coarse aggregates, or across a long time with the intermediate state stored only in checkpoints: no single node and no short window instantiates the transitions, but the whole spatiotemporal scatter does, provided the mapping from abstract states to physical ones is allowed to range freely. Yet with enough freedom in that mapping, any physical process — a weather system, a wall — instantiates any computation you like. So the criteria need constraints, and the constraints people reach for (locality, compactness, non-disjunctiveness, causal-mechanical coupling) seem either to admit the weather case or to exclude the scatter case for reasons that have nothing to do with anything's welfare.

The third is sharing. Suppose two distinct abstract computations are mapped onto the same physical machinery in such a way that particular registers do double duty — the states are shared rather than merely adjacent. Whether this counts as two implemented computations, one, or none appears to depend on whether the mapping must be one-to-one, which again seems like the wrong kind of consideration. It also raises a question I cannot formulate properly: if two putative subjects share all of their realizers, in what sense are they two?

What I take to be common to the three is this. The answer to "which computations are implemented here, how many, and with what moral weight" flips with a representational choice — the grain, the mapping, whether overlap is permitted — while nothing about that choice looks like the sort of thing that could matter morally. The nearby difficulties I know of do not seem to be this: I am not puzzled about vagueness in where one object ends and another begins, and I am not puzzled about which among several nested candidate systems is the subject. I am puzzled about whether there is a non-arbitrary fact of the matter about what is going on inside a system at all, prior to any such question.

So: is this one phenomenon or several, and if several, what is the load-bearing difference between them? What are the nearest established concepts, and where exactly does each fail to fit the case above? If nothing established fits, I would like a name, a definition, and an explicit set of boundary cases — including cases that resemble these but fall outside the phenomenon. Finally, I would like to know what a non-ad-hoc constraint on the implementation relation would have to look like: what test would distinguish a principled fix from a gerrymander designed to give the answer one wants about the weather system? And does the difficulty tell specifically against computational criteria, or against any structural criterion of moral status? A label alone will not be much use to me; I need the carving, because I am deciding whether it is worth organizing a chapter around.
