Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, one defending the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas and one defending the no-genuine-conflict view, on which every apparent conflict is settled by one requirement outweighing the other and the losing requirement being thereby nullified.

Stage the dispute around a recurring rather than a one-off case: a doctor with one free afternoon a week must choose between visiting her dying mother, to whom she has made an explicit promise of weekly visits, and covering a shift at an understaffed rural clinic where, on the evidence available, her presence prevents serious harm that no substitute would prevent. The same choice returns every week for a year.

Have the argument pass through four points, in this order: whether her lingering guilt is warranted or is merely sorrow rooted in her own agency; whether a losing requirement can be overridden without being erased, so that it still generates duties such as apology, explanation, or repair; whether two requirements that cannot be conjoined into a single ought are really requirements at all; and finally the skeptic's portfolio reply to the case's serial structure — that morality assesses the pattern over the year rather than any single afternoon, so what is required is a distribution of visits, not any one visit — together with the dilemma theorist's reply that this relocates the conflict rather than dissolving it, since a sequence whose individual acts are each forbidden when performed does not become permissible by being optimal as a whole, and residue attaches to every week in the pattern.

Each speaker must answer the objection actually put to her rather than restating her position, and each must concede at least one genuine point. Do not end in agreement: end with a precise statement of the single claim on which they still diverge, and of what each would have to show to win it.
