The occasion: my mother is retiring after almost forty years of teaching, and I'm giving the speech at her party next month in Nagoya, where she's lived since she was twenty-eight — she's Dutch, came over for my father and never really came back. She's famous in our family for what we call the two truths rule: at Sunday dinners everyone had to say one true critical thing and one true kind thing to each person at the table, and she held herself to it, and she thought white lies were a kind of theft — her argument, roughly, was that a lie takes away something the other person could have done with the truth, an apology they could have made, a goodbye they could have said, a repair they could have attempted. I've started calling this the errand test: a white lie is worst not when it's comforting but when it cancels an errand. The room is going to be split down the middle. Her Dutch brothers and cousins are flying in, and for them a speech that's nothing but praise is flattery, i.e. dishonest, and would feel like I didn't actually love her. Her Japanese colleagues, her former students, my aunts and uncles by marriage, and a lot of teenagers are the other half, and for them open criticism of the guest of honour at her own celebration would land as cruelty, and warmth is something you convey by implication rather than by stating it — think of the ordinary, unremarkable gap between what someone feels and what they say out loud in public, and how nobody in that half of the room thinks that gap is a lie.

Here's what makes it hard, and why I can't just write it myself. I found out last year that she broke her own rule exactly once, badly, in a way only I and one other person know about: she told her sister — my aunt, who will be sitting in the front row and who has no idea — that their brother had spoken warmly of her before he died and had forgiven her for whatever it was they fell out over in the nineties. He hadn't. He'd said more or less the opposite. My aunt has been living off that sentence for years. So I can't stand up and say she never told a lie, or that she always gave people the truth, and I obviously can't say the actual thing, and I'm also angry at her about it in a way I havent processed, and yet I do love her and I do want to honour her honestly. What I want is a five-or-six-minute speech that says something true about bluntness and about indirection, that takes a real position on whether being blunt is a duty you owe the hearer — because they might have an errand to run — or really a way of keeping your own hands clean, and that lands for both halves of the room without turning into a philosophy lecture: no named dead philosophers, no jargon, my uncle will go to sleep. And above all, it has to honour a woman who broke her own rule without either announcing that or lying about it. If the honest way to honour her turns out to be by leaving something unsaid, I'd like the speech to do that on purpose rather than by accident, and I'd like to know in your note afterwards what it cost.
