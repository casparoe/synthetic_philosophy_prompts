I'm a science journalist working on a reported feature about whether the computer metaphor for the mind is still doing useful work, or whether it has quietly become something people assert rather than argue for. The piece started from an interview with a systems neuroscientist who pushed back hard: she said the brain is nothing like a digital computer — there is no stored program kept separate from the data, no clock cycle, no clean read/write memory, no one who writes the code — and the basic processing element is roughly a million times slower than a transistor, so anything the brain finishes in a couple of hundred milliseconds cannot be running anything remotely like a serial instruction stream. She then drew a conclusion I want to test rather than just relay: that multiple realizability — the doctrine that a mental state is a functional role that could in principle be occupied by neurons, by silicon, or by anything else with the right causal organization — is not a discovery about the mind at all but an artifact of taking the metaphor literally, and that once you notice the brain is not a von Neumann machine, the idea that mentality is substrate-neutral starts to look like something the picture donated rather than something anyone argued for. A philosopher of mind I spoke to shrugged this off: functionalism, she said, never required brains to be digital computers, so the neuroscientist is attacking a straw target, and the interesting version of the view is about causal roles rather than programs and depends on no empirical claim about neural architecture whatsoever.

I'd like to audit the analogy instead of picking a side, and I've been using two distinctions in my notes that I'd like you to use as well. Call them vehicle-independence and description-independence. Vehicle-independence is the familiar claim that the same mental state could be built out of physically different stuff. Description-independence is the further claim that any given physical system can be correctly described as implementing many different formal structures at once, depending on how finely or coarsely you parse its states and which transitions you count as the elementary ones — so that, if you are allowed to choose the mapping freely, a wall or a stomach can be described as running any program you like. My suspicion, which I can't quite make stick on my own, is that the neuroscientist's objections only really touch vehicle-independence, that her conclusion needs to be about both, and that the two claims get run together in exactly the passages where the metaphor is doing its heaviest lifting. (I've also been toying with an alternative comparison for a mental state — not a program but something like an economic institution, whose identity is fixed by what it does while its physical base is arbitrary and diffuse and it has no clean boundary in space or time — but I can't tell whether that earns anything the computer picture doesn't, or just relocates the same problems into a vocabulary with less mathematical discipline behind it. My editor, meanwhile, wants the piece to land somewhere more definite than it's complicated.)

So please audit the analogy: what maps onto what when someone says the mind is to the brain as the software is to the hardware; which of those mappings survive the neuroscientist's complaints and which are only decorating the claim; where the analogy imports something the target lacks or hides something the target has; and whether her conclusion about multiple realizability survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or was always independent of the analogy. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing summary: (1) the mapping, compressed — what plays the role of the hardware, the software, the instruction set, the memory, and the programmer; (2) the single disanalogy that does the most damage to the use she is making of the analogy; (3) one disanalogy that sounds devastating but turns out not to matter for her argument, and why not; (4) what the computer picture quietly imports into the target, or hides about the brain; (5) whether her conclusion survives, and whether my institution analogy is any improvement or merely relocates the difficulty. Please write it in American English.
