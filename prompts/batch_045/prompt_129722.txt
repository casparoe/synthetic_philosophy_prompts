I spent rather more than forty years as a structural chemist — protein crystallography, for the most part — and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy reading group run by the university's continuing-education programme, which is the only philosophy I have ever studied in any organized way. This term we drifted, by a route I could not now reconstruct, from the philosophy of science into questions about the interpretation of texts, and last week we came to grief over a novel. Half the group read a recurring image in it — a flooded quarry, say, returned to at three points in the book — as anticipating the industrial unrest of the 1840s; the other half read it as private, standing for the narrator's father's bankruptcy. One member then produced a recently published volume of the novelist's correspondence in which she says, in a letter to her sister, quite plainly what the image was meant to stand for. Another member said, rather witheringly, that the correspondence settles nothing: 'the author is dead'. (I gather the phrase comes from a 1967 essay by a French critic. The group had already been tossing it about, together with 'the intentional fallacy', without, I thought, anyone being quite sure what either amounted to.) What I want to know is what that slogan would have to mean for it to do the work it was being made to do there.

My own attempt at getting clearer has been to separate three things which I think we were running together: what the words mean, given the conventions of the language and the circumstances of their utterance; what the writer meant to convey by writing them; and what the work has come to mean to us now — sentence meaning, authorial meaning and significance, if you like labels. I also want distinctions drawn inside 'intention': what the author meant to say as against what she meant to bring about in her reader, whether to alarm, to flatter or to conceal; her intention at the moment of writing as against what she later said she had been doing; and the intention of one writer as against that of a text with several hands in it. Two further cases, I think, make the question sharper, and I should be grateful if every reading you offer were tested against them. The first comes from my own discipline: a scientific paper can contain a derivation that is sound for reasons its author never grasped, or a formalism that later turns out to license consequences its author would have rejected outright, and it has always seemed to me obvious that what such a paper means — what it establishes — is not settled by what its author took himself to be doing, which is presumably not obvious at all in the literary case. The second is the novelist writing in a second language, whose command of idiom outran her intentions, so that what she meant and what the sentences mean come apart systematically rather than by accident; I have some fellow-feeling here, having lately taken to writing in Italian.

So what I should like is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, from the platitudinous to the wildly implausible — that authorial intentions are in principle undiscoverable; that they are discoverable but carry no authority over meaning; that meaning is constituted by the practice of a readership or an interpretive community; that the author is only one strand among the cultural materials a text is woven from; that questions about meaning should simply be replaced by questions about what can be done with a text; and any others you can find — and for each, say whether it is a truism, a substantive claim with something to be said for it, or straightforwardly false. Then, which is the part I actually need before the group meets again in a fortnight: say for each reading what it implies about the letters — that they are not evidence at all; that they are evidence but not authoritative; that they are decisive about one of my three questions and silent about the others; or that they bear on a question the reading takes to be uninteresting. Tell me which reading a person reaching for the slogan in a seminar room is most likely to have in mind, and mark anywhere the underlying disagreement turns out to be merely verbal, a collision between senses of 'meaning' rather than a dispute about texts. A numbered list I can photocopy as a one-page handout would be ideal, with a line on each reading saying what would have to be established before it could be taken seriously.
