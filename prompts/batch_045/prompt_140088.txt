I TA for a second-year ethics course and every year the same thing happens in the seminar on the integrity objection: half the class hears "one thought too many" and files it under "so you're allowed to favour your own projects and people," and the other half files it under "morality asks too much of us." Both are, as far as I can tell, different complaints wearing the same coat, and I want a handout that forces students to see the difference by the cases they bite on rather than by the labels they like. So: build me a decision tree, and write it out as prose (no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous paragraphs, the way I'd write it up in an email to the other TAs), in the order a student would walk through it.

Rough shape: three branch questions, each with three answer options, six to eight leaves at the bottom, each leaf a named position or "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it, plus a one-line note on the most awkward thing that leaf is committed to. Build it from the logical space first — set out the answer combinations the three questions allow, then place the real positions in that space, and for the combinations nobody occupies tell me which are empty because they're incoherent, which because they're just unattractive, and which are merely unexplored. Don't let any branch question be answerable by already knowing the label: each one should be a concrete case or claim a student could react to cold.

The cases I want doing the work (swap in better ones if you have them, but keep the structure):

One. Two people are trapped, you can get to one, and one of them is your spouse while the other is a stranger who you happen to know will go on to save a lot of lives. The candidate thought is "she's my wife," had while standing there deciding. Then the coda: an hour later, safe outside, someone asks why you went for her and the same sentence comes out and sounds completely fine. Whether that retrospective version changes the verdict should be doing real work in your tree, not be a footnote.

Two. Is the relationship a reason, or is it a fact that makes some other reason apply — the way the wire being live is a background condition rather than a consideration you weigh? I want students to have to pick, because I suspect a lot of them are inconsistent here.

Three. The third-personal version: you are not the one in the relationship, you're the person allocating the single remaining rescue slot, and one of the two candidates is somebody's spouse. Is "she's his wife" still one thought too many when it's someone else's attachment doing the justifying? My guess is this node is where the views that looked identical two nodes up finally split, but I could be wrong.

Then three control cases that aren't branch nodes but that the finished tree has to be able to place, each with a note on which leaves it separates and which leaves it sails straight through: (a) the person who acts with no articulable thought at all and can't say afterwards why they did it — I've been calling this "one thought too few" in my notes, and I want to know whether that's the ideal of character or a failure of self-knowledge, and whether the tree even has room for the difference; (b) a doctor doing triage where one of the two patients is her own, so the thought is institutional rather than intimate; (c) a case where the relationship turns out to have rested on a mistake, so the thought is still perfectly available but nothing real is tracking it.

Leaves I specifically need: one for impartial consequentialism on which the thought isn't merely permitted but is the right thing to be thinking; one for the view (Scheffler? I think that's who, don't worry about exact titles or years, I don't need a reading list) where you're permitted to favour your own but not required to; one for the reading on which the objection is only about the phenomenology of deliberation and says nothing about which reasons exist; one for the reading on which integrity is just another item to be weighed in the balance — and tell me whether that one is a genuine position or a misreading of the objection, and why; and one for the demandingness complaint in its own right, separated out from the integrity one. If two of those collapse into a single leaf, say so explicitly rather than pretending they're distinguishable. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't pull apart, and tell me at the end which single question I should drop if I have to cut the handout down to two.
