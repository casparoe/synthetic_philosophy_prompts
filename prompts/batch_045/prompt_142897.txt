Hey — I'm teaching a first-year seminar this spring (working title "Capes, Utilitarians, and Terrible Decisions," it is as much of a shambles as it sounds) and Watchmen is up in two weeks. I've done the "is Ozymandias just a utilitarian" session twice before and the students were bored to tears, so I want to try him through the unilateralist's curse instead, and I want the reading to be sharper than what I'd get from a search result on either topic.

Statement of the idea so we're on the same page, fix it if I've flattened it: when a course of action has big irreversible consequences and any one of several people could trigger it on their own, the person who actually triggers it is disproportionately likely to be the one whose estimate of the upside is most badly wrong, because everyone who thinks it's a bad idea simply doesn't act. The group ends up with a decision procedure that selects for error, and the standard remedy is a norm of the "don't go it alone, defer if the others aren't convinced" kind.

Four things I want, plus one where I expect you to tell me I'm wrong:

1. Veidt as a case, and where the model stops fitting him. The stock setup assumes the agent is sincere and just miscalibrated, and that other people's private evidence would dilute his confidence. Veidt doesn't fit: he's the one designing the information environment too. Everyone who worked on the project ends up dead, Manhattan is set up to take the blame and exiled for it, and the plan only works if the survivors never learn what happened. I've been calling this a "self-certifying unilateralist." Does that make the curse argument bite harder, or does it slide off the edge of the argument into something else entirely? I care because real-world would-be saviors are usually at least as good at managing the record as Veidt is, and if the argument only covers the honest ones it's doing less work than advertised.

2. The symmetric version, which is the part I actually want. Is there a mirror-image curse for vetoes, where the person who ends up blocking something is the one most badly wrong in the pessimistic direction? Watchmen is stuffed with veto players: both superpowers, Manhattan, and Rorschach trying to veto the cover-up and getting killed for it. Is the asymmetry between the two cases real, and what's the deep reason — optimism bias in our error terms, or the fact that "do nothing" isn't neutral because the status quo is somebody's choice too?

3. The outcome problem. The story mostly tells us the plan works, for now. Everyone insists the evaluation has to be ex ante. Does the comic make that line harder to hold, and does it play fair? Veidt is written as a narcissist, and the peace is visibly fragile, and there's that last exchange in Antarctica where he asks whether he did the right thing and Manhattan gives him the nothing-ever-ends non-answer. Tell me if those are stacked dice.

4. Where the reading fails. Rorschach is not a deontological hero, he's a wreck. The book is formally obsessed with symmetry (the rigid nine-panel grid, the clock face), and I half-suspect I'm reading the grid into the argument rather than the argument out of the book.

Work from the comic if you know it, from Snyder's film if you don't, but say which — I know the endings differ (squid vs. blaming Manhattan) and I'd rather have one honest reading than a blend. Also: no plot summary, and please don't quote the book at me — my copy's at school and I'll just be standing there correcting misquotes in front of twenty freshmen.
