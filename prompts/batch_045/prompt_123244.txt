I run a small in-person reading group — about half of us have been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, the other half are friends who mainly came for the movies — and each month we pair one work of speculative fiction with the technical idea it seems to be secretly about. Next month we are doing the 2002 Spielberg film Minority Report (not the Philip K. Dick short story, which we will only mention in passing) alongside Robin Hanson's futarchy: the proposal that a democracy should keep the value question — what we want to maximize — and outsource the factual question — which candidate policy actually produces more of it — to conditional prediction markets, with the market's favored policy being the one adopted. Half the group has never read any Hanson, so I want handout notes that state the mechanism accurately but briefly before using it. On the face of it the film looks like a futarchy that has gone wrong: an institution (Precrime) produces forecasts of a specified bad outcome, and the state acts on those forecasts before the outcome occurs. But I have a suspicion the resemblance is misleading in an instructive way, and I want a reading that gets at exactly where it misleads.

The scaffolding I have been playing with, which I would like you to use and to push back on if it is wrong, is a three-way taxonomy of failure modes for any institution that governs by forecast. First, proxy or instrument risk: the metric the institution cares about (murders that do not happen) is not what its instrument measures (what the precogs see), and the people who maintain the instrument have an interest in its apparent infallibility. Second, reflexivity or closed-loop risk: a forecast that is disclosed to the person it is about can help bring about the event it names — Anderton's flight is the obvious case — which is not the same problem as the one futarchists usually discuss under manipulation, where someone moves prices by taking costly losing positions. Third, unscoreability: because the intervention prevents the predicted event, the institution's track record is permanently unverifiable, and the forecast that was acted on can never be graded against its counterfactual. I have two speculative fixes I want tested against the film: one is a sealed-forecast design, where the market or oracle runs but its numbers are withheld from the officials who act, and payouts are resolved only later by an audit body; the other is an exploration levy, where a futarchy deliberately adopts the disfavored policy some fraction of the time so that the counterfactual stays legible. I also want you to consider the minority report itself as a signal: in a well-designed market a single dissenting or thinly-backed position is treated as information, and disagreement is something you subsample rather than suppress, whereas the institution in the film treats one precog's dissent as noise to be expunged from the record.

What I would like from you is a reading of roughly 900 to 1,200 words that uses this material to do real criticism rather than decoration: which specific scenes it would illuminate for the group (I am thinking of the Temple, the eye-transplant escape with the precog, and the confrontation with the man Anderton is supposedly going to kill), where futarchy genuinely explains what goes wrong in the film, and where the film's failure mode is one that market design has no answer to. I especially want the places where the film resists the reading — my worry is that it is really a piece about determinism, prophecy, and whether it is legitimate to punish someone for an act they have not performed, and that treating the precogs as a prediction market is a category error, particularly since they are captive persons rather than willing traders, which has no clean market analogue. Please also say what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that the papers and blog posts do not: my sense is that the interesting objection is not epistemic but about legitimacy and how citizens experience being scored, but I would rather you argue it than agree with me. Please do not settle for the observation that predictions change the world — everyone in the group has read that a hundred times; if that is where the analysis bottoms out, say so and take it somewhere better. Please write in American English.
