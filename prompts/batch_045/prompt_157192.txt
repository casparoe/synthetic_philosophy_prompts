A friend in my reading group has an argument about insect suffering that I think proves too much, and I cannot locate the move that breaks it. He writes it out like this:

(1) Rank interventions by expected value: the probability that the patients in question have the relevant moral status, multiplied by the size of the effect on them.

(2) Never round a small probability down to zero. Expected value already discounts for it; a one percent chance across a population that outnumbers humans by seven or eight orders of magnitude still beats a certainty about a human-sized population, and "the number is large" is not by itself a reason to treat a live hypothesis as dead.

(3) The probability is not close to zero anyway. Insects detect and learn to avoid noxious stimuli, they alter their behaviour for extended periods after injury, and they sometimes trade that avoidance off against competing motivations. Take one percent as a floor.

(4) Now take the intervention he actually cares about: changing the management of a large tract of cultivated land so that it supports a much smaller standing insect population. Its effects come in two sizes. It kills the insects living there now — call that k, a one-off — and it prevents the insects that would otherwise have lived there later, generation after generation — call that N, larger than k by many orders of magnitude. Against a killing of size k there is a prevention of size N.

(5) Prevention is cheap in a way that killing is not. If insect lives are net negative, not creating N of them is a benefit on the N scale. If insect lives are net positive, not creating them is no loss to anyone, since there is nobody it is worse for. So the bad branch of the coin flip has the size of k while the good branch has the size of N, and the expected value is positive and vast.

(6) That estimate only shrinks linearly with how much of the insect population the intervention reaches and how well it works. Even one insect-year in a million leaves the conclusion standing.

(7) But run (1)–(6) again on soil nematodes, and then on bacteria. Each time p falls and N rises, and N rises faster than any credence we can honestly motivate falls. Since (2) forbids ruling any candidate class out, nothing in the argument selects insects; it selects whichever class of candidate patients is most numerous, and then tells us to spend everything on that class. That is absurd.

(8) So one of (1)–(6) is false.

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than accept the conclusion in (7): giving up (2) looks like it collapses the whole scale argument into "ignore any population whose sentience is contested," which is exactly what the scale argument was supposed to refute. Tell me which step fails and why it looked acceptable when written that way; then price the alternatives — what does someone who gives up (2) instead have to pay, what does someone who gives up (1) have to pay, and is there any version of the scale argument that survives whichever rejection you recommend? Say how confident you are in each of your claims, and flag the one you are least sure of.
