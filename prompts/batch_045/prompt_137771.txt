I am a philosopher by training, and this term I am teaching a graduate seminar on the concept of democracy; I have also been trying, without much success, to write a short paper on what makes a political change an instance of democratic backsliding rather than merely an illiberal reform, and on who is entitled to apply that label. My method so far has been to go case by case, trusting my own verdicts as data, and the verdicts refuse to line up under any principle I try. Hungary after 2010 — a two-thirds parliamentary majority rewriting the constitution, redrawing constituencies and changing the electoral rules, packing the constitutional court, and building a compliant media regulator — is backsliding for me, and it is my paradigm. So is Poland between 2015 and 2023: neutralising the constitutional tribunal, forcing Supreme Court judges out through a lowered retirement age, and creating a disciplinary regime over the judiciary, even though the governing party won an outright majority and said it was dismantling an unaccountable post-communist bench. So, with slightly more hesitation, is Turkey's shift to an executive presidency approved by a narrow referendum, combined with the prosecution and removal from office of opposition mayors and the imprisonment of Kurdish politicians and civil-society figures; my hesitation is that I don't think Turkey was a full democracy beforehand, and I don't know whether backsliding needs a satisfactory baseline to slide from. I also count the American episode of 2020–21 as backsliding — a sitting president pressuring state officials and legislators to substitute their own slates of electors, the spurious certificates, the pressure on the vice-president, the crowd at the Capitol — even though every one of those efforts failed, the rules held, and the incumbent left office. But I don't count the imaginary Republic of Verdania: a government that restricts speech, channels advertising money to friendly outlets, and governs illiberally, yet holds elections whose count is honest and has handed power to the opposition four times in thirty years. That looks to me like illiberalism, not backsliding. And then there is Saldena, also imaginary: elections there are scrupulously fair, power alternates, turnout is high — but every significant party has an explicit understanding that a hereditary minority making up a sixth of the citizens will never be represented in government, and its members are informally barred from standing. I want to say Saldena is backsliding, or anyway democratically defective in a way that belongs to the same story, even though my reason for excusing Verdania was that democracy is about the fairness of competition rather than about liberal rights.

The pairs that trouble me are these. If fair competition is what matters, Saldena's elections are fairer than Hungary's, so my two verdicts pull in opposite directions. Hungary and Poland involved the same mechanisms as the imaginary case I call restoration: the incoming majority in Poland after December 2023 moved against the public broadcaster and against disputed judicial appointments by means its own critics called legally dubious, defending itself as dismantling a capture its predecessor had built — and here I oscillate, wanting to say that this is repair rather than backsliding although the mechanisms are the ones I counted against the previous government. I also seem to want it both ways about who decides: when an external union suspends funds and threatens voting rights on rule-of-law grounds and the electorate responds by re-electing the government with a larger majority, I side with the external body; but when outside observers cry backsliding and the opposition then wins the next election, I am inclined to side with the electorate. My own tentative principle is that backsliding is any change, enacted by those currently in office, that lowers the probability of their being turned out at the next election under the rules they themselves have made. It handles Hungary and Poland, but it gives the wrong answer on the American case, where nothing was actually changed, and it says nothing about Saldena, where incumbents are perfectly removable. My repair is to add a counterfactual clause about fragility: an attempt counts when the rules survived only because enough officials declined to break them, so that the outcome depended on norms rather than on legal constraint.

So: is there a single principle, or a small ordered set, that captures most of these verdicts, and where does it force me to give one up? Please state it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and then tell me what it says about a case I have not listed — say, a long-standing democracy where a referendum-backed amendment gives the cabinet power to rule by decree for renewable six-month periods, used so far only for measures with majority support. I would also like it to say something about whether backsliding can occur where the baseline was already defective, whether a state can keep backsliding once it has plainly stopped being a democracy, and whether the correct principle has an official sayer built into it — domestic courts, the electorate, external bodies, or retrospective judgement — or whether the verdict is irreducibly someone's. I am not asking for a literature survey or citations; I have read enough of those, and I want the argument. Please write your answer in British English.
