Could he tell, by sight alone and before touching them, which is the cube and which is the sphere? Part B. Now a variant I made up. A woman blind from birth has used, for about ten years, a device that works like this: a camera mounted on glasses feeds into a grid of small vibrators strapped to her back, so that shapes in the scene show up as tactile patterns. She is very good with it — she can use it to tell a cube from a sphere, and a mug from a bowl. Her sight is then restored by surgery. Ask Molyneux's question of her: would she be able to tell which is the cube by sight alone before touching it? Here is one student's answer, verbatim:

"Molyneux asked Locke whether a man born blind, who has been taught by touch to tell a cube from a sphere of the same metal and bigness, could, once his sight is restored, tell by sight alone and before touching them which is which. The question is really about whether there is one idea of shape available to both senses, or two quite separate ideas, one visual and one tactile, that experience has to teach us to go together.

Locke's answer, agreeing with Molyneux, was no. The man has plenty of experience of how a cube feels but none yet of how something that feels that way looks, so nothing in what he now sees points him to cube rather than sphere. This is just what you would expect from an empiricist: no innate ideas, so nothing to recognise the shape by at first sight. Berkeley, an even more thoroughgoing empiricist, also answered no, and for the same kind of reason — association and experience have to do all the work. Those of a more rationalist cast of mind tended to answer yes, since the correspondence between the senses can be worked out by reason.

But we dont have to speculate. People blind from birth whose sight is restored in adulthood — after cataract surgery, for instance — cannot identify objects visually at first; at first they seem to get little more than patches of colour, so they cannot even tell the two objects apart by sight, let alone name them, until they have handled them. So the question has been settled empirically, and the answer is no: cross-modal transfer fails. The philosophical debate was really only speculation waiting for data.

In the device case things go the other way. The woman has already had visual experience, because the camera on her glasses was seeing the scene for ten years, and she has learned to read its output as cube or sphere. So once her sight is restored she should name the shapes straight away. This shows that Molyneux's question is really just about whether the subject has had any visual experience at all: any experience, success; none, failure.

It also shows the problem isnt about shape in particular. What the newly sighted lack is not the concept of a cube but speed and practice in applying it — a processing difficulty, not a conceptual one. So Molyneux's problem is a straightforward empirical question about cross-modal transfer, answered no, with the caveat that any prior visual experience of any kind lets the transfer happen."
