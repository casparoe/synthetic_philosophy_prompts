I have the last twenty minutes of the final session of a two-term graduate micro theory sequence, and the topic is what the utility function in a rationalization actually is. By then every student can recite the axioms and none of them believes in the number. I would like to close by reading a poem instead of by summarizing — a real poem, which someone with no economics at all could hear once and be moved by, while the students can go home and say which line does which piece of work.

Write me a sestina: six stanzas of six unrhymed lines, in loose iambic pentameter, followed by a three-line tornada. The six end-words, rotating by the standard sestina permutation, are BUDGET, BUNDLE, CHOOSE, NUMBER, ORDER, WANT; in the tornada all six return, with WANT and NUMBER sharing a single line.

Write it in the first person of the number: the index the rationalization assigns to the bundles, addressing the chooser whose choices it was fitted to. It should be the number's poem rather than the econometrician's — self-aware about its own standing, but neither smug about what it is nor wistful about what it is not. If you want a second voice, let it be the chooser's answer when someone simply asks her what she wants; you may put that answer on the page, but the poem must not let it settle anything.

The formal conceit should earn its keep: the same six words in six arrangements is what a positive monotone transformation does to a representation — the ordering held fixed, the numbers free — and I want the poem to know that. Four things have to be right, and I would rather they be carried by image and by the arrangement of the stanzas than asserted:

1. What the number is. It encodes an ordering and nothing else; any strictly increasing transformation of it says exactly the same thing, so nothing in it records how much better one bundle is than another, or how far apart any two of them are. The poem should not smuggle in intensity.
2. What the evidence is, and what the test is. The only data are choices from budget sets, and the test is a consistency condition across them: if she takes x while y was affordable, she must not turn around and take y while x was affordable; and the version of the condition that tolerates indifference — no cycles in the revealed-preference relation — is necessary and sufficient for some locally nonsatiated utility function to rationalize the data, with the proof constructing explicit numbers that are one assignment among infinitely many.
3. The blind spot. A chooser who ranks bundles lexicographically — all of the first good first, and only then the second — is complete, transitive, and a maximizer, and no real-valued function represents her at all; yet no finite pile of observations can expose this, because choice generated by such an ordering is consistent, and consistency is enough to guarantee some ordinary utility function that fits. So the poem's number should be able to be the wrong shape in a way that nothing about BUDGET or BUNDLE will ever reveal.
4. The silence. A fitted index says almost nothing about how she would choose at prices and incomes she has never faced: the revealed-preference relation licenses only the handful of comparisons it can chain together, and past that the numbers are assumption wearing a number's clothes. I want the unvisited region of commodity space present in the poem as an absence — a region the poem gestures toward and declines to fill, not one it describes.

The line in the tornada where WANT and NUMBER meet is the moment the number is asked for the thing revealed preference cannot supply: how much better off she is, or whether she is better off than someone else. The word "welfare" (or "wellbeing") occurs exactly once in the poem, there and nowhere else; the words "utility," "preference," and "axiom" occur nowhere at all, and the end-words should carry that vocabulary between them if the poem needs it.

No sneering at the discipline and no elegy for it either — the finding is a limit of the method, not a scandal. Keep the poem to a page, long enough to fill the form honestly and short enough to read aloud in under two minutes, and give it a title. After the poem, in four lines of prose, one per item above, tell me which line does that item's work: if a student who has done the problem sets cannot point to the line, the poem has not done it.
