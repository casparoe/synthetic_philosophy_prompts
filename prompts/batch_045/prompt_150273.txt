Im a novelist, about halfway through a book that cuts between two timelines: Mainz in the 1450s, in and around a printing workshop, and a near-future AI lab. One of my characters — a historian of technology, the closest thing the book has to a wise person — keeps telling the protagonist that AI is basically the printing press again. The gist of her speech is that nobody in 1450 could have gotten from a better way to make books to the Reformation or to modern science, and yet the world absorbed it fine, so all the talk about existential risk is just a failure of imagination pointed the other way. She also uses it to say where the interesting action is: in the institutions that have to absorb the thing, not in the technology itself, so writing policy about capabilities is barking up the wrong tree.

I love the speech. Im worried Ive written myself a beautiful line that is doing argumentative work it cant actually carry. So audit it, please.

- Give me the correspondence table first. What plays the press, the printed page, the scribe, the reader, literacy, the church, the flood of cheap pamphlets, the few decades before anything visibly political happened. And on the other side: the model, the output, the person whose task gets automated, the institutions doing the absorbing, the lag.

- Then the breaks. And separate the ones that matter for the conclusion shes using it for from the ones that are just cosmetic. I have my own candidate for the big one: the press automated the reproduction of text but not the production of the judgment inside the text — it copied what a human had already decided, cheaply. What feels new now is that were delegating the deciding, not just the copying, and I think that changes what kind of thing can diffuse and how fast. Is that a real distinction or am I smuggling my plot into a metaphor?

- Then what the analogy quietly imports that isnt in the target. My guesses: it imports an enormous amount of time (printing gave universities, states and churches a couple of centuries of slowly rising literacy to adapt inside, and diffusion was throttled by how fast people learned to read), and it imports inertness (printed output sat there and waited for a human to read it, whereas generated output is produced on demand, in response to whoever is asking, and can be different for each of them). Tell me if Ive got either of those backwards, and what else it smuggles that Im not seeing.

- Finally: does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — both the relax-and-dont-panic bit and the its-about-institutions-not-capabilities bit? And if the printing press is the wrong frame for the world will absorb this, is there a better analogy that would actually carry that conclusion, rather than just sounding more serious — the Industrial Revolution, nuclear weapons, something else? If the honest answer is that no historical analogy can carry it, say that and tell me what I should have my character say instead.
