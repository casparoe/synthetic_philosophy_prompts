Please use British English throughout, and please don't give me citations, references or suggestions for further reading — I have the literature; what I lack is a verdict on this particular exchange.

The speakers: Dr Ilse Vartanian, who works in the cognitive science of religion, and Professor Tobias Adeyemi, a philosopher of religion who is friendly to the empirical work but resistant to its debunking uses.

(1) Ilse opened. The core finding of her field, she said, is recurrence. Give human beings ordinary cognitive equipment — a trigger-happy agency-detection system, a theory-of-mind capacity that overextends, a memory bias in favour of slightly counterintuitive agent concepts — and you should expect gods in every culture, with roughly the family resemblance gods actually display. And you should expect this whether or not any god exists. That, she said, is the shape of a debunking argument, and the shape is what matters, not the details of any one mechanism.

(2) Toby replied that a causal story and an undermining story are different things. Every belief whatever has a causal story. Causal stories undermine only where the producing mechanism is unreliable in the relevant domain: agency detection misfires in the grass, but it is not thereby unreliable about other agents. So, he said, a debunking argument needs an independent premise about reliability, and the cognitive science has supplied an origin story instead — a different article altogether.

(3) Ilse said the reliability premise is available and comparative. Perception and memory can be calibrated: outputs are checked against the world and the mechanism is corrected. Nothing calibrates god-belief; no procedure has ever retired a god on evidential grounds in the way we retire a perception. Worse, the outputs conflict across cultures, and the machinery cannot adjudicate between them. And note, she added, what the causal story actually says: the machinery was not built for gods at all. It was built for predators, prey, allies and rivals; gods are what you get when that equipment runs in a world of mortality, deception and uncertainty. That is what a byproduct account claims, and a byproduct is not an answer to anything.

(4) Toby said the test in (3) is only 'is it calibrated?', and very few domains pass it in any demanding sense. He also pressed a point about content rather than recurrence: the morally concerned, punishing, knowledge-unbounded gods of large-scale societies are not what the raw machinery predicts. They are late, patchily distributed, and tied to social scale and to cooperation among strangers — which suggests that at least part of the content of religious belief tracks features of the social world rather than merely leaking out of misfiring modules. And he asked where she stops: run the debunking template consistently and it seems to reach moral and mathematical belief as well.

(5) Ilse conceded the overgeneralisation worry, and said it is a reason to reformulate rather than retreat. The better formulation is not uncalibrated mechanism but explanatory dispensability: once the causal story is complete, the truth of the belief does no work in explaining why anyone holds it. And she offered a sharper version she called counterfactual fragility. God-belief, she claimed, tracks variables that are irrelevant to whether gods exist — disease burden, existential insecurity, the scale of the society — whereas belief in other minds does not track anything of that kind. A belief's sensitivity to variables irrelevant to its subject matter is evidence that the mechanism is not tracking the fact.

(6) Toby said that test proves far too much. Belief in human rights, or in the reliability of institutional science, tracks education, affluence and political regime; if sensitivity to irrelevant variables is undermining, then everything social is undermined, including Ilse's own scientific beliefs. So the test is either vacuous or indiscriminate. And as for dispensability: a causal story that never mentions the truth is perfectly compatible with the truth being what makes the mechanism's output stable, so dispensability shows less than she thinks.

(7) At this point a third panellist intervened with an idea I had not encountered, which she labelled liturgical calibration. Some traditions, she observed, build institutionalised doubt into practice: periodic ritualised reversal, licensed mockery of the gods, formal rehearsal of the possibility that the gods are absent or silent, monastic disciplines of arguing against one's own settled position. If error-correction is internal to the package, she argued, then the uncalibrated-mechanism premise in (3) and the fragility claim in (5) are aimed at the wrong target. What needs a reliability assessment is the whole package — mechanism plus its own corrective — not the bare mechanism in isolation.

(8) Ilse answered that those practices are better explained otherwise: as costly signalling, since risking doubt is precisely how you demonstrate the strength of a commitment that cannot be demonstrated cheaply; or as management of the psychological costs of doubt; or as an elite refinement of traditions that already exist. She added that such practices are rare, late and concentrated in literate, socially complex traditions — exactly where you would predict a commitment problem, and not where you would predict improved access to the facts. So liturgical calibration is itself explained without appeal to truth, which is dispensability again.

(9) Toby said that reply is the problem rather than the solution. Any error-correcting practice whatever can be redescribed as signalling, so the explanation in (8) is compatible with every possible arrangement of the data. An explanation that cannot be embarrassed by anything is not doing empirical work; it is the protective belt of a research programme. Ilse said that is unfair, that costly-signalling accounts make testable predictions about who performs, when, and at what cost.
