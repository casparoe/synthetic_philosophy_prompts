Hey — I sit on the internal safety review board where I work (I'm a software engineer on the planning team for our self-driving stack), and we have to put an actual written policy for unavoidable-crash scenarios together before the next release ships. Draft the decision memo I'll circulate: recommend a concrete policy, with reasons the losing side could actually respond to.

The fight on the board is basically this. Half of us want the planner to just minimize expected harm — brake hard, and if braking isn't enough, pick the trajectory with the fewest weighted casualties, passenger counted in like anybody else. The other half thinks that's radioactive and wants hard constraints instead: the car may never steer into someone who wasn't already going to be hit even if that saves two other people, and it may never trade its own passenger's life for pedestrians. Someone said the second view is basically the trolley-problem / doctrine-of-double-effect distinction. I've got no philosophy background, but as I understand it the claim is that there's a moral difference between harm you bring about as a side effect of aiming at something good and harm you use as a means to get it, plus something about intending versus merely foreseeing.

What I need in the memo:

- A clean mapping of our real engineering choices onto that distinction. Our system is a learned policy, not a rulebook — nobody writes "if five pedestrians then swerve," the behavior falls out of a cost function and training data. So whose intention is the doctrine even about: the engineers', the company's, the passenger who bought the car, or nobody's? If the doctrine genuinely has nothing to say about a system with no intentions, say that plainly instead of stretching it to fit.
- Where the distinction actually bites for us and where it doesn't. Two cases I care about: (a) the car can brake and hit one jaywalker, or swerve and hit one person standing safely on the sidewalk — same number of deaths, different people; (b) the car can swerve to put the crash onto its own passenger and thereby avoid killing two kids. My gut says the doctrine lets (a) through but not (b), and I can't tell whether that's the doctrine talking or just my gut.
- The strongest version of the pure harm-minimization side, written well enough that those people feel heard — including whether a "never sacrifice the passenger" constraint is really about the passenger at all, or about nobody buying the car and regulators losing their minds, i.e. whether we're dressing up a PR judgment as ethics.
- Anything the framework is silent on that we'll have to decide anyway. I suspect near-ties and uncertainty are one: what does "fewest expected deaths" even mean when the planner is guessing at 60% confidence? Say where it's silent rather than forcing an answer.

Keep it to about a page of actual content, no citations, and don't lecture me on the canon — I want something I can drop in front of the board on thursday.
