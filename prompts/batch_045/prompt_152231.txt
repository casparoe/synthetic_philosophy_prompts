hey — I need a satirical piece for a reading-group session on the politics of anti-corruption measurement, and I want it as a mock corporate deliverable rather than an op-ed.

Write the services brochure (700–800 words) that a consultancy puts out to governments that have been ranked as badly corrupt, offering to raise their score. Here's the world, which I'm stipulating so you don't need to look anything up: the headline global corruption index is *perception*-based — it aggregates what country experts and executives believe about public-sector corruption — and it's a relative scale, not a headcount of bribes paid. Its methodology also carries what the manual euphemistically calls a "definitional bias": it codes as corruption whatever departs from arm's-length, impersonal, rule-governed administration. Those are only mild exaggerations of real complaints people make about such indices, and that's where I want the jokes to come from — not from invented silliness.

The offer, and the reductio I want followed straight-faced: the consultancy does not ask clients to change a single practice, only to change how each practice gets *coded*. Give me an invented mechanism for it — I'd suggest a "familiarity discount," where any practice with a close analogue in a rich OECD-style democracy is recoded as ordinary politics rather than corruption — plus a warning section explaining why actually reducing corruption is a rookie mistake (relative scale; improvements get absorbed by the annual recalibration; the appearance of reform can even re-baseline you downward). Then walk through three conversion pathways in one invented country: gift-exchange reclassified under a cultural-hospitality exemption, patron–client hiring reclassified as stakeholder inclusion, informal payments turned into itemized facilitation fees. The same money and the same favours should visibly keep moving while the score climbs.

Voice: deadpan airport-business-book register, title in that register, nobody in the brochure ever notices the problem, and at least one leftover dissenting footnote from the in-house ethnographer that makes a reader recoil. Close on a testimonial or a guarantee that lands it. Don't name real indices, real organizations, or real people, don't wink, and don't tack a moral on the end — someone who knows the anthropological critique of "corruption" as a Western export should come away thinking the satire was fair rather than just mean. A typo or two in your prose is fine, I'd rather it read like it was actually written.
