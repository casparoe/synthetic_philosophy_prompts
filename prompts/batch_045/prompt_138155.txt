hey — I'm teaching the first session of an intro philosophy of biology course and I want to open with Blade Runner (the 1982 one, plus a bit of 2049) instead of a journal article, using the replicants as a borderline case for the defintion of life. The catch is I want the definitions-of-life reading, not the usual are-they-persons / consciousness reading everyone does with this film.

So: which criterion of life is the film actually running on? I want three kept apart — metabolism and self-organising organisation; being part of a reproducing lineage; and having a life in the biographical sense, ie a story that can go well or badly for the thing itself. My hunch is the pathos depends on the third while the film keeps gesturing at the second: Roy is manufactured, can't reproduce, and has a built-in four-year expiry, and in 2049 the fact that a replicant was born rather than made is treated as enormous, but not for any ordinary biological reason.

Also, is the Voight-Kampff test a test for life at all? As far as I can tell it measures affective responsiveness, which would sort a human psychopath in with the non-living — does the film notice this, or does it quietly rely on us not noticing?

And be honest about where the reading breaks: is there a scene that only works if you stop asking whether they're alive and ask something else instead? What, if anything, does the film add to the definitional debate that a journal article can't?
