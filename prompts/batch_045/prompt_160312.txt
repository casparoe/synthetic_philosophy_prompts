I'm finishing a literary novel set on an upland beef farm — it's about inheritance, and about people who arrive at the right answer by a route that disgraces them — and the scene I can't make work is a kitchen argument at the farm gate. My narrator, Bea, has come home to her father Ewan's sixty head after three years with an animal-advocacy research group, and she reasons the way they taught her to: add up everything felt by every vertebrate affected, pleasant experience and unpleasant alike, the wild animals living on the land as much as the farmed ones, and accept that whatever she buys really does change how many animals Ewan breeds next spring. Her partner Nils holds the harder version, which he thinks is also the more honest one: suffering is what matters, pleasant experience cancels none of it, and a death counts only for the suffering involved in it — same animals, same land, same assumption about breeding. I need Bea's arithmetic to send her home with her father's beef, and Nils's to send her to the dried lentils in the cupboard. That is the wrong way round from how this argument is supposed to land, and the embarrassment is the point of the scene: the austere view has to turn out more restrictive than the calculating one, and neither of them may be caught reasoning sloppily, because my readers will be looking for the trick.

So please build me the case rather than assert it. One paragraph, realistic: a farm-gate or supermarket purchase, one household's protein for a week, with Ewan's pasture-finished beef, an intensively reared broiler chicken, and a plant protein all denominated in a comparable unit — and Ewan's farm sits inside a stewardship arrangement in my fictional valley such that, if he destocks, the grazed in-bye goes to tall rough grazing and birch scrub rather than to cropping, which is where the wild-animal term comes from. Then set out the numbers in three lines — the unpleasant experience undergone by the farmed animals, the pleasant experience in those same lives, and the wild-animal term on whatever the land does instead — and show what each of the two views concludes and why, so that the verdicts are generated by the views and not written into the scenario. The land-use line has to point the same way on both views, and it has to favour the beef: if it were allowed to do the work, Nils could dismiss the whole thing as a rewilding argument and Bea would learn nothing about her own ledger. What separates them has to be only whether the second line is counted at all. And the case has to survive the obvious ways of cheating it. Bea may not get her answer from Ewan's steers having lives worth living: their lives, everything counted, must come out net-negative, with the gap between the gross suffering and the net figure being the thing that matters. Vertebrates only — no insects, no shrimps, no contested sentience. No the-animal-is-already-dead, no claim that Ewan would breed the same number whatever Bea buys. No long-run consequences, no industry contraction, no what-if-everyone-did-that. Nothing from rights, duties, purity, or complicity. And no footnotes: these people are standing in a kitchen, so give me each number as an assumption a sharp reader will grant in a sentence, not as a finding from a study.

Then the four things I need in order to write the scene. First, one line of algebra stating the condition your numbers have to satisfy for the verdicts to come apart in this direction — the inequality — followed by, in plain prose, which quantity is actually doing the work and what happens to each character's conclusion if I double it or halve it. Second, where the chicken lands on each view, given that there are very many more birds per unit of protein but each life is far shorter and is fed on cropped land; if it wins on either view, tell me the smallest change to Ewan's farm or to the cupboard that demotes it. Third, which line a hostile reader will assume is doing the work, and whether that reader would be right. Fourth, the joint Nils would go for: he is clever, he does not want to be the one buying the steak, and I need him to attack the real weakness in Bea's case rather than restate his own view — so tell me the strongest objection available to him that is an objection to the case itself, and flag it separately if the soft spot turns out to be somewhere other than where I would have guessed. If you can, close with the disagreement compressed into two lines of dialogue that a reader who has never opened a book of moral philosophy would still follow.
