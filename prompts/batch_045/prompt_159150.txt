I sit on the technical advisory board of a public-interest funder that is about to commit roughly three years of support to about eight researchers working on conceptual and theoretical questions in algorithmic fairness — formal modeling, impossibility and trade-off results, and the interpretation of what those results actually establish. Not empirical audits, and not engineering. Our difficulty is not that the area is unexplored but that it is top-heavy with one kind of result. There is by now a familiar cluster of mismatch findings: independence, separation, and sufficiency cannot all hold at once except in degenerate cases such as equal base rates across groups or a perfect predictor; several natural error-rate criteria come apart in the same way; and the routes to satisfying several criteria jointly tend to run either through worsening outcomes for some group without improving anyone's, or through deliberately randomizing decisions. My colleagues split between those who treat these as essentially complete for the single-decision setting and want them treated as settled background from now on, and those who think the whole framing rests on idealizations that dissolve once real deployment is described accurately.

What I want from you is a defended agenda rather than a survey: given that budget and that kind of researcher, which open questions should the money actually go to, in what order, and by what criteria? Two features of the deployments we care about push me toward thinking the single-decision idealization is doing serious work. First, procurement: a public agency licenses a risk score from a commercial vendor, never observes protected-class membership itself, and audits using group membership probabilistically imputed by a separate third-party model, so every fairness criterion has to be restated relative to a distribution over group labels rather than to group labels. Second, composition and repetition: the score is one input to a human caseworker who can override it, and the same population is scored again and again over years, its composition shifting partly in response to earlier decisions. Please also rule on some candidate questions I keep putting on the list and then doubting: whether the mismatch results genuinely generalize to the imputed-membership and multi-stage settings, or whether in those settings they quietly turn into claims about the auditor's uncertainty rather than about the decision-maker's fairness; whether the leveling-down worry is a real objection to these results or only a sign that they should be stated as choices among feasible sets rather than as dilemmas; and whether some items on my list are not open questions at all but definitional preferences or artifacts of how someone chose to formalize the setup. For each question you rank, say what makes it worth the years: which downstream questions its answer unlocks, whether it is tractable for someone with formal training and no access to proprietary deployment data, whether it is neglected relative to its leverage, and what single result or piece of evidence over the next two years would move it up or down. Where you think the honest answer is that no further theoretical work is the right investment, say that instead.
