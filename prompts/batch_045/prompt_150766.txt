I'm revising a paper on the Formula of Universal Law and I want an audit of my own position before I commit to it in print. Here are the commitments, stated roughly as I would defend them.

(1) FUL is a test on the *form* of a maxim. What it asks is whether the maxim can be willed as a universal law, and in the perfect-duty cases the failure is a strict contradiction in conception: the universalized maxim is unintelligible or empty, not merely productive of bad outcomes. I hold this because the whole point of the test, for me, is that it is not consequentialism in a thin disguise.

(2) What gets tested is the maxim the agent is actually acting on — the agent's actual end, the circumstances as the agent takes them to be, and whatever conditions the agent treats as triggering it — rather than the description some theorist would judge to be the relevant one. I hold this because the test is supposed to be first-personal and usable in deliberation, without the agent first having to do social science about what would happen.

(3) The four standard cases come out right: suicide and false promising fail in conception and yield perfect duties; neglected talents and refusal of beneficence fail only in the will and yield imperfect duties. I also want the test itself to explain why the imperfect duties have latitude, rather than being told separately that they do.

(4) FUL and the Formula of Humanity as End in Itself are one principle in two formulations: they coincide in what they prohibit and in what they require. In practice I lean on FH whenever I need to say what the contradiction-in-the-will cases actually require of me.

(5) The machinery I in fact use is this. Call a universalization *stable* if the world it describes is a fixed point of the maxim's own application conditions, and *evanescent* if it is not — if universalizing the maxim destroys the circumstances the maxim needs in order to have any application at all. I take evanescence to be the defect in the promising case: a world of universal convenient promise-breaking is a world in which there is no promising, so the maxim has nothing left to apply to.

(6) Two case verdicts I want to keep. (i) The conditional cooperator, whose maxim is I do my part only if enough others do theirs, passes: the universalization in which everyone contributes is a fixed point, since each person's condition is satisfied there. (ii) The sunset defector, who withdraws support from a cooperative scheme precisely when they judge it likely to fail, fails: this is betrayal and free riding on other people's compliance, and morally it is of a piece with the false promise.

What I cannot see is where the pressure in this package actually lies, so I want you to audit it. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, and how badly? For each real tension, say what I would have to give up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Be explicit about any pairs that only look tense and dissolve once the terms are fixed, and say why they dissolve. If the two verdicts in (6) cannot both be had given (1), (2) and (5), tell me which one is doing the damage and why. Then name the single revision that would clean up the most, and say what I would lose by making it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the set hangs together, and if not, where the fault is — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equals. Flag how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the one claim in your audit you are least sure of.
