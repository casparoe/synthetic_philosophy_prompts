I'm in a philosophy of mind / cog sci course this term and we just did Dennett's four kinds of creatures — Darwinian (repertoire fixed by selection, adapts only across generations), Skinnerian (learns within its own lifetime by actual trial and error, with its own skin in the game), Popperian (candidate actions get winnowed down inside the head, so bad moves get discarded without the animal having to make them for real), Gregorian (imports tools that earlier generations did the R&D on — words, number systems, diagrams, reminders — and those tools change what the creature can represent in the first place). Our TA drew it as a tower, each kind built on the one below.

I made up a case for a problem set and now I can't decide what to do with it, so I want a dialogue instead of an essay.

The case: a bird I'm calling the skipper-jay. It clearly learns individually — routes, caching tricks, which humans are dangerous, all picked up by trial and error with real stakes. It also has about a dozen call types it learns from its flock that work like labels: different ones for food types, predator types, direction, distance, and the flock combines them. Critically, the labels don't let it *rehearse*: every test the field team runs suggests candidates still get tried out in the world, the labels just massively narrow which candidates it bothers trying. So the selection pressure is still outside the head, but the search space has been restructured by a culturally transmitted representational system.

Write me a dialogue between two people who've both read Dennett and disagree about how to classify it. One of them (call her Mara) should think the Gregorian level is a genuine discontinuity — the tools bring something new in, and a creature using symbols to restructure its own search is already on the far side of the line even if it never simulates anything. The other (call him Teo) should think the tower is misleading and there's no sharp line here: the same selectional story does all the work at every level, "tools" are just one more way of shrinking a search space, and the Popperian layer is the one that actually matters for anything we'd want to call a mind. Make them equally good — I don't want Teo to be a wet blanket or Mara to be a mystic. They should answer each other's actual arguments, and each of them should land one hit the other doesn't really recover from.

If it helps, let them fight over a second case too: a neural sequence model trained on human text that solves multi-step problems by writing itself notes in English in a scratchpad, and is only ever updated from external scores on its final output. It's got the tools and none of the inner rehearsal. Does it come out as Gregorian, and if it comes out differently from the jay, what's doing the work? (Please don't let this turn into a general "are language models conscious" argument — use it only as a test case for the taxonomy.)

My own hunch, which you can ignore, is that the tower picture is doing all the argumentative work and the real question is whether these four are levels of one hierarchy or independent design upgrades you can mix and match.

Two things I do want: an actual verdict at the end, not "it depends on what you mean by mind" — commit to how both cases should be classified, and say why. And then a short note after the dialogue telling me where you think I've stacked the case against one of them, where you've stretched or simplified Dennett to make the dialogue work, and which of the two you think is closer to right.
