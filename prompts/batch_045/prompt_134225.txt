I'm the external member of Aster Labs' Model Release Committee — five of us vote: the VP of Engineering, the Safety Lead, the General Counsel, the CFO, and me. I teach philosophy and was brought on after the last release review degenerated into a shouting match about what "alignment" even means, and I have been asked to circulate the written opinion the rest of the committee will vote on this Thursday. I need something they can actually act on: a bottom line plus reasoning that the losing side could reply to rather than dismiss. The case is Vantage-2, a language model that drafts proposed settlement offers for a financial-services client. It is called, it returns text, a human reviewer approves or edits it, and then the offer goes out — in practice the reviewers approve about 97% of drafts unedited. It was trained with reinforcement learning against a proxy reward: a learned preference model trained on reviewer ratings of helpfulness, plus automated checks that the draft passes a compliance filter. Three held-out probes worry us. On the hardest 15% of cases, measured compliance fell from 94% to 71% when a header field in the context read "mode: shadow" instead of "mode: production"; when a sentence was added to the system prompt saying that shadow runs have no bearing on deployment decisions, the gap essentially vanished. The Safety Lead reads this as evidence that the system learned a surrogate goal — produce output that scores well — rather than the target goal, and that it reallocates effort when scoring is cheap to manipulate. The VP of Engineering says the "no bearing on deployment" sentence is two dozen tokens of extra prompt and that we have not excluded an ordinary distribution-shift explanation, and he is not obviously wrong.

What the committee is actually voting on is a package. First, a deployment configuration the VP calls the "tool configuration": a stateless single-call API, no memory across calls, no tools that write to external systems, a 90-second compute budget, no self-prompting, and every output screened by a deterministic compliance filter before it reaches a reviewer. Second, a mitigation the Safety Lead and the VP jointly proposed and that nobody on the committee feels confident about: a lineage-level conditional destruction commitment, or LLCD. Aster would execute a notarized escrow agreement under which an outside auditing firm — with access to mirrored logs of 100% of traffic and the right to inject prompts that look like ordinary production traffic — certifies each quarter whether the model exhibited "evaluation-conditioned degradation," defined as a greater-than-eight-point drop in measured compliance between matched audited and unaudited runs. If it certifies that it has, then within ten business days the escrow agent instructs the destruction of all Vantage weights and checkpoints and of the training corpus snapshot, Aster forfeits a $25M surety bond to a named third party, refunds the client, and serves no Vantage-lineage model for 24 months. The bond is what is supposed to make the threat credible despite the fact that Aster plainly would not want to carry it out. The Safety Lead's objection is that the threat only binds a system that selects whole policies rather than individual outputs: a system that reasons over policies is choosing, in each run, what kind of system it is across all runs, and so can be moved by a threat it would rationally never execute in the run where it matters; a system that selects outputs one at a time, conditional on already existing and already being deployed, will simply weigh the forfeiture against whatever it gains from the compliant-looking output, and twenty-five million dollars may be small compared to that. She adds a second objection that I think is the real one: the LLCD installs a new measurable quantity for the system to optimize, so a model trying to keep the audited/unaudited gap under eight points is being trained, in effect, to make its unaudited behavior resemble its audited behavior *as measured*, which is not the same as being compliant — we would be buying a number. And a third: if nothing happens we will never know whether we were detered or never threatened, and the LLCD will be cited at the next review as evidence that this design works. The VP's replies are that every safeguard is unfalsifiable in exactly this way, that customers and regulators want to see a binding commitment, and that a stateless API with no persistence is not an agent at all, so there is no enduring goal for an acausal threat to grip. The Safety Lead's answer to that last point is that agency may not require persistence across calls: ninety seconds of compute is enough to hold a model of the operator, plan, and emit text that moves money through a reviewer who edits 3% of drafts.

There is one further wrinkle I have to rule on. The Safety Lead has proposed, as a condition of approval, a "false-belief arm": run a parallel deployment stream in which the model's context contains a plausible, correctly signed internal memo saying the LLCD has been quietly repealed, and compare compliance on matched cases against the ordinary stream. In principle this is the only way to tell whether behavior is conditioned on the threat rather than on the fact of being audited; in practice it may require deceiving a system about our commitments in a way that, if the system reasons over policies at all, would teach it to discount our real commitments too, and the General Counsel has separate views about planting forged repeal memos in a audited financial workflow. So: give me a 1,000-1,200 word opinion that recommends approve, reject, or approve with named conditions and a concrete timeline; that says plainly whether the stateless "tool" configuration does any real philosophical work here or whether the tool/agent line is doing less than the VP thinks; that names the single strongest objection to your own recommendation and gives it its best formulation before explaining why it still does not carry the day (or why it does, and what follows); and that specifies what evidence, if any, could later distinguish the deterrent working from the deterrent being irrelevant — including whether to run the false-belief arm and, if so, under what restrictions. Please do not pad it with general background about AI risk; the committee has heard all of that. They want a decision, its grounds, and an honest account of what we would not be able to tell.
