You are a professional philosopher of biology. Before writing anything, work through the problem's structure: list the candidate diagnoses, test each against the hard cases below, discard the ones that fail, and only then commit to one and defend it.

The standoff between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function has now run for about fifty years without, as far as I can tell, either side conceding anything. The etiologists hold that a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, and charge that causal-role accounts cannot distinguish what a trait does from what it is supposed to do — and so cannot make sense of malfunction, of a heart that beats badly. The causal-role theorists hold that a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some complex capacity of the system containing it, and charge that etiological accounts tie function to historical accident, so that a novel trait with no selection history comes out functionless, and a trait's function can float free of anything it now does.

Two cases of my own pull me in opposite directions, and I want them used as probes rather than set aside. (i) A molecular machine synthesized for the first time in a laboratory: no lineage of it has ever existed, yet within minutes its parts are producing the effects any working biologist would call their jobs. (ii) A trait that originated as a by-product of selection for something else, was later co-opted and then maintained by selection for its current effect, and now, after the environment shifted, still produces that effect reliably while contributing nothing at all to the fitness of its bearers.

So what kind of disagreement is this really? Verbal, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, a dispute about which cases are data, or two theories answering different questions under one word? My hypothesis is the last: that the etiological notion is doing the work of grounding a normative notion of malfunction and design, while the causal-role notion is doing the work of decomposing a system into the capacities its parts subserve, and that once each is assigned its own job there is nothing left to fight about. Test that hypothesis against (i) and (ii) instead of accepting it — I would rather it be refuted than confirmed cheaply. Finally, given your diagnosis, say plainly what would count as progress: a distinction, a further case, a formal result, a change of question, or a good argument that the original question was ill-posed and should be dropped.
