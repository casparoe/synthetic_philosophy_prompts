I work as a research assistant to a political philosopher who is finishing a monograph on the nature of freedom. A press reader has asked her to add a section applying her account to collective and diffuse cases, and she has asked me to prepare a memo anticipating where her own account will land. I would be grateful for a second opinion, because the more I look at her existing verdicts, the more it seems to me that they fit more than one principle at once, and that the principles come apart on the case she now has to decide.

Two things she is explicit about. She rejects the self-mastery or inner-citadel tradition: weakness of will and addiction, she says, are simply not her subject. And she insists she is not offering the familiar absence-of-interference view, since on her account a person can be unfree without anyone ever having laid a finger on her.

The verdicts in the manuscript so far:

1. A domestic servant over whom her employer has, by law, near-complete discretion, who has never once exercised it, and whom the servant knows to be temperamentally incapable of cruelty. Verdict: unfree. Her reason is that the power is what matters, not its exercise.
2. A driver subject to a speed limit enacted by a procedure she can contest, applying to everyone alike, and supported by reasons that bear on her as much as on anyone. Verdict: not thereby unfree.
3. A walker whose customary shortcut is now barred by a locked gate. The landowner would open it at once if asked; the walker does not know this, never tries the gate, and takes the long way. Verdict: her freedom is reduced, even though nothing she did was interfered with.
4. A smoker who wants to stop and cannot. Verdict: not a case of unfreedom in her sense at all.
5. A woman who has voluntarily taken a drug that will make her violently ill if she drinks and whose effect lasts a year. No one else is involved; no one may undo it. Verdict: not unfree. Her reason is that the will that closed the option was her own.
6. An applicant for an internal grant requiring the approval of a five-person committee, three of whose members are reliably supportive while two are unpredictable and apply an idiosyncratic standard she cannot anticipate. Verdict: unfree.
7. A woman who wants to train as a nurse and cannot afford the fees, though no one excludes her and no one would refuse to teach her; the fees and wages she faces are the upshot of thousands of independent decisions by people who have never heard of her. Verdict: not unfree. Her reason, as she puts it, is that no one's will stands against her; this is misfortune rather than unfreedom.

The case she must now rule on:

D is a scholar who wishes to publish an op-ed on a contentious topic. She knows from long experience that publishing on this topic reliably brings a flood of abusive messages: it has happened every time, and it will happen again. But the senders are unconnected strangers who do not know one another; which individuals write is unpredictable and different on each occasion; each writes for reasons of his own, having happened to see the piece on a day when he happened to be angry; none of them has any authority, leverage, or standing relationship with her; none intends to silence her, and most do not know that anyone else is writing; and each, if she replied asking him to stop, would stop at once and apologize. Nobody can spare her, and nobody can bring it about. She can still publish. What she cannot do is publish and be left alone.

What I want from you: identify the two or three principles that fit verdicts 1 through 7 equally well, say what each of them predicts about D, give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me what each answer would commit her to — in particular which of her existing verdicts would come under pressure if she went the other way. Finally, I have one more hour with her next week: what is the single further case I should put to her in order to settle which of these principles she is really running? I am less interested in whether D is really unfree than in the shape of the rule that generates the answer.
