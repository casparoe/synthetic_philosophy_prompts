I am writing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Nietzsche, and I need one essay question on the eternal recurrence that cannot be answered by recitation. My students have two reliable fallback moves: they summarise the contrast between the cosmological doctrine of recurrence and the use of recurrence as a thought experiment, and they deploy the vocabulary of affirmation and love of fate as though naming it settled what the thought experiment is for. I want a question that blocks both and forces a decision about the mechanics of the device itself.

The question should turn on which features of the announced recurrence are doing the normative work. In the passage where the demon announces the recurrence to the person addressed, three features are in play: the life that returns is that very life in every particular and in the same sequence; the repetitions are innumerable; and there is nothing new in any cycle, so that no later cycle improves, redeems, or compensates for any earlier one, and nothing is escaped by waiting. I want the question to present three variants, each of which alters exactly one of these features, and to ask what the verdict of the thought experiment becomes under each.

1. The near-miss variant: the loop returns a life differing from the hearer's by an arbitrarily small amount, one speck of dust falling otherwise, so that novelty is restored but improvement is not.

2. The amnesia variant: the repetitions are exact and innumerable, but no cycle carries memory or any felt sense of the earlier ones, so that from the inside each life is indistinguishable from a first and only one. The passage is silent about whether later cycles remember earlier ones, so this variant asks candidates to settle what the device actually requires.

3. The once-more variant: the life runs exactly twice and then stops.

The question should then require candidates to say what, given their handling of these three cases, the thought experiment is really measuring, and to face two further pressures. First, the scaling pressure: if the verdict is sensitive to how many repetitions there are, the device begins to look like an aggregative calculation over the goods and evils in a life, which sits badly with the claim that what is tested is a stance toward a life containing irredeemable suffering; if the verdict is insensitive to the number of cycles, then innumerability, and with it the cosmological doctrine, appears to do no work in the test at all. Second, the attitudinal pressure: the verdict seems to be delivered by what one wants, and wants can be changed directly, through numbing or self-deception, rather than by changing the life; candidates should be asked whether the device can be diagnostic rather than merely expressive, and what would have to be true of affirmation for it to be so.

Constraints on the question itself: it must be closed-book and self-contained, drafted so that it can be handed to students as it stands, and it must not collapse into asking whether the cosmological doctrine is true or whether anyone would want to relive a pleasant life. Assume ninety minutes and an answer of 1,200 to 1,500 words.

Please provide, in order:

1. The question as it would appear on the paper, including the three variants and the two further pressures, in a form a student could answer cold.

2. A model answer of roughly 700 words. It should not sit on the fence across the three cases: it should commit to which features are load-bearing and why, and it should explain why the announcement is supposed to be crushing rather than merely unwelcome.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 organised around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and, for each point, one line on how candidates typically get it wrong. Please also add a brief note on what a middling answer looks like, so that I can separate a B from a C as well as an A from a B.
