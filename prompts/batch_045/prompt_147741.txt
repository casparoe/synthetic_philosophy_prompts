I am a PhD candidate in political theory, finishing a chapter on the epistemology of inherited institutions, and I would like a satirical document to circulate among my reading group as a provocation about the Burkean presumption against innovation and the Chestertonian rule of the fence. What I have in mind is an internal policy white paper, roughly 800 words, issued by an invented body — say the Office of Latent Function, within a government "Change Governance Unit" — setting out its framework for reviewing any proposal to abolish, amend, or streamline an existing practice. It should be formatted as a genuine bureaucratic artefact: a date, a distribution list, numbered sections, a short glossary of defined terms, a worked example, and a closing performance report.

The voice should be deadpan management-consultant prose, and the committee writing it should sincerely believe itself to be a reforming body, in the Burkean sense: oriented to repair and restoration rather than innovation, convinced that the stock of inherited practice contains trial-and-error knowledge that no individual reasoner or single generation could reconstruct, and proud of never having stood in the way of a change that truly conserved what it altered. Please keep that distinction intact — the satire should not collapse Burke into mere reactionary obstruction.

The comedy has to come from exaggerating features the position really has, not invented ones. Two in particular. First, the asymmetry: one may not remove a practice until one understands why it was there, with no upper bound set on how much understanding suffices, no deadline, and no accounting of the fence's ongoing cost while the study proceeds. Second, the resulting ratchet, which I would like named in the glossary as the Curator's Regress: the investigator must live inside a practice long enough to grasp its point, but living inside it is precisely what forms the attachments and interests that make one reluctant to remove it — so the qualification for judging a fence disqualifies the judge.

Other glossary terms to define and then exploit: second-order fencing (the rule that the review procedure is itself a practice requiring review before it can be amended, and that the review of the review is likewise a practice, and so on), and latent-function reassignment (the quiet substitution of a respectable new rationale when a practice's original rationale turns out to have been exclusionary, fiscal, or merely accidental). The worked example should be something small and instantly recognizable from academic or institutional life — a committee's ritual of reading the minutes aloud, a thesis formatting requirement, the order of agenda items — and should end with the practice retained.

The final section should push the logic to its conclusion without ever winking: since the framework cannot permit the removal of the framework, and since anything left standing becomes the baseline against which future removals are judged, the Unit recommends a programme of prophylactic fencing, in which the present administration deliberately institutes new, harmless, faintly absurd ceremonies and forms — chosen for their harmlessness — precisely because their harmlessness is what will make them indestructible once a later generation inherits them and finds no reason anyone can remember. Invent and attribute a line or two to a plausible in-house authority if that helps. No real citations or footnotes are wanted, and please paraphrase Burke and Chesterton rather than quoting them.
