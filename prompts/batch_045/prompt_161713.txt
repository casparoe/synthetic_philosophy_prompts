Two researchers have spent a decade arguing past each other about whether the longue durée can be modelled and forecast at all. Call them the Cyclist and the Documentarian.

The Cyclist holds that some large-scale political dynamics are genuinely cyclical and measurable: popular immiseration, elite overproduction and intra-elite competition, state fiscal strain, and the breakdown of central authority recur together, with enough regularity that a model fitted on earlier instances can raise or lower the probability of instability in a polity it has not been fitted on, decades in advance. Quantification is not a straitjacket imposed on the past; it is the only way to find out whether the recurrence is real or merely the shape of a narrative.

The Documentarian holds that what the models measure are proxies whose meaning drifts across centuries, that the cycle's apparent periodicity is partly an artifact of how polities and periods are carved up and of which cases survive in the record, and that the transitions that matter to historians — who moved, when, on what grounds, and with what consequences — are precisely what a structural indicator cannot capture. The models, on this view, are fitted to the same events they are later credited with anticipating, and would fail on any case where the archives are silent about the mechanism.

They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and you are the scribe. Draft the joint statement they could both sign. It should not adjudge who is right, should not split the difference, and should not let either side capitulate: their goal is to make the disagreement exact and to record, in advance, what each expects to happen when it is tested. Do not attribute views to named authors and do not cite any literature; the two positions are the ones sketched above, and everything should be argued from their content.

Five cruxes at least must appear, and I want these among them: whether the cycle is stationary (a repeated oscillation) or cumulative (each breakdown resetting the state at a higher level of fiscal and organisational capacity, so that the second cycle is not the first repeated — call this the ratchet question); whether cycle length is a constant imposed across polities or a parameter estimated separately for each, and what that choice costs in falsifiability; the endogeneity of the elite-overproduction ratio, since the number of elite positions is itself a political outcome that expands to absorb aspirants; index drift, i.e. whether a single instability indicator means the same thing before and after a change in how records were kept; and what "predicted" is taken to mean — an elevated probability over a baseline, or a point forecast of a dated event of a given type.

For each crux, both sides should state the result that would count as evidence against their own view, and I want advance predictions recorded, with numbers wherever a number can be given. Where a crux turns out to be about words rather than about the world, say so and state the stipulation that dissolves it.

Structure the response in these sections, in this order:

1. Agreed claims — 8 to 12 numbered sentences, jointly signable, each specific enough that a historian or a modeller could deny it without contradiction. Roughly 450 words.
2. Disagreements — a table with columns: crux; what is at issue in one line; the Cyclist's formulation, in wording the Cyclist would accept for their own view; the Documentarian's formulation, likewise; and genuine or verbal, with the stipulation where verbal. Roughly 700 words.
3. The Amber Window protocol — three pre-registered tests, specified tightly enough to be run: an out-of-sample polity test, a matched-control test in which structural indicators are present but breakdown does not follow, and a threshold committed to twenty years ahead of a live window. For each: the data, the statistic, the exact rule for what would count as a hit or a miss, and each side's prediction written down. Roughly 600 words.
4. Residual disagreement — what each would still withhold if all three tests went their way, and what each would concede if all three went against them. Roughly 250 words.
