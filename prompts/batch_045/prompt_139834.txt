I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I'm rehearsing the Q&A for a job talk built around the third chapter of my dissertation, which defends what I call the voice-forfeiture account of hypocritical blame. The chapter's spine is a distinction between the content-conditions of blame — whether the target actually did something blameworthy — and its address-conditions — whether this person may now make this demand on this target — plus the claim that blame is essentially a demand addressed to someone, an attempt to get her to see and respond to a reason, rather than a judgment, a protest, or a prediction that merely happens to be aimed at her. Hypocrisy, on my view, leaves the content untouched and destroys the address: a hypocrite who blames does not blame badly, she fails to blame, and what she produces instead is a verdict, a warning, or a request, any of which may be perfectly in order. I also argue that the loss compounds — each unacknowledged act of self-exemption, including the act of blaming itself while still unacknowledged, is a fresh one, so the deficit ratchets instead of staying flat. My advisor thinks the whole architecture is the tu quoque fallacy in a nicer coat, and I keep losing the Q&A at the same two places, so I'd like to run it against myself.

Please play me at my best: the sharpest version of the candidate defending voice-forfeiture. Concede small points freely — you may grant that hypocrites can still judge correctly, warn, warn third parties, predict consequences, and perhaps even blame in a deputized or official capacity when speaking for someone else — but never the main claim that loss of address-standing is loss of blame itself, and never the claim that this is a normative defect rather than a merely pragmatic or etiquette-shaped one. Stay in character until I say "stop," keep every reply to a single dense paragraph, argue by distinctions and small cases rather than by appeal to authority — no bibliographies, no citations, no names of living philosophers, no "the literature says" — and if you need a detail about one of my cases, ask for it in character, as the candidate would.

Here's my opening, as the hostile questioner. Suppose a union steward who has quietly fudged her own timesheets for years now tells a manager, on behalf of a coworker who was fired for fudging his, that the firing was wrong and must be reversed. Every step of your machinery seems to misfire. Her hands are dirtier than the target's, and on your own account each past fudging was a self-exemption while this act of blaming is one more, so the deficit should ratchet rather than being cured by the fact that she speaks for someone else — yet the intuition is not merely that she may speak but that she is the right person to speak. Worse: if all she retains is a verdict or a prediction, explain why the manager's reply of you of all people is apt rather than merely rude, and explain it without quietly switching between standing and the all-things-considered advisability of blaming, because that switch is what I think the chapter is running on. And then tell me what the aptness of that reply is supposed to do: is it a reason for her to stop, or only a reason for her to do something else first — acknowledge, repair, step aside? If the latter, I don't see why we should call it forfeiture at all rather than a debt she has incurred and can discharge, and a debt, unlike a forfeited power, can be paid by someone with dirty hands.
