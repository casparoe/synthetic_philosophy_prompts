Been chewing on this on and off for months and I keep going in circles, so I want the spec before I read any more of the literature.

Setup, so we don't talk past each other. Color phi: a dot at A, then a dot of a different color at B, timings rigged so you see a single dot travel from A to B and change color partway along — at a moment when, on the standard telling, the second flash hasn't been presented yet. Dennett's move is to say there are two revisionist stories you can tell. Orwellian (as I'll use the term): you do have the "wrong" experience — red at A, then green at B — and the record gets edited afterwards so it seems the change happened mid-flight. Stalinesque: the changes-mid-flight version is what gets composed in the first place, and the wrong version never made it into experience at all. Then the punchline: nothing available from inside the subject distinguishes these, so the question "when exactly did the subject experience the color change" has no determinate answer, and the idea of a finish line in the brain where the order of arrival becomes the order of presentation was a mistake all along.

I don't want to re-litigate Dennett. I want the requirements list. What does a theory of the timing of experience have to be able to do?

Purpose, since adequacy is relative to one: I want a theory good enough for two jobs. (1) An experimenter has a subject hitting a button for "the dot changed NOW" with EEG running the whole time; the theory has to tell her which neural event to time-lock to and why that one. (2) It has to tell me in what sense, if any, a subject can be wrong about when they experienced something — because "the report is confabulated, throw it out" seems to prove too much, and "first-person report is authoritative about timing" seems to be exactly what phi breaks.

Cases it has to handle, minimum: phi and the version with the two colors swapped; the tactile analogue where taps at wrist, elbow and shoulder feel like something hopping up the arm and you seem to feel taps where nothing was ever delivered; the saccade-to-a-clock case where the second hand seems to hang before it starts moving; the Libet-ish setup where the subject dates an urge to move later than the slow potential that predicts it. And the one I made up, which is the bit I most want a verdict on: run the phi display, then at delay d after the first flash present a probe — a click, or a third dot somewhere else — and have the subject make a speeded two-choice response: had the color already changed when the probe appeared? Sweep d. If the psychometric function has a sharp switch at a d earlier than the second flash, that looks like an online readout of when the change was experienced, which would seem to finally seperate Orwellian from Stalinesque. My worry is that the probe's own experienced time is subject to exactly the same postdiction, so all you've measured is that two contents are consistently ordered in the draft, not when either one occurred. So: is there any design that breaks the tie — metacontrast masking of the second flash, a saccade deadline, time-locking to a feature-specific evoked response, some split-brain or blindsight-flavored dissociation — or is the tie unbreakable? And if it's unbreakable, is that because of the metaphysics or because of the measurement?

Distinctions I think it has to respect, but tell me which of these are fake: time-of-experience vs time-represented-in-the-experience (perceived when vs processing when); "the experience happened late" vs "the experience was revised" (delay vs editing); having a determinate temporal location vs having one we can measure; report-as-data vs report-as-authority; and whether an experience as a whole has a temporal location or whether the color, the position and the motion each get their own — i.e. whether there's one broadcast deadline or a bunch of feature-specific ones with different widths.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences. In particular I suspect "must not posit a single place where content becomes conscious" is a Dennett-camp prejudice dressed up as a desideratum, but I also can't see how to state the phi result without it, so tell me which way that cuts. Same question for "must not multiply experiences" and for "must be empirically decidable in principle." Say which subset of the desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want — and name the single requirement that does the most work excluding candidates.

Short scorecard at the end for the usual suspects (multiple drafts, the snapshot/cinematic view, global workspace ignition, an explicit integration-window or specious-present view, plain Orwellian memory editing) — a table is fine, but the criteria are the deliverable, not the scoring.

Take an actual position: commit on which desiderata are genuine, commit on whether any probe could break the tie, commit on which requirement excludes the most. Flag the places where you're guessing or where the answer turns on an experiment nobody has actually run, and don't give me the "both pictures fit all the data so it's a matter of taste" shrug.
