I write the incident write-ups for a customer-support chatbot we ship — usually two or three a week, about twenty minutes each, and they get read by on-call engineers, by our compliance person, and sometimes pasted more or less unchanged into a message to a customer whose ticket we messed up. (What we actually run is a router over a couple of models plus a retrieval step plus some hard rules, so "the system" is never really one thing.) The thing I keep tripping over is when it's ok to write that the assistant knew, decided, wanted, was trying to, or believed something, versus just describing what it output.

I got into this on my own after reading about the intentional stance — the idea being that treating a thing as an agent and predicting from what it believes and wants is a strategy you adopt, not a discovery about what's inside it. That clicked, but "adopt it if its predictive" isn't something I can hand to a writer with a deadline and an engineering lead who will sign off on nothing that sounds like philosophy.

So: give me a one-page procedure in plain english that someone with no philosophy can run in five minutes on a single sentence — the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it just says no and you rewrite, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is about what we're willing to be read as claiming rather than about what's true. Two tests I half-invented, use them or replace them: the swap test (if we swapped in a completely different system with the same outputs, would the sentence still be true?) and the copy-paste test (does the sentence survive being pasted into a letter to a regulator?). The case I can't work out is where the mental word is definately the most predictive way to talk but no part of the machine corresponds to it — the pattern lives in the training data, not in the system. I've been calling that borrowed psychology.

Run the procedure on three: (1) "the assistant knew the order number but gave the wrong one" — retrieval did fetch the right number; (2) "the assistant was tricked by text hidden in the uploaded PDF into approving a refund"; (3) "it doesn't want to disappoint people, so it stalls instead of saying the order is lost" — all we have is thumbs-down data. I want at least one where the rule tells me to do the opposite of what my team does now. And tell me what the procedure can't do, where it'll misfire on the kind of system we actually have, and — since I'm guessing at the philosophy — what would change your mind about the whole setup and where you think my framing is most likely wrong.
