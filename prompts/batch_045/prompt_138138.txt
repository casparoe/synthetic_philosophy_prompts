I have no academic training in philosophy or economics. I work as a logistics planner and, for about two years now, have run a monthly evening at my local library on how people actually make decisions — around 14 regulars, roughly my own level of technical comfort (percentages yes, algebra no), 90 minutes, a room with a whiteboard and no projector. Reading decision theory on my own, I keep coming back to the old pair of choices in which people reliably take the guaranteed sum in one case and the slightly better long shot in the other, even though — so the argument goes — the two cases differ only by a branch that comes out the same either way. I want my group to collide with this themselves rather than have me explain it, so what I need is an activity, not a talk.

My half-formed idea, which I would like the activity to test rather than assume: people are not comparing final amounts of money at all. In the risky option they are also comparing themselves to what they would have got in the branch that didn't happen, and the guaranteed option is attractive partly because it spares them that comparison. My duller rival explanation is that they simply don't handle the numbers the way the arithmetic does — a guaranteed floor is worth more to them than its expected contribution. A third possibility is that they don't believe the chances they are handed.

Please think this through carefully before you write. You are a decision theorist who also designs hands-on workshops for adults with no mathematical background. Brainstorm two or three ways of staging this, pick the mechanics that force the intuition rather than announce it, and discard anything that would need a calculator, a projector, or more than a few seconds of mental arithmetic. Constraints: real but small stakes (one $10 bookshop voucher per round, with one round chosen at random to be played out for real after all choices are in); the two paired choices must be separated by filler rounds and by different surface framings so nobody spots the pattern; include at least one variant where the shared branch is only approximately shared — 10% against 11%, with the amounts adjusted to compensate — because I want to see whether the pull tracks the structure or the resemblance; include at least one round in which people choose on behalf of another participant; and include a bidding round where they write down the smallest guaranteed amount they would accept instead of the gamble, so I get a magnitude rather than a yes or no. Nobody should hear the words Allais or independence until the debrief.

Give me the following, in this order:

The idea in plain words (~120 words): what participants should come away having felt, not having been told.

Setup, materials and stakes (~120 words).

The rounds, as I would read them out (~350 words): exact wording for each round including the filler, and what I write on the board each time.
