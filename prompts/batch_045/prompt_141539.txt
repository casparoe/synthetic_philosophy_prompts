I am a professor of philosophy, I have taught this material for years, and I no longer trust my own grip on it. I want to do reflective equilibrium on myself. My verdicts below are my actual ones, including the ones I suspect are inconsistent; I would rather be told that a verdict must go than have it silently accommodated by a clause that exists only to save it.

First, a terminological fix, because some of my unease may be merely verbal. I want to separate three things that get run together under "access":

- report-access: the content can be brought under a concept the subject can deploy in speech or in deliberate reasoning;
- use-access: the content guides a current action in a content-specific way — a forced choice, an eye movement, a postural adjustment — while remaining unreportable;
- broadcast-access: the content is made available at once to many independent consumer systems, including those subserving report, deliberate action, and encoding for later recall, and it persists long enough to be re-used.

Now my cases and verdicts.

1. Partial report. A three-by-four array of letters is flashed very briefly; a tone after offset cues one row; subjects report roughly the cued row's worth of letters but insist they saw most or all of the array. My verdict: phenomenally conscious of far more than they can report — phenomenal overflow, not a confabulated memory of having seen more. I am aware that I am here trusting the subjects' "I saw them all" against their own performance.

2. Flicker change blindness. A photograph and a modified version alternate with a brief blank field between them; the change is large and central; many subjects fail across many cycles, and afterwards deny having seen the original. My verdict: they were not phenomenally conscious of the pre-change content — and I do not want to say, as I did in case 1, that they were conscious of it and merely lost it. These two cases are the pair that worries me most: both involve rich retinal input and failure to report, and I come out opposite ways.

3. Blindsight. A patient with striate damage localizes or discriminates stimuli in the blind field well above chance by guessing, while denying any visual experience there. My verdict: no phenomenality; and I do not want to count the guidance of action here as access in any sense that matters. I recognize that this may just be me stipulating.

4. Connected consciousness under anesthesia. In the monitoring protocol where a cuff keeps one forearm free of the paralytic, some patients squeeze the investigator's fingers on request: comprehension of a verbal instruction plus a voluntary, content-specific motor response — arguably the paradigm of workspace access — yet afterwards they report no experience and no memory of the request. My verdict: no phenomenality. Pair this with case 3: there I withhold both access and phenomenality from content-guided action; here I have something far more demanding and still withhold phenomenality. Am I entitled to both?

5. Tip of the tongue. The target word is unreportable and unusable, yet there is a distinctive, mildly unpleasant state with partial content: initial letter, syllable count, near-misses that feel wrong. My verdict: phenomenal without access, and the phenomenal state is not a perceptual one.

6. Absorbed reading / driving on autopilot. You read three pages, turn each page at the right moment, feel a low boredom — and can report nothing of the content. My verdict: there was experience at the time and no subsequent access; not that there was neither.

7. The nagging sense of having forgotten something. No content available — not the appointment, not its time — but an unmistakable, affectively loaded, insistent state. My verdict: phenomenal without access again, and again non-perceptual.

8. A hypothetical, which I want you to treat as established for the sake of the exercise. Call it the deferred-probe paradigm. A masked word is presented; some hundreds of milliseconds later a probe appears. Subjects show semantic priming from the masked word, deny seeing it, but rate "something was there" above chance on a three-way confidence scale, sort it into the right semantic category above chance from a small set, and on being told the word afterwards say things like "yes — that's what it was." My verdict: I am genuinely torn. My gut says there was something it was like, faintly. That sits badly with case 2.

9. Confabulated report with chance performance. In a cortical-blindness variant, the patient insists, unprompted and in detail, that they are seeing a scene, while forced-choice performance in that region is at chance. My verdict: no phenomenality of the reported scene — here I trust the behavioural data over the subject's own say-so. Note the tension: in case 1 I trusted the say-so over the behaviour.

Control: deep dreamless sleep — neither; I take it no theory needs to strain over this.

Here is the tentative principle I have been toying with, which I would like you to test, repair, or replace: a content is phenomenally conscious just in case it is addressed to the subject, where that means it is available, without further stimulation, to at least two independent consumer systems, one of which evaluates it for the subject's sake (hedonic, affective, conative) and one of which can redirect attention or action in a content-sensitive, cross-modal way. Access, on this picture, is a further matter: broadcast to the systems supporting self-attribution and report. I already see trouble — the principle seems to predict that only the reportable letters in case 1 are phenomenal, which is the opposite of my verdict there, and it is not obvious to me what it says about cases 5 and 7, where the affect is there but the content is not.
