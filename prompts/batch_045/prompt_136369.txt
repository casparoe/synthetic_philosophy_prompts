I'm on our curriculum committee — mid-sized state university, philosophy dept, about 180 majors, most of whom will never go to grad school — and we're rewriting the history requirement, and I keep coming back to Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the second of the Untimely Meditations, as both the most useful thing anyone has written about our question and possibly a terrible guide to it. I'd like your help working out which.

The situation. We currently require two courses in the history of philosophy, picked from an approved list (ancient, medieval, early modern, Kant-and-after, and a topics course that rotates). A colleague — call her Dana — has moved to replace them with a single "problems" course: same topic every week, readings from whatever period happens to have the best arguments on it, no period requirement at all. Her two arguments. (a) What we actually grade majors on is reconstructing and stress-testing arguments, and they get markedly better at that when we stop forcing period courses on them. (b) Most of what history courses used to uniquely supply — who said what, when, against whom, in which text — is now retrievable in ten seconds on a phone, so requiring two courses of it is either obsolete or hazing.

I think (a) is probably right and (b) is half-true in a way I can't get clear about. So here's what I want: taking Nietzsche's threefold distinction between monumental, antiquarian and critical history seriously — including his framing claim that the question is never whether to have history but how much, and to what end life needs it, and including his remarks on plastic power, on the necessity of forgetting, and on how each of the three degenerates when it gets the upper hand — what are the most important implications for the decision in front of us? Not a survey of the essay, I've read it. Implications, for a department like mine, with these two courses and this motion.

Two things I'd particularly like you to push on, because they're where I feel my own thinking is unstable.

First, a distinction I've started using in committee and can't tell is doing real work or is just a redescription of something Nietzsche already has. Call it retrievable history versus incorporable history: history you can produce on demand versus history you've taken up, transformed, and can now act or think from. Nietzsche's worry is saturation — too much history crushing the capacity to grow. But if retrieval becomes costless, the failure mode inverts in a way he didn't have: the volume of carried history can go to near-infinite while the incorporated part stays flat, and this is invisible to us as teachers because it presents exactly like knowledge. Worse than forgetting, arguably, because you can't notice it from inside. Is that a genuine new pathology, or does plastic power and the need for forgetting already cover it, and I'm just restating the excess-of-history claim in modern dress?

Second, whose life is supposed to be served here? Nietzsche's unit is a person, a people, a culture. But the three modes seem to answer to different units: critical history to a self freeing itself from something it inherits, antiquarian history to a bounded community with shared objects of piety (he ties it to something like a family or a city), monumental history to an agent who needs models of greatness. A department is none of those, and its "life" is not the student's intellectual life, not the discipline's, and not the committee's political life. And note a further wrinkle: monumental history needs a limited, shared canon of greatness to work at all, and universal instant access to everything is arguably the thing that destroys the conditions under which any canon can feel monumental. If that's right, then cheap retrieval doesn't just make antiquarian history obsolete — it quietly defunds monumental history too, which is the mode my colleagues probably think they're defending.

Give me this, in this order, with rough lengths:

- Bottom line — about 150 words. What we should do, stated flatly and early enough that I can tell whether the rest is worth reading, and what would have to be true for you to change your mind.
- The three modes as decision criteria — about 250 words. What each of monumental, antiquarian and critical history would actually make of our two courses, in terms of what it would ask a student to be able to do by the end.
- Retrievable versus incorporable — about 250 words. The distinction above, pressed on as hard as you can: is it new, is it Nietzsche in translation, and does it change anything about Dana's argument (b)?
- Whose life — about 200 words. The unit question, and whether the answer to it shifts the recommendation rather than just adding a caveat.
- Where the framework quits — about 150 words. Including the uncomfortable one: the essay is itself a case of critical history, so using it as a neutral decision procedure may just be smuggling in its verdict.

Keep the whole thing under 1000 words, write it like you're talking to a colleague who's read the essay and doesn't need it summarized back, and please dont pad it with reading suggestions — I can find those myself.
