Hi — please act as an expert in philosophy of technology / STS who has also spent real time on public bodies that have to make decisions, not just write about them. Take this seriously: brainstorm two or three candidate procedures before you commit to one, and tell me briefly what you rejected and why, then give me the final version.

Here's my situation. Last spring the city put concrete planters and bench armrests under the underpass at the end of my street, where two guys had been sleeping for about a year. My neighbour and I had a loud argument about it at a block party, somebody from the councillor's office was there, and somehow that got me appointed to our new Physical & Digital Assets Review Panel. Grand name; we're really a subcommittee of the procurement committee. We meet monthly, get through 8–10 items a sitting, each of us has maybe 20–30 minutes per item, our findings get signed off by the city solicitor and posted on a public register, and we have no budget for consultants. So: real stakes, real deadlines, no theory background on the panel (I'm the closest thing and I'm a retired lab tech), and findings have to survive being read aloud by someone who disagrees with them.

Our remit is to attach a one-paragraph plain-language "political character finding" to every capital item and software purchase over 25k: does this artifact carry politics, what kind, and how confident are we. Basically we've been asked to operationalise the claim from Langdon Winner's 1980 Daedalus essay that artifacts can have politics — the examples our staff report leans on are the low overpasses on a parkway that keep buses out, and the mechanical tomato harvester that reshaped who could keep farming — plus the harder strand where an artifact's politics come not from anybody's intent but from the kind of social arrangement the thing needs in order to function at all. (Don't just summarise that literature at me; I need something I can run.)

What I want is a decision procedure, in the order the questions get asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and these things marked explicitly:

- where the procedure stops with "no finding" as opposed to "neutral" — our draft keeps conflating those two and the solicitor has already flagged it, because declaring something neutral is itself a claim that has to be defended;
- where it hands the question to a human being because it's a values call, not a factual one;
- which steps are judgement dressed up as procedure. Be blunt about that; I'd rather know than pretend.

Some things our subcommittee has been going round in circles on, and I need you to either build them into the procedure or tell me to drop them:

1. Our working draft separates three kinds: "loading" (the artifact settles a contest by design), "channeling" (it just changes the cost of a behaviour), and "latent" (no politics until the context shifts — funding dries up, a new bylaw, a different operator). I suspect the first two are the useful distinction and the third is where all our arguments are hiding.
2. The exit test someone proposed: can the affected person avoid the artifact without giving up a public good they'd otherwise be entitled to? Seems right but I can't tell how to answer it when the answer differs by person.
3. Nobody on this panel uses a wheelchair, sleeps outside, or drives for a living, and the politics of a thing are usually invisible to the people who don't meet it. Is there a step that fixes that, or do we just accept the blind spot and say so in the finding?
4. Inherited and orphaned designs: half our items are a spec copied from a provincial highway manual, or a vendor's default settings, or something installed in 1974 by someone who's dead. There's no author to interrogate about intent.
5. Maintenance and drift: a irrigation system that's only "public" while a contractor is paid to service it; a fountain that runs because a donor's money runs. Who serviced it and what it replaced both seem like they should count.
6. Time. These things outlive the council that bought them. Should the procedure have a re-review trigger, and on what?

Then run it on three real-ish cases and give me the actual one-paragraph findings I'd read out:

(a) the planters and armrests under the underpass — the thing that got me onto this panel, so I have an obvious axe to grind and I need the procedure to not care;
(b) a citywide rollout of prepaid smart water meters that shut off remotely when the credit runs out, on the vendor's default settings, with an opt-out that costs 40 dollars and a trip to city hall;
(c) the one I'm dreading — the curb extensions and raised crosswalk our neighbourhood association got installed after a kid was hit on that corner. I knocked on doors for that. I think your procedure is going to tell me it's political too, and if it does I need to be able to say exactly why in a way that doesn't sound like I'm equating it with hostile architecture. If that's where it lands, say so plainly and write the paragraph anyway.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: a short section on what the procedure cannot do. I already think it can't rank how bad the politics are, and can't tell us whether the politics in question are good ones, but tell me the failure modes I haven't thought of — especially the kinds of items where running the procedure faithfully will produce a confidently worded finding that's actually worthless.
