I host a fortnightly podcast on the metaphysics of time, and my next guest — I will call her Dr Hallam — has a book out defending the broadly Lewisian treatment of the paradoxes: travel into the past is metaphysically coherent, but no traveller can change the past, and the grandfather case looks paradoxical only because we run together a sense of "can" on which the traveller has the relevant abilities and a sense on which her success would be inconsistent with facts that already obtain. I want five questions that do not let her simply restate that view, each aimed at a point where the line is genuinely under pressure, and each with a follow-up written for the answer she is most likely to give.

One should be about bilking. If backward causation is admissible in the time-travel case, why is it not admissible generally, and what is supposed to stop a bilker — say, someone in 1920 with a hammer and a device that will detonate tomorrow unless a signal arrives from 2050? Her likely answer is that the traveller's own presence in the past is already part of the story, so the bilker's blow is no less constrained than the traveller's shot; press on whether that is a claim about anyone's abilities or only a claim about what in fact happens.

One should press on whether the two senses of "can" are doing explanatory work at all. Ask what the view says the traveller cannot do other than what did not happen; if the answer is "nothing", she has arguably told us only that the past is actual, so follow up by asking how that differs from plain fatalism about the past, and whether it leaves the traveller anything to deliberate about.

One should concern causal loops and explanation: ask where the content of a looped object comes from — a score copied in 2024 from a manuscript that will be carried back to 1824 and then recopied — and whether she is committed to facts with no further explanation. She will probably say that local causal explanation is all that is owed and that loops are no worse than unexplained initial conditions; follow up on whether that comparison is fair, given that the looped fact seems explained by nothing at all rather than by a contingent boundary condition.

One should use a case she will not have addressed. Suppose the traveller's father was conceived from a gamete frozen and stored years before the death of the man she goes back to kill, so that her existence is in no way hostage to his survival: ask whether the no-change view predicts that she can now succeed. If she bites that bullet, follow up by asking whether the view is then committed to brute coincidence in the ordinary case — a hundred travellers whose attempts fail for unrelated reasons, with nothing in the situation to explain why.

One should raise physics. Ask whether she treats consistency as a constraint on which global solutions are admissible or as something that merely happens to obtain, and whether her modality is therefore metaphysical or only nomological — relative to whatever spacetime structures our actual laws permit. Her likely reply is that the paradox is a conceptual matter and the physics is beside the point; press her on whether a view answerable to no physics has said anything about whether time travel is possible in the world we actually occupy.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each consisting of the question in one sentence and the follow-up in a second, with no introduction and no conclusion. Mark with an asterisk the item where you are least confident that the reply you have anticipated is the one she would really give, and add a few words saying why.
