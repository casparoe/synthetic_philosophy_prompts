I host a fortnightly podcast on the philosophy of music, and next month's episode is an hour-long conversation with a philosopher who has a new book out that revives and reworks Schopenhauer's account of music. Call her Dr. Ines Kollár. Her position, as I have it from the book proposal and from a lecture of hers I attended, is a deliberately stripped-down neo-Schopenhauerianism:

She keeps what she calls the detachable core: music's exceptional status rests on a formal isomorphism between a piece's temporal dynamics — the setting up, withholding, delaying, thwarting and satisfying of tendencies — and the structure of purposive striving as such. Because of this isomorphism music does not depict or describe anything; it presents the form of willing directly, which is supposed to explain at once why it needs no text, subject matter, or concepts, and why it can be understood without being about anything. She abandons what she calls the vertical analogy — the notorious mapping of the bass onto the lowest grades of the will's objectification and of the melody onto the articulate striving of human life — as an optional embellishment, and she weakens the contrast with the Platonic Ideas: for her, the only discontinuity between music and the other arts is that the others are representational and music is not. She then extends the thesis beyond tonal music: striving, she argues, can be carried by parameters other than tonal pitch relations — density, timbre, pulse, registral space — so atonal, minimalist, electronic and non-Western repertories present the same form. She adds a mechanism, claiming that the presentation depends on the listener's endogenous expectation dynamics of the sort discussed in predictive-processing work on music, and she treats cross-cultural and developmental evidence as bearing on her claim. She retains two Schopenhaueran corollaries: that music gives us grief and joy themselves rather than any particular grief or joy, and that words in song are subordinate to the music, though she defends the second more cautiously than he did.

Draft me ten interview questions for her, arranged as a progression rather than as free-standing items: start where she is most fluent and end where she has least room to maneuver. I want the sequence to cover at least the following, with one question each:

- whether the isomorphism claim actually distinguishes music from other temporal arts that also trade in tension and release — dance, narrative film, a suspense plot — and, if she answers that music is special because it is pure temporal form without semantic or practical content, why the form of willing should be capturable by something that lacks the directedness-on-an-object that willing has;
- whether dropping the vertical analogy leaves a thesis that is still identifiably Schopenhauer's, or whether what survives collapses into a non-metaphysical theory about tension and resolution that anyone could accept;
- whether "presents the form of willing" says anything over and above "expresses emotion-types," given that she also wants to keep the claim about grief and joy in general rather than particular grief and joy;
- the extension to post-tonal music: what a piece would have to be like in order to fail to present the form of striving, given that any parameter can carry it, and whether abandoning the consonance-dissonance anchor costs her the asymmetry between frustration and satisfaction that did the real work in Book III of The World as Will and Representation;
- whether the predictive-processing mechanism, if it is doing the explanatory work, makes the metaphysics idle, and what empirical result would tell against the mechanism rather than against the metaphysics;
- the double status of music in Schopenhauer, as both a copy of the will and as what the world itself would be if we called it embodied music — one of these seems to have to give, and either way it is unclear why presenting the will should count as knowing it;
- the subordination of words in song, given that a great deal of the music people actually prize is vocal or programmatic, and given his own ranking of tragedy just below music as a revelation of the same suffering.

For each question, tell me in a line what her most likely answer is and give me the follow-up keyed to it. She is well read in both Schopenhauer scholarship and empirical music psychology, and in the lecture I saw she was very good at re-describing objections as misunderstandings of the status of a metaphysical claim; I want at least one question that makes that move unavailable, and a closing question about what would count as evidence against the view that cannot be answered by saying it is a metaphysical thesis rather than an empirical one. No questions that ask her to restate Schopenhauer or explain how she became interested in the topic.
