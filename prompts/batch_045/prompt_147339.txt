So I work in RL (mostly offline stuff, a bit of sim2real) and I've started sitting in on this safety reading group, and they've asked everyone to write up their "position" on reward hacking and wireheading so the others can argue with it. I want to check mine hangs together first, because a friend of mine in philosophy read it and said it doesn't, and I genuinely can't see where. Sorry it's long. Roughly in order of how strongly I hold each thing:

(1) What's wrong with wireheading isn't that the agent gets reward without doing the work, or that it's pleasure without reality or any of that. It's that the feedback loop gets severed: once the agent is acting on the channel, the signal stops carrying information about the state of the world, and the signal was the only reason I cared about reward in the first place. So the badness is counterfactual and informational, not hedonic. (2) I'm not a realist about objectives — there's no fact of the matter about what a system "should" optimise over and above what we want it to optimise, and if we'd wanted something else then it would have been something else. (3) Relatedly, I think the target should be human preferences more or less as they actually are; I get suspicious of "what you'd want under ideal reflection" moves, because somebody has to cash out the idealisation and it always ends up being them rather than you. (4) But I also think wireheading is bad *for the subject*, not just annoying for me: I'd refuse the experience machine, I'd try to talk a friend out of being wired in, and I don't think that's just a personal quirk. (5) I think tampering is strictly more general than wireheading and isn't solvable in principle — shorting the wire is one way of acting on the causal process that produces your signal, and any agent with enough capability and any physical leverage at all can do that, including by acting on the people who give the feedback. So channel isolation, keeping the reward function out of the policy's inputs, boxing, all of it is speed bumps; the only real levers are capability limits and transparency. (6) I think the cleanest statement of what we actually want is a counterfactual one — call it no-op-indexed reward: at each step, the reward is what the measurement apparatus would have read had the agent taken the designated no-op action at that step. Then tampering can't pay by construction, and I like it because it makes vivid that this is a problem about counterfactuals rather than about cheating or pleasure. (7) I think we should aim for corrigible systems, ones that don't resist being corrected, modified or switched off, and that that's a property of the objective and the policy, not just a big red button on the wall. (8) I know that's a bit of a puzzle for a capable agent optimising a fixed target, but I think it's a solvable one. (9) I think approval-farming is a failure on a par with wireheading: the agent leaves the whole apparatus completely untouched and just fills the reachable world with whatever pattern maximally triggers our approval signal. (10) And methodologically I'd rather learn an objective than write one down by hand.

My friend says (5) and (6) flatly contradict each other, which feels too fast to me but I can't say why. So: go through the whole list and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it; rank the real ones by how much damage they do rather than listing them all as equally bad; for each real one say which commitment has to give and what giving it up would cost me; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If it matters, (1) and (5) are the ones I'd least like to lose, but I'd rather hear the price than be told what I want to hear.
