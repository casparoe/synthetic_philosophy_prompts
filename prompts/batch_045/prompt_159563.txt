We did Taurek's old paper — "Should the Numbers Count?", Philosophy & Public Affairs 1977 — in a reading group last month, and its been rattling around in my head ever since because I built an argument out of his idea that I think goes wrong somewhere, and I can't tell whether what it breaks is his view or just my version of it.

The version I got taught goes: the numbers don't count, because when five die instead of one, no one loses five lives; each of the five loses his own life, exactly as the one loses his. And then, since nobody has a stronger claim than anybody else, the fair thing is to give each person the same chance, which is where the coin flip comes in. I like that conclusion. What I don't like is where I ended up when I tried to say the general principle behind it out loud.

(1) A consideration can count in favor of rescuing someone only if it is a fact about what some individual stands to lose. No subject who loses it, no reason.

(2) When five die rather than one, each of the five loses precisely what the one loses: his life. There is no one who loses five lives, because there is no group-person who suffers the total. So the difference in numbers gives me no reason at all.

(3) So the numbers don't count, and the right thing is to give everyone an equal chance — flip a coin.

(4) Now take Ada. A doctor can either leave her alone, in which case she dies painlessly in a year, or treat her, in which case she lives nine more perfectly good years and then dies painlessly too. Think about that on a reductionist picture of persons: a person is nothing over and above a long series of mental states and bodily events hooked together by memory, character, and the usual stuff, with no further fact of identity sitting behind them. On that picture, "Ada loses nine years" isn't obviously a fact about anyone. Ada-at-forty-one isn't strictly identical with Ada-at-fifty, and even setting that aside, nobody ever experiences the nine years as a single nine-year-long unit; what exists is a run of shorter-lived stages, each of which enjoys what it enjoys as it arrives.

(5) By (1), the only considerations that can favor treating Ada are facts about what some individual loses. By (4), no individual loses nine years; each stage loses only the little bit it loses, and for the stages that never happen at all there is nobody to lose anything.

(6) So there is no reason, from anyone's standpoint, to give Ada ten years rather than one. And that seems obviously false. A doctor who could have given her the longer life and handed her the shorter one has wronged her, and we would say so without hesitating for a second.

(7) So (1) is false. And once numbers count within a single life, I don't see what is supposed to stop them counting across lives either, which is where I came in.

I'd much rather keep (3) than (6). What I can't work out is where the rot is. Maybe it's already in (2): "no one loses five lives" might not get you "no reason," since there could just be five separate full-strength reasons sitting there even if nobody is the subject of all five. Or maybe the damage happens in the move from (1) and (4) to (5), because "loses" is doing different work in the two places. Or maybe (6) is less absurd than it looks and the reductionist crowd is happy to bite it. Tell me which step you'd reject, why that step looked safe when I wrote it down, and what I pay for rejecting it — above all, what I pay if I want to go on saying the numbers don't count.
