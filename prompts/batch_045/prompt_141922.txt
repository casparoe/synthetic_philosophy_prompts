I am a retired physical chemist who, in the five years since leaving my chair, has been auditing the moral philosophy seminar and, less voluntarily, sitting as the scientific member of our university's Commemoration and Naming Advisory Group. I would like your help with a case now before us, but I want the reasoning rather than the outcome. My impression is that our past decisions are jointly consistent with more than one rule, and I cannot tell which rule we have actually been applying.

Our remit covers names on buildings, endowed posts, prizes, and monuments on land we own. None of our cases has been a Columbus or a Churchill; they have all been second-rank figures, and in every one of them the operative question has been whether the man-of-his-time defence is available. Four decisions, as I would summarise them:

1. Kesteven Hall (1874), named for Josiah Kesteven, a mill owner who paid for it. He employed children as young as eight, and one of his mills had a documented accident record including the deaths of two children. Employment of children was legal and locally unremarkable; the reform agitation of the period in his district concerned hours and sanitation, and nobody of consequence there argued that children should not be employed at all. The naming honours the gift and his municipal work, not the mills. Decision, 2019: name retained, a permanent panel installed describing conditions in the mills, and the development office required to mention the matter whenever the hall is used for fundraising.

2. The Whitlock Chair of Systematic Botany (1953), endowed in memory of Arthur Whitlock. In the last decade of his life he was a prominent advocate of legislation providing for the compulsory sterilisation of people then classified as feeble-minded; among his professional peers this was respectable, and the small number of contemporaries who objected were treated as cranks at the time. The chair commemorates his taxonomic work; the bequest came from his family, not from the campaign. Decision, 2021: retained, with no panel, the minute recording that the chair honours the taxonomy and not the later campaign.

3. The Draycott Obelisk (1908), raised by public subscription to commemorate a punitive expedition conducted by Major-General Sir Hedley Draycott. The expedition involved the destruction of crops and the killing of non-combatants; a war correspondent reported it at the time and members of the opposition denounced it in the House, while a counter-subscription was raised to defend him. The monument commemorates the expedition itself. Decision, 2020: removed from the quadrangle and installed in the regimental museum with explanatory text.

4. The Fenwick Wing of the medical school (1961), named in recognition of Gilbert Fenwick's research in tropical medicine. That research involved experimental procedures performed on patients in a colonial hospital who were not asked and did not consent; the protests of the local community and of two visiting clinicians were recorded in the medical press of the day. Decision, 2022: name removed, wing renamed, the surviving research collection retained but re-catalogued with provenance notes.

Two rules seem to me to fit this record, and they agree on all four cases only because the relevant features travel together in them: where the conduct was contested by contemporaries it was also what the naming celebrated, and where it was uncontested it was incidental to what the naming celebrated. The first rule is that the defence fails when the conduct was, at the time and in the figure's own world, condemned by a substantial contemporary minority. The second is that the defence fails when the conduct is not merely something the figure did but is integral to what the honouring commemorates, so that there is no separating them. There is a third line which my scientific colleagues offer instinctively, to the effect that the worth of a body of work is independent of the character and conduct of its author, so that a name is a matter of record and nothing else; I suspect our decisions have already foreclosed it, but I would like to know why, if they have, and whether any version of it survives them.

The new case is the Rossiter Institute of Natural History, founded 1901 and named for Sir Alpin Rossiter (1846–1919), honoured for three expeditions between 1876 and 1888 that supplied the founding collections and for the monograph that came out of them. The expeditions were mounted with the backing of a chartered company; Rossiter held shares in it, his brief included reporting on mineral prospects and on the supply of labour, and he travelled with its columns during a period of forcible dispossession, about which he wrote approvingly in published accounts. He also removed ancestral remains and sacred objects from burial places; his own journals record the objections of the community and his dismissal of them as superstition, and the remains are still held by the institute. At the time he was publicly honoured for all of this. Critics of the company existed then, but their complaints concerned its monopoly and its treatment of settlers and shareholders, not the dispossession; the removal of remains was standard museum practice worldwide, and the objections he recorded came from the people affected rather than from anyone in his own society. Today the collections and the monograph are still in use, the institute employs about forty people, and a formal request to remove the name has come both from the descendant community and from a student society. The director proposes instead that the name be kept on the building and that a permanent Rossiter Room be created to set out the history of the collections.

What I would like from you: the rules that fit our four decisions; what each of them predicts here; your best guess at what consistency with ourselves requires, with your reasons for preferring it; which precedent is the closest analog and why; and what principle the group would be adopting, in each direction, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. Two points in particular worry me, and I do not think our record settles either. First, Rossiter himself wrote down the protests of the people he took the remains from: does that count as contemporaneous contestation under the first rule, given that in our contested cases the criticism came from inside the figure's own society? Second, does it matter that the criticism the company did attract at the time rested on grounds we would now reject, or is any contemporary criticism enough? Please add one further case, of a kind we could realistically receive next, that would separate the rules you think are still in play, and tell us what it would have to contain. Finally, say how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the one point you are least sure of.

Conclude with a single machine-readable line, with nothing after it, of the form \answer{token}, where token is one of RETAIN, CONTEXTUALISE, or REMOVE, and REMOVE means the name comes off the institute entirely.
