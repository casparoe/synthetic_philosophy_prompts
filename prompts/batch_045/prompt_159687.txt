I'm in a little reading group that's been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty once a month for about a year — one guy is properly deep in the hinge epistemology literature, one is a Moorean who thinks Moore basically won and Wittgenstein was just doing Moore with fog around it, and I'm in the middle and getting more frustrated the more we meet. The argument that never moves is the one about whether hinge commitments have any epistemic status at all. One side says the Moorean certainties — that the earth has existed for a very long time, that I have two hands, that I've never been to the moon — do get some standing: a non-evidential, non-truth-directed kind of warrant that isn't knowledge but also isn't just a psychological fact about me, and that's what lets us say the skeptic's premises are rationally off the table rather than merely unassertable (I think this is the entitlement-of-cognitive-project line, Wright?). The other side says that's exactly the mistake: hinges aren't beliefs, aren't truth-assessable, they're more like the rules of the game or the scaffolding that makes giving grounds possible at all, so asking whether they're warranted is a category error, and the point of the whole thing is to quiet the skeptic rather than answer him (the non-doxastic / quasi-faith reading, Pritchard?, and then Coliva's version which I can never quite tell apart from the first one). Every session we restate these two and nobody shifts.

My own hypothesis is that the real disagreement isn't about hinges but about a single inference both sides accept: that playing the framework role excludes being evaluated by it. If you think "making evaluation possible" and "being evaluable" are compatible, you end up inventing a special warrant-status for hinges; if you think they exclude each other, you end up with quietism. Which would make the whole thing a disagreement about the architecture of epistemic normativity that nobody has stated in that form. But there are two other diagnoses I keep half-believing and can't choose between. One: it's verbal, and the entire fight is over what counts as a "belief" and what counts as epistemic evaluation, with each side picking the definition that makes its own conclusion fall out. Two: there's a hidden empirical question doing all the work — how certainty actually breaks down in people and in communities — and both sides are guessing at it. Here's the test case I keep trying on my group, which they always wave away: someone who, after a total conversion or a breakdown, reports that they have genuinely lost a hinge we all share (say, that other people have minds, or that the world predates them) while otherwise reasoning and acting normally. Does either side have a stable way to describe that person? One camp wants to call it an irrational belief revision, the other wants to call it a shift of framework that isn't assessable at all — and if those are just two vocabularies for one phenomenon, that's evidence for the verbal diagnosis. Second question in the same vein: are the two sides actually disagreeing about whether epistemic normativity attaches to persons or to practices/forms of life, so that "is this hinge warranted?" has no stable subject?

I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right about skepticism and not a survey of the literature (I've read the intros). Tell me which of these is doing the work, with reasons tied to what each side actually commits itself to — and if you think my hypothesis is wrong, say so. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction nobody has drawn, a case like the conversion case pushed further, a result from psychology or anthropology about how certainty erodes, a change of question, or nothing at all, because the question is ill-posed. Under 600 words, please, and skip bibliography-style stuff entirely — I only want the argument.
