Here's the thing I keep going round in circles about. Imagine a big public health service that has adopted a package of policies around psychiatric diagnosis, which I'll describe so we're on the same page:

1. "Clean-slate assessment." When someone is re-assessed, the new clinician is not shown the person's old psychiatric records. The stated rationale is that old labels stick — patients read about their diagnosis, absorb the symptom vocabulary, hang around in online communities, and start presenting in ways that fit the category — so a clinician who sees the old label just confirms it. Hiding the history is supposed to get you closer to what's really going on.
2. At the end of the assessment, though, the patient is given the diagnosis in writing, along with the full criteria, and is actively encouraged to join peer-support groups where people compare symptoms in detail. Early detection is the goal.
3. Clinics are graded on inter-rater agreement: two clinicians see the same patient independently and are scored on whether they land on the same category. Good clinics score high.
4. The service publishes annual prevalence figures and treats a steady rise over decades as evidence that something about modern life is making people ill — while also funding awareness campaigns that teach the public the diagnostic criteria.
5. When the diagnostic manual is revised, service-user groups give testimony about how the categories have shaped their self-understanding, and revision committees sometimes change criteria partly in response.

I find this weirdly two-sided. In (1) the label is treated as a contaminant that distorts the thing you're trying to observe; in (2), (4) and (5) it's treated as something perfectly fine to hand out, spread around and let people reorganise themselves around. My questions:

What does the package as a whole presuppose about what a psychiatric category actually is? Reconstruct the implicit theory — something about whether these kinds are discovered or made, stable or moving, and what the "noise" in (1) is noise relative to. Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with: I suspect several quite different theories of kinds would license parts of it, and I want to know where it's genuinely underdetermined.

Then: is (1) actually inconsistent with (2), or can a single view explain why you'd hide the label from the clinician but hand it to the patient? And what about (3) — if the categories are the kind of thing people grow into, does agreement between two clinicians trained on the same manual track anything beyond the shared manual? Does grading clinics that way secretly presuppose a picture it shouldn't have?

Finally, if we took seriously the idea that the people being classified are aware of the classification and change in response to it, what would have to change in (1)-(5)? Which parts would survive, which would have to be redesigned, and what (if anything) could the prevalence figures in (4) still be measuring?
