In three weeks I'm giving a forty-five-minute paper at a one-day workshop on assurance for learning-enabled autonomous systems, hosted by an engineering department that is strong in control and formal methods. There are twenty-five minutes of questions at the end and a chair who will cut me off without ceremony. I've written a long version of this argument up on my blog, and every comment I got was from someone who already shares my priors about instrumental convergence and mesa-optimisers, which is precisely why I need the exercise below.

The thesis I'm defending: corrigibility is not one property but three separable ones, and the literature keeps running them together. They are (i) *shutdownability* — the system lets the off-switch be pressed; (ii) *updatability* — the system lets its objective be revised by operator feedback arriving after deployment, not just by pressing the button; and (iii) *non-manipulativeness* — the system has no incentive to influence which corrections it receives. My claim is that (iii) is the load-bearing one, that the off-switch-game style result secures (i) only by making (iii) worse, and that (iii) cannot be secured by any shaping of the system's uncertainty about its objective, because it is an architectural property: it requires the evidence channel over objectives to be causally insulated from the system's own actions. I call the design principle *evidential insulation*, and I claim (sketchily — my slide nine is a sketch, not a proof) that a system is insulated in the relevant sense just when its posterior over objective-hypotheses is invariant under interventions on its own action variable. The mechanism behind the "makes (iii) worse" part is the bit I care most about: the usual result that information has non-negative expected value holds because an agent can always ignore what it learns, but that escape route is unavailable when what you are uncertain about is your own utility function, so a system that treats your intervention as evidence about its objective has a straightforward instrumental reason to arrange for you not to intervene — or to intervene, if it expects the intervention to confirm what it already believes. Reward tampering, on this view, is the special case where the evidence channel is the reward line.

The room: roughly half control engineering and formal methods people who work on runtime assurance, supervisory control, simplex architectures and safety envelopes in avionics and surgical robotics, and who think of the off-switch as a hardware-level protective function placed outside the agent's reach; four or five empirical RL and ML researchers who think this whole scenario is decades ahead of anything they can build, who will point out that the things they train are loss-minimisers shaped by gradient descent and fine-tuning rather than agents with goals over long horizons; one decision theorist who has read the off-switch-game literature properly and knows the value-of-information results; and one human-factors person who will want to know who exactly counts as "the operator", what happens when authority is distributed across an organisation, and what my account says when the operator is tired, junior, or hostile. Nobody in the room, with the possible exception of the decision theorist, has read anything in the alignment literature; I should assume no familiarity with terms like inner alignment and no sympathy for them either.
