I sit on the internal review panel of a large hospital trust, and next month I have to spend forty minutes with the trust's clinical safety committee explaining why the readmission-risk score we are about to procure cannot simultaneously satisfy the three fairness properties the committee has written into its own procurement specification.

The setting: discharged medical patients are ranked by a model that outputs a probability of unplanned readmission within thirty days. The two hundred highest-scoring patients per month get two home visits from a district nurse; everyone else gets usual care. The committee's specification demands that the score be (a) calibrated within each of the two patient groups the trust monitors under its equality duties — call them A and B, with observed readmission rates of roughly 22% and 11% respectively; (b) such that, among patients who would not have been readmitted, the false-positive rate is equal across A and B; and (c) such that, among patients who would have been readmitted, the false-negative rate is equal across A and B. The vendor has told them all three are achievable. They are not, except in edge cases.

Plan how I teach this, rather than teaching it to me. Specifically:

State the order in which the components have to arrive and what each depends on. I take it that the group-difference in base rates has to be on the table before the conflict can even be stated, and that calibration has to be cashed out as a statement about what a number means rather than about how accurate it is — but tell me the sequence you would actually use, and why the alternatives fail (e.g. why opening with the algebra, or with the definitions of the three criteria, or with an abstract example involving coins or urns, leaves the committee with the wrong picture).

Give me the one worked example that carries the whole session, and say what it must contain and what it must not. My instinct is a small fictitious table of scores and outcomes for a dozen patients, built so that the three criteria visibly pull apart, but I want to know which features are load-bearing — differing base rates, a score that is genuinely informative but nowhere near perfect, a scarce and genuinely beneficial intervention — and which features would mislead by importing a separate moral problem (training-label bias, a proxy variable doing the work, an intervention that harms some patients).

Map the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, and say at which point in the sequence each should be defused. The ones I anticipate: that fairness just is not using group membership, so the fix is to delete it from the data; that an impossibility theorem means the score is biased and should therefore be rejected outright; that the conflict is a small technical wrinkle we can split the difference on by approximately satisfying all three; that equalising the share of visits going to A and to B is the same as any of (b) or (c); that calibration is a claim about individuals. Add the ones I have not thought of, including the ones specific to clinicians rather than to statisticians.

Two complications I will have to handle, and I want them placed deliberately rather than bolted on. First, the committee will ask that the three criteria hold not only for A and B but for every subgroup anyone can name — each ward, each admitting diagnosis, each comorbidity. I want to know where in the sequence that demand belongs and what it does to the argument, including what a relaxation to "calibrated on average within every efficiently-identifiable subgroup" would buy us. Second, the two hundred slots are a hard capacity constraint, not a threshold; tell me where the fixed-budget framing should enter and what it does to the menu of criteria, since I suspect it makes some of the committee's phrasing incoherent.

Tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level and say so out loud — I assume causal or counterfactual notions of fairness, and the fact that patients who receive the visits have their readmission outcome partly determined by the visit itself, so that the very error rates in (b) and (c) are only partly observable.

Finally, give me one question to close on that would show me the idea had landed rather than that the committee had merely been impressed: my candidate is to double the nurse capacity and ask whether the conflict persists, but I suspect a better version changes something about the base rates or the score's accuracy instead, so propose the version that actually discriminates between understanding and deference.

Write the plan in British English, and do not point me to papers, vendors, or reading.
