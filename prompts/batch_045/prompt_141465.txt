I maintain an internal benchmark of Bongard-style few-shot visual-reasoning tasks: each task is twelve small SVG panels of black shapes on white, six in class A and six in class B, and a model sees all twelve plus a held-out panel it must classify. I review about thirty submitted tasks a month, and my recurring problem is not that the intended rules are wrong but that twelve panels never determine the rule the author had in mind. A size or stroke-width correlate fits the same data; a purely extensional rule naming the six figures in class A fits the same data; a rule stated in terms of grid position or drawing order fits the same data, since the model sees the whole grid at once. Please write a decision procedure my annotators can run on a submission to return accept, accept-with-required-edit, or reject: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the question to me because it is a judgment about what our user community finds natural rather than a fact about the panels. Constraints: the annotators have no philosophy background and about twenty minutes per task, edits have to be drawable in SVG, and I sign off on anything that is not a no.

I care about the translation more than the theory. The point that a finite sample is consistent with arbitrarily many rules, including indexical, disjunctive and grue-style ones, has to survive into the procedure; the metaphysics of natural kinds can go. Concretely, I want the annotator to be able to name competitor rules, to build a minimal pair of panels that separates the intended rule from each competitor and to reject the task when no such pair can be drawn, to check that the A-rule and the B-rule are genuine complements rather than two independent rules with panels falling in the gap between them, to check that the intended rule is not cheaper to state as a correlate of size, colour, stroke, pixel density or grid position, and to check that it does not need a vague threshold or a tolerance setting to apply. Say which distinctions you kept, which you dropped and what dropping them costs, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the procedure will misfire.

Apply it to three tasks I have on my desk. One: class A figures are symmetric under 180-degree rotation and class B figures are not, but every A figure happens to contain an even number of separate components and every B figure an odd number. Two: class A panels contain a prime number of separate components (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13) and class B panels a composite number (4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12). Three, and this is the one I expect you to rule against and I least want to give up: class A panels contain a triangle, class B panels contain a circle. We have shipped five tasks of that third shape, they are our most reliable human-verified items, and I need to know whether the procedure rejects them, what specifically it requires instead, and whether the five shipped ones have to be pulled.
