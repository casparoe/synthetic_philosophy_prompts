I convene a small fortnightly seminar series — graduate students and advanced undergraduates, about fifteen of us, ninety minutes of conversation rather than lectures — and next week's guest is a philosopher (I'll call her Dr. Marlow here, though that isn't her name) who has just published a paper that dissolves Bradley's regress rather than solving it. Her view, as I've reconstructed it from the paper and from her talk last month: propositions are not complexes whose constituents need to be tied together, because a proposition is not an object at all but a procedure — a structured routine for checking whether reality contains certain items arranged in a certain way — and what the tradition calls the unity of the proposition is really just the order of the steps in the routine. Her signature line, which she used three separate times, is the retort "You are reading the sentence's grammar back into the world," aimed at anyone who asks how a relation manages to connect its relata. I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend after that talk. He holds the cheap Russellian position that relations just relate and no glue is needed, and he claims the procedure view is only the Fregean thought that concepts are unsaturated, restated in a more expensive vocabulary, and that in any case it never touches what he takes to be Bradley's real target: not propositions but facts — what makes it the case that a, R, and b compose a single state of affairs rather than three scattered items. I came away genuinely unsure whether she has an answer to that, or whether she regards it as a separate question she is entitled to decline.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions for her, sequenced so that the grammar-back-into-the-world line becomes progressively harder to lean on, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. I want at least one question on each of the following. First, false propositions: if a proposition is the check it runs, what holds the proposition that aRb together when aRb doesn't obtain, and does she now owe an account of absence, or of negative facts, that her parsimony was supposed to avoid? Second, how fine-grained her individuation of procedures can be: can she distinguish the proposition that aRb from the proposition that bRa when R is symmetric, and the proposition that a is taller than b from the proposition that b is shorter than a, without reintroducing propositional structure through the criterion of procedure-identity — and if she makes the criterion fine enough to respect cognitive significance, hasn't the structured proposition come back through the back door? Third, whether the regress simply reappears one level up: a procedure has steps, and something must make those steps steps of one procedure rather than several procedures run in sequence, so what stops Bradley's argument from applying to her own unities, and is that a fatal symmetry or just a place where she bites the bullet? Fourth, what the procedure view buys that the Russellian answer and the Fregean answer don't already get you — in particular how she handles the difference between a proposition and a mere list of its constituents, which is the case people usually reach for when they say the unity problem can't be dissolved. And fifth, a question that forces her either to take on the version of the regress that concerns facts in the world or to say explicitly why that isn't her problem, and what it would cost her to say so. Close with one question about what finding or argument would make her give the view up. After each question, note briefly the answer you expect from her and why it's the one she'd give.
