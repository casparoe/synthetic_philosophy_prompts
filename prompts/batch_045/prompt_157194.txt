Hey — I'm a TA for an upper-level seminar on value theory and rational choice, 16 students, one 80-minute slot, and the room is split: about half of them have taken rational choice / econ classes and treat transitivity of 'better than' as practically definitional of rationality, and the other half are philosophy and literature people who will cheerfully say sure, cycles are fine. Last week we did the repugnant conclusion and they're primed to shout 'that's just the repugnant conclusion again' any time small changes pile up, so I need this one to land as a seperate problem and not as a rerun.

What I want is an activity where they run into the spectrum problem themselves. My rough idea: a swap market. Each student or pair holds a card describing some outcome, and the cards form a ring A → B → ... → Z → A, where every adjacent card looks like an obvious upgrade (big gain on one dimension, tiny loss on another) but the two endpoints are ones where, asked cold, most of the room would say A is better than Z. Rules I'd like built on: you only ever see your own card and the next one in the chain, no peeking ahead; each upgrade costs one token, worth something tiny but real (a participation point, or first pick of the snacks); and the swap happens if the holder wants it. At the end, reveal the whole ring and let them notice they paid N tokens to end up holding exactly the card they started with, then take a cold vote — would you trade A for Z straight up? — which I expect most of them to vote against, and then they have to explain how every individual step was an improvement. Two wrinkles I really want designed in, because I think they're what makes it bite: first, a fine version and a coarse version of the same endpoints, i.e. the same two endpoints joined by three big steps or by twelve tiny ones, with half the room doing each, so we can compare afterwards and ask why the number of steps matters to them when it shouldn't if betterness is transitive; second, a phase where they have to pick a single step to take back, and discover that whichever step they pick is the same shape as the ones they happily accepted. Please also design the actual card contents — I'd rather it not be about numbers of people or population ethics at all, keep it inside one life or one object (holiday packages, prize packages, intensity vs. duration vs. some quality dimension, whatever works) — plus a timeline with minute marks, the token scheme, and what the facilitator says and does not say. No lecture up front, and nobody should hear the word 'transitive' until the debrief.

The debrief is where I need the most help. I want the mapping from moments in the game onto the formal issues (the local judgments, the cycle, the money-pump structure of paying to return to where you began, the coarse-vs-fine contrast), and then a menu of escapes rather than one favoured reading: deny one of the pairwise judgments, deny transitivity, treat some of the pairs as incomparable or merely on a par, deny that tiny losses accumulate into a large one, make 'better than' context-sensitive or interest-relative — and for each, what it costs you elsewhere. I also want you to be blunt about what the game can't do: it can show that their judgments cycle, but it can't by itself settle whether the relation itself is intransitive or whether they are just unreliable judges of it, and it can't settle what the money pump is supposed to prove (does being pumpable show the judgments are irrational, or only that a certain way of letting them guide choice is expensive?), so tell me which bits I have to supply in my own words. And tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it: I tried something like this with friends once and it fizzled because someone spotted the ring by card three and just declined to play, and my other worry is that the econ half refuses the bait at the endpoint vote, or that the group doesn't reverse at all and the whole thing deflates. Give me a calibration step to run before the session and a fallback if the reversal doesn't show up, and if you can, a 25-minute compression for a reading group of six in case the full version overruns.
