I work in admin at a regional health service and I've been chewing on something for a couple of years now. Every clinic gets one letter grade, worked out by a formula from numbers we already collect anyway: how long patients wait before anyone sees them, how many come back within a month, and a satisfaction survey. That grade sets next year's budget and decides whether the managers get a bonus. Here's the bit I can't get straight. Once a year head office audits a random sample of files, and a mismatch between what we reported and what they find is a serious disciplinary matter — but only some mismatches. Editing a timestamp in the system will get someone fired. Parking patients in a corridor "observation area" so the waiting clock stops, or quietly steering the complicated cases to the clinic two towns over, gets a shrug and sometimes a bonus. From where I sit both of those make the number look better without anyone getting better care, so I definately don't understand why one is fraud and the other is being good at your job. (A friend says there's a name for this sort of thing, something like Goodhart's law, but I've never read any of it and I don't want book recommendations.)

What I actually want to know is what the system is assuming about what quality of care is. Does it secretly treat the number as what quality *is* — so that stopping the clock really does improve care — or does it assume there's some real thing behind the number that the number can drift away from, in which case why is one way of drifting away sacking material and the other one worth a bonus? My own theory is that it's running two incompatible ideas at once and that this isn't sloppiness, it's load-bearing: head office needs the number to be the definition, because that's the only way it can steer anything from fifty miles away, and it needs the number to be a measurement, because otherwise punishing people for editing timestamps makes no sense at all. Tell me if that's right, tell me what the practice strictly has to assume versus what it just happens to fit with, and tell me what would really have to change in how we do things if we said out loud that the number is only ever evidence and never the thing itself.

One more piece before you answer. A colleague proposed what he calls a shadow metric: collect the same numbers independantly, don't announce in advance which ones, and grade clinics on the size of the gap between reported and audited — punish the distortion rather than the level. My worry is that this just hoists the target up one storey and people will game the gap instead, or game getting audited. Is there anything in that idea that isn't simply adding another number, or is my worry the whole story?

Don't give me any citations or reading lists, just exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no summing up at the end.
