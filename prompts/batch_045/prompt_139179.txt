I would like help identifying — or, if it has not been identified, delineating — a configuration that keeps recurring in arguments about electoral participation. I should say at the outset that I am not after references or a reading list, since I have no way to follow them up; a bare label would not settle anything for me either. What I want is the carving: whether this configuration has a name, which established concepts it sits closest to, how it differs from each of them, and where exactly its edges are.

The configuration is this: a claim about the causal irrelevance of one's own action that is true of each individual who makes it, false of the group of people who make it, and unstable in a further way — if enough people accepted it and acted on it, it would cease to be true of them individually as well.

Two instances that I think have the same shape. First, a municipal election in which roughly forty thousand ballots are cast and the result turns on a few hundred votes. A supporter of the losing side says she stayed home because one ballot never changes anything. Of her, that is true. But if the several hundred like-minded people who said the same thing had voted, the outcome would have flipped; if the several thousand who said it had voted, it would not have been close at all. So the claim is true of each speaker and false of the set of speakers.

Second, the "safe seat" version. A voter in a district that has gone heavily one way for decades says his vote has never mattered and never will. But note that the irrelevance here is not a fixed feature of his situation: it holds only given how everyone else in the district behaves, and if the claim were widely believed on his side it would stop holding, and dramatically so. I also notice that the instability does not depend on which side accepts it. If one side alone accepts it, that side loses elections it would otherwise have won, and its abstainers turn out to have been difference-makers collectively. If everyone accepts it symmetrically, participation collapses, the result is decided by a handful of ballots or by lot, and each remaining ballot is now very likely decisive. Either route ends with the claim false. Yet no individual's acceptance of it makes any difference to whether it is true of her.

Three things I take it not to be, though I may be wrong about all three. It is not the familiar argument that voting is irrational because the chance of one ballot mattering is negligible: that argument is about whether the expected benefit of voting outweighs the cost, whereas my question is about the truth-conditions and stability of the irrelevance claim itself. It is not merely the fallacy of composition, because in the ordinary case the individual claim stays true however widely it is believed, while here it does not. And it is not a self-fulfilling prophecy, since what is unusual is precisely that acceptance tends to falsify rather than confirm.

So my questions. Does this configuration have an established name, or a standard treatment? If there is a nearest established concept, say which it is and exactly where mine departs from it, and if two candidates are roughly equally close, say which does more of the work. Is the instability I described the essential feature, or is it an artifact of how I set up the individual claim — in particular, is "made a difference" doing threshold or vagueness work here, so that the first instance is better understood as a sorites-like structure than as a self-referential one? Is there an equilibrium story available, on which the claim spreads, participation falls, and the claim becomes false only for a marginal person, and does it matter for that story that no single person's acceptance moves anything? Does the same structure appear in the parallel arguments about individual consumption, boycotts, or emissions, or is that a different phenomenon that merely resembles this one? And if nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it tightly enough that it does not collapse back into the neighbours, and give me one clear positive case and one clear negative case at the boundary.
