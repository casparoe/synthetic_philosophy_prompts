Some years ago my son was diagnosed, at nine, with Asperger's disorder. When the fifth edition of the American diagnostic manual appeared in 2013, that category was gone from it; his file was quietly rewritten as autism spectrum disorder, with severity ratings for social communication and for restricted, repetitive behavior, and a note that he had no language or intellectual impairment. He was thirteen. He had by then learned the word "Aspie," read a good deal about it, and built a working account of himself around it — why crowds are hard, why he is good at systems and poor at faces — and he experienced the change as a small confiscation. It has become personal again in a different way: he is in his early twenties and applying for workplace accommodations, and a clinic where I sit on a parent advisory board is rewriting its intake paperwork, which forces everyone involved to say what its categories are actually for.

I have started reading about natural kinds, and I want the episode analyzed through that literature rather than through the usual commentary about stigma and awareness. Please be disciplined about the mapping.

First, disentangle the claims the elimination of the category could have rested on: the empirical claim that the distinction did not hold up — unreliable in ordinary hands, largely a function of IQ and language delay, no difference in course, outcome, or family patterns; the metaphysical claim that Asperger's was never a real kind; and the pragmatic claim that the distinction did no predictive or therapeutic work and mainly generated unstable counts. Which is the strongest reconstruction of what was actually decided, and what would have had to be true for each of the others to be the real ground?

Second, say what carving at a joint could even mean for a behavioral syndrome defined partly by severity and partly by a judgment about impairment. Run the candidate accounts without naming authorities: a cluster of properties held together by shared causal mechanisms; a single underlying mechanism not yet located; a mere outcome that many distinct etiologies converge on; a category whose members shift once they are sorted into it. Which best fits, and does the failure of the finer cut tell us anything about autism spectrum disorder itself, or only about the Asperger's/autism boundary?

Third, I want you to use a distinction I have been developing, because the literature as I have met it seems to me to conflate three things that come apart: grip, meaning how much a class lets you predict and intervene on its members; depth, meaning how much causal structure the class tracks; and recutting cost, meaning what it costs in identity, eligibility, continuity of care and continuity of research to move a boundary. Apply all three to this case. Is it possible for a taxonomy to be deep but not gripping, or gripping but not deep, here? Is it possible for a real distinction to sit at a grain too fine for anyone to act on, and would that be a reason to keep or to drop it?

Then run the argument in the other direction. Does this episode support the skeptical, pluralist thought that there are many legitimate cuts and we choose among them by interest — so that asking whether psychiatry carved a joint is the wrong question? Or does it support the opposite reading: that where a discipline has no real kind, everything downstream — services, prognosis, self-description, research pooled across decades — becomes unstable, and that this is an argument for hunting harder for the joint? And does the fact that the label did social work, that people organized a community and an identity around it, count as any reason at all to retain a category you believe is not a joint?

Tell me where the natural-kinds literature is silent here: its stock examples are chemical and biological, evaluated by induction and intervention, not by what a classification does to the people inside it. Give me a bottom line — should the merger have happened, and should the label have been kept anyway — and tell me what the episode cannot settle.
