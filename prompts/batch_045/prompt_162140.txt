Next month I am presenting a paper to a metaphysics reading group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion period.

My thesis: Thomson's lamp — the switch flipped at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... of a minute, with the question of whether the lamp is on or off at one minute — has a determinate outcome once the setup is made physically realistic, and the standard moral (that the case shows supertasks to be impossible, or the description to be inconsistent) depends on an idealization rather than on infinitude as such. Sketch of the argument: replace the ideal switch with a filament whose temperature relaxes exponentially toward its target with a finite time constant; the temperature is then Lipschitz in time on the half-open interval, so it has a unique limit at the end of the minute no matter how the toggles are scheduled; because the switch intervals shrink, the filament cannot track the target, and the limit is a strictly intermediate steady temperature — the lamp ends up dim, neither on nor off; so the indeterminacy of the textbook case is manufactured by the discontinuity of the ideal switch, and what remains interesting is not whether infinitely many tasks can be completed but whether a binary predicate like "on" applies to a determinate borderline state. I also claim the same treatment dissolves the "paradox of the gods" family of cases.

The audience: about nine people. Two hold that supertasks are conceptually impossible and lean on the distinction between potential and actual infinity, so they will say it is the setup, not the answer, that fails. One is a philosopher of physics who works on classical mechanics and will treat the whole problem as a question about singularities and non-smooth dynamics in ordinary differential equations. One is a philosopher of language who will press on what "at one minute" and "after" mean, and will hear my conclusion as a claim about vagueness or about the semantics of gradable adjectives. The rest are uncommitted metaphysicians who mainly want to know what follows for the possibility of supertasks and for the openness of the future.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it and in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each, give a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft or where I should concede instead. Indicate which objections I should concede up front in the talk rather than wait for, and which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Please write the sheet in American English.
