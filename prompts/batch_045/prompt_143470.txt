You are a philosopher of science who works on induction, confirmation, and the foundations of statistical learning, and who has also sat on university data-governance and human-subjects bodies. Please take this slowly and reason carefully; I would rather have a genuinely argued document than a polished essay about artificial intelligence in higher education, and I would like you to do a short round of analysis before you start drafting.

The situation: I sit on my university's Committee on Student Success Analytics, and in ten days we have to give the provost a recommendation on whether to renew our contract with a vendor whose early-alert model flags incoming first-years as at risk of not returning for sophomore year. The contract is about $190,000 a year, which is roughly the salary and benefits of two success coaches, so the money is not trivial and the comparison is live. The pilot ran on the 2021, 2022, and 2023 entry cohorts: area under the curve around 0.83, and at the operating threshold we chose, about two in five flagged students actually did leave or drop out. Those numbers, though, came from random five-fold cross-validation on the three cohorts pooled together, so the model has never been scored on a cohort it was not trained on, and the flagged students were immediately assigned a success coach who phoned them while unflagged students got nothing, which means we cannot tell whether a flagged student who persisted falsified the prediction or was saved by the call. Meanwhile the 2024 cohort is the first admitted under test-optional admissions, our online and hybrid offerings have roughly doubled, and our bridge program for first-generation students has tripled, so the incoming population looks different in ways the vendor's model has never seen. The vendor's marketing material says the model learns from the data itself, with no hand-coded assumptions about students.

I got into a long argument about this last night with a friend who does learning theory. She said the whole question is just the problem of induction in a costume, plus the no-free-lunch theorems, and that our pilot therefore tells us nothing at all. I pushed back: as I understand it, the no-free-lunch results say that averaged over all possible problem instances every algorithm does the same, which is a claim about an average and not about our particular institution, and there is an obvious difference between having no reason at all and having the reason we actually have. But I could not shake her reply that three cohorts is exactly as much evidence for projecting past performance as it is for projecting any of the infinitely many other regularities that also fit those three cohorts and diverge afterwards, and that whether the projectible kind here is cohorts so far or cohorts before the admissions reforms is precisely what is in question. Two of my colleagues have taken up exaggerated versions of these positions: our senate's statistics representative says that if all algorithms are equivalent in expectation we should simply buy the cheapest thing, including the counselors' own gut judgment, and the associate provost says three years of results are proof and that asking for more is philosophers moving the goalposts. I think both of them are misusing the same point in opposite directions, and I need the memo to say clearly what is wrong with each.

What I want: first, in no more than a page, work out (a) what the no-free-lunch theorems do and do not establish for a decision about one particular applied problem, given that they concern averages over all possible problem instances, and (b) in what sense a three-cohort track record does and does not give us a reason to expect comparable performance on the next cohort, including the point that many incompatible ways of sorting cohorts fit the data we have and project differently, and that the choice between them cannot itself be settled by that data. Then write the memo itself, about 1,000 to 1,200 words, addressed to the committee with the recommendation at the top: renew, renew under stated conditions, or let the contract lapse. If you recommend conditions, make them concrete enough that a vendor account manager or a dean could act on them and we could verify compliance a year from now, and say something about temporal hold-out validation, pre-registered thresholds and metrics, whether and how to randomize or stage the rollout, a sunset date, an equity audit across the subgroups whose mix is changing, and whether we should treat the counselors' unaided judgment as a competing predictor to be scored rather than as the neutral baseline. Close with an honest paragraph on the strongest thing that can be said for the option you are not recommending: in particular that the symmetry behind the no-free-lunch results undercuts the in-house model and the counselors' intuitions just as much as the vendor's, that we must act either way and doing nothing is itself a policy with its own implicit predictions, that a cheap and non-coercive phone call might be worth making even on a low-precision flag, and that deliberately withholding outreach from a control group in order to get clean measurements raises a problem of its own. Please do not include citations, footnotes, or a reading list; the committee will not check references and I cannot verify them.
