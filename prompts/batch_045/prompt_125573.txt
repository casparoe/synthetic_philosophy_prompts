Hi — I'm doing an MA module on the philosophy of action and I've dug myself into a hole with a paper that's due Thursday, and my supervisor is away until then. I've been arguing about it with a friend (she works on semantics, which is maybe why she keeps going on about embedded questions) and I now can't tell whether I've refuted the propositional view of knowledge-how or whether I've refuted pretty much everything.

Here's how I wrote the argument up.

(1) If S knows how to φ, then S is able to φ: being able to do the thing is part of what the knowing consists in, not just a lucky fact about your body. Call this the grip condition.
(2) Propositional knowledge never has an ability for a condition. For any proposition you like, it's possible to know it while being unable to do anything: someone totally paralysed from the neck down can know that such-and-such wrist movement is a way to steer a bicycle, or that the station is on the corner by the bakery, or that the soufflé is ready. On this picture abilities are what let you aquire knowledge and act on it; they aren't ingredients in it.
(3) So knowing how to φ is not a matter of knowing that w is a way to φ, for any proposition about ways of φ-ing.
(4) A check on (3) that I quite like and haven't come across anywhere: call it the instruction test. Hand someone a complete and true set of propositions describing exactly how to ride a bicycle — every muscle, every correction, every sensory consequence — together with unlimited memory and reasoning power, but no practice. They still can't ride. If knowing-how were knowing a proposition that should be baffling; if it isn't, it's exactly what you'd predict. I also keep using a dissociation pair: the choreographer who can notate the whole ballet but can't dance a step of it, and the dancer who dances it flawlessly and can't notate or even recognise a step on paper.

Then she pointed out that (1) to (3) generalise, and that's the hole.

(5) Take knowing where the station is. The grip condition seems to hold here too: someone who knows the grid reference by heart but couldn't find it, point to it on a map, recognise it, or get there doesn't really know where it is. So the grip condition covers knowing-wh as well.
(6) And (2) covers knowing-wh too, in whatever form is needed.
(7) But knowing where, who, what time is propositional knowledge — knowledge of the true answer to an embedded question — and if it isn't, you lose the unified semantics of wh-clauses and owe us some acquaintance-style or objectual story about those constructions.
(8) So (1) to (3) don't just refute intellectualism about knowledge-how; they refute propositionalism about knowing-wh as well, and by the look of they'd do the same to knowing what a lychee tastes like, knowing a person, knowing a tune by heart. I end up an acquaintance theorist about everything, which I definately didn't sign up for.

So: which step is the rotten one, and what does giving it up cost? I keep being offered the practical mode of presentation reply — roughly, that you can know the very same proposition under a practical guise, and that having the guise is where the ability comes in — but that feels like it just relocates the mystery rather than answering the instruction test, and it seems to make the whole thing an empirical question about psychology rather than one about what knowledge is. If I have to choose, I'd much rather keep (1) and (7) than my own conclusion (3), but when I try to drop (3) I can't see what to say about (4). Could you name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the main alternatives, including what a defender of the propositional view would themselves concede and what they'd dig their heels in about?
