im running the discussion section for intro phil of science on tuesday and we're doing Goodman's new riddle of induction — emeralds, grue (green if you check it before the cutoff, blue if you don't), bleen, the whole business. i want to open with a poem instead of the usual "ok so imagine every emerald is green" routine.

Make it a pantoum: each stanza's second and fourth lines become the next stanza's first and third, and the poem closes on the line it opened with. Five quatrains. The reason i want that form is that the same lines keep coming back but landing in new surroundings, which is kind of the problem itself — one pile of observations, describable under either predicate, and nothing in the observations settles which description to project. So let the repeated lines do that work. One repeating line should be the bit of evidence we all agree on (every emerald anybody has actually checked came out green), and one should be the grue generalization, and when they circle back in the last stanza the reader should feel that piling up more evidence won't break the tie — that whatever breaks it comes from outside the evidence.

Two things i specifically want. First: don't settle it by saying grue is more complicated or less natural-looking. my understanding is that Goodman's own answer runs through entrenchment — which predicates we've actually gone on projecting in the past — rather than through simplicity, and i'd rather the poem nod at that than at "grue is a gerrymandered predicate." Second, here's a wrinkle i made up: in this imaginary lab the field notebooks go back sixty years and the technicians have always logged their finds as "grue" or "bleen"; green and blue were never their basic colour words. So for them it's "green" that's the odd one out. I want the poem to sit inside that without deciding whether they turn out to be vindicated after the cutoff.

After the poem, a short note: where you took liberties or leaned on a contested reading, how confident you are that you've got both the riddle and the entrenchment answer right, and the single line you're least sure about. It should be accurate enough that my prof could read it aloud without wincing, but still work as a poem for people who've never heard of grue.
