hey — I run a first-year seminar and the last session is on Zeno, and every year I end up closing it with some version of "the series converges, so there isn't really a problem," and every year the students are visibly unsatisfied, and I think they're right to be. I'd rather end with a poem this time. Specifically a villanelle in the voice of Thomson's lamp, where the form does the arguing instead of me.

What I want it to do:

- Villanelle, 19 lines, two refrains — but the refrains shouldn't be two rival positions facing off, that's not the interesting part. One refrain should be the switching schedule (on, then off at the half-minute, then on at the half of what's left, all of it packed inside two minutes), and the other should be the question that keeps coming back: what state am I in when the two minutes are up. The point of the villanelle is that the second refrain returns completely unchanged every time the schedule runs through, and it never gets answered.
- The philosophical bit I actually want someone who knows the literature to recognise: at every moment *before* the two minutes are up, the lamp's state is perfectly determinate — ask about any time less than two minutes and there's a flat answer, on or off. It fixes nothing at two minutes. And there is no last switch, so the lamp has no way of reporting a final state at all. I'd also like the poem to get at why the usual "the infinite sum is finite" move doesn't transfer from the runner to the lamp: the walk has an end-state the positions are already heading toward, the lamp's states don't converge on anything. That asymmetry is the thing my students choke on, and I'd rather the poem carry it than explain it.
- Don't resolve it in the closing quatrain, and don't smuggle in a verdict on whether the supertask is genuinely impossible versus whether there's simply no fact of the matter about the lamp at the end. Both readings should still be live at the last line. The ending should feel like the two minutes expiring, not like an answer.
- One extra thing if it fits: Zeno's own book is lost, and we have these paradoxes at all only because Aristotle wrote them down in order to refute them. If the lamp can be aware, somewhere, that its paradox exists only in an opponent's handwriting, I'd love that — but drop it if it feels bolted on.
- It should still work as a poem for someone who's never heard of a supertask: lamps, switches, two minutes, no jargon.

After the poem, give me three or four lines of prose on how confident you are that this is a fair rendering — where you're paraphrasing something contested, where you've invented something for the poem's sake — and single out the one line you're definately least sure a philosopher would sign off on. I'd rather know than have the whole thing sound smooth. It doesnt have to be perfect, just honest about where it's shaky.
