I teach one 75-minute session of a graduate seminar on collective decision-making: 18 students, mixed preparation (roughly half have had a first course in microeconomics, two or three have traded on a prediction market, none has studied futarchy), one room with a projector, and a strong preference for a paper-and-pencil procedure over anything that depends on students having phones or laptops. I want to run futarchy itself as a classroom game: the students should decide something real about the seminar by voting on a welfare metric and then letting conditional prediction markets pick the policy, and only in the debrief should the word futarchy be used and the mechanism placed in its intellectual context.

What I want them to feel, in this order, is first that conditional markets genuinely aggregate dispersed private information better than discussion or a show of hands — the pull of the proposal has to land before it is named — and then that something goes structurally wrong with it. My own suspicion, which I would like the activity to test rather than assume, is that the standard objection (that people who hold contracts paying off on a bad outcome have an incentive to bring that outcome about) is not the deepest problem. The deeper one is that in futarchy the treatment is assigned by the forecast of its own effect: the market is asked for a number that is supposed to be read causally, but the number is also public and is read by the very people whose subsequent effort and cooperation determine the outcome, so the price is a fixed point of a loop rather than an estimate of a policy effect, and the market partly destroys the informational conditions that made its own price informative. I would like the activity to make that loop visible as something the students do and watch happen, not as something I assert, and I would like the debrief to distinguish which moment in the session displayed the sabotage incentive, which displayed reflexivity, and which displayed gaming of the metric itself.

Please give me: the decision and the welfare metric to put to them (I am happy for the metric to be one they can cheaply inflate, such as self-reported comprehension, if that serves the design); the trading mechanism, runnable on paper with 18 novices in one room, including how the two conditional markets are opened and how the verdict is computed from their prices; the assignment of at least one role whose holder observes the market price before taking an action that affects the outcome, together with that role's payoffs; a payoff scheme using points or trivial stakes rather than money; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief, mapping particular moments onto particular features of the mechanism; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover; and an honest note on which feature of real futarchy the session cannot reproduce and which therefore has to be supplied by me in discussion.
