hey — i need a satire piece for a little zine thing i put together with some friends from my political theory seminar, and i want it to be the kind of thing that's funny because the theory is right, not just because someone said "random people lol"

what i want: a parody of a corporate HR welcome packet — the cheerful onboarding document — for a citizen who has just been selected by lottery to serve in the national legislature of a country that abolished elections a while back and runs everything through sortition. call it 800-900 words. format it like a real onboarding doc: numbered sections, bullet points, a bolded "what we ask of you" block, and a sidebar or FAQ near the end. voice is upbeat, caring, faintly threatening HR. totally deadpan — no character ever breaks frame to explain the joke, and nobody gives a speech.

the thing being satirized is the descriptive-representation defense of sortition: the idea that the chamber's authority comes from it being a statistical mirror of the population, plus the deliberative stuff bolted onto it (random draw, then balanced expert briefing dockets, trained moderators, small-group deliberation, and out comes legitimacy), plus the anti-faction pitch (no campaigns, no donors, no re-election, so no one to corrupt you). i want the humor to come from pushing those three commitments, not from making up weaknesses. so: the stratification quotas should get finer and funnier as the packet goes on — income decile, region, renter vs. owner, commute length, chronotype — and the document should treat the fact that the mirror can never quite close as a routine maintenance issue rather than a problem.

a few bits i'd like you to build in:

- something i'll call the Inattentive Quota: a set of reserved seats for citizens who report having spent under ten minutes in the past year thinking about politics, justified straight-faced on the grounds that a chamber that mirrors the people has to mirror how little most people think about it. let this one run a bit — it should feel like the logic arriving somewhere the reader doesn't want to go.
- the Mirror Audit: an annual grade on how closely the chamber matched census distributions, with member bonuses tied to it, and some passing sign that the chamber now spends a chunk of its time managing its own composition instead of legislating.
- for each selected member, a "shadow" — a statistically identical citizen paid the same salary to not serve, whose recorded counterfactual votes get read into the record so the Ministry can isolate the causal effect of deliberation. every so often the shadow votes differently. no one comments on this.
- the regress problem, handled as a feature: the variables used to stratify the draw are themselves chosen by a committee chosen by lot, using variables chosen by another such committee, "democracy all the way down," and the governing file is now 4,000 pages long.
- declining service is your right, but decliners are replaced by redrawing, which means the seated pool is "statistically somewhat more willing than you are" — presented as reassuring.
- the competence worry answered in pure HR: no prior experience required, prior experience is noted and "corrected for" where possible, plus a list of training module titles.
- and the no-re-election thing: there is genuinely no one to impress, yet there's still an Influence Disclosure form where you log everyone you spoke to, and it slowly becomes clear that influence now flows through whoever drafts the briefing dockets and trains the moderators.

one more: the assemblies are single-issue and time-limited, no body coordinates across them, and a thing that should obviously be connected to something else quietly dies because the relevant remit expired.

rules: don't name any real political theorists, living or dead, and don't footnote anything. don't let any character state the standard objections (who designs the lot, whether mirroring is even the right ideal, whether deliberation just launders the randomness, whether competence is smuggled back in through staff) — someone who reads it should feel all of them anyway. keep it readable for a friend who's never cracked a political theory book. and please dont let the tone slip into outright dystopia; it should stay warm and bureaucratic the whole way through.
