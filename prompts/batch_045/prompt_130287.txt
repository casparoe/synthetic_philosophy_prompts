Got a first-year seminar on causation next term and I keep tying myself in knots about omissions, so help me out before I have to stand in front of thirty people and pretend I know.

Here are my gut verdicts:

1. Lifeguard on duty is on her phone, toddler drowns in three feet of water two metres away, she'd have had him out in five seconds. Her not getting up caused the death. Not just 'she's blameworthy' — really caused it.
2. A sunbather twenty metres away, also could have waded out in time, no relation to the family, no job, no promise. His sitting there did not cause the death. (Same counterfactual shape as 1, and that bugs me.)
3. I promise to water your tomato plant, I forget, it dies. I caused it. Though somehow less than if I'd poured salt on it, and I also caused there to be no tomatoes, and I can't tell if that second claim is even the same kind of claim.
4. A stranger who has never heard of you or your plant also doesn't water it. Obviously not a cause.
5. Your plant would have died anyway — the flat flooded. My not watering didn't cause the death.
6. Automated rinse cycle at a chip fab silently skips a step because of a firmware bug, whole wafer batch is ruined. The skipped rinse caused the ruin, and there is no agent, no promise, no duty anywhere in that story.
7. Nurse is meant to give a painkiller at midnight and gives it at 2am. Patient dies at 4am and would have died at 4am either way. Her delay didn't cause the death, but it did cause two hours of agony. Are those two verdicts even in the same line of business?
8. Ten thousand people see the appeal, nobody sends five pounds, the kid dies. I don't want to say each of them caused the death, I don't want to say nobody did, and I go back and forth on whether the group's omission did.
9. Frank was never ever going to come to the faculty meeting (books a fishing trip every year without fail). His absence still sinks the quorum. Did his not showing cause the resolution to fail? I lean yes but it feels like cheating.

My working principle is roughly: an omission causes an outcome when the outcome depends on the not-doing and the non-doer was the sort of thing that was supposed to be doing it — job, promise, mechanism, whatever the relevant 'supposed to' is. That handles 1 through 7, I think, and does nothing for 8.

Can you give me one principle, or two at most, that gets as many of these as it can? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it says about a pair I keep dithering over: the gardener who withholds water out of spite versus the one who forgets because he's hallucinating with a fever. And be explicit about what would change your mind about the principle, and where you think it's most likely to break.
