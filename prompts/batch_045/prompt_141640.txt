hey — you're a professional philosopher of science who specializes in Bayesian confirmation theory (the real stuff, not the pop-Silver version), and you've also done a two-year stint embedded with an evidence-assessment team, so you know how this kind of thing actually gets used when the clock is running. Before you write anything final, take a pass at brainstorming: list the distinctions from the old-evidence literature that you think could survive being turned into a checklist, and the ones that are hopeless in practice, and say briefly why you're dropping each. Then give me the actual procedure, and think carefully about the hard cases rather than sketching them.

Context: I run a small evidence-assessment unit at a large medical research funder. Twice a year we re-score each funded programme's central mechanistic hypothesis H against the whole evidence base, and we report a log likelihood ratio (we use decibels, we call them "credits") that rolls up into a confidence rating the renewal panel sees. My team is six people, no philosophers among them, and they get about half a day per programme. The thing that keeps blowing up our meetings: most of the evidence we want to count was already known — sometimes for decades — before anyone wrote H down in its current form. Taken literally, if E is already in the background knowledge then its probability is 1, the ratio is 1, zero credits, done. But that's obviously wrong sometimes (a theory that quietly explains away a long-standing anomaly looks like it earns a lot) and obviously right sometimes (a mechanism invented to fit a famous dataset that everyone already had open on their desk earns nothing). I need a procedure that separates those two, definately.

What I want:

- An ordered set of steps my team can run on each (H, E) pair in about 40 minutes, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and what to write down when we can't get a number.
- Where the procedure says stop, zero credits, and the reason it stops there.
- Where it says this is a judgment call, escalate to the panel — instead of pretending to be mechanical. Be explicit about which steps are judgment in a lab coat.
- Real handling of: (i) evidence that was used to construct H vs. merely known before H; (ii) H entailing E only with help from auxiliaries that were themselves adjusted along the way; (iii) evidence that was an open anomaly for the previous theory vs. evidence that everyone already had a decent explanation for; (iv) the "data is old but the quantity is new" case — re-analyses nobody performed until H told them what to compute; (v) how to avoid counting the same evidence twice, once as motivation for H and again as a prediction of H; (vi) what to do when nobody can say what H would have predicted if E had come out otherwise, including whether it's legitimate to fall back on "would the field have been surprised?"

Then run it on three cases:

1. A calibration case from outside medicine: a new theory of gravitation from the 1910s that nobody built in order to explain the odd behaviour of Mercury's orbit, but which gets the anomalous part right. Does it get credits, on which step, and how many does it deserve compared to a genuine novel prediction? Then: someone later proposes a rival explanation that soaks up part of the anomaly. Do the credits come back out, and if so, when — at the moment the rival is proposed, or only once it's tested?
2. Our problem child. Programme B's hypothesis is that a specific gut-bacteria-derived metabolite drives insulin resistance. Its strongest support is a widely-used cohort dataset; the metabolite was picked, out of a panel of dozens of candidates, by seeing which one tracked the outcome in that dataset. The team loves this programme and I expect your procedure to tell me something I don't want to hear. Say it plainly, don't soften it.
3. A retrodiction case: an old dataset, and a quantity nobody had ever computed from it. H pins that quantity down to a fairly narrow range, and when someone finally computes it, it lands in the range. Old evidence or new prediction? Where does your procedure land, and what single change to the facts would flip the answer?

Finally, a short section on what this procedure can't do: where it will misfire even when my team runs it correctly, which disagreements it settles by fiat rather than argument, and what I should tell the panel so they don't read the credits as more precise than they are.
