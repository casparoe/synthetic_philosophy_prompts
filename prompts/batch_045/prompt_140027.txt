I'm writing a novel — my third, but the first two were kitchen-sink realism and this one is set in a valley where a family runs a small, slightly grim boarding school, and basically the whole book is two characters arguing about how children should be raised. One of them, Ines, has inherited the place and runs it on what she calls the unweeded-garden principle: you more or less leave the kids alone until they're nine or ten, because (her words) the grain is already in them and everything you do to it before then is just trampling the shoots. Her brother-in-law wants bells, timetables, uniforms, memorisation. I don't want the book resting on a garbled version of a philosophy I did one module on as a first-year — all I actually remember is a child and a well and something about trees on a mountain — so I did a bit of reasearch and the one book I could get hold of, a used paperback intro to Chinese philosophy, sums the debate up more or less like this:

"Mencius says human nature is good. His evidence is the famous example of the man who suddenly sees a child about to topple into a well: before he can calculate anything at all he feels a jolt of alarm and compassion, which proves that we are naturally altruistic — the moral sense is simply there at birth, and education can only water it, never plant anything new. Xunzi, a later Confucian, says more or less the mirror image: our inborn nature is greedy, envious and disorderly, and everything good in us is an artificial product, drilled in from the outside by teachers, ritual and habit, working against our spontaneous desires. So Mencius is the philosopher of nature and Xunzi the philosopher of nurture, and the two of them are the extreme poles of the tradition. The only thing they share is the assumption that what matters is not what you're born with but what gets made of you." And a page or two later the chapter concludes that since the tradition eventually sided with Xunzi, Chinese education became a matter of rote learning and examinations, and the Mencian line had to be revived much later by the neo-Confucians as a kind of protest.

The trouble is it reads as both plausible and too neat, and I can't tell where the seams are. I'm also inclined to believe Xunzi is obviously the more realistic of the two, which makes me suspicious of myself. Could you go through that paragraph and sort it out for me: which bits would someone who actually works on this let pass, which sound right but are subtly off (a conflation, an overstatement, a technical term used in its everyday sense), and which are just wrong? The two I care about most are whether it's fair to say Mencius's point is that we're naturally altruistic, and whether it's fair to say Xunzi thinks virtue is something imposed from outside against our own inclinations. And the bit that really matters for the book: if you cleaned up all the distortions, is there still a genuine disagreement left, or do the two turn out to be answering different questions — because if Ines and her brother-in-law aren't really disagreeing about anything I need to know that now. Does that last bit about rote learning and the exams follow from anything true in the summary, or is it a non sequitur the author slipped in?
