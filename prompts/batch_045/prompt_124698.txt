So I'm a novelist (midlist, crime-adjacent stuff, don't judge) and I've been reading Sor Juana's Reply to Sor Filotea for something I'm poking at, and now I keep seeing my own work life in it, which is annoying.

A private girls' school near me asked me to run a six-week creative writing thing for their sixth formers. I said yes. Then the head of english emailed me the ground rules and I've been sitting on them for a week.

Rule one: no male writers in the programme. Not because of any written policy — it's just that a few years ago a male poet did a workshop, there was some business with a sixteen-year-old emailing him afterwards, parents complained, and now they quietly don't book men. I get the worry. But it also means a girl who wants to write, I dunno, war stuff has to learn it from me.

Rule two: the girls can't write about their own families, their bodies, or religion in the workshops. Last year someone wrote a piece about her mother's drinking, it got read out loud, parents went nuclear. So now it's genre material only — invented worlds, nothing confessional.

Here's what I can't sort out. My gut says rule one is the one Sor Juana would have gone after, and rule two is different, but I can't say why the difference matters or which way it definately cuts. As far as I remember her move is basically: if the danger is in who's standing in the room, change who's standing in the room — have older women teach the girls — don't take the learning away from the girls. But rule two isn't about who's in the room, it's about what ends up on the page. And still, it feels like the school is protecting itself by shrinking what the girls get to do.

So: are these the same argument in different clothes, or genuinely different? If different, which one should I push back on, and on what grounds? Does it change anything that I'm the one who benefits from rule one — I got the gig partly because I'm not a man? And if they won't budge on either, is taking the job still the right call, or am I just lending my name to it?
