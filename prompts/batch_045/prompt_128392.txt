Hey — pretend you're a professional political philosopher who's taught the freedom literature to first-years for about fifteen years, and think carefully here: I want you to brainstorm the logical space first and only then commit to a structure, because the obvious three-way summary is exactly what I don't need.

I'm putting together a handout for a first-year seminar — a "which kind of freedom do you actually care about?" decision tree. The setup is that my students all say they're in favour of freedom in the abstract and then come apart completely the moment I give them a case, and I want the handout to sort them by what they say about cases rather than by which label they've half-remembered. Some constraints:

- Every branch node has to be phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim that someone who has never heard the words "negative", "positive" or "republican" could still answer. No node may name a theorist, a book, or an -ism.
- Build it from the questions, not from the list of positions: give me the three or four diagnostic questions that really do the carving, then show which cells the familiar views land in, then tell me which combinations of answers are empty and why (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unfashionable?).
- Leaves have to include at least: the view that only actual interference by other people reduces your freedom; the view that being exposed to someone else's arbitrary power reduces it even when they never exercise it; the view that freedom is a kind of self-mastery, so internal obstacles count; and the view that what matters is what you're actually able to do and be, not whether anyone is blocking you. If you think one of those is really two views, split it, and if some cell deserves a view nobody has settled a name for, invent a descriptive label and mark it as unnamed.
- These cases must all be used, and each should be doing real work: the kindly slaveholder who never interferes; the wheelchair user and the town hall with no ramp; the prisoner whose cell door is unlocked and who doesn't know it; the addict who wants to stop versus the addict who doesn't want to stop; the worker whose hours are set by a scheduling system that could reassign or fire her at any moment but in practice leaves her alone; the citizen outvoted on a law she voted against, under a procedure she endorses; the cult member who sincerely reports being happy; the person no one is stopping from buying bread, who simply has no money; and someone threatened with a gun that is secretly jammed and never fired.
- For each leaf: one line stating the view, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and the single case from my list that it handles least comfortably.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, and tell me what further case would pry them apart. Also tell me if any two of my cases are doing the same work and which one I should cut.

Aim for roughly eight branch questions and five to seven leaves. Definately don't pad it with history-of-ideas background — my students won't read that part anyway.
