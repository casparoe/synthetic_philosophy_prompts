I want a map of the exposure of a position I have come to rely on in my own work, and I would rather you try to break it than help me defend it.

The position, stated carefully. Take the no-free-lunch results of the sort that appeared in the mid-1990s for supervised learning and for search: fix a finite input space, average a learner's error on unseen points uniformly over every possible target function on that space, and every learning algorithm does exactly as well as every other; the same holds for optimizers averaged uniformly over every possible cost function. My claim is that these results do real philosophical work on the problem of induction, and specifically that they *strengthen* it rather than restate it. Hume's worry, as I read it, is that induction cannot be justified deductively. The no-free-lunch results add something further: no inductive bias is better than any other in general, so the only thing that could favour simplicity, smoothness, locality, or uniformity is a contingent match between that bias and the actual world — and establishing that match is itself an inductive task, which requires a bias to get started. On this reading induction lacks not a deduction but a bias-independent reason for having the biases we have, and the circularity is structural rather than merely skeptical. Call this the *no justificatory lunch* claim; I want to distinguish it sharply from the near-trivial claim that no method works everywhere, which I will call the *no engineering lunch* claim, since I suspect the position is sometimes defended by sliding between them.

What I want from you is what would count against the no justificatory lunch claim, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ranking argued for rather than asserted. In particular:

Say first which of three problems you take the claim to be about — the classic demand for a deductive justification of a uniformity principle, the problem of which prior or inductive bias to adopt, or the problem of whether our methods track the truth at all — and tell me plainly if you think the claim equivocates between them, and whether answering for one version leaves the others untouched. That is the failure mode I am most worried about and I would rather hear it than get a compliant answer to a muddled question.

Then work through the defeaters. Here are the ones I can see, which you should treat as candidates to be assessed and re-ranked, not as a checklist to fill in:

The uniform average over target functions is dominated by targets with essentially no structure at all, since almost every function on a large input space is algorithmically random. So the theorem may describe a space almost none of which resembles any world we could learn about. I am personally drawn to this objection, but I suspect it is only awkward, because my claim concerns what could *justify* a bias, not how well the bias performs, and a defender can concede the representativeness point entirely while keeping the claim.

Relatedly, there are restricted-lunch results: confine the average to targets admitting short descriptions, or otherwise weight the space in a structure-favouring way, and stable preferences among learners reappear, sometimes robustly so. Does the existence of such results, or their degree, count against the claim, given that choosing the restriction is itself an act of bias-commitment? Is the defender's reply that this merely relocates the arbitrariness a real answer or a degeneration into the platitude that every bias needs a bias?

There is also, I think, an internal worry I have not got straight: the move from an average over target functions to a conclusion about justification seems to require something like a principle of indifference over possible worlds, and such a principle would be exactly the kind of unearned a priori commitment the claim says we cannot have. Develop this if it is sound, and kill it if it is not.

Finally, consider what a defender should concede: is there any *positive* result that would defeat the claim — for instance a dominance result for a universal prior, which for any computable target does nearly as well as the best computable prior for that target, up to a constant depending on the target's description length, or some convergence or Dutch-book style argument that singles out one bias without circularity? Does such a result settle anything, or do its own assumptions — that the world is computable, that the choice of reference machine is arbitrary up to a constant, that dominance says nothing about rates — give the defender everything they need?

Two standing requirements. First, tell me which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the claim, as opposed to what only a critic would say; I do not want a list of objections the other side has already answered. Second, give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into something no possible finding could touch, and if it has, whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what a claim about the limits of justification should look like. Mark your confidence on each ranking, flag any place where my framing rests on a confusion, and do not cite or recommend reading — I do not need the literature, and I want the argument to stand on what can be said here.
