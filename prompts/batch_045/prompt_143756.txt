I TA an undergrad decision theory course and we finally got to Arrow last week, and a few of us stayed after class while one of the econ majors went up to the board and wrote out an argument I've been chewing on ever since without finding the exit. He isn't just saying voting is a mess — his claim is that Arrow shows weighing reasons *at all* is incoherent, including inside a single head, and then he ran the same argument on a person over time and concluded that I can't have a coherent ranking of my own options across a week unless one of my time-slices is the dictator, which is where I got off the bus. As I reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) Whenever you rank some options "all things considered" using more than one consideration — or more than one advisor, or your own Monday/Tuesday/Wednesday selves — what you are doing is aggregating: the input is one ranking of the options per consideration, and the output is one complete, transitive ranking. That is just what "on balance" means: the parts come apart, the whole is a function of them.

(2) So any such deliberation is a social welfare function in Arrow's sense, and Arrow's theorem applies to it: with three or more options and at least two input rankings, no rule gives you all of (a) Pareto — if every input ranks X above Y then the output does too, (b) IIA — the output's ranking of X against Y depends only on where each input puts X relative to Y, and (c) non-dictatorship — no single input whose strict preference always carries the day.

(3) So every all-things-considered ranking of three or more options violates (a), violates (b), or is dictatorial.

(4) Violating (a) is flat irrationality: every one of your reasons points one way and you go the other.

(5) Violating (b) is flat irrationality too: your preference between X and Y gets moved by facts about a third option Z that you are not choosing — whether Z is on the menu at all, whether some candidate dropped out. That is what the I in IIA is there for. A rule that flips X and Y when Z appears or disappears is responding to something other than the reasons.

(6) So the only way to be sane is dictatorship: one consideration always gets its way. But that isn't weighing, that's just having one reason.

(7) So there is no such thing as weighing reasons. Every apparent case is either one consideration silently dictating or an irrational sensitivity to what else happens to be on the menu.

The case that makes me suspicious of (5): I was actually deciding between two postdoc offers, call them X and Y, plus a third thing Z I'd almost certainly turn down, on five criteria (pay, fit, city, advisor, teaching). On three criteria the order was X above Y above Z; on the remaining two it was Y above Z above X. Head to head X beats Y 3–2, but under a Borda count Y beats Z on all five while X only beats Z on three, so Y comes out ahead overall — delete Z from consideration and X wins again. And I don't experience that flip as irrational. It looks to me like Z's position is telling me something about how *wide* Y's two wins are compared with X's three, information I just don't have in the two-option menu. But I also don't want to end up saying that spoiler candidates in a real election are fine, so I'm not sure I can afford to drop (5).

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) than (5) — I don't want the moral to be that all-things-considered judgment isn't even a function of the reasons — but I want to know whether the argument is valid in the first place, and (2) is the part I trust least: doesn't Arrow secretly build in that the inputs carry only ordinal, non-comparable information, so that the theorem says less about "weighing" than my friend thinks, and aren't the input profiles in the intrapersonal case restricted anyway, so that unrestricted domain fails? Someone else suggested we dodge by outputting just a winner rather than a full ranking; he said you can reconstruct a ranking from choices regardless, which I doubt. Tell me the step, why it looked fine, and the price of each alternative — and finish with one line in the form \answer{(n) short-label}, e.g. \answer{(5) equivocation on irrelevance}.
