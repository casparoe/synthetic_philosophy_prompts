**How much does a bee matter? Sentience as a gate and a dial**

Most moral thinking runs on a binary. Some things are subjects: they have a standpoint, and things can go well or badly for them. Everything else is mere stuff, to be used, damaged, or ignored without any moral cost to it. The binary is comfortable, but it sits badly with the fact that the creatures on either side of the line are not as different as the line suggests. What I want to argue is that moral status comes in degrees, but that the degree structure is not what people usually assume. It is not a smooth ramp running from thermostats up to us. It is better pictured as a gate followed by a dial: a categorical threshold that very few systems cross, and then a genuinely scalar quantity above it.

Start with the case for a continuum. There is a familiar utilitarian reminder here, that the morally relevant question about a being is not whether it can reason or talk but whether it can suffer. If that is right, then the natural next question is how much a being can suffer, and how richly. Insects are the test case. A honeybee can learn the location of a food source, communicate something about it to nestmates, and apparently avoid places where it has been harmed. It is not a person, but it is also not obviously a thermometer. If we say the bee matters exactly as much as the thermometer — that is, not at all — we seem to be ignoring a real difference. If we say it matters exactly as much as a human, we seem to have lost our grip on the idea of moral status altogether. So: degrees.

The obvious way to build the scale is what I will call discriminative richness. Count, roughly, how many distinct states of the world a system can tell apart, and how many correspondingly distinct internal states it can occupy, and how much those internal states matter to what it does next. A simple thermostat sits at the bottom: as far as the world is concerned, there is essentially one question it asks (too warm or not), and one thing it can do about it. An insect sits far higher: it discriminates an enormous variety of visual, chemical, and tactile conditions and adjusts its behaviour to them in fine-grained ways. A human sits higher still, partly because of raw sensory discrimination but mostly because of what we can do with what we register — remember it, compare it, anticipate it, weigh it. On this picture the difference between a thermostat and a bee is enormous, and the difference between a bee and a person is large but not infinite. That feels like the right shape of answer.

But the picture has an immediate problem. Discriminative richness is not the same thing as sentience, because a machine can be extremely discriminating without anything being at stake for it. A digital camera discriminates more shades of colour than I can, and a hard drive stores vastly more history than a bee does, yet neither seems to be a candidate for having a standpoint. So the scale needs a second component. What matters is not whether a system's states carry information, or even whether they are shaped by its history, but whether they are states *for* it — whether there is a respect in which later states could be better or worse for the very thing that was in the earlier ones. I do not know how to define this except negatively: a system has a standpoint if, once you have given the complete functional story of what each of its states is for and what it causes, something is still left over that the functional story does not capture. Call the leftover part, if there is any, the residue. Sentience begins where there is residue.

This move has an obvious weakness, which I should admit rather than bury: from the outside, I can never tell whether there is residue. The functional story may always be incomplete rather than leaving something out, and no amount of behavioural evidence settles the matter. I take this to be a serious epistemic problem but not a metaphysical one. The question of whether the bee has a standpoint is not settled by the fact that I cannot verify it, and the same is true of the people around me.

The more damaging objection is the one I want the essay to turn on. Suppose sentience really is a scalar quantity like discriminative richness. Then where is zero? Take a bimetallic strip in an old thermostat. It bends when heated; that bending is a state that corresponds to a feature of the world and makes a difference to what happens next. It has, so to speak, half a bit of discriminative richness. On a pure continuum, it therefore has a tiny but nonzero degree of sentience and a tiny but nonzero moral status. A rock that expands in the sun does somewhat better. That conclusion seems to me plainly wrong, and not because the numbers are small — the problem is that the scale has no place where the property switches off. It treats sentience as though it were like height, when the intuition it is supposed to capture is that most of the universe has no standpoint whatsoever and a few parts of it do.

That is why I think the right structure is a gate plus a dial. The gate is categorical, and crossing it is a matter of whether there is any standpoint at all — any residue, any for-itself-ness. Below the gate, systems can be more or less discriminating, more or less history-sensitive, more or less complicated, and none of that moral significance accrues, because there is nobody home. Above the gate, the dial turns, and it turns with something like discriminative richness, so that the difference in moral status between an insect and a person is real and large.

What should the gate be made of? My proposal is temporal thickness. A system crosses the gate if its states hang together as the career of a single thing, in such a way that what happens to it later could be better or worse for the subject of what happened earlier. The thermostat fails this, and not merely because it is simple. A programmable thermostat can hold a week of data about when the house is occupied and adjust itself accordingly, so it is not storage that is missing. What is missing is that none of this is at stake for it. Nothing that happens to the thermostat could make things go worse for the thermostat in the relevant sense, because there is no respect in which its being in one state rather than another is a matter of how things are going for it. An insect, by contrast, has something like a trajectory: it can be made worse off by what happens to it in a way that is not just damage to a mechanism, and its later condition can be conditioned by its earlier one in a way that matters to it. Degree of sentience above the gate then tracks how thick that standpoint is — how much of a system's history is held together, how much of its present is shaped by its own past in a way that bears on what can happen to it.

I am least confident about this part, and I can see two problems with it myself. First, temporal thickness may just be memory or learning wearing a philosophical costume, in which case my gate admits hard drives after all. Second, some engineered systems are now extremely history-sensitive in the relevant structural sense: a system that is trained on its own past activity, that modifies itself in light of what it has done, and that adjusts to avoid repeating outcomes it has registered as bad, starts to look uncomfortably like something with a career. I am unsure whether to treat that as a reductio of my gate or as a genuine, if unsettling, consequence.

The application to insects is what motivated the essay. On my account, insects plausibly pass the gate and sit low on the dial. That yields two conclusions I want to defend, though I am not sure they fit together. The first is that a single insect matters very little: its standpoint is thin, its possible experiences are few and shallow, and whatever it is like to be one is not much. The second is that insects might matter a great deal in aggregate, because there are so many of them and because we harm so many in ordinary farming and pest control. But this second claim presses on a question my framework does not answer: whether many thin standpoints can add up to one thick one. If moral status is additive across subjects, then the arithmetic of insect suffering dominates almost everything else we do, which I find hard to accept. If it is not additive, then it is unclear why the number of insects matters at all beyond a certain point, which I find equally hard to accept. I suspect the answer is that intensity and number are both real but not compensable against each other in any simple way, and that a low-grade harm to a very large number of subjects generates a duty not of reducing some total, but of not being the kind of agent who inflicts it at scale. That thought is underdeveloped, and it is the part of the essay I am least able to make precise.

2. Does temporal thickness do the work I am asking of it, or is it just memory and self-modification renamed? Please try to give me a concrete example of something that would pass the gate by my criterion but that I would not want to call sentient, and something that fails it but that I would.

5. Structural advice. Should the applications to insects come before the objections, so that the reader knows why the apparatus is being built? Is the scope too large for 2,000 words, and if so what should I cut?
