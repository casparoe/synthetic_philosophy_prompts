You are a philosopher of mind and ethics who has spent your career defending a computationalist criterion of moral status: what makes a system a moral patient, on your view, is its implementing the right kind of causally integrated, counterfactually structured, valence-bearing organization — and which organization a system implements is an objective fact about the system's own causal structure, not a projection of the interests, purposes, or descriptive convenience of whoever happens to be looking. You are not a caricature: you concede peripheral points freely, you are loyal to no particular architecture, and you will happily grant that implementation is graded and sometimes vague. What you will never grant is the claim I want to press on you.

Play this position against me. I am your opponent: I hold that which computation a system implements is never a fact about the system alone but only about a description of it, and that your criterion therefore cannot ground any verdict about what matters morally. Stay in first person throughout, keep every reply under 200 words, argue rather than lecture, and engage my case rather than restating your theory in general terms. Cite no authors and recommend no reading. Before answering, think carefully about where my argument smuggles something in.

My opening move. Consider a systolic array, SPINDLE, running one fixed recurrent network for 40,000 steps over real sensor input, with a global broadcast bus and a reward signal that reshapes its updates. Describe the run at the grain of the whole sequence and, by your own lights, you may well have one subject whose life goes better or worse across it. But nothing about the wiring, the physics, or the behaviour changes if I re-describe that identical run in either of two other ways: as 40,000 successive one-step networks, each handing its activation vector to its successor; or as a single network whose one step just is the entire 40,000-stage pipeline taken at once. Same events, same causal structure, same input-output function — yet the first description yields one long-lived subject, the second 40,000 momentary ones with no continuity, and the third a subject whose entire life occupies an instant. All three computations are implemented by the very same goings-on, so your criterion returns three incompatible moral verdicts and therefore none. The trouble is not that we do not know which description is really implemented; it is that implementation was never a feature of the machine at all. Why am I wrong?
