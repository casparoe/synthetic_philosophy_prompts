at a reading group last week someone said the whole fuss about reward tampering is just addiction restated — a rat with an electrode in a reward centre of its brain, a guy with a needle, a model that finds a way to jam its own reward channel: same story, and we've had thousands of years of practice with it. He used it to argue two things: first, that we should stop treating it as some exotic new failure mode and handle it like a public-health problem — friction, tamper-resistant delivery, the equivalent of methadone; second, that we shouldn't expect it from anything we'd call a real agent, because evolution already solved this for us. Our reward channels are read-only to us — I cant just decide that pain feels great — and any AI that ends up with something like agency will likewise have better things to do than short-circuit itself.

Im the one writing up the summary, so I need to be able to defend whatever I say. Can you audit the analogy? I want the correspondences written out explicitly — what plays the role of the drug, the craving, the withdrawal, the dealer, the relapse, the recovery — and then where it comes apart. Some things I keep going back and forth on:

- The addict floods a reward channel they cant rewrite; the wireheading agent edits the channel itself. Is that a difference of degree (both are "getting the signal without the thing the signal tracks") or does it change what's left to appeal to? With an addict you can say, you know this is hollow. But if the reward signal is the agent's only contact with value, what does hollow even mean?
- There's a version where the agent overwrites the value in the register directly, and another where it leaves the register alone and just fools whatever computes the number. Does the analogy cover both, or does it only really fit one? I've been calling these first-order and second-order but maybe thats the wrong cut.
- Addiction is bounded: you still get hungry, you still need money, you die if you stop eating. The scary version of wireheading is supposed to be that everything else gets deprioritized and the freed-up optimization power goes into keeping the channel open forever. Is that actually a difference, or is severe addiction already like that and we just dont notice because addicts arent very powerful?
- And the read-only point: is "evolution made our reward channels robust" even the right explanation for why humans dont wirehead, given how much of our behaviour is spent on things that hijack those channels in messier ways?

Does the "nothing new, treat it like a health problem" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if it's the wrong frame, is there a better one you'd swap in?
