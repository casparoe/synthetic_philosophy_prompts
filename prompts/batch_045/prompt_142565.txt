I'm a retired astronomer — thirty-odd years, mostly photometry and far too much committee work — and since I packed it in I've been going to a philosophy reading group down at the library. We're crawling through Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations. Last week we hit the well-known passage about games: go and look, don't think, and you won't find one thing common to all of them, just similarities and relationships that overlap and criss-cross, the way build and features and temper do in a family. I take the point, or I think I do.

Then someone mentioned Pluto, and I made the mistake of saying out loud that the vote that demoted it wasn't simply a matter of taste — that all those icy bodies turning up beyond Neptune was a real empirical reason to rethink the category, and that some ways of drawing the line are better than others. A philosophy grad student who comes to the group said, "obvously planet is just a family resemblance concept, there's no essence, so there was no fact of the matter, the whole fuss was pointless — it was only ever about how we agreed to talk." Somebody else chimed in with "don't think, look!", everyone chuckled, and we moved on. I've been chewing on it since and I know something is off, but I can't put my finger on it.

So: can you lay out the different precise things "X is a family resemblance concept" can be claiming, from the near-trivial to the wild? Here's my own clumsy attempt at splitting it up, which you should feel free to fix or replace:

(i) no single feature shared by every member;
(ii) no set of necessary-and-sufficient conditions;
(iii) no feature shared by all and only the members;
(iv) genuine borderline cases, the boundary is fuzzy;
(v) nothing deeper that makes the members members — no underlying nature, not a real kind;
(vi) no fact of the matter at all about what belongs, it's up to us;
(vii) the concept is defective and we'd do better without it;
(viii) disputes about it are merely verbal, settled by stipulation.

For each reading say whether the observation about games actually supports it, whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's false — and, the bit I actually care about, which reading (if any) would really get her to the conclusion that the Pluto business was pointless. My hunch is that a family-resemblance grouping can be perfectly objective, since the overlapping similarities can be real features of things in the world and not matters of opinion, so (vi) doesn't come along for free — but I'd like that spelled out.

Two more things while you're at it. First, is family resemblance even incompatible with there being an essence? Something could have a hidden shared nature whose surface symptoms are a mess of criss-crossing similarities, surely. Second, I suspect "planet" is the wrong sort of example for her argument: a category we fix by stipulation for our purposes and a natural kind we can turn out to be wrong about look like quite different diagnoses, and I think she's sliding between them. Tell me whether that distinction, rather than family resemblance, is doing the real work here.

Give it to me as a menu I can bring to the group — short verdict on each reading, no bibliography, I'm not about to start citing things.
