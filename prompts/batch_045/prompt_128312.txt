I am a third-year PhD student in the history of science, working on early modern natural philosophy, and I am writing the historiography chapter of my prospectus. A friend of mine, a historian of cartography who has a manuscript on how period labels get attached to bodies of material evidence, has backed me into an argument I cannot accept and cannot break. I do not need sources or further reading; I have those. I need a diagnosis of the chain of reasoning, and I would be grateful for your help locating the step where it goes wrong and for an account of what each possible repair costs.

My friend's central device is a test she calls the actor's-horizon criterion, which she states as follows:

AHC: A period P with a lower boundary at t marks something real only if a well-informed agent living shortly after t, restricted to the sources available at that time and ignorant of everything later, could have had good reason to judge that a change of the kind in question had occurred at t.

The argument, in the form I have reconstructed it. Steps 1 to 3 are hers; steps 4 and 5 are mine; step 6 is jointly ours; steps 7 and 8 are what she says when I object.

(1) AHC is the correct test for whether a period is real, rather than merely convenient.

(2) Every canonical periodization of European history fails AHC. Nobody in 1500 could have known that they stood at the start of a Renaissance, since the label and the three-part schema it belongs to were supplied later by people who thought of themselves as living after a dark interval. Nobody in 1540 or 1660 could have known that they were inside a Scientific Revolution, since what made the change a revolution was where it ended up. The Middle Ages was named by people who believed they were no longer in it.

(3) So the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not discoveries about the shape of the past. They are retrospective impositions by later schematizers, and the debates about their dates are debates about which impositions suit our purposes.

(4) But AHC does not stop with the big three. My own scattered counts suggest that it also rules out the Industrial Revolution, whose participants lacked both the comparative series and the concept; the Enlightenment, whose canonical dating was fixed long afterwards; the Neolithic; and the Anthropocene.

(5) And the reason it rules these out generalizes. It is not an accident that these periods fail the test: what makes a change historically significant is very often what came after it, and consequences are precisely what an agent at the boundary cannot see. So for nearly every period historians care about, some part of its identity is hindsight-dependent.

(6) Therefore almost nothing that historians call a period is real. The classificatory vocabulary of the discipline is, in the main, a set of useful fictions, and the difference between a good periodization and a bad one is the difference between a useful fiction and an unusable one, not the difference between truth and falsehood.

(7) AHC is not an ad hoc sceptical device. A boundary that no one at the time could have discerned is exactly what you would expect if the boundary were an artifact of the historian's own vantage point, and hindsight-drawn boundaries are exactly the ones that generate the pathologies we already criticize: teleology, the hunt for precursors, the compression of a century of messy practice into a single turning point. Notice too that when you plot different series across the same decades, the breaks do not coincide. In my material the shift in the proportion of natural-philosophy titles committed to Aristotelian matter theory, the shift in where such work was actually funded and done, and the shift in the genre in which evidence is reported each break in a different decade, and several of them break at decade or century edges in a way that makes me suspect the binning of my sources rather than the past.

(8) Nor can you simply reject AHC. If a period can be real even though no contemporary could have discerned it, then what makes it real must include its later consequences, and then the reality of a period becomes revisable by later events. On a counterfactual branch of history in which European mathematical science stalls after 1700, there would have been no Scientific Revolution, and so the question whether there was one could not be settled by anyone living through it, or indeed by us, until it was far too late for the answer to do any historical work.

I am willing to grant a great deal here. I am happy to say that the big three are in worse shape than most historians assume, and I am happy to say that some of what passes for periodization is stipulation. What I cannot accept is step 6 combined with step 8, because I want to keep two claims: first, that for a fixed body of evidence some periodizations are genuinely better than others and not merely more convenient, and second, that the question whether there was a Scientific Revolution has an answer that is not simply a decision.

One complication I should flag before you answer, because it may bear on step 2. I think step 2 may be empirically false for the Renaissance in particular. Some fifteenth- and sixteenth-century actors did describe themselves as recovering something lost and did organize their practice around that description, and by the late seventeenth century there were participants who explicitly styled themselves moderns against the ancients. If acting under a description of that sort is enough to make a period real, then the Renaissance arguably survives AHC and the Scientific Revolution does too, and my friend's argument collapses at step 2. But if it is enough, then it looks like almost any period with a self-aware participants' narrative survives, which is not a distinction I trust either. So I am also unsure whether this rescues anything or merely relocates the problem.

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look sound enough to get into the argument in the first place? Please price the alternatives rather than simply naming one: if I hold on to AHC and accept step 6, what exactly do I lose; if I reject AHC, what do I put in its place, given that step 7's motivation for it is not silly; and if the flaw is in the inference rather than in any premise, show me the equivocation, the scope shift, or the slide and say which reading of which sentence the argument needs in order to be valid and which reading the premises actually support. I would also like your view on the revisability worry in step 8 specifically: it is meant as a reductio, but periods like the First World War and the pre-Columbian era seem to be consequence-dependent in just this way without thereby being unreal, and I cannot tell whether that is a benign analogue that disarms the reductio or a difference that shows the analogue is not really the same case.
