I sit on the Joint Committee on Research Computing, which governs the shared GPU cluster used by six laboratories at our institute. Two of them — the Molecular Design Lab and the Climate Modelling Group — have submitted a joint request to run a six-month pilot that would change the mandate of the autonomous scheduling agents that bid for contested nodes on their behalf.

The background, as the committee has it: each lab currently instructs its agent simply to maximise that lab's own realised throughput. The two agents bid against each other in a daily contention protocol and are, by all accounts, extremely competent at it. The outcome is what the applicants call a wasteful equilibrium — both agents have learned to claim capacity they then release late, with the result that roughly a fifth of contested node-hours are lost to each side. Neither lab can escape this unilaterally, and no one on the committee is able to predict how the agents would behave under any proposed change: they are adaptive systems, and we have no reliable model of which outcome a modified pair of agents would settle on.

The proposed modification has two parts.

First, reciprocal restraint: each agent is instructed that, if the other lab's agent has for that day publicly registered a matching commitment to restraint, then it may not use the over-claim action and may not bid above the honest value of the jobs it actually has ready.

Second, a surrogate objective: each agent's objective function is adjusted so that it also counts, at a weight of 10%, the other lab's wasted node-hours.

The applicants' central argument is that the package is safe in a specific sense: it does not require the committee to predict how the agents will in fact settle, because in every case in which the added instruction changes anything both labs are better off, and in every case in which it changes nothing neither lab is worse off than under the present arrangement. They call this a safe Pareto improvement and ask us to approve the pilot on that basis.

Three complications are on the record. Part two is not conditional: as drafted, each agent carries the 10% weight whether or not the other agent does. The matching commitment in part one is a flag that the agents post themselves; it is not verified against the other agent's actual bidding behaviour, and it can be withdrawn after the day's bids are in. And the version submitted to us contains one asymmetry: the Climate Modelling Group's agents may still over-claim on the last three days of each month, to cover grant-cycle deadlines, which the Molecular Design Lab has told us it accepts.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee ahead of our vote. I need a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — the reasoning that supports it, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side. In the course of that reasoning, please address the following, all of which have come up in our discussion and none of which we have resolved:

whether the two parts stand or fall together, given that they differ in whether their benefit depends on reciprocity;

whether the improvement claim really is independent of predicting how the agents settle, and what the gap between a registered commitment and actual restrained behaviour does to that claim;

whether it matters that the resulting surplus is divided unevenly, and that a lab's interests are themselves an aggregate over principal investigators, postdocs and students — one graduate student has written to say that the current over-claiming is the only way she obtains burst capacity for her overnight runs;

whether our mandate is the welfare of the two applicants or the welfare of the cluster, given that four other labs and the teaching queue draw on the same contested nodes and are not party to the pilot.

Any conditions should come with a time frame and a trigger for suspension. Please keep the memo under nine hundred words and write it so that the two labs could respond to it substantively.
