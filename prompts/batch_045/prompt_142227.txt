Bit of a weird one. I've just taken over as head of people at a ~200-person organisation (officially as of last Monday) and the first thing that lands on my desk is a 'generational inclusion' strategy from a consultancy that wants us to run separate onboarding for Gen Z. At home I've got my stepson, 17, and my dad, 74, both living with me at the mo, so I get both ends of this discourse over breakfast. And I was born in one of those cuspy years that every quiz sorts differently, so I've never really known which box I'm in.

I don't want to bin the document on a hunch, and I don't want to sign it off either. So before I read any more about generations I want to know what I should be demanding of an account of them. Write me the spec: what would a theory have to get right for a sentence like 'Gen Z are like that' to be more than a vibe?

Purpose, so you know what adequacy is relative to here: good enough (a) to decide whether a generational policy at work could ever be coherent, and (b) to say whether cohort talk is tracking something real, or whether its just a polite way of saying 'young people' or 'the economy'.

Here's what I've got so far, some of which I suspect are just one camp's prejudices. Tell me which requirements are genuine, which aren't, and which ones I'm missing entirely:

1. It has to seperate cohort effects from age effects and period effects. Otherwise 'Gen Z are X' might only mean 'people who are 22 are X' or 'people in 2025 are X'.
2. The boundaries have to come from somewhere other than whoever named the thing, and whatever the theory says shouldn't flip if you nudge a cut-off by a year or so.
3. If there's a mechanism behind it, the effect should grade with how much of it you actually got rather than step at a birth-year line. My stepson and his mate born eleven months apart are not different animals.
4. It has to cope with migration. Kid arrives in the UK at 13 from a completely different school system — is he in the cohort his birth year says, or not? And is 'Gen Z' even exportable, or is it an Anglophone thing that goes a bit odd in, say, Poland or Nigeria?
5. It has to cope with within-cohort spread. If one birth year contains wildly diverging lives — class, region, religion, whether your parents had any money — maybe the cohort isn't picking out a shared thing at all.
6. It has to say whether the label is doing any explanatory work, or just sitting on top of the actual causes, which don't line up neatly with year boundaries.
7. It has to deal with the fact that once people believe in generations they start treating each other accordingly, so the categories partly make themselves true. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a requirement or a trap.
8. It has to be clear whether generations are the same sort of thing as family generations — my dad, me, my stepson — or a completely different animal that just happens to share the word.

One more thing I keep tripping over: people throw around 'real kind' as if we all know what that would take. Is there a requirement in there somewhere, or is the whole ontology question a red herring for my purposes?

For each requirement you keep, give me the case behind it and mark it non-negotiable or negotiable. Flag any pair that can't both be satisfied — that's the bit I most want, because I think my list might be asking for something impossible. Tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the ways people actually talk about generations, and add the ones I've missed. Don't skimp on the criteria. And give me a short scorecard too, on four views: cohorts as demographic kinds fixed by birth year and fertility rates; generations as groups with a shared formative history; generations as self-fulfilling human kinds; and the view that there's nothing here but age and period effects. Ta.
