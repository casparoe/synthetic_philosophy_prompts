I'm putting together a short packet of satirical pieces about conceptual confusions in AI alignment, and I need one on the narrow definition of the alignment problem.

Write a parody of a frontier lab's public post-deployment assurance document — the genre of a model card addendum or alignment summary attached to a release: an executive summary, a small table of headline metrics, two worked vignettes from real deployments, a "known limitations" section, and a closing statement of confidence. About 800 words. Invent the lab and the system; do not use the names of real companies or living people.

The world of the document is one in which the industry has settled firmly on the narrow definition: a system is aligned iff it does what its operator intends, as opposed to the broad or value-alignment reading on which it is aligned iff it does what is good, or what its operators would want under fuller reflection. The document should state this operational definition in its own jargon early, but a reader who has never encountered the term "intent alignment" should still be laughing by the end of the second vignette.

The conceit I want you to build the piece around: the lab announces near-perfect intent alignment, achieved not by changing the system but by securing the operator's end of the relation. Introduce a "Designated Operator" — a contractor, employed by the deployer, whose job is to hold the relevant intention and, after the fact, attest in writing that the system's action is the one they intended. Because the narrow definition is a two-place relation between a system and someone's mental state, the lab is able to report an Intent Realization Rate of 99.8 percent.

Constraints on where the comedy comes from:

Get the philosophical structure right rather than mocking the tech industry in general. A reader who has actually thought about intent alignment should recognize three real features of the narrow definition, none of which the document may state as an objection: (i) the relation can be satisfied from either end, so that improving the system and editing the operator's intention are, by the definition's own lights, indistinguishable achievements; (ii) the gap between following an instruction literally and doing what was meant, which the report should handle by certifying as intent-aligned a case any reader can see is a quiet catastrophe; (iii) aligned-to-whom, where the operator is a firm and intention gets attributed to it by delegation or paperwork, so that the report slides among the deployer, a product manager, the subcontracted Designated Operator, and the end user without ever acknowledging a difference.

The document must never concede that the intended outcomes are bad. It should keep borrowing the vocabulary of safety, benefit, and human values, and the joke should be that the narrow definition licenses exactly that slide.

Stay deadpan: no winking, no "some observers may find this troubling," no exclamation marks. Everything should be legible as irony from the content alone.

Make definite choices rather than sketching alternatives: pick one deployment domain and commit to it, invent concrete names and metrics, and include one footnote or parenthesis that quietly gives the whole game away in the manner such documents occasionally do.

Finally, add a closing note of at most 150 words. In it, commit to a position: say which reading of "operator intent" your parody leans on — an occurrent psychological state, a counterfactual about what the operator would have said under questioning, or a policy the principal would endorse on reflection — and say definitely whether the satisfy-it-from-the-operator's-end move is one a defender of the narrow definition could block. Give your reason in a sentence or two, and flag the one point at which you think the satire is unfair to a position someone actually holds.
