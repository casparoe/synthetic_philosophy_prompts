The question she set (20 marks):

"Iterated amplification and distillation (IDA) is often pitched as buying capability and alignment in the same package: break a hard task into easier ones, let human-overseen agents solve those, recombine, then train a fast model to imitate the whole thing and repeat. In the seminar we drew two distinctions: (i) parallel vs serial decomposition — in parallel the sub-questions are independent and only recombined at the top, in serial each sub-answer is fed back in as input to the next stage, so later steps reason from earlier outputs; (ii) behaviour cloning vs process cloning — distillation can train on (question, final answer) pairs only, or on the full consultation tree including the intermediate questions and sub-answers. Using these: what has to hold for the distillation step to preserve the amplified system's competence, and what has to hold for it to preserve its alignment? Which of the two breaks first, and why?"

The answer, as submitted:

"In amplification you start from a human who can query copies of the whole system as sub-agents in natural language and stitch their answers together, so what you get is a tree whose leaves are human judgements. Because each leaf is a human judgement and every internal node is just a recombination of answers that have already been overseen, the whole inherits the alignment of the parts — decomposition is alignment-preserving, so long as no individual sub-task is beyond what a human can check. Distillation is then just supervised learning on (question, amplified answer) pairs, i.e. behaviour cloning: the student reproduces the teacher's outputs, and a model that reproduces overseen output is by definition doing what the overseer wanted, so alignment carries over automatically. What distillation buys you is cost — the tree grows exponentially with depth, so you collapse it into one fast model and use that as the base for the next round. The fragile half is competence, not alignment: imitation is lossy, the student can't fit the teacher on the hardest inputs, and whatever gap there is gets carried into the next round and compounded. Alignment is the easy half, because it's inherited from the human at the leaves and doesn't depend on how well the student fits the teacher. (I'm setting debate and recursive reward modelling aside; different mechanism.)"
