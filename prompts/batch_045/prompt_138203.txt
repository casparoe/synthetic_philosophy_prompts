I am writing a DPhil thesis in bioethics on the determination of death, and I sit as the lay-philosophical member of my teaching hospital's Determination of Death Working Group. The Group has been asked to approve a retrieval protocol it has never considered, and I have been asked to draft the minute. What I need from you is the reasoning rather than any references: an account of which principle the Group has in fact been applying across its own previous rulings, and what that principle requires of it now.

The Group's record, as I read it:

1. It accepted a neurological determination of death in a patient whose heart was still beating and whose body remained warm and perfused, and it permitted organ recovery in that state, minuting that the organism as a whole had died even though its parts had not.
2. Where clinical brainstem testing was equivocal because some neuroendocrine function appeared preserved, it declined to confirm death on clinical grounds alone and required an ancillary study of cerebral perfusion; it confirmed death when that study showed absent intracranial flow.
3. It declined a family's request, made on religious grounds, to disregard a neurological determination, but allowed a fixed short period of continued support so that relatives could gather, and refused to extend it indefinitely.
4. It refused a family's request to declare death in a patient in a permanent vegetative state, on the ground that loss of consciousness is not one of the criteria it recognises.
5. In controlled donation after circulatory death it insists on five minutes of continuous asystole before determination, and it rejected a proposal to accept ninety seconds, minuting that auto-resuscitation must be excluded and that the fact that no one intends to restart the heart is not by itself enough.
6. When a patient had a witnessed arrest, failed cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and was then cannulated for extracorporeal support, the Group refused to record that the patient had died during the arrest: it treated the institution of support as resuscitation and the patient as living on support, whose treatment was later withdrawn.
7. It permitted continued somatic support of a pregnant patient after a neurological determination of death, for foetal benefit, and minuted that the patient was dead and that what was being sustained was a part of her body.
8. It has repeatedly approved ex situ normothermic machine perfusion of retrieved kidneys and livers without treating the reperfusion of an organ as casting doubt on the donor's death.

The new proposal concerns a forty-one-year-old with a devastating brain injury who does not meet neurological criteria, from whom treatment is to be withdrawn. Cannulae are to be placed in the femoral vessels beforehand, and heparin given, while the patient is still alive. After asystole and the Group's usual five-minute interval, death is to be determined by circulatory criteria; an occlusion balloon is then to be inflated so that the cerebral and coronary circulations are excluded, and in-situ normothermic regional perfusion of the abdominal organs is to be run from an extracorporeal circuit. The patient's heart is to remain asystolic and will not be recovered.

What I would like from you: the candidate rules that fit the record as a whole — several do, and they do not agree here; what each predicts both about the declaration of death and about the pre-arrest cannulation and heparinisation; your recommendation for the decision that would best cohere with the Group's own precedents, with the closest analogue identified and the reasons for preferring it; and, for each of the two live options, a sentence or two of wording the Group could minute as the principle it is thereby adopting, together with an honest note of where its precedents simply run out. One member says the sixth item settles the case; another replies that perfusing the abdomen with the brain excluded is not resuscitation; a third says the fifth item does the work; a fourth says the eighth shows reperfusion is not the issue. Please also tell me which part of their disagreement is substantive and which is only about how far the word circulation extends.

Finally: I had thought the case that would tell these rules apart would be a proposal to begin regional perfusion after only ninety seconds of asystole. Does that case actually discriminate between the surviving rules, and is there a better one? And please mark clearly any part of this the Group should refer elsewhere — to the coroner, to the trust's legal advisers, or to the courts — rather than settle for itself. I would be grateful for British English throughout.
