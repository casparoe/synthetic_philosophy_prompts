Grading a pile of decision theory problem sets tonight and want a second opinion on this one, I think I'm being too generous with it.

The question was:

A driver drives a route with two intersections he can't tell apart — no memory of having passed one, no cues, nothing he could condition on that differs between them — so a strategy for him is just one number: the probability p of exiting at an intersection. Exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, driving past both pays 1.

(a) Find the strategy that maximises expected payoff from the standpoint of before he sets off, and give that expected payoff.

(b) Assess the following argument (call it the deviation argument). At an intersection the driver should think he's at the first one with probability 3/5, since he only reaches the second if he continued at the first. So exiting now is worth (2/5)·4 = 8/5, while continuing is worth (3/5)·4 + (2/5)·1 = 14/5. Continuing beats exiting, and 14/5 also beats the 4/3 from (a), so the pre-drive calculation leaves money on the table: under imperfect recall you shouldn't plan at all, you should just maximise at the moment of action.

(c) A classmate objects: the deviation argument can't even be stated, because the driver can't choose to continue here and exit there — the two intersections are the same decision situation, so any "deviation" is really a change of strategy, and p = 1/3 is already the best strategy there is. So there's no conflict between the planning standpoint and the standpoint of the driver at the intersection, and the 3/5 is doing no work. Does this objection settle it?

The student wrote:

(a) Let p be the probability of exiting. Expected payoff = 0·p + 4·p(1−p) + 1·(1−p)² = 1 + 2p − 3p². Derivative 2 − 6p = 0 gives p = 1/3, and the value is 4/3.

(b) The 3/5 is right: he reaches the first intersection for sure and the second only with probability 1 − p = 2/3, so P(first) = 1/(1 + 2/3) = 3/5. And the deviation argument is sound. Exiting now is worth 8/5. If he continues he gets to the second intersection and takes the 4, except in the case where he was already at the second and drives off the end for 1. That gives (3/5)·4 + (2/5)·1 = 14/5, which beats 8/5, so at the intersection he should continue. The lesson is that imperfect recall breaks the planning standpoint: you can strictly beat the ex ante optimum by deciding at the time, so p = 1/3 isn't really the answer to the problem.

(c) I think the classmate is basically right and it does settle it. The deviation argument cheats by holding the rest of the plan fixed while moving only the current decision, which isn't an option the driver has. And once you see that, the 3/5 stops mattering: wether it's 3/5 or 1/2 by the principle of indifference is one of those Sleeping Beauty style disputes about indexical belief, which is an evidential question and not a decision-theoretic one. So the planning standpoint wins by default and there's no real inconsistency left to explain.
