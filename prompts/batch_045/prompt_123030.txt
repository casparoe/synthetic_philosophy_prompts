I am a novelist, and I need a piece of in-world writing to drop into the middle of a book I have been working on for some years: a short story of roughly a thousand words that one of my characters reads, and is unsettled by, and does not fully understand on first reading. I would be grateful for your help with it. I stress that what I want is a story, not a lecture with characters attached; but I also want the story to be conceptually load-bearing, which is why I am going to be pedantic about the setup.

Take the following physics as stipulated — I am not asking you to defend it, only to take it seriously and work out what it would be like from inside.

First: there are two adjacent regions of the world whose entropy gradients run in opposite directions, separated by a moving boundary, the seam. Each side's inhabitants call the direction of increase their future, so no one on either side experiences anything as reversed; each regards the other as the reversed one, and each is, from its own side, right. This is the point I most want the story to make visible without stating: that a thermodynamic reversal is not available as an experience, that the asymmetry shows up nowhere except at the seam, and that even there it shows up as an asymmetry of knowledge rather than of time itself.

Second, and this is the part that is mine rather than the textbooks': a minor character in my novel, a geophysicist named Ilse Rademacher, is supposed to have proposed a rule about what happens at such a seam. Her rule is that a boundary between opposed gradients is never stationary. It drifts toward whichever side has the shallower gradient, at a rate given by the difference between the two, and everything it passes over is re-oriented: the rocks, the records, the people, and, supposedly, what the people can remember. Nobody in my novel knows whether Rademacher is right, and I would like the story to leave that open too.

The consequence I want dramatized is this. Across a seam, an answer can arrive before the question that prompted it. Suppose a correspondence office on the near side of the seam, in its final weeks, because the seam is advancing on it and the office is to be closed. Its clerks send letters across and receive replies that precede them; the office protocol requires that a clerk, having received a reply, write the letter the reply answers, and write it so that it agrees. One clerk has received a reply in which he refuses to write the letter at all.

What I would like from you:

1. The story. Set it in the last weeks of the office at Anvil Flats. Give me a clerk, someone he has been corresponding with, and the specific texture of the work — the registers, the schedules, the etiquette of a correspondence that runs in two directions at once. No omniscient narrator explaining the physics; no character who gets it right at length; no one who says the word entropy. I want the reader to work the asymmetry out from what happens. I want the deadline of the advancing seam to matter emotionally, and I want the story to leave genuinely undecided whether the clerk could have written otherwise — undecided, not coy, and without winking at the reader about paradoxes.

2. Then, separately and in no more than two hundred words, a note to me as collaborator: which of the asymmetries in play — the gradient, the asymmetry of records and traces, the asymmetry of deliberation and action — your story treats as the basic one, and where you think the phenomenology I have stipulated is most likely to break down. I would rather know where my premise is thin than have it flattered.
