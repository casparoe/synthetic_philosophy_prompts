A philosopher whose essays I follow defends a broadly Cartesian position on animal minds, and over several years she has published verdicts on a long series of cases. Three cases she has never addressed are now live in the literature she writes about, and I want to know where her view lands on them — but more than the individual answers, I want the rule she is running.

The two tests she appeals to, in her own paraphrase: first, a creature has a mind only if it can use words or other signs to declare its thoughts to others, in the way we do; second, a creature has a mind only if its successes are not confined to a narrow domain, because reason, unlike an organ, is a universal instrument that serves in every kind of circumstance, whereas a mechanism needs a particular arrangement of parts for each particular action.

Her verdicts so far:

- Parrots and border collies trained on hundreds of labels, which produce combinations they were never taught: no mind. The performances are associations shaped by reinforcement, not declarations.
- Great apes taught gestural or lexigram systems, including individuals who combine signs in new ways and pass them on to others: no mind. Nothing they produce, in her reading, could not be explained as learned signaling about a present situation, and none of their signs is about a sign or about their own states.
- Honeybees, whose waggle dance encodes distance and direction: no mind. A symbolic code is not a language, since nothing in it is offered as the expression of a thought.
- A dog that appears to grieve, to anticipate its owner's return, and to mislead a rival about where food is: no mind, and no pain in the sense that matters — although she adds that cruelty to dogs is still wrong, because of what it does to the character of the person who does it.
- A deep reinforcement-learning system that defeats the world champion at Go: no mind, for the obvious reason that it does one thing.
- A human newborn: mind, despite doing nothing that could pass either test.
- A man in a coma after cardiac arrest: mind. She holds that thinking is the essence of a mind, and that a mind attached to a damaged body does not thereby stop thinking, even if nothing shows.

She also insists, in a footnote I found puzzling, that the beast-machine doctrine is a thesis about the explanation of behavior — that everything animals do can be accounted for by the arrangement of their organs — and not the claim that they are insensible in the way a stone is. I do not know whether that is historically right, and I care less about that than about whether it is doing any work in her argument or is just protective coloration.

The three undecided cases:

1. A large language model that produces fluent, novel, and apparently self-referential text, is deployed across an enormous range of verbal tasks, and has no body, no development, and no interests of its own.
2. A woman with complete locked-in syndrome, with no voluntary movement whatsoever, not even of the eyes, who later reports having been conscious and frightened throughout.
3. An octopus that opens novel puzzle boxes, learns by watching another octopus, uses flexible camouflage, and shows something like play, with two-thirds of its neurons in its arms and a lifespan under two years.

For each, I would like the range of rules consistent with her track record, what each of those rules predicts, your best guess at her verdict along with the reasons for preferring it, and a further case of your own design that would most cleanly separate the rules. The particular thing I cannot settle is whether her position has the two tests as the criterion with human beings as their contingent satisfiers, or humanity as the criterion with the tests merely as our evidence for it — and if it is the latter, whether the universal-instrument argument retains any force at all against case 1, or whether it was always doing less work than she thinks. If rules that fit her verdicts diverge on the ethical question too, say where, since that is what I am eventually going to have to act on.
