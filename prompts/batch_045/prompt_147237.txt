I am preparing an application for a one-semester research fellowship and need a two-page proposal, together with a working title and a 150-word abstract. I have a half-formed idea and I want it either sharpened into something fundable, or told honestly that it is a taxonomy in search of an argument. Please do the second thing if that is what it is.

The idea. Lewis's analysis treats a convention as a regularity in a recurrent coordination problem, held in place by common knowledge of the regularity and by each agent's preference to conform conditional on the others' conforming. The later evolutionary and reinforcement-based literature drops the common-knowledge and rationality requirements and explains conventions instead as stable outcomes of a repeated learning process; in doing so it makes recurrence, if anything, more constitutive rather than less. Both families therefore build recurrence into the account from the start, and neither gives independent purchase on what I want to call terminal-instance norms: cases in which a population faces a coordination problem of a wholly familiar type but will never face it again. The last meeting of a committee that is being dissolved. A single irreversible institutional transition, whose rules are set once and never reset. A one-off payment owed to a group that will have no further dealings with the payer. Agents in such cases conspicuously conform, and they offer recognizably convention-flavoured reasons for conforming — that is how we do it, that is what was settled — but there is no future instance for the expectation to be about, and no repetition for a learning dynamic to work on.

What I suspect is load-bearing is that recurrence is not one thing. It can be recurrence of the problem form, recurrence of opportunity for the same agents, or recurrence of the payoff structure. If these can be varied independently, they generate a space of cases in which the common-knowledge account and the dynamical account should be expected to come apart, and the terminal case sits in the corner of that space where both should be most exposed. My tentative claim is that what explains conformity at a terminal instance is not convention at all but normativity borrowed from some other source — an agreement, an authoritative imposition, a precedent one is already bound by — so that a large class of what gets called convention in the norms literature is really a distinct genus that both families of account have misclassified. A rival and more conservative claim is that the Lewisian machinery generalizes cleanly, by replacing the expectation of future conformity with common knowledge of a counterfactual pattern of conformity: agents conform at the terminal instance because they know that, had the situation recurred, the others would have conformed. That reading makes convention a modal rather than a temporal property, and it would preserve the extension of the standard account at the cost of severing its connection to any actual dynamics.
