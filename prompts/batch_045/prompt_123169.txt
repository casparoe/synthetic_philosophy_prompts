Hey — I'm writing the final for my upper-division normative ethics seminar (18 students, mostly juniors and seniors, plus a couple of econ majors who've had game theory and will definately try to turn everything into a utility function). In-class final, 75 minutes, three essays, so this one has to be doable in about 25 minutes. Worth 20 points.

Topic is the aggregation of tiny harms — torture vs. dust specks. We did it once on the discussion board and everyone just restated their gut reaction, so I want a question that makes the gut reaction actually do some work. Basically I want to pry apart three things that I think people quietly run together:

1. Whether the refusal to aggregate is about how big each individual bit of harm is, or about how many people it's spread over. My idea: a case where the total amount of stuff is fixed but the distribution is unknown. A machine will deliver some astronomically huge number of barely-noticeable annoyances (a speck in the eye, a two-second itch), and it's wired either to hand out one apiece to that many different people or to pile the entire load onto a single person. They have to choose between running the machine and one person being tortured for fifty years, and they don't get to know which way it's wired. If they say torture either way, then the number-of-victims thing apparently wasn't doing the work, and I want them to have to own that.

2. Where the line is. If no number of specks outweighs the torture, what about a million people each with a bad headache for an afternoon, or ten thousand people each losing a finger? Push them to either draw a line somewhere and say why there, or go lexical and then deal with the fact that lexical is mostly just a label for the intuition rather than an answer to it.

3. What I think is the real crux: fifty years of torture is itself a very long sequence of momentary pains, each of which on its own is far less bad than the whole. So anyone who won't sum small things across people has to say why summing small things across moments inside one life is any different. And if the answer is because it's the same person, I want them to say what work the boundary of a person is doing there.

I'd like the question to force an actual commitment — pick an option and defend it — not discuss the issues. And nobody in the class has read any of the literature on this, so everything has to be self-contained in the handout: no author names, no papers, no references to the version that circulates online with the number written as a tower of arrows.

Can you give me: the question as I'd actually hand it out, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around maybe five must-hit points? For each point I want how many marks it's worth, the specific move that earns them, and one line on how students typically screw it up. The A/B/C boundary should be about whether they made the move, not about prose quality — tell me what the B answer does that the A doesn't. I also want the model answer to end by being honest about which move in it is the weakest link; I don't want something that reads like the question is settled.

Last thing: what do I do if half the class writes the specks option is fine because nobody is really harmed and stops there? Is there a version of the question that blocks that answer up front, or does blocking it make the question worse?
