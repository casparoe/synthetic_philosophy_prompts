I write novels — four published, and I'm about sixty thousand words into a fifth that's set in a culture I made up — and I've got one of those festival panel things in three weeks where somebody in the audience always asks about guilt and shame, and I always waffle. I'd like to actually have a view, and I'd like it to be the view my own verdicts add up to, if they add up to one.

Here are my verdicts, from scenes I've written or am writing.

A. Hallam, in the last book, siphons money out of a parish burial fund for four years, is never found out, and pays it all back with interest after her mother dies. Nobody ever knows. What she feels in those four years is guilt, it's the right thing to be feeling, and I think it's worth more morally than anything the parish could have done to her if they'd caught her. I also think that after she's paid it back she still owes something she can't pay, and that this is right too, not a glitch.

B. A man in the same book is caught stealing oats or whatever, and what destroys him is standing in the square while people look at him. That's shame. I want to say it's worth less than Hallam's guilt — but I also think it isn't worth nothing, and that it reaches people guilt can't reach. My bullying patriarch in book three doesn't change a single thing about how he behaves until his daughter says, in front of the whole family, that he's the sort of man who does that sort of thing. Twenty years of guilt did nothing for him. So shame does work there that guilt definately can't.

C. A twelve-year-old girl whose father is a torturer for the occupying regime. She's done nothing. I'm clear that guilt is the wrong description and even wrong for her to feel. But I don't think her shame is a fault, and I think a version of her who felt nothing at all would be a worse person.

D. A man who does something genuinely bad and feels only shame and no guilt — no sense of a debt, just exposure — but who stops, confesses, and repairs everything. I keep going back and forth. Part of me says he's got hold of the wrong emotion and is missing the point of it; the rest of me says that if it gets all the right behaviour out of him, in what sense is guilt better?

E. A man who was meant to be watching a child who drowns in a pond while he looks away for thirty seconds. He's wrecked with guilt for years. My verdict is that the guilt is a mistake — not just painful, wrong, and I'd want him to stop — but that someone who felt nothing would be monstrous, and I can't say what he should be feeling instead.

F. In Thurn — the invented place — everyone's wrongdoing is dealt with out in the open, in the square; they've no word for forgive, the nearest is roughly "let it be forgotten"; and offering someone money for a harm is a serious insult, because you can't weigh a person against coins. A beta reader said "so it's a shame culture with extra steps" and I didn't have an answer. My own verdict is that this set-up makes shame do all the work and that something's lost, but I can't say what, and I don't trust my reaction: I've half-remembered the old mid-century anthropology thing about shame cultures and guilt cultures, and I gather it's now reckoned to be a bit of a caricature, so I don't want to lean on it.

What I'd like:

– A principle, stated tightly enough that I could test it against a scene, that sorts these, with a note on which case forces each bit of it. My own stab: guilt is what you feel when you've broken something you could in principle mend, and it aims at the damage; shame is what you feel when you've been seen, or could be seen, as the sort of person who breaks things, and it aims at your standing. Guilt is better because it aims at the right thing. I don't think that survives C and E together, or F — scrupulous guilt about trivia and silence about real harms looks just as arbitrary as shame's tracking of who happens to get seen. So repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts I have to drop and which one is the actually confused one.

– What the repaired thing predicts about cases I haven't listed. Two I care about: a woman who did something bad, was never found out, feels nothing at all, and is frightened by her own not feeling — what is that, on your account? And in Thurn there's a year-long rite at the end of which the wrongdoer's name is simply used again and nothing is said about what occured: what emotion would that actually grow in people?

– Whether "which is the better moral emotion" is even a question your account can answer, or whether it comes out depending on features of the culture — how public wrongs are, whether anyone can leave, whether reputations recover, whether there's any practice of repairing. If it does depend, on which features, and is my Thurn set-up on the wrong side of them?

– And the bit I actually care about for the book: can a culture choose? Not by decree, but could a reformer in Thurn — someone who wanted more guilt about them and less shame — actually bring that about, and what would she have to change? If the answer's no, that a culture gets whatever moral emotions its institutions grow and nobody picks them, I need to know that too, because I've been writing as if somebody could, and the book rather depends on it.

If there's a third thing I'm missing — something that isn't either guilt or shame and does the work I keep awkwardly assigning to both — say so and name it.
