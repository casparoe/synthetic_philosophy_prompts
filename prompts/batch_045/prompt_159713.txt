I am drafting a policy memo for a state government that must choose between two ways of financing eighteen weeks of job-protected parental leave at eighty percent wage replacement. Option A is an employer mandate: each firm funds and administers leave for its own employees. Option B pays the same benefit out of a pooled social-insurance fund financed by a levy on all covered employment. Under both options, six of the eighteen weeks are reserved for the second parent on a non-transferable, use-it-or-lose-it basis.

At our last meeting a member of the committee dismissed the difference between the two options as cosmetic. His argument rested on an analogy: a benefit that government compels employers to provide is nothing but a payroll tax with extra steps. Who is legally obliged to write the check is irrelevant; what matters is who bears the cost once labor markets adjust. On the standard incidence story the cost is shifted into wages, into slower wage growth, or into hiring, and it lands on the group whose labor costs went up. From there he concluded that my two options are the same policy in different costume, and that the mandate is at best a wash and at worst self-defeating, because it reduces the pay or the employment of the very workers it was designed to help.

Audit that analogy for me. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the tax base, the statutory remitter, the economic bearer, the rate, the base erosion, the exemption — and then the places the mapping fails. I have three hesitations of my own, and I want to know whether they actually bite or whether they are already compatible with the tax framing:

- A payroll tax takes cash from everyone in the base; a leave entitlement is a tied purchase whose value to a given worker depends on whether she expects to use it. That makes the incidence question look like a problem about compensating differentials across heterogeneous workers rather than a problem about a uniform tax, and it is unclear to me whether the wash conclusion survives the switch.
- Two components of the package have no analogue on the tax side: job protection, which governs the employment relationship rather than the paycheck, and the non-transferable second-parent quota, which is not a cash cost at all but changes what an employer can infer about any individual woman of childbearing age. A tax is levied on a measured base, not on a group average used as a proxy for an individual.
- The shift may not all land on labor. Some of it may go forward into prices or backward into rents, especially where employers have wage-setting power, which is the case in much of the local labor market the memo concerns.

Then tell me which disanalogies matter for the conclusion he was using the analogy to reach and which are cosmetic; whether Options A and B genuinely come apart under the analogy or collapse together as he claims; and whether the reserved six weeks are a case where the analogy's own logic runs the other way. If there is a better analogy available, say what it is — I have been toying with pooled social insurance on the model of unemployment insurance, and with a labor standard on the model of paid holidays, but I cannot tell whether either of them does work the tax analogy merely appears to do.

One thing about register, please. Separate what follows from the logic of incidence and from price theory alone, which I take to be settled given the assumptions, from what is genuinely contested among labor economists: the realized size of any wage offset, employment and hiring effects, how much wage-setting power employers actually have here, the effects of long versus moderate leave on mothers' careers, and what reserved leave for fathers does to bargaining inside the household and to statistical discrimination in hiring. Do not present any of the contested items as the consensus view. Where your answer depends on an empirical magnitude, say that it does, say what kind of evidence would settle it, and do not supply figures, study names, or citations you cannot stand behind.
