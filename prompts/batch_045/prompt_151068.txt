hey, im in 11th grade and I'm the person who writes the practice exam for our schools philosophy club (we run a mock olympiad thing every spring, so questions need to be hard enough for the kids who are actually serious about this but still answerable in one sitting).

I need one essay question on whether "better than" is transitive — the sorites/spectrum style argument where you walk from one option to a much worse one by steps so small that no individual step looks like a worsening, so you end up with three claims that can't all be true: the first option is better than the last one; every step is (at least) not a worsening; and betterness chains. I do NOT want a question that can be answered by saying "so transitivity fails, the end" — everyone in the club can already do that, and it does nothing to separate the people who get it from the people who've memorised the shape of the argument. The question has to force a real choice about which claim to drop and what dropping it actually costs.

Stuff I want it to do:

- concrete case: a council or a committee picking between versions of something, nothing science-fiction and no trolleys. It has to be self-contained — define every term you use, so someone who's read zero philosophy could answer it.
- they should have to name which claim they're dropping and cost it out against the alternatives. What do you really lose if you give up transitivity: can you still have a best option, can you still choose by comparing pairs, can you still put options on one scale? And what do you lose if you instead say that a difference too small to notice can still flip the verdict?
- i have this idea for a second part: a second committee with the same two endpoints but a much coarser path — four big steps instead of two hundred tiny ones. I think that forces them to say whether their diagnosis depends on how finely you slice the chain, and then either explain why a relation between the options themselves should care about that, or accept that big obvious improvements can fail to chain. Use it if you think it works, fix it if you think it's confused.
- there's also something about "better than" being different from "better by enough that anyone could tell or reasonalbly judge". I'm not sure whether that's a real move or just a way of dodging, so only put it in if it does real work, and say what it leaves unsolved.
- some part of the question should be about what the committee should actually DO: if you keep transitivity vs drop it, does comparing pairs still work, does the order you compare in start to matter, do you have to look at all the options at once instead. That's the bit I think people skip.

What I want from you:

1. the exam question itself, 45 minutes, 20 marks, written the way it would actually appear on the page, with any part (a)/(b) and the split of marks.
2. a model answer of 700-ish words. No citations, no name-dropping philosophers, no reading suggestions — we have no library access and I want an answer a student could produce by reasoning alone.
3. a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, marks for each (20 total), a one-line note on how people usually get each one wrong, and a line on what separates an A answer from a B one — naming the specific moves each has to contain.

Start with the question as your very first line — no "here's a question" or "sure" type intro — and don't restate it before the model answer. The model answer should lead with its verdict in one sentence and then give the reasoning.
