I run a reading group on promissory obligation, and the argument has been going round in circles for a year, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. One camp says the practice does the work: to promise is to invoke a social institution with a point — giving others reliable assurance about one's future conduct — and the obligation is to do what that institution, properly described, requires; whether keeping the promise does anyone any good is beside the point, and the wrong in breaking it is infidelity rather than harm. The other camp says a practice can only ever be a transmission mechanism: what matters is that the promisee has been given a normative power over the promiser's conduct, or that there is something intrinsically bad about being let down by someone one trusted, so a promise no one relies on, or whose keeping benefits no one, raises a quite different question. I am not asking about contracts or legal enforcement.

My hypothesis is that these are not two answers to one question. One camp is asking what the practice we actually have requires of someone who has invoked it; the other is asking what would make such requirements genuine moral requirements rather than conventions. Each side slides between the two, so every exchange stalls: one side produces cases where the practice's rules and morality come apart, the other replies that those are cases where the practice has been misdescribed, and neither point is one the opposition ever denied. Behind that I suspect a further disagreement nobody argues for — about whether an act of will can generate a reason at all.

Test it against a case I think neither side handles well. Call it the disowned promise: before Alf says anything, Bea tells him that she does not accept promises, does not want that kind of claim on him, and will not recognise one if he makes it. Alf promises anyway. Later Bea finds she has relied, is let down, and resents it. Is there a promissory obligation here, and if not, what is left? Now vary one feature: the same disclaimer, but the two have for years relied on each other's bare word, and Bea knows she will rely again.

What kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, a dispute about which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions about the authority of the will? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? I would rather have a distinction that dissolves part of it, or an argument that no convergence should be expected, than another survey of the two positions.
