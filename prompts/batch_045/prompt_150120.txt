I'm in a third-year epistemology seminar, and the problem set asks each of us to construct an original Gettier-style case. I don't want to hand in one of the classics with the nouns changed, and I want the case to do real work in the short paper that accompanies it. The claim I want to defend is that the safety condition is not sufficient: that there can be justified true beliefs which are safe and which nevertheless fail to be knowledge, for some further reason. That means I need a case a safety theorist cannot wave off as merely unsafe, and I would like you to build one to specification.

Constraints on the scenario itself:

1. Realistic and contemporary, drawn from academic or professional life — a lab, a clinic, a field site, a newsroom, an admissions committee. No demons, brains in vats, evil neurosurgeons, or perfect deceivers; no lotteries, fake barns, painted mules, stopped clocks, sheep behind hills, or disjunctions about who owns a Ford. One paragraph, under 160 words, and no editorializing: the scenario should not contain the words know, knowledge, luck, or Gettier, and should not tell the reader what to intuit. The non-knowledge verdict has to fall out of the case, not be stipulated into it.
2. It must be a genuine case of justified true belief: the subject is epistemically blameless, does everything a careful person in that role would do, and would be able to cite their reasons if asked.
3. The belief must be safe. State the target proposition, the basis on which it is held, and what you are holding fixed as the same basis across the modal neighbourhood, then run the check over the nearby worlds.
4. It must not work by way of a false lemma. The subject should infer the proposition from no false premise and hold no false beliefs about their own evidence; the misleading element should be non-doxastic — a misleading experience, an accident of arrangement, a coincidence of timing — rather than a false proposition the subject accepts.
5. The belief-forming method must be reliable in the environment the subject is actually in, at whatever grain you individuate it: coarse and fine. The point is to make the case robust to the generality problem, so that process reliabilism cannot supply the explanation of the failure, and so that the safety verdict in (3) does not depend on an artificially narrow description of the method.
6. The subject's total evidence should make the proposition highly probable, so that the failure cannot be attributed to weak or thin evidence.

After the scenario, I need the audit: verify (3) through (6) explicitly, and run the sensitivity check as well and report what it gives. Then tell me which single structural feature is doing the work, give me the minimal alteration that turns the case into a case of knowledge, and say which of the accounts we have covered — safety, sensitivity, the no-false-lemmas condition, process reliabilism, anti-luck and virtue-theoretic accounts, and the requirement that the belief be true because of the facts that make it true — comes out worst here, together with the strongest objection that account's defender would make to my case. Our teaching fellow is a reliabilist and will say that whatever has gone wrong is a defect of the process, so I would especially like to know what to say to her.

Finally, be straight with me about one thing: constraints (3) through (6) may pull against a robust non-knowledge intuition, since making the truth modally sturdy tends to make the subject look like a knower. If you think they cannot be jointly satisfied, build the closest case you can, identify precisely where it breaks, and argue that the breakdown is principled rather than a failure of imagination — that would itself be worth writing up.
