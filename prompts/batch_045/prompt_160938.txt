Hey — I'm taking a formal epistemology seminar this term and I have to lock in a topic for a ~5000-word term paper by the end of next week, and I keep circling the same thing: whether the "presumption of independence" can actually be formalized, or whether it's several different things that happen to wear one name. My supervisor leans the second way and thinks a paper on it would be a category error, but she also said she'll back whichever angle I can actually defend, so I need to commit to one instead of hedging.

Here's roughly where I've got to. Three candidate formalizations:

(1) Ignorance / max-entropy: independence is what falls out when you maximize entropy subject to knowing only the marginals, so "no reason to think they're correlated" gets cashed out as "no information beyond the marginals." Worries I have: it's relative to how you carve up the space, and it isn't invariant under recoding — X independent of Y doesn't give you X independent of X+Y.

(2) Structural / causal: independence is a claim about the causal graph, the causal Markov condition, screening off; the real "presumption" is then a presumption of causal sparsity, not of ignorance. But it doesn't seem to give me anything when I don't know the graph, which is exactly the case I care about.

(3) Algorithmic: two things are independent when describing both together costs about as much as describing each separately. This one I know almost nothing about formally.

Why I think it matters: I worked out the variance of a sum of n equally-variable quantities with a common pairwise correlation rho and got n·v·(1 + (n−1)rho) versus n·v if you assume independence, so the mistake scales like n·rho — for big aggregations even a correlation too small to ever detect empirically dominates the answer. Independence defaults seem to silently decide whether large conjunctions and aggregates are sane at all. And as I remember it, the Cramér random model for the primes — treat primality of each number as independent with probability about 1/log n — gets a lot right and then gives wrong answers about primes in very short intervals, which looks like the presumption being licensed at one scale and not another. That's the kind of thing I'd want a formalization to explain rather than just describe.

Constraints: about 14 weeks, 5000 words, armchair only — no data, no experiments. I'm fine with probability and Bayes, I did one logic course, but I've never properly done causal inference and I know essentially no algorithmic information theory.
