I'm putting together a small funding program — call it five postdocs for three years, all of it armchair work (logic and philosophy, no empirical component, no field experiments) — and the theme I've been handed is in-principle checkability. The sentences that keep getting said out loud, by people who write policy and by people who build systems, are things like "every safety-relevant fact about a deployed system should be checkable in principle" and "no decision should ever be made that couldn't in principle be audited." Everyone nods along. Fitch's paradox of knowability is the standing reason to think those collapse into "every such fact is already checked." I want to spend the money on the questions that actually decide whether any nontrivial version of those sentences survives, and I want the ordering argued for, not surveyed.

Two hobby-horses of my own, which I want you to be willing to shoot down rather than flatter:

(a) The principle people rely on in practice isn't "we could know p," it's "someone could know p" — some agent with more time, better instruments, a bigger memory, a different sensory suite, maybe an agent we'd have to build first. So index the knowledge operator by agents arranged in a space of cognitive powers, and ask whether the Fitch construction still runs. The case I care about is the diagonal one: the truth that p and no agent anywhere in the hierarchy knows p. My worry is that this is just the old argument with subscripts on it and no new content, and that the only genuinely new question is what happens when better agents can't tell that they're better.

(b) In practice the checking is done by a procedure with a fixed, expressively limited report language — an audit checklist, a proof system, a battery of tests — and the collapse needs the Moore-style sentence (p and it isn't certified that p) to be formulable inside whatever that procedure reports on. If it can't even be stated there, the argument never gets started. My suspicion is that this is where the real content lives, and that it turns into a question about self-reference and expressive strength rather than a question about knowledge at all. Tell me if that's phoney.

Also on the table, and don't feel bound by my list: whether this is a special case of the general argument against luminous conditions, and if so whether the interesting work is in the general result rather than in knowability specifically; the provability analogue, and whether "knowable" is doing any work beyond "verifiable in some formal system"; whether the right move is to give up the closure and factivity style principles or to give up the knowability principle; whether the paradox has any real bite for the anti-realist motivation it was supposed to support, or is a modal-logic curiosity that got dragged into metaphysics; and what changes if resource costs get built into the operator from the start instead of being bolted on afterwards.

Give me a ranking of three or four of these, plus anything you'd add that I haven't thought of, and defend it. The criteria I care about: how much of the rest depends on the answer, whether it's tractable in three years for people who do logic rather than experiments, whether it's neglected relative to how much it matters, and how much the answer would change what a reasonable person should be willing to say out loud in a policy document. Where those criteria pull apart, say so explicitly — I'd much rather hear "this is the most important one and I'd still fund it last because nobody knows how to make progress on it in three years" than a mushy compromise ranking. And tell me what would move things: what result, if somebody produced it in the next couple of years, would make you re-order the list.

Please don't give me a literature survey or a reading list; I can read the encyclopedia entries myself and I don't want to fund a bibliography. I want an agenda with the reasoning that generates it, and I want to know what you'd refuse to fund and why.
