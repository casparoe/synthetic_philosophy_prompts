hey — I'm putting together a section for a talk on whether herding can be a genuine information failure even when every single person involved is reasoning perfectly and nobody is just copying to fit in. I keep building examples that sneak in somthing cheap, so let me hand you the specs and have you build it.

Setup first: a line of people, each making a once-and-for-all choice between two options, one of which is right, and everyone just wants to be right (payoff 1 for matching the true state, 0 otherwise; nobody cares what anyone else does). Each person gets one private hint about which option is right, reliable maybe 60-70% of the time, same reliability for everybody, no experts. Each person in turn sees everything everyone before them chose, then picks. Keep it realistic — a clinic deciding whether to switch to a new treatment, a hiring panel, farmers picking a seed variety, whatever — no oracles, no perfect predictors, and something I could tell a room of non-philosophers without losing them.

Part one, with no intervention: I want a case where every single choice maximizes that person's own expected payoff given what they know, a herd forms anyway, and the herd is wrong — the private hints, added up, would of pointed the right way, but running the decisions in a sequence destroyed that. Walk me through the realized history: what each of the first few people saw, what they chose, and why that was the right call for them personally.

Part two, the intervention. The point I want to make is that the fix isn't more information or better information, it's changing who gets to see what. So: pick one person well after the herd has formed — position eight or whatever, someone who would otherwise have seen the whole history — and keep the history from her, while everyone still sees what she chooses and everyone knows she was kept in the dark. Call her a sealed decider (my term, not from the literature). She gets the same private hint as everyone else: no better, no extra, no payment, no odd preferences, nobody forced to act against their own interest. The only difference is what she's allowed to observe. Show that this one change unravels the wrong herd and gets the group to the right answer with high probability, and say which realization of her hint you're assuming. Then do the accounting honestly: what does the sealed decider give up, what does everyone behind her gain, and roughly how many people have to be behind her before the whole thing comes out ahead.

After that, tell me what's actually doing the work. My guess is that the tie-breaking rule is load-bearing, so state explicitly what your people do when they're exactly indifferent, and tell me whether the case survives if they flip a coin instead. And give me the best objection you can from someone who thinks the whole cascade thing is an artifact of how you set the puzzle up, not a real phenomenon.

Also: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just one continuous piece of prose, the way you'd write an email.
