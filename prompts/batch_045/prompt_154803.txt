I am compiling a dialectical map of the aggregation problem for contractualism, and I want a scored ranking rather than an essay. Please work from the cases as stated, from your own reasoning, without appealing to what any particular author argued.

Set-up. Take contractualism in its reasonable-rejectability form: an act is wrong just in case it is disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject, where each person's objection is weighed by the strength of that person's own complaint and not by summing benefits and burdens across different persons — call this the individualist restriction.

Define pressure as follows: how much a case forces a contractualist either to accept a verdict that is intuitively wrong, or to bolt onto the formula a substantive, contestable theoretical move — a new device for comparing complaints, a new reading of what makes a rejection reasonable, a new account of whose complaints count or when they count — rather than merely restating the formula. Score 0 for a case that puts no pressure on the view at all, 100 for one that by itself breaks it.

I want you to sort the cases along four diagnostic axes, which specify as follows:

- severity gap — how much worse the single person's loss is than each of the many's losses. Assign one of small / medium / large yourself.
- whittleability — yes or no: whether the case can be reached from the "save the one" case by a chain of neighbouring cases, each differing from the last only trivially, so that someone who saves the one at the first step cannot, without arbitrariness, refuse to save the many at the last.
- identification veil — whether the many are named or otherwise identifiable individuals at the time of decision, or only statistical.
- subject-dispersion — whether the losses fall across many distinct persons or across one person over time.

The cases:

C1 — The broadcast case. A man trapped in a transmitter room will suffer several hours of severe but fully recoverable pain unless you cut the power; cutting it deprives forty million people of a live football final they were watching. Nothing else is at stake.

C2 — Triage. You can save one identified stranger from death, or ten thousand identified strangers from a fractured wrist apiece.

C3 — The anonymous cohort. You can save one identified person from death, or prevent ten thousand cases of mild but permanent hearing loss in people who cannot be identified in advance; a statistical model fixes only how many such cases there will be, so no one can now say "I am the one who would have been affected."

C4 — The staircase. You can save one person from death, or spare each of two million people one second of moderate finger numbness. This case sits at the far end of a smooth ladder: save one person from death, or save two people from something very slightly less bad than death, or four from something slightly less bad than that, and so on down, with adjacent rungs intuitively indistinguishable.

C5 — The long self. One person must either die now or endure two million mild headaches spread over the following fifty years. The same individual bears every loss.

C6 — The policy flip. A public-health regulation would spare two million people a mild seasonal allergy, but only by causally imposing, on one identified person, a permanent disabling injury. The one is made worse off by what the regulation does, not left to suffer by what it omits.

C7 — The authorized association. Two million members of a mutual-aid association have each signed, freely and in advance, a standing instruction that trivial losses to themselves may be weighed together in order to avert grave harm to any one of their number. You must decide whether to honor that instruction or instead to avert the grave harm to the one. The authorization is genuine and unanimous.

Deliverables.

First, a table, one row per case, with exactly these columns:

| case_id | severity_gap | whittleable | veil | pressure_score | tier | forced_move | one_sentence_reason |

where pressure_score is an integer from 0 to 100; tier is one of A (forces a new theoretical move), B (answerable, but only at the cost of a refinement the view did not obviously have), C (answerable with resources the view already has), D (not a real problem for this view); forced_move names, in a few words, the substantive move the contractualist is pushed into, or "none"; and one_sentence_reason says why the case lands where it does. Give distinct scores unless you think two cases really are equally pressing, in which case note the tie in the reason.

Second, a consistency requirement. If case X is assigned a severity gap at least as large as Y's, has at least as many beneficiaries (or, where the losses fall on one person, at least as many loss-events), is whittleable whenever Y is, and has beneficiaries no more veiled than Y's, then X's score must be at least Y's. If you break this, you must record the violation explicitly rather than let it pass silently.

Third, after the table, a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "ranking": array of objects, ordered from most to least pressure, each {"case_id": string, "pressure_score": integer, "tier": "A"|"B"|"C"|"D", "forced_move": string}, covering all seven cases.
- "dominance_check": object {"satisfied": boolean, "violations": [ {"pair": string, "explanation": string} ]}, listing any pair that breaks the requirement above and the further feature you take to override it.
- "decisive_axis": string — one of "severity_gap", "whittleability", "identification_veil", "subject_dispersion" — naming the axis that did most of the work in your scores.
- "not_genuine_aggregation_cases": array of objects {"case_id": string, "reason": string} for any of the seven that are not really instances of the aggregation problem at all, with a one-clause reason each.
- "most_underrated_case": object {"case_id": string, "why": string} — the case you expect contractualists to wave away too quickly.
- "confidence": number between 0 and 1, your confidence in the overall ordering rather than in the exact integers.
