I'm in a small reading group — half of us from the philosophy department, half from the divinity school — and we've spent three sessions on the problem of evil without converging on anything. At the last one, one of the philosophers, Marta, wrote an argument on the whiteboard that nobody has answered, and the more I stare at my photo of it the more I suspect it proves too much, though I can't locate the move. I'm presenting a diagnosis next week and would rather not embarrass myself in front of both halves of the room.

Here it is, tidied up, with a case we added specifically to block the escape routes people kept reaching for. First a principle she called the Avoidability Condition: (AC) an agent A is morally justified in permitting or risking a harm H only if every course of action open to A involves H or some harm at least as bad. Now the case: God creates a single free creature, Solitary, in an otherwise empty world, for one reason only — to give Solitary one morally significant choice. Solitary, being libertarian free, with no determining past and no downstream history that could redeem anything, wrecks the one other thing in the world. There is no one else, there is no aftermath, and no greater good comes of it. The argument:

(1) (AC) is correct; it is just what it means to call a harm gratuitous, and if we drop it the claim that a good God would not permit gratuitous evil becomes empty.
(2) God's power is limited only by logic, so the courses of action open to God are exactly the logically possible worlds.
(3) Libertarian freedom means Solitary could have chosen otherwise in exactly the circumstances Solitary was in.
(4) So there is a logically possible world — same creature, same circumstances, same history up to the choice, no downstream consequences — in which Solitary refrains: the harm does not occur and nothing as bad occurs instead.
(5) By (2) that world was open to God; so by (AC) God was not justified in permitting the wrecking.
(6) Nothing in (3)–(5) depends on Solitary's being the only creature. For any freely chosen evil there is, by the same reasoning, a possible world that matches the actual one right up to the choice and diverges at it. So no freely chosen moral evil is ever justified.
(7) And nothing depends on freedom either. For any natural evil — a child's cancer, an animal burned in a wildfire — there is a possible world exactly like ours except that the mutation does not occur or the fire does not spread. So no natural evil is justified either, and the whole apparatus of theodicies is idle: no story about soul-making, natural laws, or the goods of compassion and forgiveness can ever justify anything, because there is always a nearby world containing that same story and not the evil.

That conclusion is stronger than Marta wanted, and it keeps growing. Run the argument on absent goods instead of present evils: for any world God might create there is a better one with more goods and no compensating evil, so by the same reasoning God is not justified in creating any world at all. And run it on us: I take a drive purely for my own pleasure, I could equally have stayed home, and staying home involves no comparable harm — so the collision I risk is, by (AC), one I am not justified in risking, which would make every optional act carrying any risk of harm to anyone impermissible.

So which step goes? I don't think it is (1): I want "gratuitous" to mean something, and I notice that most of us use something like (AC) when we judge human agents. I also don't want to give up libertarian freedom, since some version of the free will reply is the only part of the theistic package I find at all plausible, and Marta's argument is aimed straight at it. That leaves something in the neighbourhood of (2) and (4), and the way "open to" and "possible" are being used. We sketched a distinction between God's being able to bring about a world come what may and God's being able to bring it about only if the creature cooperates, but we couldn't agree whether that distinction does real work or merely relabels the problem, and someone objected that if it does work then God's power is limited by more than logic after all. What I'd like is a line-by-line diagnosis: name the step, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives — in particular whether the theist who takes the cooperative reading has to accept that God genuinely risked creation, whether that is compatible with anything the tradition wants to say about providence, and whether someone who likes this argument should be worried that it survives only by being much too strong.
