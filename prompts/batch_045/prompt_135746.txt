Consider the following pair of decision problems. Assume throughout that your utility is linear in money and that amounts are perfectly legible numbers.

**Problem 1 (the swap).** A benefactor secretly selects a positive amount a, seals a into one envelope and 2a into another, and shuffles the envelopes. You choose one, open it, and find X. You may now keep it or exchange it for the other envelope. The tempting argument: the other envelope contains either 2X or X/2 with equal probability, so exchanging has expected value (2X + X/2)/2 = 5X/4 > X, and you should exchange — and by the same argument exchange back, indefinitely.

**Problem 2 (the multiplier).** You are handed a single sealed envelope; you open it and find X. You may now press a button. If you press, a fair coin is tossed by a device that has no access to the envelope's contents: heads doubles your money, tails halves it. The arithmetic is identical — (2X + X/2)/2 = 5X/4 > X — so press; and, apparently, press again, and again.

The computation is the same and, naively, so is the agent's epistemic situation: "I hold X; if I act, I end up with 2X or X/2, fifty–fifty." Yet the received verdicts differ. In Problem 1 the argument is a fallacy: before opening, switching is worth exactly nothing, and after opening, whether switching is worthwhile depends on your prior over the smaller amount (positive for some observed values of X, non-positive for others, with ex-ante expected gain zero). In Problem 2 the argument looks sound and pressing looks clearly right.

Please compare the two problems and say precisely what is doing the work. In particular:

1. The standard diagnosis of Problem 1 is that the fifty–fifty is an illicit equiprobability: conditional on your having observed X, the chance that you are holding the larger envelope depends on your prior over a, and no proper prior makes switching profitable for every X. In Problem 2 the fifty–fifty is a physical coin stipulated to be independent of X. Is the entire difference between the problems captured by that — i.e. by whether the multiplicative factor that your act trades against is independent of the observed amount or is informationally coupled to it? Or is something further at stake, such as a difference in counterfactual structure between "receiving the envelope I did not receive" and "changing the amount in the envelope I hold"?

2. Suppose a decision theorist insists that rational choice depends only on the probability distribution over the outcomes of the available acts together with the utilities of those outcomes, and not on causal structure. Can that theorist account for the difference in verdicts without illegitimately importing extra information about the generative process? If not, what does the case show about which partition of possibilities — an act-conditional one, or one over background states — expected utility must be computed against?

3. Problem 2 has a wrinkle that Problem 1 lacks. Repeated pressing multiplies your holdings by independent random factors with arithmetic mean 1.25 and geometric mean 1. So after N pressings your expected wealth is (1.25)^N X, while with probability one the trajectory has no limit: its lower limit is 0 and its upper limit is infinite, and its median stays near X. Does this divergence between expectation and typical outcome tell against the "press" verdict in Problem 2 in the way that the re-switching regress tells against the "exchange" verdict in Problem 1? And if it does, does that soften the contrast you drew in (1) — i.e. is the one-shot argument in Problem 2 less clearly sound than it first appears?

4. Is there a genuinely unified treatment: a single principle stating when a calculation of the form ½(2X) + ½(X/2) is legitimate, which returns the standard verdict in Problem 1 and a defensible verdict in Problem 2 (one-shot and iterated)? If instead you think the two problems must be handled by different principles, say what the cost of that bifurcation is.

Answer self-containedly: define any technical notion you rely on — conditional expectation, propriety of a prior, independence of a factor from one's evidence, causal versus evidential decision theory, almost-sure behaviour of a stochastic process — and argue rather than cite literature. Where a question admits no settled answer, say so and defend the answer you find most plausible.
