I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I lead a four-person modeling team inside an infrastructure organization. We build the simulation and forecasting models that our own decisions run on: a discrete-event simulator of a request-routing fleet, a cost model behind our autoscaler, a digital twin of a warehouse robot fleet, and a physics simulator used to train a robot grasping policy that is later transferred onto real hardware. These are not academic exercises. Their outputs size fleets, choose scaling thresholds, decide how many robots we buy, and once a quarter feed a recommendation on multi-year infrastructure commitments. Every week we hold a forty-minute design review in which the same argument recurs: someone proposes a model or a change to one, someone else points out that an assumption inside it is flatly false, and the proposer replies that every model is false somewhere and what matters is whether the predictions come out right. I have to rule on that argument, and I want a better instrument than my gut.

The reply my team gives is, as far as I can tell, a version of a position from Milton Friedman's 1953 lead essay in Essays in Positive Economics: a hypothesis should be judged by the accuracy and scope of its predictions rather than by whether its assumptions describe reality, and — the part Samuelson is said to have nicknamed the F-twist — the more significant a hypothesis is, the more unrealistic its assumptions will tend to be, because a powerful hypothesis strips away everything that does not matter for prediction. The examples I have seen secondhand make the point vividly: an expert pool player sinks shots as if he had solved the geometry, and firms behave as if they maximized profits, whether or not anyone inside them does the calculation. As a slogan this has been useful to us. As a rule for design review it is far too permissive: some of our false assumptions have cost us money, one of them contributed to an outage, and predictions-are-what-matter does not separate those from the false assumptions that have never harmed us.

What I have been fumbling toward on my own is something I call the sealed-falsehood rule. A false assumption is licensed for a given use only if all of the following hold. (1) Swap-stability: the quantities the model exports to the decision are insensitive, within a tolerance named in advance, to replacing the false assumption with any member of a stated family of alternative falsehoods. (2) Regime match: the model's track record comes from a regime that includes the one it will be used in, not merely from a convenient historical period. (3) Decision-invariance or known direction: either the consumer of the model uses only an ordering, a threshold, or a sign that the error cannot flip, or we can state the direction in which the falsehood pushes the answer. (4) No free pass for compensating errors: if two falsehoods push in opposite directions and cancel, each must satisfy (1) through (3) on its own, because a cancellation that holds in one regime need not hold in another.

I do not trust this rule, and I can see at least one place where it may be too weak. Here are five cases from our own work, with my verdicts.

Case 1. The fleet simulator assumes service times are exponentially distributed. Real traces are heavy-tailed and bimodal, and a nightly batch job changes the shape. Used to size the fleet against a p99 latency objective, the simulator has been backtested against load tests and gets the required fleet size within a few percent. My verdict: the falsehood is fine for fleet sizing. But the same simulator, unchanged, is now proposed for predicting what happens if we triple the retry budget, which makes the workload far burstier than anything in the load tests. My verdict: not fine without new experiments.

Case 2. The autoscaler cost model assumes Poisson arrivals. Arrivals are actually bursty and correlated, and absolute predicted queue lengths are off by roughly a factor of two. But we use the model only to rank two candidate scaling thresholds, and a load test confirms the ranking is identical under synthetic and real traces. My verdict: acceptable. The absolute numbers are wrong and it does not matter, because the consumer consumes only an ordering.

Case 3. The robot-fleet digital twin assumes robots never collide with one another and that the floor is empty of humans. Both are false. Ignoring collisions inflates predicted throughput; ignoring humans, who cause robots to slow and yield, deflates it. In the one pilot we ran the two errors roughly cancelled and the model predicted throughput well. The pilot, however, ran on a quiet shift with very few humans on the floor. My verdict: not acceptable, and I actively distrust the good prediction. I think my regime-match clause catches this, but I also think swap-stability is satisfied by construction if I am allowed to define the swap family narrowly — swapping no collisions for a few collisions — so I need to know what constrains the choice of family, who chooses it, and how wide it must be.

Case 4. The grasping simulator assumes perfectly rigid bodies and frictionless contact. Both are false: real objects deform and friction matters a great deal. We train the policy in this simulator and transfer it to real robots, and it works, because we randomize friction, mass, and stiffness over wide ranges instead of committing to single false values. My verdict: acceptable, and I suspect my swap-stability idea is exactly what explains why — domain randomization is the swap test run with a distribution. But I do not know what the procedure should demand about the width or the centering of that randomization when we do not know the true values, and I suspect that passing here is partly luck.

Case 5. The capacity forecast behind the three-year commitment assumes the mix of request types stays fixed at last quarter's mix. The backtest over eight quarters is excellent. A large new customer with a structurally different workload begins onboarding next month, so the mix will not stay fixed. My verdict: I want to use this model, because it is the only forecast we have and the commitment deadline is real. I expect an honest procedure to refuse to bless it.

Cases 2 and 3 are the pair I cannot reconcile on my own: in one, a badly wrong exported number is harmless; in the other, a right exported number is worthless. If your account has to overturn one of those two verdicts to stay simple, say so and say which.

What I want from you has two parts. First, test my rule against the five cases and repair it: tell me which of my verdicts it cannot produce, which clause does no work or is too vague to apply, and what you would add or delete. Second, turn the repaired rule into a written procedure my team can actually run in a forty-minute review: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the gates at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the decision to a named person because the question is one of risk appetite rather than of fact. The reviewers are engineers who will not read philosophy, so keep the translation honest — mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and do not let the checklist imply more precision than it has.

Then run the finished procedure on three live proposals: reusing the fleet simulator from Case 1 to choose request timeout values, a quantity it has never been validated against; the three-year commitment in Case 5, where I expect the procedure to tell me something I do not want to hear; and a forecast for a workload that launched last week and for which we have no historical data at all. For each, give the verdict, the gate it turns on, and who has to sign off.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular how it behaves when there is no validation data, when a model is quietly reused for a purpose nobody wrote down, when the failure mode is an unknown unknown, and whether passing the procedure is any evidence at all that the model's assumptions are true. Please do not give me a reading list or citations. I need something I can paste into our review template.
