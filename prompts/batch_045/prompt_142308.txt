Next month I'm defending a thesis chapter (fourth-year, quantitative psych) in front of my committee plus whoever turns up from the department, and what I want is a Q&A prep sheet — not a defense of the chapter, I can write that part.

Short version of the claim I'm making: the number everyone quotes — the share of attempted replications that "worked" — is the wrong summary statistic, because the comparison class is wrong. You shouldn't compare an observed replication rate to 100%, or to chance, or to some flat benchmark someone made up; you should compare it to the replication rate you'd predict from the original studies' own reported effect sizes and standard errors, fitted before you ever look at the replication outcomes. I fit a hierarchical model to the original estimates only, get a predicted replication rate, and compare it to what actually happened. I've been calling the difference the calibration gap. The point of it: two literatures can have the same headline replication rate and completely different calibration gaps, because in one of them the originals were low-powered and the survivors were badly winner's-cursed in magnitude, so a lot of replication failure was already predicted and tells you nothing except that the studies were underpowered; in the other the originals were adequately powered and still failed, and that is evidence that the estimates were artifacts. Second half of the chapter: I argue that a lot of the shouting about the replication crisis is a disguised disagreement about priors — skeptic's prior versus advocate's prior — and that replication data alone can't settle it, but that the gap measure is at least prior-robust because the prior gets integrated over rather than fixed. (In my simulations, 200 originals, half of them at n=25 per cell, publish only if p<.05, replication at 2.5x the original n, the predicted-vs-observed thing separates the two cases cleanly, which is of course because I built the simulation.)

Who's in the room: my committee is a social psychologist who was an early preregistration advocate and now mostly says the whole crisis was a measurement problem all along; a biostatistician who works on post-selection inference and is convinced error probabilities are the only thing doing real work in any of this and that Bayes factors are a way of laundering assumptions; and a philosophy of science person who works on evidential pluralism and will want to know whether my gap is meant to be a statistical quantity or a causal one, because if it's causal I haven't identified anything. Then the department: experimentalists who've each done one preregistered replication and think of themselves as reformers, two econ-adjacent people for whom power analysis is the beginning and end of method, and at least one person who will want me to quote exact numbers from the big multi-lab replication projects — I always get those mixed up, so please just give me a line to say when that comes rather than the numbers.
