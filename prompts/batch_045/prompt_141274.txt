Hey, I need a diagnosis here rather than a verdict. I'm in a historiography reading group — mostly history grad students plus two philosophers of social science — and we have been stuck on Whig history for about a year now in a really particular way. Everyone agrees on the set-piece cases: a nineteenth-century constitutional history that reads a medieval charter as the seed of modern parliamentary liberty, or a history of science written as a march of correct theories converging on what we now believe, is doing something wrong. Everyone denounces it. And then ten minutes later the same people defend some narrative of their own that is plainly organized around a later outcome: they pick which of a dozen seventeenth-century religious disputes to spend three chapters on because one of them turned into something big afterwards, they describe a practice as an early form of the thing it later became, they explain why an institution survived by pointing at features that only look useful in hinsight. I got into this myself because I rewrote a chapter of my own thesis and realized every paragraph was secretly structured around what came later, and when I said so my supervisor just shrugged and said, well yes, otherwise why would anyone care.

My hunch, which I want you to attack rather than politely agree with, is that Whig is doing at least three separable jobs and the argument only looks hopeless because nobody separates them. Call the first retrospective selection: letting a later outcome decide what you study at all. The second I've started calling outcome-licensing, by which I mean letting the later outcome do explanatory work inside the account — the fact that X succeeded gets used as the license for explaining X's earlier features by appeal to that success. The third is retrospective rating, implicitly scoring past actors or institutions by how nearly they approximate us. My guess is that selection is unavoidable and not even a fault, that rating is the genuinely objectionable one but also the easiest to drop, and that outcome-licensing is where the real fight lives — and that it is at bottom a question about whether the domain is cumulative, whether there is an actual selective-retention mechanism at work as there arguably is in the history of a technology or a science, as opposed to a domain where the outcome was one live possibility among many and the path was genuinely contingent, like a succession crisis or a schism. So: is that the right map, or is the stall somewhere else entirely — a verbal dispute about progress and teleology, a disagreement about what an explanation in history is supposed to do (make the outcome expectable? support counterfactuals about what else could have happened? recover what the actors could see from where they stood?), a disagreement about who history is written for, or just two research temperaments that were never going to converge?

Please engage the strongest version of each side rather than the easy one: the best case for you cannot avoid it, given that archives are themselves shaped by what happened to survive and every narrative needs somewhere to stop, and the best case for no, it really is a mistake, because it misdescribes a causal structure in which the actual outcome was not where things were heading. Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress — a distinction like my three-way one, some test (I keep imagining a blind exercise: write up the same period twice, once with access only to sources available up to a cutoff date, then see whether readers can tell which is which and whether the two accounts differ in substance or only in emphasis), a reframing of the question — or tell me honestly that nothing will move it and why. I don't need citations or a lit review and I've read the standard definitions, so please don't just walk me through those. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the diagnosis in one sentence — then give me the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
