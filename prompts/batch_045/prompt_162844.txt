I'm a journalist — I mostly cover physics and the weirder ideas-adjacent stories — and I'm putting together a short audio segment on duplication for a podcast series we're doing about identity: identical twins, cloned livestock, a stolen violin that turned out to be a copy so good the owner couldn't tell, that sort of thing. Somewhere along the line I got sucked into the metaphysics end of it, after interviewing a philosopher in Glasgow who works on symmetry in physics. She walked me through Max Black's famous 1952 thought experiment — a universe containing nothing but two perfectly alike iron spheres a couple of miles apart, no landmarks, no observer — and then said something I haven't been able to shake. She calls it her "asymmetry test": Black's setup, she says, is only a case of indiscernibles if the entire description of the universe (the objects, every relation between them, and the laws) stays exactly the same when you swap the two spheres. The moment you let a single *asymmetric* relation hold between them — her example was that one sphere is the cause of the other's collapse, or that one of them perceives the other and not the other way round — the two spheres come out qualitatively different after all, because one of them now has a feature the other lacks. So she thinks the counterexample is way more fragile than it's usually presented as, and that people who reach for it are quietly sneaking in a difference that Leibniz would happily have counted. I definately don't trust myself to judge that.

The part where I keep tying myself in knots is the primary text. My producer dug up this from Leibniz's *Monadology* (1714), paragraph 9:

"Each monad, indeed, must be different from every other monad. For there are never in nature two beings which are exactly alike, and in which it is not possible to find a difference either internal or based on an intrinsic property."

What I can't get straight is what work the words are doing. Is he reporting an empirical generalisation about how nature happens to be, or claiming it couldn't possibly be otherwise — and if it's the second, why phrase it as "there are never in nature"? And what is he ruling out with "internal" and "intrinsic"? Being two miles from another sphere is a relational feature that both spheres share, so it can't separate them; but "being distinct from sphere A" is had by only one of them, and presumably *that* isn't what he means either, or the principle is empty. Also, why the "either/or" — is "internal" and "based on an intrinsic property" meant to be two different kinds of difference, and does the second one quietly let in something the first one excludes?

So, concretely, the things I'd love help with: (1) can a difference that consists *purely* in how a thing stands to something else ever satisfy this passage, or is Leibniz only counting differences the thing carries in itself? (2) why do people say the "being distinct from A" move is circular or question-begging — what exactly is doing the begging? (3) is my Glasgow source's asymmetry test just that same circularity dressed up in physics language, or does specifying an asymmetric relation *without* naming either sphere ("there are two spheres and one is the cause of the other's collapse") escape the circularity in a way the "distinct from A" move doesn't — and if it does escape, does that mean her fragility point actually lands? And (4) another philosopher I emailed said Leibniz would simply deny that the spheres are substances at all, that they're aggregates whose constituent monads differ; is that a real reply or a cop-out? If you can, tell me what I should actually say to listeners in the five minutes I've got, and what the one or two honest caveats are that I shouldn't paper over.
