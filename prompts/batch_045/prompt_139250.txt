I produce a scripted interview series in which each episode features a historical figure, played in character, who answers only from the books he himself wrote: no modern scholarship, no editions, no sources he could not have had to hand, nothing published after his death. The next subject is Thomas Hobbes, and I want the episode to be about his mathematics rather than his politics. Earlier episodes have already covered the state of nature, the covenant and the sovereign, and I do not want any of that rehashed; I want the geometry, and specifically the way it was supposed to underwrite the claim that politics can be a demonstrative science.

Please draft eleven or twelve questions for that interview, plus one further question I should probably not ask. Write the questions exactly as I would put them to him, addressed to him in the second person, in British English and in a fairly formal register.

The material the questions should work over, none of which can be assumed familiar to a listener:

- his ranking of geometry as the only science God has so far seen fit to bestow on humankind, and the matching claim that the science of justice and policy began only with him, because only he made it proceed in the manner of geometry;
- his insistence that a science must begin from definitions which state how the thing in question is generated, that these definitions are made rather than discovered, and that the terms they fix are names of our own imposing;
- his treatment of a point as a body considered while its quantity is disregarded, and of a line as the trace of a body in motion, so that even the most rarefied objects of geometry remain bodies under a certain manner of considering them;
- resolution and composition as the method he takes over from the geometers and applies to the commonwealth, and the contrast he draws between what men make and can therefore know and what they merely observe and cannot;
- his published attempt, in the 1650s, to square the circle, its refutation by John Wallis, his amended re-defence of it, his claim that the angle between a curve and its tangent at the point of contact is a genuine and divisible quantity, and his complaint that algebraic methods are not science at all because they do not proceed from causes;
- his standing grievance that the university mathematicians and the new scientific societies shut him out, and the awkward fact that his mathematics won no following while his politics made him notorious.

Four things I want pressed especially hard:

1. The fork about abstraction. If geometry is about bodies, then no drawn circle is exact, and no diagram can underwrite a demonstration that holds universally. If it is about abstractions from body, then it is not a science of nature, and so it cannot be the model for a science of commonwealths, which are made of real men. I want a question that forces him onto one horn and a follow-up for whichever horn he chooses.

2. The transfer question. Which feature of geometry is doing the real work when he says politics can be demonstrative: the synthetic order that runs from definitions to conclusions, the fact that the object is generated by us, or the fact that its terms are our own names? Frame the question so that he cannot answer by listing all three, and give me the follow-up that presses the case where they come apart, since the commonwealth is made by us but its matter, the passions and motions of the mind of man, is not.

3. The self-application question. He explains the persistence of error by interest, by the authority of the schools and by the abuse of insignificant words. I want one question that asks him to turn that explanation onto the failure of his own mathematics to persuade, and a follow-up on what he would have accepted as evidence that the geometry itself, and not merely the age or its institutions, was at fault.

4. The question about nominalism and reality. If the principles of a science are definitions we impose, and the necessity of a demonstration is necessity in the consequences of names, how can such a science deliver truths about bodies that are none of our making?

For every question, give me, in one sentence each, the answer he is most likely to give, and then the follow-up that answer makes available; no question may be one he can discharge simply by restating his position. Finally, give me the one question I should leave out, tell me why it would make him refuse to answer or lose his temper, and show me what a careful version of the same question would look like if I did decide to ask it. The whole answer must be in British English; do not cite or name any modern commentators, and do not supply reading lists or references of any kind.
