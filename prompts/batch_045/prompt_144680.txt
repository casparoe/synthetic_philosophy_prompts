My uncle Ray died on tuesday and I'm doing the eulogy on saturday. Background, because it bears on what I can and can't say up there.

He was an entomologist for about thirty years and then spent the last fifteen of his working life on the question of whether insects and crustaceans might be sentient — mostly very unglamorous applied stuff by the end, faster ways to kill farmed insects, that kind of thing. But he was incredibly stubborn, in a way that used to annoy the younger people in his field, about the fact that nobody knew. He thought the evidence genuinely cut both ways: nociceptors, learning, some surprisingly flexible behavior on one side; nothing remotely like a mammalian cortex on the other; and no agreed way of adding that up. He also thought the question "are they conscious?" was badly posed and that the real questions were how much, of what, per unit time.

His actual argument was arithmetic. If there's some real chance that there's something it's like to be a shrimp, and there are unimaginable numbers of them, then a small probability times an enormous number is a large number, and it's worth some money and some inconvenience to shorten the bad parts. He also had this thing he'd repeat about time — that a fly's second and your second might not contain the same amount of experience, because of how fast their visual systems sample the world, so that a life that looks short from the outside might not be short from the inside. I have no idea wether that's right and I don't think he did either.

The room will be roughly half people from that world — several of them much more confident than he was, a couple of them think it's basically settled that insects are conscious — and half family, neighbours, and people from his church who think bugs are bugs and found the whole obsession endearing and a bit funny. And then directly afterwards the family is doing the crawfish boil he went to every single year, which he ate, which drove his colleagues up the wall and which he never fully explained.

So: a five-minute eulogy, read out loud. What I need from it:

- Don't put certainty in his mouth. That's the one thing he'd get properly cross about, and a funeral seems like exactly the place someone would finally say he'd proved it.
- Don't tell the family their instincts about insects are contemptible, and don't tell his colleagues that the science came down on their side.
- Don't turn lunch into a grievance. The crawfish are happening either way.
- Don't have him watching over us, or at peace, or knowing how we're doing — he was a blunt materialist about minds and would have found that humiliating.

I'd like the actual shape of his view — the agnosticism plus the arithmetic — to be doing structural work in the thing, so it isn't just "he was a sweet weirdo who liked bugs" with some philosophy sprinkled on. He kept honeybees for forty years if that's useful. And it should make both halves of the room laugh at least once.

Just start with the speech itself, no preamble and no notes afterwards about what you were going for.
