hey — hoping you can help me think through something I'm stuck on at work.

I sit on the review panel for our region's supported-will system. When someone loses capacity — dementia, a bad stroke, whatever — a care plan gets built for them, and the hard cases land on us. The rule we're supposed to apply is that the plan tracks the person's *extrapolated* will rather than their current one. The gloss we all work from: the preference set you'd end up with if you let this person think and learn for as long as they wanted, with full information and nobody pushing them. What we actually do is rule on individual preferences: counted (the plan has to respect it), not counted (the plan may override it), or discounted and set aside (we don't treat it as their will, but we don't swing the other way either).

Our precedents so far, more or less as I remember them:

1. Mr Okonkwo, 61, early-onset dementia, smoked for decades and thought it was "not that bad." Given the real numbers and a few weeks to sit with them, he quit on his own. We did not count the smoking preference.

2. Ms Hallberg, same preference, but she'd read everything, tried to quit four times, and still wanted to smoke with the risks accepted. Counted.

3. Ana, mid-thirties, severe depressive episode, wanted to be left alone and refused treatment. Six months later, recovered, she said she was glad nobody had listened to her. Not counted.

4. Miriam, raised in a very closed religious community, taken out at 19 by people from outside it, spent a decade away, then went back. She'd had years of exposure to alternatives — the panel noted the exposure wasn't her idea at all and said that didn't matter — and she wanted to stay. Counted.

5. Dev: wanted to be a cellist, a hand injury at 21 ended that, and by 45 he had a life he says he'd pick again and no desire for the music at all. Fully informed, stable, said outright that he didn't want the cello and didn't want to want it. But whenever anyone got onto the injury years he changed the subject. We discounted the indifference — didn't count it as his will, didn't reinstate the cello either, just treated it as not load-bearing.

6. Bea: pushed into caring for a disabled sibling from about thirteen, and at forty she's a nurse who loves the work. She's thought hard about the coercion, calls it what it was, says she'd want it stopped for anyone else in that position — and says she wants the work. Counted.

7. Eddie: said his faith wasn't up for review and he didn't want the process poking at it. We counted the faith and honored the no-probe request.

New case. Yusuf, 70, mild cognitive impairment, came here alone at nine during a war, spent time in a camp, then a foster family in a language he didn't speak. The preference in question is extreme frugality: heating off in winter, cupboards full of tinned food, mends everything, and he's actually comfortably off now. He knows he doesn't need to live that way, ran the numbers out loud with the assessor, and when asked directly he says he knows it isn't needed and he wants to live this way. The assessor tried the usual reflection prompts and it went the wrong direction — the more he talked about it the more attached he got. Two other things came up. He's said more than once that he wouldn't take the childhood back, and he's specific that he means the whole thing rather than just the parts that were good. And he's said that if going through the extrapolation procedure was likely to take the frugality off him, he doesn't want the procedure run on it.

So:

- Should we count the frugality?
- What do we do with the part where he opts out of the procedure itself? Honoring that seems to mean not applying the test we just used to decide everything else, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a deep problem with our whole setup or just a man being allowed to decline something.
- Which precedent is the closest analog, and what's the rule each of us would be running if we picked that one?
- If our precedents really don't settle this, I want it stated plainly what principle we'd be locking in with each choice — including whether the thing that settles it for us is the origin of the preference, how he relates to that origin, whether the reflection actually happened rather than merely being available, or whether the preference is bad for him.
- What one follow-up case, if we ever actually got it, would separate the rules? I mean a case that could realistically come across our desk, not a philosophers' hypothetical.

I've changed my mind twice already on this one, so give me the reasoning and not just the call — and tell me which of the sub-questions my answer is really riding on.
