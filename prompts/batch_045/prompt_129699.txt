I'm teaching Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* on an undergraduate module on markets and morals, alongside the philosophical literature on repugnant markets and commodification, and I want to use the novel for more than illustration. What puzzles me is how little of the standard case applies to it. Nobody sells anything: there are no prices, no bargaining, no contract, no moment at which a donor trades an organ for money. The clones are simply reared to donation, and the institution's defence is that Hailsham was the humane alternative — they were cared for, taught, given poetry and art, and had better lives than they would otherwise have had.

So I want a reading that asks where the novel locates the wrong. My working distinction is between *transaction-centred* objections, which find it in the exchange itself — the price, the bargaining, what it expresses to put something up for sale — and *institution-centred* objections, which find it in a standing arrangement that makes some people's bodies available for others' use, whether or not anything is ever bought or sold. Test the distinction against the novel. Which scenes support only the second kind, and which, if any, need the first? And does Miss Emily's defence work? Her position is essentially that respect is shown by how you treat people rather than by whether anything changes hands — the same move defenders of permissible markets make when they say that selling a kidney is compatible with valuing the seller. Does the novel undercut that reply, or does it leave it standing?

Two further threads. The Gallery: the pupils' best work is collected and, we learn, was exhibited to prove to the outside world that they had souls. The novel's closest thing to an exchange is of the products of the very inner life that the donation programme's rationale denies — is that Ishiguro loading the dice, or is it doing argumentative work about what the wrong consists in? And the deferral: the rumour that a couple who can prove they were truly in love may have their donations postponed, and Tommy's attempt to secure it with his late drawings, made to an institution that never set a price and, it turns out, had nothing to trade. Read that as a parody of a market. What would a real market have given the donors that the deferral could not?

Lastly, be honest about the resistance in both directions: the novel is not a polemic, its concern is mortality and what a life amounts to, and forcing it into the commodification frame may miss that. Say where it resists, and whether, in the end, it adds anything to the debate that the arguments alone do not.
