I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences were still being posted, and I give two or three talks a year to the meetup that grew out of them. Next month's is 40 minutes plus 30 minutes of questions, around 35 people in a rented room, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis. Hofstadter's claim — most fully in I Am a Strange Loop (2007), and in the way he writes about his wife's death: that a person's "I" is not confined to one brain, that some nonzero chunk of her was still running in him afterward, in rough proportion to how much of her pattern was still doing work in his head — is right, and everyone mis-reads its logical form. I want to defend two things:

(1) Degrees, not identity. In the fission cases the tradition is obsessed with, "is this the same person?" is a badly formed question; the well-formed one is "how much of that person is instantiated here?", and the answer is a non-negative real number, not a yes-or-no.

(2) Measure, not probability. The number attached to a successor behaves like a measure and not like a credence. It does not have to sum to one, even across all the successors there are: most of a person's pattern isn't in any brain, some of it is in their notebooks and in their children's habits, and some of it is now in language models trained on their email. Measures can also be created and destroyed, so there are choices where the total varies — one very-high-fidelity successor versus ten moderate-fidelity ones, say — and there the measure rule and the "which one will I turn out to be?" rule come apart. What a person should care about, on my view, is the measure-weighted welfare of their instantiations, not the welfare of the one they "turn out to be."

My argument, briefly. A person at a time is a pattern — a self-referential structure of the kind Hofstadter calls a strange loop — and not an organism or a bare continuant; what matters in survival is instantiation of the pattern, and instantiation admits of degree because a pattern can be partly realized, with the degree fixed by how much of the "core" is preserved, where the core is the set of dispositions that would actually drive the successor's behavior in the situations the original cared about. Degrees of instantiation are measures: they don't sum to one, they don't give you a fact about which successor you become, and they're additive across non-overlapping instantiations. Caring about yourself is caring about the measure-weighted total.

The soft spot, which I already know: nearly everyone's first objection will be that on this view a hard drive full of your email is 3% of you. My reply is a distinction between a live instantiation — one whose pattern is causally poised to generate the same counterfactual behaviors, something that would respond as she would — and an inert trace, which merely correlates with the pattern: a recording, a book, a set of frozen weights. I expect to spend most of the question period on this, and I expect it to be where I lose the room if I lose it.

The audience, as precisely as I can describe it. About 35 people, mostly working programmers, a handful of ML people, a few philosophy-adjacent grad students, one retired analytic philosopher of mind who comes to all of these to argue. Concretely:

- Two or three people who have actually read Hofstadter. One of them thinks the whole "I"-and-"soul" vocabulary is mysticism smuggled in through the back door and will say so early and loudly.
- Three or four ML people, at least one of them in interpretability. They will go immediately to the cheap-pattern or over-distribution worry in its modern form: a model fine-tuned on everything you have ever written instantiates more of your pattern than your daughter does, and if that is you, the view is absurd.
- One or two expected-utility types who will say that (2) is merely verbal: once I normalize and weight, I have reinvented subjective probability, and the measure/probability distinction is decoration on an ordinary neo-Lockean view.
- Someone from the anthropics-and-many-worlds end of the room who will ask how this differs from quantum measure, and whether I'm entitled to borrow the machinery without the physics.
- The retired philosopher, who will object in the vocabulary of the literature: animalism, psychological-continuity views, Parfit-style fission, the reductionist's "no further fact." He'll press either that (1) is just neo-Lockeanism with numbers bolted on, or that it commits me to a further fact I have no room for.
- A couple of meditators who hold that the premise — that there is an "I" to be distributed — is the confusion, not the conclusion.

What I want from you. The eight or so objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way these people would phrase them rather than the way a journal referee would. For each: a two-minute reply in sentences I could actually say out loud, not notes toward one; and an honest note on where that reply is soft, evasive, or changes the subject. Flag the ones with real force that I should concede in the talk itself rather than defend in Q&A. Give me the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. Tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to be worth giving. And flag any objection that is really a strawman my own framing invited — I know I've made it easy to hear me as saying something spiritual — with a one-line way to defuse it without sounding condescending.

Please don't include a reading list, citations, or a bibliography: I've read the literature and I won't be looking anything up between now and the talk, so I need the arguments themselves, not pointers to them. No general advice on handling Q&A or public speaking. Assume the room is friendly but not deferential, and that anyone in it will happily tell me my view is incoherent in about nine words.
