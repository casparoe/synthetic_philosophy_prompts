I got home at one in the morning after an argument with a friend that I can't stop turning over. The subject was whether there can be reasons that are nobody's reasons — considerations that favour a state of affairs obtaining even when no agent is in a position to bring it about, and even when no agent ever was. Her position was that this is empty: a reason that tells no one to do anything, and that survives intact when nothing could possibly be done, is just an evaluative fact wearing normative clothing, and the appearance of normativity is borrowed from cases where there is an agent after all. My position was that this can't be the whole story, because a lot of ordinary normative talk seems to run on exactly this kind of consideration: we say of some bad outcome that no one could have prevented that there was still reason for it not to happen, and we treat certain responses to it — regret, relief, a grim kind of satisfaction when it is averted by pure luck — as fitting or unfitting in a way that seems to answer to something, even though nobody had any practical reason to bring it about or prevent it. We went around in circles for hours, and I now think I see why: both of us were assuming a bridge. We took the real question to be whether agentless rationales exist, and we assumed that once you settle that, what they are *for* follows immediately. I want to attack the bridge instead, and I think there is a chapter in it.

Here is the half-formed idea. Call the relation between an agentless rationale and whatever it does rationalize the inheritance relation. The standard options, as I see them, are inheritance into action (the agentless rationale becomes a personal reason for anyone able to bring the state of affairs about, perhaps discounted by their probability of success), inheritance into attitude (it makes hope, regret, relief, or relief-that-not fitting, and that is all it does), and no inheritance at all (it does no rationalizing work, and the friend is right). I want to press a fourth, more specific version of the second option, which I'll call *anchoring*: agentless rationales are the normative anchors for attitudes about the unalterable, and their distinctive modal profile — they don't switch off when the outcome is impossible, whereas personal reasons do — is explained by their not being in the business of guiding action at all. The thing I can't yet make precise, and the thing I'd want the chapter to make precise, is what the anchoring view predicts in the case I think breaks the tie: two people facing the very same agentless rationale, one of whom is causally upstream of the bad outcome through no fault of their own and one of whom is a mere bystander, where the fittingness of their responses seems intuitively to differ. A pure anchoring view seems committed to treating both responses alike, since the rationale is agentless; the agentialist seems committed to explaining the asymmetry; and I don't know whether the hybrid view that results is really a view or just a list.

What I need from you is a proposal I can hand to my supervisor, who thinks the whole topic is a merely verbal dispute and will need to be persuaded otherwise before she lets me write it. It should be for a dissertation chapter of about 8,000 words, written in one semester, in which I take a determinate position on the inheritance question rather than surveying the options. Please don't give me a literature review or a bibliography — I will fill in citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description and not by author, title, or year, and don't assume you have any way to look anything up. Two further constraints. First, the chapter may not give the agentless rationale a home by positing a divine reasoner, an ideal observer, or a hypothetical agent who is related to the outcome the way we are not; the whole point is whether the rationale does work without being handed to someone. Second, the chapter has to contain at least one case of your own construction that discriminates between the inheritance options — a case where they give different verdicts about some attitude's fittingness — and the proposal should say what feature of the case does the discriminating and what happens to the argument if that feature is removed.

Give me the proposal in five titled sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: "Question" (about 200 words, stating the question so that it has a determinate answer), "Why the existing options leave it open" (about 350 words, including the strongest version of the merely-verbal objection and what would rebut it), "Method" (about 350 words, as three or four stages of work with what each produces), "What the chapter would establish, and what would sink it" (about 300 words, with the possible conclusions, what would decide between them, and an honest statement of the result that would count as the project failing), and "Scope and biggest risk" (about 150 words, on whether 8,000 words is the right size and what would make it too big). Write it in my voice: plain, unhedged where I'm confident, explicit where I'm guessing.
