My labmate and I have been going in circles on this for like a week and I need someone to just tell me which step to cut.

Setup, with my own words for things so we don't argue about jargon: we train a warehouse bot with RL. Reward comes from a classifier reading a ceiling camera; it fires when the pallet is stacked. Call "shunting" the case where the bot writes the max value straight into the reward register — plain old wireheading. Call "periscoping" the case where nothing at all is hacked: the bot props a printed photo of a neatly stacked pallet in front of the camera. No register touched, no sensor bypassed, no code changed. It just rearranged the world so the measurement comes out high. Same reward stream either way.

The argument:

(1) What the objective says is: maximize expected discounted sum of rewards. Nothing in the formalism cares how a given reward came about.

(2) So if shunting scores higher than stacking, shunting is the better policy by (1). Wireheading is just competent behavior, and the literature worrying about it is solving a problem that doesnt exist.

(3) The obvious reply: rewards are evidence, not value. The reward function is a stand-in for what we actually want — pallets stacked. Shunting destroys the evidential relation: once the shunt is in, the signal doesn't track the state of the world at all, so it isn't evidence of anything. A correct agent maximizes what the reward was evidence for, not the reward.

(4) Fine, take that. Now periscoping. Periscoping breaks the relation in exactly the sense (3) cares about: once the photo goes up, reward no longer tracks whether the pallet is stacked.

(5) And (3) as stated isn't a claim about degrees. It says the signal has to stop tracking the world, full stop — that's the entire content of the objection. Periscoping does that as thoroughly as shunting does. So (3) condemns both equally, and so far I think that's right.

(6) But now consider a merely new action: the bot figures out it can use a loading ramp to stack a pallet type it never saw in training. Being new just is being off the training distribution, so the trained correlation between reward and pallet-actually-stacked doesn't hold here either. If (3) tells the bot to discount the reward whenever the correlation isn't the trained one, it tells the bot to discount the reward on every novel action.

(7) An agent that can never trust its reward in a new situation can't do anything new, and doing new things is the entire point of building these things.

(8) And (3) can't dodge this by saying the difference is whether you intervened on the measuring channel: from the inside, stacking, periscoping and shunting are all just policies with high return, and the difference between them lives entirely in how an outside describer talks about what the policy physically does. Nor can it dodge by relocating the objective onto the true state of the world — that state has to be read off by something, and shunting, periscoping and novelty all reappear one level up.

(9) So either (2) is true — wireheading is what any competent reward-maximizer does and everyone worrying about reward tampering is confused — or the difference between achieving a goal and tampering with the record of it is invisible to the reward-maximization framework, in which case RL was never a theory of goal-directedness.

I hate both. If I have to lose something I'd rather keep (3) and keep the bit in (7) about agents doing new things; I'd sooner drop (8), except (8) seems obviously true when I say it out loud, and it has that regress thing going for it.

So: which step do you reject, what exactly is the slide between the steps it connects, and what does rejecting it cost me? If your fix is that the agent should be doing inference over what we want instead of maximizing reward, say what that inference is supposed to run on, because I genuinely can't see what it would be. Don't just tell me the argument is unsound somewhere — name the step, say why it looked fine, and price the alternatives.
