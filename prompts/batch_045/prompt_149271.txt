I'm putting together the first chapter of my thesis (philosophy of mind, with a side habit of trying to say something careful about ML systems) and before my supervisor picks it apart I want to know whether the pile of things I believe actually hangs together. Here's the profile, roughly — and I genuinely don't know which part is load-bearing, so please don't assume I've spotted the problem:

(1) I'm a full-on externalist about content. Twin Earth convinces me: Oscar and Twin Oscar are molecule-for-molecule identical and their "water" thoughts still come apart in content, so meaning ain't in the head.

(2) I also accept the social version. Someone who only half-grasps a condition they themselves have — thinks it covers inflammation of the joints anywhere, say — is still thinking about that condition, because the linguistic community's usage does the fixing and they defer to it.

(3) But I don't think there's any such thing as narrow content. What gets called that looks to me like intrinsic functional or inferential role dressed up in semantic vocabulary, and I'd rather drop the word "content" than multiply notions.

(4) I think psychology has to be solipsistic in its taxonomy: the generalizations we want in cognitive science should be statable over the intrinsic properties of the organism, since those are what do the causal work, and a generalization that floats free of what's inside the head isn't a psychological law.

(5) I'm a representationalist about experience: what it's like for you is exhausted by what your experience represents.

(6) I'm an internalist about what it's like: an intrinsic duplicate of me anywhere at all is having exactly the experiences I'm having, and nothing about the difference in what our words refer to shows up in how things seem to us.

(7) I want first-person authority: I can know what I'm thinking without going out and investigating my environment first.

(8) I want the brain-in-a-vat scenario to stay coherent as a skeptical hypothesis, and I want to be able to say I can't rule it out.

(9) On the ML side: a language model trained only on text and never hooked up to any sensors has no thoughts about cats — its "cat" states don't refer, or refer only to text-patterns — while a robot with cat-sensitive sensors whose internal states play the same functional role does have cat-thoughts. And I want that difference to be a difference in content, not merely in how reliable or how well-grounded the system is.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually collide, which only look like they do, and for each real collision what's the cheapest thing I could give up and what does giving it up cost me somewhere else in the set? Rank them by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad, and if there's one revision that cleans up the most at once, tell me which. Please write the answer in British English — my supervisor is a stickler about -ise endings and will circle every "color" and "behavior".
