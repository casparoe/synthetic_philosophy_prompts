I am the ethics advisor to a joint commissioning board that allocates a single pooled public-health budget for overdose response in a mid-sized city. The board's three voting members hold value systems that do not reduce to any common currency: a secular harm-reduction organisation that treats the provision of safer-use equipment and naloxone as a duty and regards abstinence conditions on aid as coercive; a faith-based recovery ministry whose objection is not primarily to the outcomes but to enabling continued use and to lending its name to what it holds to be a misuse of the body; and the health department itself, which is accountable for population-level outcomes and for legal defensibility, and which therefore has a procedural preference for visible neutrality. The funder will only consider a single joint application, so the realistic alternative to agreement is not the status quo but a split budget with a considerably smaller award and no shared referral infrastructure. We assess roughly six such proposals a year; each involves six-figure sums and, at the margin, lives; each member holds a veto; and the board must produce a written record of its reasoning that a funder and possibly a court could later read.

What I need from you is a decision procedure the board can actually run — in a half-day session, with members who have no philosophical training, on any proposal that asks two or more of them to act together while disagreeing about why they are acting. The idea I want operationalised is the one I keep encountering in the literature on moral compromise: that parties with different value systems can sometimes both gain from a trade, because each concedes what it cares about less in order to secure more of what it cares about, and the package is better for each than any non-cooperative outcome. I want that turned into ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, clear stopping points where the procedure returns a no, and explicit hand-off points where the remaining question is one of values for the board rather than one of fact for staff.

I have sketched some candidate tests, and I would like you to correct, reorder, merge or discard them rather than simply elaborate what I have written — tell me which are real and which are fake precision:

a. The own-values, own-baseline test: each party must prefer the package judged by its own values, and against the best outcome it could secure without cooperating, not against the current situation. I also want the addressee pinned down: a gain to the coalition as an agent is not automatically a gain to each member.

b. The complicity ladder: distinguish doing a thing, funding it, authorising it, benefiting from it, and being publicly credited with it, and specify which rungs are blocking for which parties and which merely impose a cost.

c. Modularise before trading: where the ladder blocks, first try converting a deep compromise — shared reasons, joint endorsement, common branding — into a shallow one, in which separate agents act on their own stated reasons and coordinate only in logistics, and then check whether the trade still exists once it is shallow.

d. The reverse-package test: present each party with only the component that serves the other side's values and ask whether it still prefers that to walking away. My aim is to catch agreements bought with cheap symbolic goods, such as acknowledgements and disclaimers, that satisfy one side's values at almost no material cost to the other.

e. The ratchet condition: whatever we agree becomes the template for later decisions and for other claimants on the same budget, so each party must be willing to have its own concession taken as the default next time.

f. The separate-sphere condition: the compromise must leave each party some domain in which it acts on its values uncompromised; a party may trade here only if it is not thereby made to speak for the whole of what it believes.

g. The absent-parties test: who bears the cost without a seat at the table, and can they exit?

Two difficulties I want handled inside the procedure rather than left as caveats. First, every party has an incentive to overstate how much a concession costs it, so I need questions that get at real bottom lines without turning the session into a negotiation. Second, these same parties will face each other again next year, so the procedure has to work in repeat play, where credibility and precedent are part of what is being traded.

Then apply the finished procedure to three live proposals, and for each draft the written record you would leave in the file:

1. A joint programme with a shared referral pathway in which harm-reduction clients are referred into abstinence-based housing and the ministry's clients into safer-use services, operating under a single brand, with a clause barring the distribution of certain supplies on the shared site.

2. A conscience clause permitting the ministry to decline any component it objects to while remaining a funded partner drawing a share of the overhead, in a form the department says is legally necessary. It is the only version available before the funding deadline, and I expect it is the one the board will want to accept.

3. A public-education component on which the only text all three can sign is a neutral sentence saying that support is available, carrying neither safer-use information nor the ministry's message: an agreement to say nothing.

Run all three and tell me which one the procedure returns a no on. I would rather hear that from the procedure than from the deadline. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and where it will misfire — in particular whether it will systematically favour whichever party has the more articulate spokespeople, or the party whose values happen to be cheap to satisfy symbolically.
