After a long argument with a friend last night I want to get this down before I lose the thread. Her position was that the anti-luminosity argument settles the fate of the KK principle outright: if no state is such that being in it puts you in a position to know you are in it, then knowing that you know cannot be a condition on knowing, and every picture of knowledge that leans on reflective access goes with it. My stubbornness was that the argument only ever gets a grip on what I would call discriminative luminosity — the claim that at the border you can tell being in the state from not being in it — and that it is run on states arranged along a continuous dimension, in the standard gradually-cooling case, where there is no last clear instance left to discriminate. It seems to say nothing about deliberative luminosity: whether a subject who has the concept of knowledge and is explicitly asking herself whether she knows can, by attending to her grounds rather than to a borderline sensation, reach a verdict that is itself knowledge. My half-formed claim is that these two come apart; that a restricted principle — roughly, if S knows that p and S has explicitly asked herself whether she knows that p, then S is in a position to know that she knows that p — survives the margin argument; and that this restricted principle is the only version of KK that ever did any work, since the places KK is used, in arguments about assertion, inquiry, or knowledge as the norm of practical reasoning, are all reflective uses. I also suspect the distinction will be tested by cases of misleading higher-order evidence, where first-order grounds are excellent and the second-order picture is not, and I am not sure in advance which way that cuts.

I am applying for a one-year research master's fellowship and need a two-page proposal for a thesis of roughly 12,000 words that either makes this distinction out or shows that it collapses. Please write it: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the standard positions leave it open; the method, stage by stage, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives, such as trying to rebut the margin argument on its own terms or weakening KK until nothing can touch it; what the two or three possible outcomes are and what would decide between them; and what would count as the project failing rather than merely coming out against me. Assume a realistic scope for one year and say plainly where you think my idea is too big. Do not include a bibliography; I will supply the references. Finally, add a short closing note on how confident you are in the framing you have proposed and which single part of it you would be least surprised to see fall apart.
