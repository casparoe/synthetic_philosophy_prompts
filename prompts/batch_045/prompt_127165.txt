I'm in the third year of a PhD on moral worth — the chapter I'm currently writing turns on whether worth is best understood as a fact about how an action is explained or as a fact about what the agent takes herself to be doing — and I could use some help predicting how my own supervisor will rule on two cases, because about nine hundred words of my chapter are hostage to the answer. She has given me verdicts on seven cases over the past two years, always crisply and always consistently, and I've written them up below more or less as she gave them to me. Ana runs a lost-property desk and returns a wallet full of cash because the five-star reviews are worth more to her than the money, and she would have kept it had no one been able to trace it: no worth. Ben visits an old friend in hospital purely out of affection, never entertaining a thought of duty, and would have visited even if visiting had been pointless: worth, and not diminished by the absence of duty. Cleo helps a colleague she has been brought up to despise, is wretched about it and believes as she does it that she is betraying her own community's code, but whose attention and effort are entirely governed by the colleague's predicament: worth, and she said more of it than Ben's if anything. Eve keeps a burdensome promise because it was a promise, while also knowing her reputation hangs on it and admitting she'd have kept it for the reputation alone: worth, but thinner than Cleo's. Femi intends to return a wallet and has it knocked out of his hands by a thief so that it rolls into the owner's hallway: right act, no worth. Gita is hypnotised without her knowledge into caring about returning lost property; she returns a wallet genuinely guided by the thought that it isn't hers and that someone is missing it, but she finds the urge foreign and would rather it stopped: no worth, or as near nothing as makes no difference. Ismail returns a wallet because he believes, wrongly though not unreasonably, that his religion requires the return of lost property come what may; he is unmoved by and indeed unaware of the fact that the owner needs the medication inside it: no worth, though the act is right and he is not to be blamed. So: guidance by the right-making feature seems to matter to her more than the agent's own description of what she is doing, the guidance has to be non-deviant, and degrees are real.

The two new cases are a matched pair, and I've built them so that everything except one variable is held fixed. Priya is, by her own admission, almost unmoved by other people's minor troubles, and she finds a wallet containing medication a year into a placement in a care home. In version A she enrolled in that placement herself, partly because she was ashamed of how little she felt for people and partly because the certificate would look good on her CV; in version B the placement was a compulsory component of her degree that she thought a waste of time and completed only to avoid a mark on her record. In both versions the year has left her durably and reliably responsive to what other people need, in both she returns the wallet promptly and at some inconvenience, troubled by the thought of the owner without her medicine, in both she has come to value the change in herself and would not undo it, and in both she would now cite the owner's need as her reason if asked. In both, finally, she'd have got round to posting the wallet back eventually out of a vague sense that she ought to, so there is a baseline of dutifulness in place independent of the placement. What I want from you is: how she will rank A and B against each other and against Ben, Cleo and Eve on her own scale; the two or three candidate rules that fit all seven earlier verdicts and that come apart on the pair; which of them she is most likely running and why, given that she also thinks the phrase acting for the right reasons is doing more work than people notice; where, on each rule, the worth is located — in the year-long choice or in the act of returning the wallet; and the further case I should put to her that would actually settle it. The reading group split three ways on this, two of us convinced the difference between A and B matters and one convinced it obviously doesn't, and none of us could say what would adjudicate. Please don't give me references or tell me who to read — I've done the literature; it's the shape of the rule I'm after, and the argument for preferring one shape to the others.
