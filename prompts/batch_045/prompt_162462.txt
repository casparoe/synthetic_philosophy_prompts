I sit on the board of a small land trust, and this spring we vote on whether to breach a drainage ditch on retired farmland and let the parcel revert to marsh. I raised an unwelcome consideration: if the lives of wild animals are mostly short, hungry and net-negative, then re-creating habitat may add more suffering than it removes — and, symmetrically, some habitat loss might be a welfare gain. A fellow trustee closed the discussion with "nature knows best," meaning it to cover both halves: that a restored marsh will settle into the right sort of self-regulating order, and that it isn't ours to second-guess anyway.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct claims that slogan could be making, from the near-trivial to the substantive to the false, and say which of them would actually do the work she needs. I can see at least four: an epistemic-humility reading (complex systems have unforeseen consequences, so intervention backfires); a homeostatic reading (populations self-regulate around carrying capacity, so there is a stable equilibrium to return to); an optimizing reading (something in nature is good at producing the best available outcome, so whatever it settles on is near-best); and an intrinsic-value reading (natural processes are not ours to improve, even if we could) — but add any I am missing.

Two constraints on the menu. First, the gap between fitness and welfare: whatever selection tracks, it is not the net welfare of the creatures being shaped, and in the r-selected species that will supply most of the vertebrate biomass on that parcel — many offspring, nearly all dying young and badly — a healthy, self-regulating population looks less like a good equilibrium than like a machine for manufacturing very short bad lives. Which readings survive that, and which quietly assume what needs arguing? Second, the arithmetic: given density dependence, does adding or removing habitat change the number of welfare-relevant lives at all, or mostly relocate them and alter how they end? Flag each reading as platitude, contentious but defensible, or false, and say whether it bears on what the board should do with the field.
