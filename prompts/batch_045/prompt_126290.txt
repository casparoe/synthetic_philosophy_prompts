I'm a journalist working on a long magazine piece about the quiet return of "national character" talk — not the old claim that a people has an essence, but the newer version in which researchers report reliable average differences between countries on traits like trust, conscientiousness, or how tightly norms are enforced, and then use those averages to explain aggregate outcomes: institutional quality, growth, cooperation, public-health compliance. Over about six months I've been interviewing two people who have been arguing with each other about this for years: Marta Reuter, who runs a cross-national survey lab, and Tobias Lenz, a political theorist who works on the history of the concept. Separately, each of them is sharper than the published literature makes either side look, and I've more or less talked them into letting me host an adversarial collaboration.

Their actual positions, as they've put them to me: Reuter says the country-level means replicate, that they predict aggregate outcomes better than a lot of what political science relies on, and that the concept has been purified of essentialism — a population-level parameter is not a claim about any individual, and no one on her side says "Germans are like that." She adds that the researcher-degrees-of-freedom worry (which country, which trait, which survey wave) is an ordinary specification-search problem that pre-registration and multiverse analysis already address, and that declining to measure a difference because someone might misuse it makes social science hostage to its worst readers. Lenz replies that countries are not a sample drawn from any population at all, so there is no sampling frame, and the standard errors attached to a "country effect" don't mean what they appear to mean. He says national borders are the wrong unit — religion, language, region, and birth cohort cut differently — that within-country variation dwarfs the between-country differences she is measuring, and that a hedged true claim is reliably taken up as a claim about people anyway, which is a variable she controls by choosing whether to publish at all. He also notes that this research program has been revived after disgrace more than once, and that while the vocabulary changes, the inferential habits don't.

What I want from you is the document they should produce: not a summary of the debate and not a verdict on who wins. Start with a numbered section of claims both of them could actually sign — I'd guess they overlap more than they think (on essences, on overlap of distributions, on the historical abuses, maybe on what would count as a well-specified aggregate parameter). Then a disagreements table in which each disagreement is stated twice, once in wording Reuter would accept for her own view and once in wording Lenz would accept for his, with no strawmanning on either side. Then the cruxes. Three I suspect matter, though use your own judgment: whether the "no sampling frame" objection is fatal or merely forces a different statistical framing (countries as a fixed set, purely descriptive comparisons, and so on); whether the more the aggregate claim gets hedged enough to survive Lenz's objections, the less explanatory work it can do for Reuter — a kind of tradeoff between defensibility and usefulness; and whether the uptake question is empirically tractable at all, i.e. whether they could pre-register a study of what readers actually infer from a hedged aggregate claim, and what each would predict about the effect size. For each crux, write down the test, case, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction in advance, before anyone sees the result. If some part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. Please skip citations and literature reviews entirely — I need the argument, not the footnotes — and keep it tight enough that a reader could follow it in one sitting; it should read like something two people could put their names to, with the residual disagreement left exact rather than blurred.
