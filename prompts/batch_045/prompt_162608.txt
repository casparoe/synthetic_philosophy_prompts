I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and for a couple of years now I have kept a private diary of my own updating behaviour: episodes in which my confidence in some proposition moved without my having learned any proposition with certainty, together with my gut verdict on whether the move was the right one. I would like to hand my students a principle instead of a pile of anecdotes, and the pile will not settle into reflective equilibrium. The entries are these. (1) A glance at a folded cloth in a badly lit closet takes my credences over the colour partition from (.30 red, .50 orange, .20 yellow) to (.70, .20, .10) while leaving the ratios of crimson to vermilion within the red cell and of amber to ochre within the orange cell exactly as they were; I am confident that holding those ratios fixed is correct here. (2) Scratching in the attic raises my credence that a raccoon got in from .10 to .60, and it simultaneously raises, conditional on raccoon, my credence that it came through the soffit from .30 to .65, because the noise now makes me think the soffit is loose; I am equally confident that this within-cell movement is correct and that a plain Jeffrey update on the animal partition alone would get the case wrong. (3) Two only partly trusted reports about the same search-committee outcome arrive an hour apart — an email from a colleague, then a remark I overhear — and I am troubled to find that the order in which I take them changes where I land; my verdict is that it should not. (4) An hour after the closet episode I learn that the closet is lit by a sodium lamp that makes everything look orange; my verdict is that I should redo the earlier update rather than stack a new shift on top of it, and that a framework with no retraction operation is missing something. (5a) Two glasses of wine at dinner drop my confidence in a claim about how a departmental vote went from .90 to .55 while nothing else changes; my verdict is that no update is called for, the new number is noise. (5b) But when I realise I formed that very belief over a semester in which I was chronically exhausted and over-caffeinated, my verdict is that I should move, though only to about .75. (6) An expert who has seen data I have not tells me her credence in a hypothesis went from .20 to .90, and I move to roughly .80; a colleague who shares all of my evidence tells me she is at .70 on the same proposition, and I barely move at all. I take both verdicts to be right and want the difference explained.

My tentative rule is that what makes a shift admissible is its provenance plus a repair procedure: refine the partition until the shift no longer discriminates within any cell, then Jeffrey-conditionalize on the refined partition, holding conditional probabilities inside cells fixed; ignore shifts produced by a change in my own processing rather than by news about the world; and when two shifts arrive, merge them into a single shift before conditionalizing, so that the outcome is order-invariant. I can see two ways this is thin — refinement looks trivializing, since I can always cut finely enough to turn any change into a change between cells, and merging needs a rule I have not supplied — and I do not know how it handles the fact that some processing changes are defeating and some are not.

Please give me the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts: state each clause precisely and say which case motivated it, tell me what it predicts about two cases I have no settled verdict on — (i) I learn that the closet glance was made while I was wearing tinted sunglasses whose effect I cannot quantify, and (ii) I learn tonight that tomorrow I will be shown a photograph that will shift my credence in a colleague's misconduct by some unknown amount in an unknown direction — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what I should say instead in that case. Please close with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in one sentence, either the principle you recommend or the verdict you would have me give up if no single principle does the work.
