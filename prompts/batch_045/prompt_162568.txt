so i fell into this whole anthropic reasoning rabbit hole maybe six months ago (youtube video about the doomsday argument, then a bunch of blog posts, then this reading group on discord for high schoolers that a grad student runs on sundays) and I put together an argument for last weekend's session that I'm now pretty sure proves way to much, but I can't find the broken step and its driving me crazy. Here's the setup, and then the thing I built on it. Two hypotheses about how many humans will ever exist — call them Few (10^11 total) and Many (10^20 total) — and suppose the physics gives us no reason at all to prefer either, so 50/50 priors. Call my birth rank r, roughly 6×10^10, since about 60 billion humans have lived before me. Then: (1) The fact that I exist at all is evidence. A world with more people in it has more slots for me to be one of them, so a hypothesis with N times as many people gets N times the weight, before anything else comes in. (This is the move behind the presumptuous philosopher: it says Many is a billion times more likely than Few, from an armchair, with no telescope.) (2) The fact that I am this person rather than that one is also evidence. I should treat my position among all the people there are as a random draw, so if a hypothesis says there are N people, the chance I'd turn out to be this particular one of them is 1/N. (This is the move behind the doomsday argument: my rank r is a billion times more likely under Few.) (3) But (1) and (2) answer different questions — (1) is about why there's anyone here at all, (2) is about why I'm this one — so I don't have to pick between them, I should use both. (4) Multiply them and they cancel: posterior ∝ prior × N × (1/N) = prior. So my existence plus my exact place in history tells me nothing whatsoever about Few vs Many. No presumptuous philosopher, no doomsday argument, priors untouched. (5) And none of that depends on the numbers or the priors; it only depends on my evidence pinning me down to one exact person-slot. But I always know exactly who I am, at least in principle. (6) So self-locating evidence never tells me anything about how big or how long the world is — not for Few vs Many, not for anything. Including fine-tuning: finding myself in a universe whose constants permit life is supposed to be evidence that such universes aren't vanishingly rare, and on (6) it buys me nothing. (7) That's absurd, since that case is basically why anybody takes anthropic reasoning seriously in the first place.

And then my friend made it worse, which is why I think I'm the confused one. She says the argument proves even more than I thought: if, instead of being handed your exact rank, you're only told something coarse about where you are — say, that you're somewhere in the earliest 1% of all humans who ever live — then the 1/N in (4) never shows up, you just get the same 0.01 on both hypotheses, the N from (1) survives, and you're back to being near-certain of Many. So whether you learn anything seems to depend on how precisely you can point at yourself: vague information gives you a gigantic update and exact information gives you none, which feels exactly backwards. I genuinely don't know whether that's a reductio of the whole thing or a feature I'm supposed to swallow.

So: which step do I give up? I'd much rather keep (1) and (2), they both seem obviously right to me separately and I've seen people defend each, so if something has to go my guess is (3) — but I don't actually know what giving up (3) commits me to, or whether (4) and (5) are quietly doing something they aren't entitled to (is multiplying those two factors even allowed, or is that double counting?). And if the honest answer is that (1) or (2) has to go, tell me which one and what keeping the other one costs me — I want to know what I'm paying either way, not just which line to cross out.
