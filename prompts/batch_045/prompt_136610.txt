For the past eight months I have been the trustee of a modest fund established for my late brother's two children. It is to be drawn down over roughly twenty-five years, and almost nothing about it turns on a single grand decision. What I actually face is a long drift of small ones: whether to renew a policy with a higher deductible, whether to lock money into a slightly illiquid placement for a better yield, whether to help a relative now or preserve capital, whether to rebalance during a bad month. There is also a floor. If the fund's value drops below a certain level, a care arrangement lapses, and nearly everything else I am trying to secure becomes worthless at once.

A friend who works in decision theory told me, more or less, to maximize expected utility and stop worrying. When I asked why, I got two answers that struck me as quite different in character, and I have been turning them over since. I would like a careful comparison of them, because I suspect they do not support the same practical advice.

The first is the familiar coherence argument: the Dutch-book and money-pump style of reasoning. If my preferences are not representable as maximizing expected utility — if they are cyclic, or violate the independence axiom in the way the Allais patterns suggest — then there is a sequence of trades or choices, each of which I am disposed to accept, whose net effect is to leave me with strictly less of everything I care about. Coherence, on this view, is not a substantive moral or prudential demand; it is a consistency condition, and incoherence is a defect you can exhibit without knowing anything about what the agent wants.

The second I have been sketching myself, and I call it the archipelago argument, mainly so that I can stop losing it. Suppose (i) I will face a large number n of decision problems whose outcomes are probabilistically independent of one another; (ii) my good across the whole sequence is the sum of a bounded "local" good contributed by each problem; (iii) in each problem the act I am inclined to choose falls short, in expected local good, of the expected-utility-maximizing act by at least some fixed ε > 0. Then, by a concentration-of-measure bound, the probability that my aggregate good over the sequence fails to fall short of the expected-utility maximizer's goes to zero, and does so exponentially in n. No bookie, no exploiter, no sequential trading, and no assumption that I am dynamically inconsistent: merely that a small, repeated, systematic shortfall in expectation becomes a near-certain shortfall in fact. This is the thought that actually moves me when I sit down to sign things, and I would guess it may be a garbled version of something in the literature — but please assess the argument rather than try to place it. If it is confused, say so plainly and tell me where.

Some questions I would like addressed.

1. How far does each argument actually reach? My sense is that the money-pump machinery is most at home with the probability axioms and with acyclicity, and that it is much less clear how far it gets toward the independence axiom; but I would like your view on whether a genuine money-pump argument for full expected-utility representation exists, and what extra premises it would need. Does the archipelago argument do better or worse on this score? It looks to me as if it speaks directly to independence but only delivers maximization with respect to a local utility function it does not itself derive.

2. Is the archipelago argument independent of expected-utility maximization, or is it simply an application of it one level up — expected utility applied to the grand lottery over the whole sequence, plus additivity, from which per-problem maximization follows? If the latter, is there any residue? A bootstrapping or self-consistency constraint can still bite, and I would like to know whether the same is true of the coherence argument, which also seems to presuppose rather a lot about what it is for one continuing agent to be consistent over time.

3. My floor. Because the problems share one pool of capital and because there is a threshold below which things collapse badly, separability across problems fails: what a given small decision is worth depends on where the fund then stands. What happens to each argument in that setting? Can the archipelago argument be repaired — say, by reinterpreting local good as marginal contribution to the risk of ruin, or by permitting departures from maximization only at a safe distance from the threshold — or does it fall silent? And how does the coherence argument treat a threshold constraint: does it convict the constraint of irrationality, which seems too strong, or does it have nothing to say?

4. Both arguments need a currency of comparison. The coherence argument typically runs on something money-like, traded one-for-one across choices; the archipelago argument runs on an additive local good. Which presupposition is more demanding, and which is more likely to be question-begging against someone whose departure from expected utility in small stakes is not confusion but a genuine concern with the shape of her overall prospects?

5. Finally, the practical upshot. I have to write a standing instruction for a co-trustee and a successor, who will not share my judgment about individual cases. Does accepting one argument rather than the other change what I should tell them — for instance, about whether to evaluate decisions one at a time or by the outcome of the whole programme, and about whether small-stakes caution is permitted or forbidden? If the two arguments diverge in their recommendations for my situation, that is the single thing I most want to know.

I should mention that I am travelling and have no library access, so please argue the points rather than point me to books or papers; I am after the substance, not the sources. Where a question is genuinely contested, say what turns on it. About a thousand words, if you can.
