I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about statues and names by going case by case, and my verdicts don't add up:

- Confederate general on a courthouse lawn, put up by a Jim Crow–era campaign, in a Southern town that now votes to move it: take it down, obviously.
- Colston in Bristol, toppled by protesters with no vote, no hearing, no process at all: I'm not sorry it's gone, and I don't feel bad about the method either. Which bothers me, because I'd be furious if a state government pre-emptively removed the same kind of statue over a local referendum's objection. So procedure seems to matter to me in one case and not the other.
- Lenin statues pulled down across Ukraine in the 2010s: fine — though here I'd of kept one or two somewhere, as evidence.
- A hospital wing named after a paediatrician whose technique still saves kids, and who we now know also ran non-consensual experiments on his own patients: keep the name, add a plaque. Renaming it feels wrong to me.
- A street named after a suffragist who also campaigned hard for compulsory sterilisation: leave it. This one nags at me, because the view we now repudiate is close to the whole reason she was famous.
- The Rhodes statue on the Oxford facade: leave it up, contextualise it. Which dosen't sit well with my first verdict at all.

My rough principle has been: take it down when the thing it was put up to honour is the thing we now repudiate; leave it, maybe with a plaque, when the good is what it honours and the bad is incidental. I've also been toying with something I call endorsement decay — the thought that a monument's endorsement fades with age, so a 300-year-old statue is closer to a ruin than to a statement, and old ones are less urgent than new ones. Both feel half-right, and both have obvious problem cases (a 300-year-old statue that a far-right group still marches to every year, say).
