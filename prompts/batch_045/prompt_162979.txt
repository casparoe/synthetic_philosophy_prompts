You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of social philosophy, collective action, and game theory, and who has spent real time on races to the bottom, positional competition, and the family of failures that now get labelled Moloch or multipolar traps. Please work slowly. Start by reading my cases and, informally, noting which patterns you see and which of my verdicts strike you as unstable or stolen from something else I believe. Only then try to write a principle down; do not settle for a slogan, and do not start from the labels. I do not want reading recommendations or citations of any kind, only your own analysis.

Here is where I am. I use Moloch loosely for a dynamic in which each party's locally sensible escalation leaves everyone worse off and no one can stop escalating. But when I go case by case my verdicts stop lining up, and I want to know what principle I am actually using, whether it holds together, and what it says about the case I cannot decide.

1. Eight families fish a shared lake. Each takes an extra boatload. Verdict: a genuine trap. A quota with real enforcement is justified, and the family that takes the extra boatload is doing something mildly wrong, not merely something unlucky.

2. Two airlines in a mature market cut fares until both are flying at close to marginal cost. Both would rather collude. Verdict: not Moloch at all. This is competition working, travellers win, and any coordination between the firms would be the bad thing rather than the remedy.

3. Two consumer-goods firms escalate advertising until the spending roughly cancels out and prices absorb the cost. Verdict: a trap, and yet I do not blame anyone, I am not sure regulation is warranted, and it feels distinctly less bad than the lake. My guess is that this is because in the lake the harm lands on people who never played, whereas here it mostly lands on shareholders and customers of the two firms.

4. Two jurisdictions bid against each other to host a factory, cutting corporate tax rates and loosening labour rules. Verdict: a trap, and the jurisdiction that undercuts is blameworthy in a way the fishers are not. It is auctioning off something that was not its to auction.

5. Parents in my city hire private tutors so their children can pass a selective exam. Every parent would prefer that nobody did this. Verdict: a trap at the collective level, and yet this parent is doing nothing wrong, I would do it myself, and it does not feel like the tax case at all. This is the pair I cannot reconcile: a unilateral escalation that I excuse in case 5 and condemn in case 4. My only thought is that the tutoring actually teaches the child something, while the tax cut produces nothing and only moves surplus from residents to shareholders. I do not trust that thought.

6. The always-on work culture in my industry. No manager I have met wanted it. A few firms adopted it and won contracts; the rest matched to keep staff and clients; firms that tried to opt out lost people to firms that had not, and two of them were acquired. Everyone is now worse off and almost everyone would be relieved if it were banned. Verdict: the purest trap on my list, and also the one that breaks my own way of thinking about it, because there is no defection to prohibit, no defector to blame, and nothing anyone chose at the moment it mattered. Call it escalation without escalators: the outcome spreads by differential survival rather than by decision.

7. A solvent but illiquid bank, and a rumour. Depositors who withdraw are not wrong, are not blameworthy, and are doing what any of us would do; the failure is a coordination failure with two equilibria rather than a trap. But the waste looks exactly like the waste in the lake, and I do not know why I blame the fishers and not the depositors. That is my second unreconciled pair.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong: the label applies just when (a) each party has a dominant or near-dominant reason to escalate whatever the others do, (b) the escalation is dissipative, burning resources without producing anything anyone outside the escalating group wants, and (c) the parties cannot coordinate because they are too many, too anonymous, or too rarely repeated. Case 6 seems to fail (a) while being my clearest trap. Case 7 seems to satisfy (c) and fail (a). Case 2 fails (b) only. Test this against all seven, repair it, or replace it.

The case I go back and forth on: a group of parents is circulating a pledge not to hire a tutor before year 10, and I have seen the sign-up sheet. I think roughly a third will quietly break it. Signing feels like opting out and also like handing my child's place to the children of the parents who cheat, and I cannot tell whether the pledge is the coordination that dissolves a trap, a sucker's bargain I should refuse, or a new and smaller race in visible virtue.

What I want from you: a principle, or two or three ranked candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without you; the case that motivates each clause; verdicts on at least three cases I have not listed, including at least one selection-driven case like case 6 and at least one where the escalation produces real benefits for people outside the race; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which of those you would overturn, and why overturning that verdict costs less than complicating the principle to save it. Then apply the result to the pledge, tell me what it says I should actually do on Monday, and name one empirical question whose answer would move my verdict on case 3 or case 5.
