You are a professional philosopher who also happens to know international criminal law very well — ideally someone who thinks about thick moral concepts, legal kinds, and how a category gets stabilized by the people who have to apply it. Please think carefully and don't just start typing: spend your first pass brainstorming the logical space (the questions, and which combinations of answers are even available), and only in a second pass place the named positions into the boxes. Take it step by step; I'd rather have a well-built tree than a fast one.

Some context: I'm a novelist, not an academic. My work-in-progress is set mostly in the press camp outside a fictional international tribunal, and a lot of the good scenes are two characters — a prosecutor and a human-rights reporter — fighting about whether what happened to a certain population counts. Reading back through the draft I've realised they're not really having one argument, they're having four or five at once and neither of them notices, and I don't want the disagreement to be a strawman where one side is obviously the villain. So I want a diagnostic instrument I can use two ways: as a genuinely fun quiz for my beta readers and newsletter people (who are literary-fiction readers, so nothing that assumes they've read a word of international law), and as a private check that each character actually holds a coherent package of views rather than a grab bag. Call it a which-theory-of-the-crime-of-crimes-do-you-actually-hold decision tree: 9 to 11 branch questions, each with two or three answer options, landing on something like 10 to 14 leaves, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves.

Here's what I need the questions to do. They have to be concrete cases or plain claims someone can answer without already knowing which camp it leads to — no asking is genocide bad, no asking do you think intent matters, nothing where the label is in the question. The distinctions that seem to be doing the real work in my book are roughly: whether you need something like specific intent (dolus specialis) to destroy the group as such, or whether it's enough that the perpetrators knew their policy would destroy it, or were reckless about that, or merely wanted to terrify or expel or dissolve it and didn't care how; whether the target has to be one of the four kinds of group the 1948 UN Convention lists, given that Lemkin's own usage was much broader and he wanted political and social groups covered; whether destroying a group has to mean killing or rendering its members unable to reproduce, or can mean destroying it as a group — banning the language, breaking up the households, abolishing its legal personality, re-educating the children so nothing is passed on — while every individual survives; how big the part has to be, since the definition says in whole or in part, so does one town count, one valley, one named region, and does it matter whether that part is the remnant the group could have rebuilt from; whether you need a state or an organized plan or whether a crowd, or a diffuse economic process, or a slow bureaucratic policy over decades can be the perpetrator — can there be a genocide with no genocidaires; whether genocide is a distinctive moral kind at all, a wrong against a group's existence that is worse than the same number of individual murders, or whether the moral wrong is just the murders plus a shared structure and the label is doing legal-triggering work rather than moral work; and then the naming question proper, whether the word should be kept strictly for what a court would convict on, or used early and loosely as a warning and prevention trigger, or avoided altogether as a rhetorical escalator that distorts everything downstream.

Two invented wrinkles from my manuscript that I want the tree to actually have to handle, so please don't build something that quietly assumes they're impossible. First: the targeted population in my book is not a nation, race or religion. It's a hereditary guild of river pilots — its own working language, its own initiation and apprenticeship, endogamous in practice but not in principle, spread across three countries, and it recruits as well as reproduces. Second: in one stretch of the book nobody is killed at all. The guild is dissolved, its children are redistributed into other households under new names, its language is criminalised in public; the individuals live, and within a generation the thing they belonged to has no one to be passed on to. And there's a sub-case I love: some of the people who get targeted were only ever classified as guild members by the people persecuting them — they had no such self-identity before the roundups started, so the group is partly brought into existence by being aimed at. I want at least one branch question to turn on whether a group that only exists because it was targeted can be the object of the crime.

Then the part I care most about: build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Give me the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space — the strict Convention orthodoxy, the expansive Lemkin-style view, foresight-or-knowledge-based expansions, the version that includes political groups or politicide, the cultural-destruction version, the structural or slow version, the deflationary view that says the category is not a moral superlative, the legalist view about when to use the word, the prophylactic early-warning view. Where a cell in the space is empty, tell me why you think nobody lives there: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And where two leaves your questions can't actually separate end up in the same box, flag that rather than pretending the tree sorted them. One last thing — please don't give me a further-reading list, footnotes, case citations or anything I'd need a library to check; I can't verify any of that and it would just slow me down. Just the instrument, written so a smart reader with no background could take it and be surprised by their result.
