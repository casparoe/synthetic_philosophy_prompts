I've been hanging around the alignment-adjacent side of philosophy for a while, and I keep coming back to a view I cant quite shake, so I'd like someone to take a real swing at knocking it down. The view is that HCH — the recursive set-up where you have a human who can ask questions of copies of itself, each of which can ask further copies, with the whole tree held together by some rule for breaking questions into subquestions and recombining the answers — is a pretty good idealization of what it is for a human to deliberate well. I mean that more modestly than it sounds: not "this is what a superintelligent system should be aimed at", and not really a theory of ideal moral judgement either. I mean it as an idealization of *my* deliberation — what I would land on if I had unlimited time, no pressure to sound decisive in the moment, and the ability to actually think through every subquestion instead of hand-waving past the ones I find uncomfortable.

What I want is the defeater map. What would have to be true for that to be false? I care about the difference between whats fatal and whats merely awkward, and I especially want the things that people who *like* this model would themselves concede count against it, not just the objections from people who never liked it in the first place.

Some of what I've been turning over, so you know where I'm starting from:

- Decomposition-dependence. Its not that the tree fails to give an answer; its that in the cases I actually care about (moral and political questions, mostly) how you break the question up seems to do all the work. Aggregate by persons or by person-moments, ask "what should be done" versus "what should each actor do", and you get different outputs out the top. To make the limit even well-defined I've been imagining a variant where the decomposition rule is fixed in advance — a grammar for which subquestions are legal — and then asking whether the fixed point depends on the grammar. If it does, is the whole thing just "whatever the decomposition encoded", wearing a hat?
- Correlated error. Every node is me, or someone shaped a lot like me, with my habits of motivated reasoning. Nothing in the set-up obviously cancels those, and it might amplify them. A bigger tree could just be a louder version of my own blind spots, in which case "more deliberation" and "more of the same mistake" come apart.
- Fixed points that are self-consistent but wrong. I can imagine the tree settling on something no node up the chain would have started from, and staying there under further consultation because every subanswer supports the others. What would that even look like from inside the run, and how would I tell it apart from having actually got it right?
- The new-concept problem. Some answers aren't in the span of the parts: results that need a definition nobody in the tree has yet, or cases where the right move is to reject the question's framing outright. If HCH can only recombine whats already distributed across the humans, then its an idealization of being well-organized rather than of thinking well.
- The endorsement test I keep toying with: hand one human the full transcript of a run and a week, and see if they still endorse the output; if they revise it, the run wasn't ideal. But that just pushes the question up a level, and I genuinely dont know whether that regress is a bug or a feature.

Anyway — what I find most persuasive right now is the decomposition worry, so please dont spend the whole answer there just because I flagged it first; tell me if I'm overweighting it. And does the view actually *risk* anything? My sense is that it predicts convergence: bigger trees, more time, better decompositions should produce answers that are more stable, less order-dependent, less sensitive to who happens to be in the tree — and if people actually run these trees and get fragmentation instead, that counts against it. But I'm not sure how much of that is a prediction and how much the view can absorb post hoc.

Which brings me to what I'm most suspicious of in myself: I worry I've quietly defined "deliberated well" as "whatever HCH outputs", in which case the view is unfalsifiable and boring. If it has been insulated like that, say so plainly. And then tell me whether that's actually a problem for a claim of this kind, or whether every idealization of deliberation — reflective equilibrium, ideal-advisor accounts, whatever else — is in the same boat and the interesting question is just which one insulates itself more honestly.

No need for citations or reading lists, I've read around plenty; I just want the arguments and the ranking.
