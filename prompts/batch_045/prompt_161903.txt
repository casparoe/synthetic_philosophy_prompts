I've been argueing with my housemate for about a week now and I want a third opinion before we give up. He works in nonlinear dynamics, on systems where a tiny perturbation genuinely blows up in a mathematically precise way, and he rolls his eyes every time someone says chaos about history. I think he's half right and I want to work out which half, using Sarajevo as the test case. The facts as I have them, roughly as I remember them and correct me gently if I've got a detail wrong: 28 June 1914, Franz Ferdinand and Sophie visiting Sarajevo, an earlier bomb in the morning that bounces off the car and hurts people in the vehicle behind, then later the driver takes a wrong turn, the car stalls right in front of the cafe where Princip happens to be standing, two shots, two deaths. Then the July crisis: the German blank check to Vienna, an ultimatum to Serbia written to be refused, Austrian declaration of war, Russian mobilisation going from partial to full, German declarations of war, the Schlieffen plan's requirement of marching through Belgium, Britain in by the first week of August.

Here's the scaffolding I want you to use, because I really don't want a generic history-is-contingent essay. Separate two things that I think everybody runs together. Call the first trigger sensitivity: how much the outcome depends on which particular small event fired. Call the second outcome robustness: how likely some event of the same broad shape — a general European war — was within a given window, regardless of which trigger did the firing. My hunch is 1914 is extremely high on the first and close to one on the second, and that the for-want-of-a-nail picture gets its force from sliding between them. So, concretely: walk the sequence and say, at each step, which axis you're on — the bouncing bomb, the wrong turn, the stalled engine versus the blank check, the unacceptable ultimatum, the move to full mobilisation, the Belgian route. And give me a rough number for what I'll call the counterfactual horizon: conditional on the archduke not being shot that afternoon, how long before a war of roughly that scale and shape breaks out anyway? A year, five years, decades, never? My own number is about two years; my housemate says never without this exact trigger.

Then the bits I actually care about. One, resolution: I suspect contingency claims are claims at a grain of description, and a great-power war sometime in the 1910s and a war beginning in early August via an invasion of Belgium have totally different modal profiles — so what grain is the proverb really operating at, and does it survive being made precise? Two, where the metaphor breaks: chaos is about deterministic systems with a specifiable state space and dynamics you can in principle iterate, and history has neither, so is the proverb doing any real causal work or is it only a warning about neglect and about how bad we are at imagining alternatives? Three, what the case does to the theory in the other direction: is Sarajevo evidence for small causes with big effects, or is it a counterexample where a spectacularly contingent trigger sits on top of a structure that was going to deliver something like this anyway — and if so, where would you look for the actual nail? Related idea I want you to test rather than accept: when background pressure is high, triggers crowd, so removing one trigger just frees up another, which makes the interesting counterfactual not removing the trigger but removing the pressure. Four, responsibility: if the trigger is knife-edge contingent but the outcome is robust, is Princip less responsible for the war, more, or just differently responsible than people usually assume? Please be disciplined about the mapping and tell me where your framework goes silent instead of forcing it to speak — especially on what would have happened instead, which I suspect is where all of this bottoms out.
