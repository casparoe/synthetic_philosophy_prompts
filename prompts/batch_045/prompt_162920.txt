At work I build the eval harness for a bunch of forecasting-style LLM tasks, so I basically live in log loss and Brier scores, reliability diagrams, calibration-before-vs-after-planner, that whole world — plus a bit of market stuff, since some of our internal tooling prices model outputs as if they were contracts in an automated market maker (LMSR-ish), and I've read enough Kelly/log-utility and online-learning-with-regret stuff to be dangerous. A few months ago someone on infra added what we call the shakedown: it takes every probability a model states about a family of related events (marginals, and conjunctions/disjunctions when we bother to ask for them), treats each stated number as a price the model would buy or sell a $1-if-true contract at, and then runs a small solver to look for a portfolio of those contracts that makes the model lose points guaranteed — negative score under every assignment of truth values to the atomic events. It finds one against basically every model we test, including ones that beat our human baseline on the actual tournament metrics, and also against one model that is beautifully calibrated and nearly useless (it says 50% to almost everything and rarely resolves anything). I went looking for where this idea comes from and ended up on Dutch book arguments, which on the surface look like exactly what we built: violate the probability calculus and a bookie can arrange for you to lose come what may; obey it and no such arrangement exists. But I cant tell whether my shakedown is really the same claim or a weaker/stronger one wearing the same clothes.

So here's the ask. Take the Dutch book case for probabilism and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: proper scoring rules, calibration versus refinement, arbitrage-free pricing and market makers, risk sensitivity / log utility, regret bounds and the folklore that no-regret learning ends up looking Bayesian-ish under repeated play, and ensemble or mixture-of-experts dominance. Do both directions — incoherence implies a book can be made, coherence implies no book can be made — and also do the diachronic version about updating and conditional probability if there's a clean translation, and separately the version that apparently doesn't use bets or money at all and just says you'd score better if you reported some other set of numbers instead. I want it honest: if a step of the argument only works because the target vocabulary quietly assumes it, say so rather than smuggling it in.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Four things keep bugging me and I want to know which of them are real. One: our solver assumes the model would take either side of any bet at its stated price, including on contracts referencing events we never actually asked it about — so how much of "bookable" is a fact about the model and how much is a fact about an extrapolation we made? Two: our models are scored with log loss, so they're effectively log-utility in score rather than linear in it, and I have a vague sense that the identification of fair betting odds with credences needs linearity (or tiny stakes) somewhere — where exactly does that break? Three: bookability doesn't seem to track being any good at forecasting, and calibration and coherence feel like different properties entirely (our useless 50%-sayer is coherent-ish, our sharp miscalibrated model might be worse by score but no more bookable), so what is incoherence supposed to cost me, and why isn't it showing up in my metrics? Four, the big one: in my vocabulary "you're exploitable" is just a fact about an adversarial environment, and "some other report you could have made would have scored better" is just a fact about a comparison class — is the philosophical claim that incoherent credences are irrational anything more than one or both of those, or does it say something my vocabulary has no slot for at all? For each piece that doesn't translate, tell me whether that's my framework missing something (a notion of the agent's own standpoint, maybe, or of self-defeat) or whether it's evidence the original argument is less substantive than it looks.
