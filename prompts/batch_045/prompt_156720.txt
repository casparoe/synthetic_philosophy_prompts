The thesis, short version: functions are selected effects, but the population doing the selecting doesn't have to be a lineage of organisms. My central case is somatic cell-lineage selection. Within a single body, cell lineages compete — stem cells turning over in a tissue, clones expanding in a tumor — so a trait of a cell lineage, say an activating mutation in a growth-factor receptor or a change in a cell-surface protein that lets a clone dodge immune clearance, has a selected effect relative to that population of cells even though it has no organismal selection history at all and is straightforwardly bad for the organism. So I generalize the etiological account: function is indexed to a population of replicating or persisting units, and the unit has to be the right kind of thing, not necessarily an organism. I call this the indexed selected-effect account. The payoff I advertise is three cases the standard history-based account handles badly: somatic clones, de novo genes that arise out of previously non-coding sequence with no selection history behind them, and horizontally transferred genes whose role in the donor lineage isn't their role in the recipient. Second half of the argument: once functions are indexed, dysfunction isn't the absence of a function but a conflict between functions indexed to different populations — the clone versus the organism — and I claim the causal-role story can't say that, because on that story there's one containing system and one capacity being analyzed at a time.
