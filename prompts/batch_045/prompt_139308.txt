Next month I am presenting to the charter revision commission of a mid-sized American city a proposal to adopt what I call bounded futarchy, and I want a Q&A prep sheet.

The proposal: for an enumerated and narrow class of administrative decisions — transit fare structures, density variances, street-parking pricing, sanitation procurement — the city would commit to the following sequence. A sortition citizens' assembly fixes the welfare metric and the horizon (say, median commute time and rent burden for affected residents at twenty-four months) and the option set; a subsidized conditional prediction market then prices that metric under each option; the highest-priced option is adopted automatically unless it fails a floor requiring it to beat the projected status quo by a stated margin. Elected officials settle what the city is aiming at, traders wager only on what will in fact happen. Three features are mine rather than standard: participation limited to residents of the affected districts with a flat cap on stake and subsidized losses; the metric set upstream by the assembly rather than by the council; and a circuit breaker — realized outcomes are audited against closing prices, and a miss rate above a fixed threshold suspends the mechanism pending review by the commission.

The commission is six people: a retired state appellate judge who chairs it and worries about unlawful delegation of legislative authority; the city's finance director, concerned with market integrity, liquidity, and staff cost; a staffer from a municipal workers' union local who has noticed that procurement is on my list; a community organizer from a neighborhood that has been rezoned repeatedly and that has been on the losing side of every aggregate statistic anyone has ever quoted about it; a political scientist who studies direct democracy and has argued that sortition bodies are captured by the already-engaged; and a data scientist from a local tech firm who already likes prediction markets and will defend me when I do not want defending.

Give me the objections in the order they will actually arrive, each phrased the way that particular member would phrase it, with a ninety-second reply, what I should concede outright, what I should refuse to concede, and where each reply is softest. Tell me which two I should pre-empt in my opening two minutes, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
