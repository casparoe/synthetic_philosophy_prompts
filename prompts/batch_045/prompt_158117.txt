I've been going to a Buddhist centre for about six years and I help run a small study group there, and because I'm the one who's read a bit of philosophy I end up being the person people come to with hard decisions — someone whose father is dying and who wants to know whether to tell him the truth, someone being pushed at work to shade the numbers on a report, that kind of thing. So I finally sat down to write out where I actually stand, and my partner (who did a bit of philosophy at uni years ago) said my list doesn't hang together but couldn't tell me where. I don't have the books here and I can't look anything up, so please work from the ideas themselves rather than from who said what — and please don't just tell me which named position I should adopt, I want to know which of my own claims is doing the damage. Here's the set:

(a) I think Buddhist ethics is basically consequentialist. The bad thing is dukkha, the whole path is about ending suffering, and I read the five precepts as instruments for that rather than as commands with authority of their own.
(b) I think karma is a matter of intention: what fixes the moral quality of an act is the motive behind it — greed, aversion and confusion versus their opposites — not how things turn out. So a rescue that goes badly is still fine karmically and a smooth act done for a rotten reason isn't. I also don't find convincing the move that intention only matters because of its downstream effects on your character; I want to say the intention is where the karma is, right there at the time.
(c) In practice I think you don't weigh the precepts case by case, you just keep them.
(d) I think the Mahayana stories where a bodhisattva breaks a precept out of compassion — lying to save someone's life, or the one about the ship's captain who kills a man who is about to murder everyone aboard and takes the consequences on himself — are evidence that consequences override the precepts, so they support (a).
(e) I think no-self is what rules out a rights-style or dignity-style reading: if there aren't ultimately separate persons, there's nothing to be inviolable, so any constraint has to be justified by what it does downstream. That isn't decoration, it's part of my reason for (a).
(f) But I also don't think it's just utilitarianism in foreign dress: the point is the transformation of the agent — uprooting greed, aversion and confusion, growing compassion — not a ledger of how much suffering got prevented.

When (a) and (b) come apart in a case, like the woman who tells her dying father a softened version of the diagnosis partly out of love and partly because she can't face the conversations that would follow, my way of coping is to say two things at once: it was the right thing to do because of the suffering it prevented, and its karmic quality is mixed becuase the motive was mixed.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? Rank whatever you find by severity, for each one say exactly what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me, and tell me whether my two-level way with the dying-father case actually repairs (a) and (b) or is just papering over them. And if there's one revision that cleans up the most, name it — I'd much rather drop a single claim than rebuild the whole view.
