I'm not a philosopher — I fix bikes for a living and think about this stuff on the drive home. I've been trying to work out which things actually matter for their own sake and how much, and I can't get one rule to fit all the cases. So here's my gut on a bunch of them, and I want to know what principle I'm secretly using, or what principle I should be using instead.

- Thermostat switching the furnace on: nothing matters to it. Same for a houseplant, even a venus flytrap that snaps shut on a fly.
- Mosquito I swat: nothing. The same mosquito drowning slowly in my beer over a minute: I feel a flicker, and I don't think that's just squeamishness.
- One ant on the pavement: nothing, I step on it without a thought. But I won't pour boiling water on the ant nest by my back door, and I think that's a real judgement and not just the creeps. And yet I really resist the idea that enough ants add up to more than my dog — even though I just said the nest thing. That's the pair I can't square: each ant seems to count for a little, but no pile of ants seems to reach the dog.
- Shrimp: I feel a bit bad about the boiling thing and I still eat them. Fish: deffinitely something at stake. Chicken: more. Dog: a lot more. Pig: my gut says about the same as the dog, and I eat bacon, which I know is me being inconsistent — I just don't know which of those two has to give.
- Painlessly killing something in its sleep: dog, big loss. Fish, small loss. Shrimp, nothing. Houseplant, nothing. But a machine built to track one long project, deleted the day before it would have finished: I get a flicker there too, and I can't tell if that's a real verdict or me being soft about an object.
- A creature that reacts to damage, learns to avoid it, struggles to escape, but where nothing feels like anything to it (imagine one built that way, or a person born unable to feel pain): I care much less about it than about the real thing, even though from outside you couldn't tell them apart. So behaviour isn't the test.
- A person under anaesthetic or in a coma they'll wake up from: nothing is going on right now, but killing them is awful. So the present-moment stuff can't be the whole story; having a future you're headed into seems to be part of it.
- A creature that can suffer but can't remember anything or anticipate anything — every bad moment is brand new, it never sees one coming: I feel painlessly killing it is worse than killing the shrimp but less bad than the dog, and I also feel its suffering counts fully while it's happening. Both feel right to me and they seem to pull in different directions.
- Same pain, same intensity, same length, in a chicken and in a person: I feel the person's matters more. But "person" can't be the actual reason, that's just species snobbery. Richness of the mind, or having a picture of yourself going forward? If that's the reason, then a machine with a much richer mind than a chicken would outrank the chicken, and I'm not ready to say that.
- An hour of mild misery versus one second of agony: I actually think the hour can be worse. So tiny amounts do pile up over time inside one life — which makes it stranger that I won't let them pile up across different lives.
- One I made up: a creature whose experience runs in two streams at once that can't share anything with each other, each with its own feelings. I don't feel like that's two full creatures, more like one and a bit. No idea what to do with that.

The rule I keep coming back to is: something matters to a thing iff swapping it out for a perfect copy that behaves identically but has nothing going on inside would lose something for it; and how much a bad stretch matters = how bad it feels × how long it lasts × how much of a future it has a stake in; and you add those up within a life but not across lives. That last clause feels like I bolted it on just to save the ant case.

So: is there a cleaner rule that gets most of this? What clause is motivated by which of my verdicts? Which verdict is the one I should deffinitely drop? And what does the rule say about the cases I haven't settled — the ants in my back garden, an octopus (I feel way more strongly about octopuses than about fish and I don't know why), a bit of lab-grown brain tissue in a dish, a computer system that talks like it has feelings and long-term projects, and an animal whose pain has been dialled down to 5% so it still flinches and learns but barely minds?
