So: you're a philosophical aesthetician who has spent your career on the metaphysics of artworks and who has also done a bit of expert-witness work in art fraud cases. Think carefully before you commit — do a first pass of brainstorming for yourself about how the two accounts below could come apart, note the cases where they'd say the same thing, and only then write the answer.

The two accounts I want compared. Call the first one the look-again view: an original and a perfect forgery really can differ aesthetically, because knowing where an object came from changes which of its features you can actually see and which you even bother looking for; the difference shows up in how the thing gets looked at over time, not in a single glance, so "you can't tell them apart" settles nothing. Call the second one the who-did-it view: what a forgery destroys isn't a look but a speaker — a work is closer to an utterance or an act than to a pattern, its value is the value of something done by a particular person at a particular time, and a forgery is a lie about who acted, so what you lose with a copy is the person, not the picture.

Here's the case I can't get either of them to handle, I've been calling it the twin problem in my notes. An artist paints two versions of the same picture in the same fortnight, same hand, same materials, same everything, and she genuinely doesn't mind which one gets called the original. A century later one of them has an unbroken chain of ownership and the other one's paperwork was destroyed in a flood, so it's now merely "attributed to". Both had her hand on them and nobody is lying about either. My worry is that both accounts seem to say the two canvases should be worth the same, while the market and the visitor behaviour say otherwise — so either the accounts are missing something or the extra value is paperwork-worship after all.

What I want back, in the end, is exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, no intro and no summing-up at the end. In this order: (1) where the two accounts genuinely disagree, in one crisp sentence; (2) the thing they agree on that neither of them would enjoy admitting; (3) what the look-again view says about the twin problem; (4) what the who-did-it view says about it, and whether it does any better; (5) your call on which one survives, and what it implies for people who feel short-changed by an honestly labelled copy. Plain English I can paraphrase for a general reader, no term of art I'd have to stop and define, and keep the whole thing under about 250 words.
