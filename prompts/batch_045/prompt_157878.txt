I'm a senior at a large public high school in Ohio, and I host a student-run philosophy podcast that my school posts alongside its visiting-lecture series. Our next guest is a philosopher who writes on pain for a general audience and who defends what I would call the motivational account of pain's badness: the claim that what makes a pain bad for the person who has it is not how the pain sensorily feels, but the fact that it pushes that person to stop it, escape it, or avoid it in the future. On her version, the unpleasantness of pain is not a further felt quality layered on top of the sensation; it is that push itself. The evidence she leans on is the dissociation literature — subjects who can still locate a pain and report its quality and strength, and who yet say, without any visible distress, that it does not bother them and that they feel no urge to get away from it.

I have roughly thirty-five minutes with her and want nine or ten questions, none of them the kind of opener that just lets her restate the view. Assume a bright listener with no philosophy background, so gloss any term of art when you use it. For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Her stock moves are: that the dissociation is never complete, since the affected subjects still withdraw; that her claim is constitutive rather than causal, so it is beside the point that we ordinarily cite a pain's badness to explain our avoidance rather than the reverse; that any desire for pain is instrumental, aimed at something other than the pain; and that a two-factor view which simply adds a felt quality to the motivational push is not a genuine rival. Please do not build any question around asking her to name books or papers — our listeners have nothing to look things up in.

The lines of questioning I want covered, at least one question apiece:

How clean the dissociation evidence actually is. If the subjects still flinch, still guard the limb, or still rate intensity accurately, then something aversive survives at the level of reflex or habit, and I want her to say whether that counts as the kind of push her account needs or whether the push has to be something the subject could in principle act for a reason.

The mirror case. Stipulate a subject, M, whose injury-detection system is fully intact and driving the whole pattern — flinching, guarding, asking for medication, insisting that what just happened to him was awful — but who reports, with no apparent confusion, that there is nothing it feels like. Ask whether M is badly off at the time, and press on whichever way she goes: if he is, something besides how a state feels is doing all the welfare work; if he is not, the mirror image of her central case is symmetric with it and the dissociation evidence no longer favors her side over the sensory one.

The two-drug pair. Suppose we had one drug, A, that removes the urge to escape while leaving the felt quality and intensity unchanged, and another, B, that removes the felt quality entirely while leaving the urge intact. If badness tracks the push, then A is the one that helps and B is not, and I want her to say whether she is willing to bite down on that, or whether she wants to describe what A does as removing the badness only because the subject reports not minding.

Scaling. Whatever makes pain bad should get more of it when the pain gets stronger or lasts longer. Ask which of the two quantities her account has badness track, and then follow up on her likely answer that intensity talk is just talk about how hard the system is recruiting avoidance resources: the follow-up should use a stipulated case in which a subject's reports of how strong the sensation is and how much it bothers her come apart in both directions over time, and ask what the account then predicts about which pains are worse.

Wanted pain. There are people who seek out pain — contact sports, endurance running, deliberate self stimulation — and if badness is being such as to move you away, then a pain you are moved to seek is not bad for you while you seek it. Follow up on her instrumental-desire reply with the stipulated pure case: a runner who wants the burn in her legs precisely because of how it feels, and not for the time, the achievement, or anything downstream of it.

The direction of explanation. Ordinary thought has it that we escape pain because it is bad, and her account reverses that order. Ask her what, if anything, the reversal explains, and follow up on the constitutive-versus-causal move: if the account is only telling us what badness consists in, does it still have anything to say about why pain gives a person a reason to act, rather than merely causing her to act?

Coverage. Newborns and many nonhuman animals almost certainly lack anything recognizable as a reason-responsive motivational state, yet we treat their pain as a welfare matter. Ask what the minimal push has to be for the account to cover them, and follow up with the collapse worry: if the minimal state turns out to be something that any creature with an intact injury-detection system has, the account may have stopped being an alternative to the sensory view and become a redescription of it.

The unmoved mover. Stipulate a subject with a frontal syndrome that leaves her reporting severe, distressing pain while doing nothing about it — no guarding, no requests for relief, no change in what she chooses. Ask whether she is badly off, and follow up on the apparent implication that the account makes welfare hostage to how much a person happens to be moved by anything at all.

Finally, one closing question about what would count against the account — what finding, clinical or otherwise, would make her give it up — together with a follow-up on the practical upshot I keep getting asked about by listeners: if she is right, is the thing clinicians should be measuring and treating the strength of the sensation or the size of the push, and do those ever come apart in ways that should change practice?
