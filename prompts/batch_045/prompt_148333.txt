My friend and I spent a good part of last night arguing about moral foundations theory, and I came away unable to tell whether I had been disagreeing with the theory or merely with his version of it. I would be grateful for an audit of the position he defended. What follows is my best reconstruction of it, as nearly in his own words as I can manage:

"Moral judgements run on a handful of innate foundations — harm/care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity and liberty — each of which evolved to cope with a different recurring problem of living in groups. Liberals are effectively running on only two of them, care and fairness; conservatives run on the full set, which is why they can build coalitions around nation, tradition and religion and the left cannot. Your profile is largely temperamental: substantially heritable, more or less fixed by early adulthood, and it pulls you into a political camp rather than the other way about. Judgement comes first and reasoning comes afterwards. Present people with a harmless taboo violation and they condemn it instantly, flounder when asked to say what was wrong with it, and then manufacture reasons on the spot. So a political argument is never really an argument; both sides are rationalising intuitions they were always going to have. And once you see that, you see that there is no answer to the question of who is right. Morality is a bundle of evolved reactions, people are wired with different weightings of them, and asking whose weighting is correct is like asking whose taste in food is correct. The most that can fairly be said is that the right, having more foundations in play, commands a wider moral vocabulary, and therefore understands the left better than the left understands it."

I objected at several points and was not convinced by my own objections. Three in particular I could not settle. First, whether the "liberals run on two foundations" line, as he put it, is what the research actually claims, or whether something has gone missing in the retelling. Second, whether the claim that reasoning arrives after judgement, even if we grant it, supports what he took it to support — it struck me that it might be a claim about the order in which things happen to us rather than about whether our reasons are any good, but he said that distinction was evasive. Third, the step from "our moral judgements have a psychological explanation" to "there is no fact of the matter". I accused him of committing the genetic fallacy. He replied that if you learnt your belief that two and two make four was being produced by a brain lesion you would abandon it, and that if I thought the moral case was relevantly different I should say precisely how. I could not.

I also tried him with a hypothetical. Suppose a large longitudinal study found that foundation profiles are strongly heritable, stable across the life course, and better predictors of later party identification than party identification is of them. He said that would settle the matter outright and show that there is no fact of the matter about whose political morality is correct. I doubt it settles anything of the kind, but I could not say what it would establish if it were true, or what it would leave exactly where it was.

Could you go through his account claim by claim and separate what the theory's proponents would accept, what is a defensible simplification for a popular treatment, and what they would reject as a misstatement? Please flag anywhere his summary runs together two things the framework keeps apart, and note where he has quietly converted a relative claim into an absolute one. Where he draws a conclusion — about who is right, or about what the psychological findings would show — say whether the conclusion survives once the misstatements are corrected, and whether the argument as he gives it cuts against the left and the right symmetrically or only against one of them. Finally, I should say that I am not a psychologist; I have read one popular book on this, and he has read the same one more carefully than I have, which may be the whole difficulty.
