Next term I'm teaching Slaughterhouse-Five in a sophomore metaphysics seminar — half time module, half philosophy-through-fiction — and I've taught it before with the usual "are the Tralfamadorians eternalists?" prompt. The essays were all identical, so I want something sharper this time, and I'd like you to stress-test my angle before I build the handout around it.

Here's roughly the angle. The obvious bit everyone goes for: the Tralfamadorians hold that every moment exists at once, that past, present and future are just regions you can look at, and Billy keeps coming loose from the chronological order of his own life, landing in his childhood, the firebombing, the alien zoo, his own death, in no causal order. Fine — textbook B-theory. What I want to press on instead is a problem I don't think the standard eternalist account of temporal experience handles, and then ask whether the novel is secretly evidence for it.

Call it the sequencing problem. Eternalists normally explain why experience seems to flow, and why we seem to be located at one time rather than another, by pointing to indexical belief about where you sit in the block, plus asymmetric memory (you remember earlier, not later), plus the causal arrow. Fine. But now take a subject whose conscious stream isn't aligned with the causal order of the events it contains — which is exactly Billy — and yet from the inside it's still one directed stream with a now in it. So: does Billy's case show that this package is only describing a contingent feature of creatures like us (causal order and experiential order happen to line up) rather than explaining what temporal experience is? Or does it cut the other way and support the B-theory, on the grounds that the only way to make sense of a subject who can be at any point in his own life and still have a unified present is to say all those times are equally real?

Then the half where I think the book is just wrong, and I want that said plainly. The Tralfamadorian metaphysics is doing consolation work — it's what lets Billy bear Dresden and his own death — and it does that by sliding from "every moment is equally real" to "nothing can be changed, so don't bother grieving, and free will is an illusion." I take it eternalism gives you none of that: the block can contain your deliberations and your choices just as happily as it contains the bombing, and the Tralfamadorians themselves are the ones who end the universe, which they can see coming and will do anyway. Separately: seeing all of time at once looks like it needs a standpoint outside time, and a block universe has no outside. Is the novel committed to something incoherent there, and does that matter for the reading or is it just a bit of stage machinery?

Last thing, the bit I keep going back and forth on: Billy's episodes are also legible as symptoms. The book gives you a psychiatrist, a head injury, a man who can't say what happened to him in the war, and a refrain pinned to every death. On that reading the time travel is dissociation dressed up as ontology, and my whole angle collapses into a metaphor for trauma. Which reading does the book actually earn, and can I run both in a seminar without cheating?

Give me the reading I'd hand out: what the novel shows about the sequencing problem, the one or two scenes you'd put on the table, where my angle is forced or just mistaken, and what someone who works on the metaphysics of time would say the book adds that the journal articles don't. Don't quote the text at me, I have it. Prose, under 600 words — it's going on the handout.
