Next month I am defending a thesis chapter on eliciting latent knowledge, and I want a question-period prep sheet written for the specific room I will be standing in, not a generic inventory of objections to the ELK problem.

The chapter defends a proposal I call Blindfold Consensus Reporting. Here is the version of the setup I use, so that you are attacking my argument and not a neighbouring one. A large neural predictor is trained only to predict future camera frames of a vault containing a diamond; it never sees labels about whether the diamond is present. At deployment we want to know whether the diamond is still in the vault in the case where the feed has been replaced by a fake that shows the diamond intact, so that the human overseer, who sees only the feed, is systematically wrong. The usual move is to train a reporter: a second model that takes the predictor's activations as input and answers our question, trained only on episodes where the diamond is definitely present and the feed is definitely honest. The familiar failure mode is that the reporter learns to tell us what the feed appears to show, or what a human looking at the feed would say, rather than reading off the predictor's internal model of the world.

Blindfold Consensus Reporting adds two things. First, two reporters are trained in parallel, each seeing the predictor's activations plus exactly one of two independent camera feeds, and each blind to which feed the other one saw. Second, during training a separate generator, co-trained adversarially against the reporters, tampers with one randomly chosen feed per episode in a way that is meant both to look like a plausible real hack and to maximise the disagreement between the two reports; the training loss penalises that disagreement. My argument is that because either feed can be the tampered one and neither reporter knows which, the cheapest stable strategy is for both reporters to treat the feed as defeasible evidence and read the truth off the predictor's world-model, rather than reporting the feed or simulating a viewer of it. As a diagnostic I also propose the one-eyed probe: withhold one feed from a reporter entirely and test whether its answers track the content of the withheld feed above chance.

The room is a university interpretability and alignment reading group of about fifteen people. Most of them are mechanistic interpretability researchers who work on sparse autoencoders, linear probes and circuits in open-source language models, and who think in terms of features, superposition and distribution shift. Three or four of them regard the vault setup as a misleading toy and think the honest version of this question is how to get truthful answers out of deployed language models. Two people have their own competing proposal, which trains a reporter on a curriculum of counterfactual scenarios generated with the help of the model itself, and will be listening for whether my method is a special case of theirs. One senior member is sympathetic to reporter-based schemes in general but denies that the evidence supports any clean separation between a model's world-model and its output behaviour. Nobody in the room works on formal verification, and nobody is a moral philosopher.

Draft the prep sheet as follows. Give me roughly eight objections I will actually get from this room, ranked by how much trouble each would cause me if I handled it badly, and phrased the way these people would phrase them, in their vocabulary rather than in the vocabulary of philosophy or of the alignment blogosphere. At least three of the objections must be internal to Blindfold Consensus Reporting, in the sense that they would not arise for reporter-based schemes generally; I especially want the ones that exploit the two-feed assumption, the blindness condition, or the fact that the tampering is produced by a generator that shares a training loop with the reporters. For each objection give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, say explicitly what I should concede before I start replying, and mark where the reply is soft or depends on something I have not actually shown. Tell me which objections are really objections to the framing rather than to the mechanism, so I know which ones I can defuse with a concession and which I have to answer on the merits. Finally, name the question I am hoping nobody asks, explain why it is the dangerous one, and give me the honest answer in case I have to give one.

Two practical constraints: the first answer I give sets the tone for the rest of the session and I will have about ninety seconds for it, and the senior member is likely at some point to demand a concrete, checkable prediction about what we would observe in the predictor's learned features if my story about the reporters were correct and what we would observe if they had instead learned to discriminate my generator's artefacts.
