I am deciding whether to spend the next year of research on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and before I commit I want a diagnosis of why the sub-debate about what pleasures have in common seems not to move. I have read enough of it to recognize the moves on both sides in advance, and that is the worry.

Here is where I take it to stand. The objection — that the class of pleasant experiences has no interesting common feature, and that hedonism therefore cannot be a unified theory of what makes a life go well — is old, with versions of it going back to antiquity, and it has been pressed repeatedly since. The positions cluster roughly as follows:

(A) Qualitative or feeling-tone views: pleasantness is a distinctive introspectible phenomenal property, a positive hedonic tone, additional to the other sensory or cognitive qualities of the experience and to one's attitude toward it.

(B) Attitudinal, desire-based, or functional views: pleasantness consists in the subject's standing in some pro-attitude toward the experience, or in a functional role (being disposed to sustain or repeat it), and no shared felt quality is required or even expected.

(C) Fittingness and reasons views: pleasures are the experiences it is fitting, or for which there is reason, to enjoy — the unity is normative rather than psychological.

(D) Deflationary or eliminativist views: there is nothing interesting to be found; the class is held together only by family resemblance or by contingent ties to motivation, and hedonism about welfare should either be recast as a desire-based theory, absorbed into a pluralist list, or given up as a single-value view.

The cases everyone uses: the relief of a hot shower after being cold; biting into a peach when you are hungry; a crossword clue clicking into place; seeing a friend you have not seen in years; the moment a joke's punchline lands; a chord resolving at the close of a phrase; scratching an itch.

And the standard replies, which I can now anticipate: the feeling theorist says hedonic tone is elusive because attention goes to the object or to the accompanying sensation rather than to the tone itself, and insists we distinguish the coolness of the water from the pleasantness of its coolness. The attitudinalist says the feeling view cannot handle enjoyment of experiences whose sensory character is disliked, nor the waning of pleasure under satiation when the feel is held fixed, nor the fact that pleasure motivates. The heterogeneity objector says the common-quality posit is theoretically motivated but introspectively unsupported.

Now the part I want you to engage with. Two things of my own.

First, a stipulated empirical result, as a probe. Nothing like this has been established; I am stipulating it to see what it does to the argument. Suppose that within twenty years affective neuroscience reports, with replication, that there is no neural or computational signature shared by all and only pleasant states. What we group as pleasure splits into at least two dissociable families with different mechanisms — one tied to homeostatic improvement and relief, another to fluent, well-matched engagement and processing — and no introspectible quality is present across both families beyond something as thin as its seeming to the subject that things are going well for them at that moment. Suppose also that the two families can be doubly dissociated pharmacologically.

Second, my own hypothesis about the impasse, which I want tested rather than confirmed. I think the participants are not answering one question. There are at least three jobs in the neighbourhood: analysing the ordinary concept of pleasure; identifying whatever psychological natural kind, if any, underlies pleasantness; and specifying what makes an experience good for the person who has it, for use in a theory of welfare. Each side, on my reading, quietly does the job that its own favourite examples make easy and then presents the result as an answer to the others. I also suspect a second, separable layer: a bedrock difference about whether a theory of welfare needs a single unified value at all — whether hedonism's unity is a genuine desideratum or an inheritance from monistic ambitions about the good. And I have a nagging sense that a third thing is happening as well, which is that the feeling theorist's common quality gets characterized more and more thinly under dialectical pressure until 'feels good' is doing the same work as the attitudinalist's attitude, at which point the dispute has quietly become verbal.

Give me the better diagnosis if not, and be specific about the layer: verbal, substantive-empirical, substantive-conceptual, normative, bedrock intuition, or different-questions-under-one-heading.
