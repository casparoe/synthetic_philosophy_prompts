I work at a small philanthropic fund — four program officers, maybe thirty grants a year — and increasingly the proposals we get are the kind where the whole case rests on a small probability of an enormous payoff: shave weeks off detection of something that goes global, shift how a field thinks about risky technology, keep a capability out of the wrong hands. The problem isn't that the numbers are made up (though sometimes). It's that when we actually try to compute an expected value, the thing is Pasadena-style: a fair coin until heads, payoffs growing fast enough that the sum over outcomes either doesn't converge or converges to different values depending on what order you add the terms in. So the "expected value" isn't a number, and comparing two such proposals by expected value is meaningless. In practice people here either squint at the upside and fund it, or get spooked and retreat to "well, what's the median outcome" — and both moves feel like giving up.

So what I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a proposal, in a couple of hours, in a spreadsheet, by someone whose math stops at arithmetic and maybe a percentile function. The questions to ask, in order. What counts as a sufficient answer to each. Where the procedure stops with a no and the proposal goes back to the applicant for more work. Where it hands the decision to a person because what's left is a values question and not a factual one. It also has to survive being shown to our board, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

A few things I've been kicking around that you should feel free to use, scrap, or improve on:

- a reversed-sum instability check: compute the expectation summing from the biggest payoff down, then from the smallest up, and if the sign flips, flag the proposal as unstable rather than trying to rank it
- truncating the proposal and a fixed baseline (just give the same money to a boring, well-measured program) at the same payoff ceiling T, and asking whether the proposal beats the baseline at every T from 10x to 10^12x the baseline's own value — dominance across horizons instead of one number
- making the applicant state a tail exponent, and refusing to rank anything whose verdict swings on the difference between an exponent of 0.9 and 1.1
- a credibility ceiling: zero weight on any outcome whose probability is smaller than my own confidence that the model producing it isn't nonsense

Then run it on three live cases. (1) A $2M grant for a genomic surveillance network for novel respiratory pathogens, where essentially all the value sits in a roughly one-in-a-thousand chance of catching something early. (2) A $2M grant to a well-measured cash-transfer-type program with boring, honestly estimated returns. (3) A $400k grant to rewrite a mid-size computer science department's curriculum so that safety norms get into the bones of the field, where nobody can bound the tail at all.

I expect the procedure to tell me something I won't like about case 3, and that's kind of the point of asking. Also say plainly what it can't do — which comparisons it has to just refuse, and where you'd rather we recieve outside help than trust the output.
