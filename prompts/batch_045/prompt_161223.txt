Someone in the reading group I go to — half linguistics grad students, half ML people, very informal — keeps closing arguments with "the poverty of the stimulus". Last week it was about whether a system trained only on text could end up with real grammatical knowledge if you just gave it enough text. He said no, poverty of the stimulus, end of story. Someone else said the phrase doesn't do any work until you say which poverty you mean, and then they spent twenty minutes talking past each other. I went home and tried to seperate out the things people might mean and came up with this:

- kids are never told which sentences are bad, they only ever hear ones that are fine
- for any finite batch of sentences there are alot of different generalizations compatible with it that contradict each other, so the batch can't single one out
- kids end up able to do things the sentences couldn't have told them: reject a brand-new sentence as ungrammatical, hear one sentence as ambiguous in two ways, know that a question word belongs way back inside a subordinate clause
- kids land on the same grammar as everyone else around them even though what they hear is fragmentary and differs from kid to kid, and even when the "simplest" rule you could read off the examples isn't the one they land on (the case I always think of: the man who is tall is in the room turns into is the man who is tall in the room, not into is the man who tall is in the room)
- and the bit he seemed to actually need: that throwing more text at the problem wouldn't help, because the new data is more of the same kind, so the problem isn't quantity

Can you turn that into a proper menu of distinct precise claims, ordered from trivially true to wildly strong, and tell me which of my bullets are really one bullet, which secretly assume another one, and which are false or at least don't say what people think they say? Two things I especially want. First: is the never-told-what's-bad one even true, and does the argument need it to be true, or is it just a colourful way of starting? Second: which reading would actually get him his conclusion against the ML guy — because under one reading I can see the argument just helping itself to the conclusion (the data only "selects" from an innate menu of grammars, so of course the data can't do the job alone), and I want to know whether that's a genuine circularity or whether I'm being dumb about it. And if it does survive, what would have to be true for the argument to say something about a system trained on text, and not only about children?

Also, if you can: which reading is doing the work when people use the phrase to win an argument in five seconds, as opposed to the reading that would actually be worth defending?
