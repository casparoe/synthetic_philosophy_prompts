Hey — I've been reading about consciousness on my own for a couple of years (no philosophy background, just blog posts, a few podcasts and whatever I can get past a paywall), and I think I've basically cracked the thing people call the meta-problem. What I can't work out is whether that means I've also solved the hard problem, or whether I've quietly changed the subject. Here's where I'm at.

As I understand the setup — I'm pretty sure this comes from a paper by Chalmers — the hard problem is why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all, i.e. why the physical facts don't just hold without anyone being anyone. The meta-problem is meant to be the easier, neighbouring question: why do we *say and think* the things we do about experience? That it's inner, that it's ineffable, that no amount of functional description captures it, that zombies are conceivable, that Mary learns something new, that no analysis of the concept will ever feel satisfying. Fine. That distinction seems clear enough.

Here's my theory. We've got a monitoring system that reads off our own internal states, and a second system that stamps whatever the monitor hands it with a kind of "this is not like anything else" flag. The flag does all the work. It explains why we call experience inner (the monitor has no access to the external cause of its own inputs), why we call it ineffable (the flag carries no descriptive content, it's just a tag), why functionalist stories feel like they leave something out (the flag isn't in the functional story and can't be), and why all the classic thought experiments feel compelling (they're just reliable ways of triggering the flag). I'm fairly confident that accounts for every datum the meta-problem is about — every report, every intuition pump, every stubborn refusal to be satisfied by an explanation.

So: if my story handles all of that, what's left for the hard problem? People keep telling me there's a residue, that even a perfect account of why we judge and talk the way we do wouldn't settle whether the phenomenal facts are fixed by the physical facts, or whether consciousness as we naively conceive it is even real. That sounds to me like moving the goalposts. If you've explained all the reports and all the appearances, what is there left to explain? Which makes me want to ask: are the realists just people who haven't noticed that giving the mechanism of a belief is the same thing as explaining the belief away?

Concretely I guess I want to know which of these is right: (a) the meta-problem is just the hard problem under a new name, so a solution to one is a solution to the other; (b) it's a genuinely seperate and easier question, but a full solution to it would leave the hard problem completely untouched and maybe undecidable in principle; or (c) it's separate, but a full solution would *dissolve* the hard problem — there'd be nothing left to answer, and the realist/illusionist fight would just evaporate. I've gone back and forth, and I genuinely can't tell whether that fight is a real disagreement about how the world is or just people using "consciousness" to pick out different things.

Don't just give me a tour of the literature, please. Tell me where I've gone wrong if I have, and be specific about which step in my argument is the bad one — is it the theory itself, the claim that it covers all the data, the inference from "explained the mechanism" to "nothing left over", or the way I've split up the options? I keep a spreadsheet of questions I've asked and the answers I got, so please finish with a single last line in exactly this form: \answer{X}, where X is a, b or c followed by a dash and a few words naming the bad step — like \answer{b — explaining a belief's cause isn't refuting it}.
