Hey — I'm a 4th-year PhD student working on peer disagreement and higher-order evidence, and I co-edit our department's end-of-term satirical newsletter (last year's issue was on the preface paradox, it went down well with about six people). This year we're doing metaepistemology, and I've volunteered a centrepiece: an agony-aunt column. So please act as a professional philosopher who has published on higher-order evidence and who also writes comedy on the side; before you draft anything, brainstorm for a bit about which real commitments of the conciliationist / total-evidence / steadfast positions are the most fun to push one step too far, and only then start writing. The whole thing should be in British English (organise, agonised, etc.).

Here's the set-up I've got in mind. The column is called "Ask Aunt Akrasia" and runs in the staff newsletter of a fictional body, the Institute for Metaepistemic Hygiene. Aunt Akrasia is a completely sincere, cheerful, mildly bureaucratic conciliationist who genuinely believes her own advice; she never winks at the reader, never says "of course this is absurd", and never relents into steadfastness or into "just use common sense" — the comedy has to come entirely from how carefully she applies her doctrine to ordinary life. The doctrine (I'm inventing this bit, so treat it as mine, not as anything real): the Institute's official **Recursive Deference Protocol**, which says that higher-order evidence must be met with equal deference at every level — when you get evidence that your first-order judgement is unreliable, you must defer to that evidence; when you get evidence about how much to defer, you must defer to *that*; and so on — together with the Institute's practical **Three-Level Rule**, which says that after three levels of deference you should stop and record a "Provisional Credence (Non-Binding)" in the log, while continuing to act as if nothing had changed. I want three letters, plus a short closing "Notes from the Helpdesk" footer, about 800–900 words total. Suggested letters, but change them if you have better ideas: (1) a writer who disagrees with a friend of equal intelligence and identical evidence about something mundane but action-guiding — whether they locked the back door, what time the train is — and has now conscientiously moved to 50%, and can't see how to ever leave the house again; (2) someone who forms a judgment and only afterwards learns their judgement-forming was compromised (they'd taken an antihistamine, or been awake for 26 hours, or were furious at the time), and wants to know whether they should stop believing while all their first-order evidence is untouched; (3) someone whose colleague insists they are not epistemic peers because he is her superior, so she owes him no deference — and she wants to know whether she's allowed to settle *that* question with first-order reasoning.

The bit I really care about: the jokes should be jokes that only land if the epistemology is right. So I want the letters and replies to quietly work through — without ever naming a position or a person, and with no footnotes or citations at all, the newsletter has a strict no-references rule — the following: whether higher-order evidence is just one more piece of first-order evidence to be fed into your total evidence, or whether it operates only on the rationality of your first-order attitude; whether you can rationally be in the state of believing p while believing that your belief that p is irrational (and if not, why not — what makes it akratic rather than a mere tension); whether it's spineless or sensible to let a disagreement undo a view you reasoned to carefully; whether there's a unique rational response to a body of evidence or a permitted range of them; and how, if at all, any of this is supposed to guide action when your credences have gone to exactly a half. Aunt Akrasia should have an answer to every one of these, and every answer should be one a real defender of the view could defend, just taken further than they'd like. By the end an actual conciliationist should feel the piece was unfair in a fair way — that we've followed their logic somewhere they wouldn't want to go, rather than inventing a silly version of it to knock down. The footer can be where it curdles slightly: readers writing in to report what the Protocol has done to their ability to hold opinions at all. Oh, and if you can work in the Institute's annual "Retreat from Judgement Day" somewhere, I'd be definately grateful.
