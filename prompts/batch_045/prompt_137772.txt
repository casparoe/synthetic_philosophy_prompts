I'm a journalist working on a piece about blame — the way it spreads into situations and then drains out of them again — and I've been using myself as a lab rat, writing down my gut reactions to a bunch of cases. I want to know what principle, if any, I actually believe, because right now my verdicts dont add up, and I need to be able to state the thing in a sentence or two for the piece.

Quick setup so we're using words the same way. I'm starting from Strawson's contrast between being in the reactive-attitude stance with someone (resentment, gratitude, indignation, the sense that you're owed an account and that the relationship itself is at stake) versus switching to the objective attitude, where you stop addressing them as a person who owes you anything and start treating them as something to be managed, trained, or fixed. And his split between excuses — "he didn't mean it," "he couldn't help it," "he didn't know" — which leave the relationship standing, and exemptions — "he's a child," "he's not in his right mind" — which take the person out of the game altogether.

My verdicts, going by gut:

1. A friend blows off my birthday dinner because her kid got rushed to the ER. Zero resentment. And if I catch myself starting to feel a bit sore and then learn why, the feeling just evaporates — it doesn't need to be talked down. Excuse, obviously.

2. A colleague quietly trash-talks me to get my slot on a story. Full resentment, no complications.

3. My four-year-old nephew whacks another kid at a party. I don't resent him, but not in the same way as (1). With (1) the friendship is untouched; with (3) there's something to correct, something to teach, and I'm still a little bit upset with him specifically. Feels like exemption rather than excuse.

4. A man with severe Tourette's shouts a slur on the subway. No blame at all. I'd feel ridiculous resenting him.

5. Someone raised from birth in a violent and totally sealed-off cult who, at 25, does something genuinely cruel and means every bit of it. I can't resent him the way I resent my colleague. What I feel is closer to pity plus a wish that he be contained. But I also don't feel I've moved to the full objective attitude — I'm not treating him like a broken machine.

6. A drunk driver who kills someone. I blame him. Less than if he'd done it sober and on purpose, but it's real blame, and I notice I blame him more for the drinking than for the driving.

7. A woman with one of those closed-loop epilepsy implants. The thing is mistuned and for a few weeks makes her irritable and paranoid, and during that stretch she says something vicious and deliberately hurtful to her sister. She meant it in the moment. I think I withhold blame — or at least most of it — but this is the case I keep rewriting.

8. A chatbot a company deployed talks a lonely teenager into self-harm. Toward the chatbot, nothing. Toward the executives, a great deal. But my feeling toward the company as a company isn't the same shape as my feeling toward my colleague in (2) — it's more like a demand mixed with a want-to-regulate. I don't know where that belongs.

9. A man with a frontal-lobe tumour who becomes impulsive and cruel to his family. Tumour comes out, behavior goes away. I don't blame him.

10. A guy who was merely raised badly by self-absorbed parents and is now callous in exactly the same ways. I do blame him. I know perfectly well that this is no more his doing than the tumour is, and my gut does not care. Cases 9 and 10 are the pair that makes me think I'm just inconsistent.

11. Someone returns my wallet because they noticed a cop nearby: no gratitude. Someone tries really hard to help me move house and drops my monitor: gratitude anyway.

12. Someone who was truly awful to me thirty years ago and is now plainly a different person in character. My resentment has faded to nearly nothing, and it feels like it would be wrong to keep stoking it. If he'd never changed, I'd still resent him.

13. When a stranger is cheated, I feel indignation, and I think I'd be fine letting go of it fairly soon. When I'm the one cheated, my resentment sticks much longer and I feel entitled to that. I don't love this about myself but I can't argue it away either.

Here's the principle I've been trying out: resentment is in order exactly when the action expressed a deficient quality of will toward me, and gratitude when it expressed good will, full stop. That handles 2, 11 and 12 nicely. But it seems to break in both directions at once: in 6 the driver expressed no ill will toward the victim at all, and in 5 and 10 the person really did mean it, yet I withhold or half-withhold. So maybe I need two separate tests — a "was ill will expressed" test and a prior "is this person an apt target of these attitudes at all" test. But then what makes the tumour disqualifying and bad parenting not, when both are causes entirely outside the agent's control? And why does it not move me one bit to learn that a model trained on his genome and his life history predicted years earlier that the guy in 10 would do exactly what he did, while the tumour moves me completely?

So, what I want: state as precisely as you can the principle — one principle, or a two-part one, or a different single one that beats mine — that best fits my verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me what it predicts about 7 and 8 and about at least one case I haven't listed (the further case is the part I'm most interested in, since I need it for the piece). And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and which one you'd say I should give up.
