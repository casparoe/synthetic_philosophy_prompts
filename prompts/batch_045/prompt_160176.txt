I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep coming back to the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal as the cleanest real-world instance of something the AI alignment crowd worries about: reward tampering and wireheading. The case as I have it: through the 2000s and into 2016 the bank pushed branch staff hard on cross-sell numbers, roughly eight products per customer, staff responded by opening something like 1.5 to 2 million deposit and credit accounts without consent — using their own contact details on applications, setting PINs to 0000 so they could enroll people in online banking, moving money out of real accounts to fund fake ones — and in 2016 the CFPB and others hit them with a combined $185 million in fines and the CEO resigned.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a metaphor. Which part of this is the reward channel, which part is the proxy, and who is the agent — the teller, the branch manager, the executive suite, or the bank as a corporate actor? Then sort the actual behaviors into the categories the literature uses: wireheading proper, tampering with the measurement apparatus, and ordinary proxy gaming out in the world. My hunch is that pinning accounts and falsifying contact information belong in a different box from badgering customers into credit cards they didn't want, but tell me if my boxes are wrong.

Then the bit I'm more interested in. I've been kicking around a distinction I haven't seen written up anywhere, and I want you to stress-test it: call it hierarchical tampering. Nobody at any level touches the real reward signal or necessarily knows what it is; each level just games the proxy handed down by the level above and reports a sanitized number upward, so tampering shows up as an equilibrium across a hierarchy rather than as anyone's plan. Does the standard picture of tampering as a convergent instrumental goal for a single agent have room for that, does it predict it, and would adding it change the threat model?

Last thing, run it the other direction: what does the case do to the theory? To me it looks like an awkward case for the doomier versions, because the tampering was caught — by customers noticing fees, by whistleblowers, by a Los Angeles Times investigation — that is, by channels outside the optimization loop. I read that as an independent monitoring channel doing real work; say whether that generalizes into something the alignment literature should take more seriously, or whether it's just a bank happening to have a regulator. And be blunt about where the theory goes quiet: agency, blame, deterrence, and what to do with the fact that the people at the top were being measured too.
