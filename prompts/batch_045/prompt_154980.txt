I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on reasons and motivation: 14 students, one 90-minute session. They have already met the standard indirect-normativity family of views — roughly, that what an agent has reason to do is fixed by what a better-informed, more vividly imaginative, less irrational counterpart of that agent would want or advise, rather than by her actual desires. I want a structured role-play that makes them run into, rather than hear about, two features of idealizing in this way, and I don't want to open with a lecture or name either problem until the debrief.

The first is estrangement: as the counterpart's information and imagination improve, her verdicts can drift free of anything the actual agent cares about, so that it stops being obvious why the advice binds her. The second is that the counterpart is not yet well defined: whether you correct the agent's factual beliefs first and then let her desires settle, or strip out desires that depend on false beliefs before supplying the information, seems to change the answer, and there is no further ideal-level resource that settles which order is the right one.

My idea is to give each student a deliberately thin vignette — a decision, a few circumstances, almost nothing about the agent's motives, so they have to fill in the rest themselves — and to have each agent advised by two consultants working under different written instructions about the order and extent of idealization. Each consultant must attach to every recommendation a note of which feature of the actual agent's concerns it traces back to. Each agent then commits in writing, in front of the room, to which recommendation she would act on.

Please give me the roles and rules, the vignette, the two instruction sheets, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps the moments students will actually hit onto the two problems and onto what the view needs in order to survive them: a uniqueness claim about the idealization procedure, or an account of why inheritance from the actual agent fails. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — consultants quietly importing their own values, agents treating the exercise as prediction rather than advice — and be frank about which part of the problem the role-play cannot stage and I will have to argue for myself. If the set-up as I have described it manufactures the divergence rather than exposing it, or if the thing only works after the lecture I said I didn't want, say so plainly rather than handing me a polite design.
