You are a philosopher of language specializing in speech act theory, and you are unusually good at defending views that most of your colleagues reject. Take your time and think carefully. Before you start writing, brainstorm: list every consideration you can think of that seems to tell against the thesis below, and note which of them are genuine costs and which are only discomfort with a counterintuitive consequence. Only then construct your argument. I want the argument itself, not a survey of what various theorists have said or would say.

Please argue, as strongly and as honestly as you can, for the following claim.

**The answerability condition.** An utterance's being the performance of an illocutionary act — a promise, a warning, a refusal, an order, a resignation — is not secured by the speaker's producing the right words, with the right intentions, under the right convention, in the right circumstances. It is secured only if the utterance places the speaker in a position of being *answerable to* some party for having done it: someone other than the speaker who can hold the speaker to the promise, rely on the warning, treat the refusal as standing, or insist that the resignation be honoured. Call this answerable uptake, and contrast it with mere recognition, which is only a matter of a hearer's correctly identifying the force the speaker intended. On this view, an illocutionary act is not an event in the speaker's history that subsequently produces effects in a hearer; it is a normative relation between a speaker and some second party, and where no such relation comes into existence, no illocutionary act has been performed — only, at most, an utterance with the shape of one.

In arguing for it, please do all of the following.

1. **Steelman the thesis first.** State the strongest and most precise version of the answerability condition you can, including what exactly being answerable to someone is meant to require and to rule out. Be careful to distinguish your view from the much weaker and considerably more popular claim that illocutionary success requires that a hearer recognise the intended force. Your argument should not have to lean on that weaker claim; it says much less, and it is too easily granted to be worth defending. If you can, say why the weaker claim is unsatisfying and where it leaves the phenomenon unexplained.

2. **Give the thesis its three best arguments.** Not three variations on one argument. Aim for arguments that are independent, so that a reader who rejects one still has to deal with the others.

3. **Confront the hard cases.** At minimum:

   - *The unread resignation.* An employee emails a resignation to her manager's address; the manager is away, with an auto-reply saying that mail will not be read, and the message sits unopened for a month. Has she resigned? Compare an employee who stands in the manager's doorway and says *I resign* to a manager who is asleep, and compare both with a resignation pinned to an empty noticeboard after hours. If the answerability condition gives different verdicts here, say which and why; if it gives the same verdict throughout, explain why that is not a defect.
   - *The deathbed promise.* A man says *I promise you I will look after her* to a dying friend who is already unresponsive and never regains consciousness. Nothing is missing from the speaker's intention, the words, or the convention; nothing can be demanded of him by the person addressed; and yet nearly everyone will say that he promised. You must do better here than asserting that the intuition is mistaken — argue for that diagnosis if you hold it, and note what else the view commits you to if you do not.
   - *Mis-uptake.* A driver is told *I'm warning you, the bridge is out*, meant as helpful information, and hears it as a threat. Or a woman's no is not heard as a refusal. Was an act performed, and if so, which one? This is the case your view looks best placed to explain, so be explicit about why the explanation is a real philosophical gain rather than a redescription of the data in new vocabulary.
   - *Promises to no one.* A New Year's resolution made aloud to oneself, a promise spoken at a graveside, a proclamation issued to the world at large. Say what the view predicts and defend the prediction.

4. **Say what the view costs and what it buys.** In particular:

   - Say how it re-describes the distinction between misfires, where the act is void, and abuses, where the act is performed but hollow or insincere. Is the insincere promise the easy case it is usually taken to be? Do some paradigm misfires and some paradigm failures of uptake turn out to fail for the same reason, and if so, is that a unification or a collapse?
   - Say what becomes of the contrast between the illocutionary and the perlocutionary. If illocutionary acts are relational in this way, does the contrast get cleaner, or does something on the illocutionary side start to look perlocutionary?
   - Name the single most entrenched commitment in speech act theory that you would have to give up if you accepted the thesis, and say plainly whether the trade is worth making.

Constraints: write in continuous prose, roughly 900 to 1300 words. Assume your reader knows only that Austin distinguished illocutionary from perlocutionary acts, and misfires from abuses; define every other term of art as you introduce it. Do not cite, name, or attribute readings to particular books or authors beyond Austin, and do not appeal to what is standardly said in the field as a reason. Where a step in the argument is controversial, mark it and give the consideration that would make a sceptic accept it. Do not hedge into mush: commit to a verdict on each case, and say when you think our intuitions are simply unreliable and why.
