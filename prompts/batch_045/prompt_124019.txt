I hold a particular view about why the Knobe effect happens, and I want to know what would show I am wrong.

Background so we are talking about the same thing: in the well-known pair of vignettes from Knobe's 2003 studies, a company executive is told that a new program will increase profits and will also harm the environment; he says he does not care about the environment, only about profit; the program runs and the environment is harmed. Roughly four-fifths of participants say he harmed the environment intentionally. In the otherwise identical case where the side effect is helping the environment and he says he does not care about helping it, only about a quarter say he helped it intentionally. My view is an attempt to explain the gap without saying that moral judgment distorts an otherwise clean folk-psychological judgment:

(1) Non-moral core. What drives the asymmetry is the presence of a norm that the outcome violates, not badness as such. Moral badness is simply the most efficient way for a one-paragraph vignette to make a norm salient. So the effect should also appear, in attenuated form, for side effects that flout non-moral norms: rudeness, aesthetic garishness, a breach of etiquette, a departure from a technical or statistical standard.

(2) Reason-flouting mechanism. The norm matters because it hands the hearer a reason the agent had for acting otherwise. A foreseen side effect that the agent had a standing reason to avoid, could have avoided, and did not bother avoiding does not read as something that merely happened to him. In the help case there is normally no standing reason to help that the vignette makes salient, so the agent's indifference is not a reason flouted, and the outcome reads as incidental. The asymmetry should therefore track the perceived strength of the flouted reason, not the perceived badness of the outcome.

(3) Pragmatics is downstream only. Conversational reshaping of the question — the idea that hearers reinterpret a question about intentionality as a question about blame — does no independent work. Once the reason structure is held fixed, question wording, answer format, and whether participants see one case or both should make little difference.

I have run some informal probes on colleagues, small and self-selected, and I do not know how much weight to give them. In a help case where the firm had signed a public conservation pledge, the asymmetry narrowed a lot. In a case where the side effect is neither good nor bad but merely strange (the new program causes the office lights to glow faintly purple), there was no asymmetry at all. When the decision is made by a 400-person committee rather than an individual, the asymmetry shrank. When I replaced the yes/no question with a scale whose endpoints were explicitly labeled "purely accidental" and "deliberately brought about," the asymmetry was about the size it usually is. I never ran the comparison that matters most to me: a harm case where avoiding the harm would have been costly versus one where it would have been free.

The pledge case worries me in particular, since adding a pledge plausibly changes how much the agent is blamed and not just whether a reason was flouted.

There is a real risk that "badness is just the way a norm gets made salient" lets me find a norm post hoc in any case that produces an asymmetry, which would make (1) and (2) unfalsifiable.
