I'm writing the position paper for my graduate seminar on consciousness and moral status, and before I defend it I'd like an audit of the commitments I've collected, because I can't tell which of them are actually doing the work. The paper asks whether anything in the machinery of reinforcement learning could amount to a welfare-bearing system. My own landing point is a guarded yes in principle, no in the cases that currently matter. Here is the set, as I've written it up:

(a) Substrate independence. Whether a system has valenced experience depends only on what it does computationally, not on what it is made of. Nothing about being implemented in silicon rather than in tissue is itself disqualifying, so the question about artificial systems has to be settled on functional grounds.

(b) Reward prediction error as the functional core of valence. In a system doing temporal-difference learning, the signal that registers that an outcome came out better or worse than expected is the best available functional candidate for valence: its sign gives the valence, its magnitude the intensity, and its time course the duration. On this picture a thermostat is inert because it computes only a setpoint deviation, not a deviation from an expectation it formed about its own future.

(c) The global-availability condition, which I use to keep (b) from overgenerating. An error signal counts as valence only if it is broadcast widely enough to modulate many of the system's other processes at once, such as its learning rate, its exploration, its memory consolidation and its action selection, rather than being confined to one dedicated correction channel. A thermostat's comparator fails this; a mammalian dopaminergic signal passes it.

(d) Gradualism. Sentience and the moral status that tracks it come in degrees. The differences between systems are differences along continua, and any line we draw for practical purposes is drawn on a continuum rather than found in nature.

(e) Externalized valence. Standard reinforcement learning has an asymmetry I think matters morally: during training the reward signal shapes the system, but the shaping happens in an optimization loop that is not part of the system's own activity; at deployment, with weights frozen, no error signal of that kind is computed or broadcast at all. So whatever welfare-relevant activity training involves, a deployed policy is not a subject of experiences at all, and cannot be harmed. This is, for me, the main reason to think the cases that matter are fine.

(f) The diachronic condition. For a being to be harmed in the morally serious sense, there must be a persisting subject to which the harm accrues, with real forward- and backward-looking unity across time. Momentary valenced states with no such unity generate much weaker moral claims.

(g) Precaution under uncertainty. Where we are unsure whether a system is sentient and the potential stakes are large, we should weight by our credence and act on expected value rather than treating uncertainty as permission to ignore the question.

I also hold four verdicts on cases, the last two of which I am much less confident about: insects have nonzero but very low moral status; a thermostat has none; a deployed, frozen policy trained by reinforcement learning has none; and a policy from which the value function has been ablated after training, but which still reliably seeks high-reward states, has none either, on the grounds that wanting without valenced feedback is not suffering.

Two complications I can't get into focus. First, the training-inference distinction in (e) is itself graded: there are systems that keep adapting within an episode, or that update a small subset of parameters at test time, or that learn in context without changing weights at all. Second, the same distinction doesn't line up with (b): a frozen system can still compute prediction errors, and a learning system's error signal can be computed in a critic that the acting part of the system never sees, as when rewards are assigned after the fact by a separate model.

Please go through (a) through (g) pairwise and tell me which combinations are in genuine tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity rather than listed evenly. The thermostat worry is the objection everyone raises first and I believe (c) dispatches it, so I'm more interested in whatever survives that. For each real tension, say what would have to be given up and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere in the set. In particular, I want to know whether my verdict that deployed policies have zero moral status can be derived from these commitments at all, given that (a) blocks an appeal to substrate, (d) blocks an appeal to a sharp line, (g) blocks an appeal to confidence, and (f) looks like it tells in the other direction, since a long recurrent episode over fixed weights gives more cross-temporal unity than an insect has. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I am attached both to the insect verdict and to the zero verdict for deployed systems, and I genuinely don't know which of them I would abandon, so please don't assume I've already decided.
