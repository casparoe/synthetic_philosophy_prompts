I sit on the committee drafting the ethics guidance for a hospital's preconception and prenatal counseling service — the clinic where prospective parents arrive with a known genetic risk, an abnormal screen, a failed IVF cycle, or simply the question of whether to try at all. Counselors there must say something actionable, inside a consultation, to people who share no particular metaphysics of merely possible people. Before I let any account of the morality of creating people serve as the moral backbone of that guidance, I want to know what I should be asking of it.

Write me the specification. What must an adequate theory of whether, when, and whom to bring into existence be able to do, if it is to be good enough to generate case-level advice in a counseling room? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that earns it its place on the list. The following are the cases our committee actually argues over; use them, and supply your own wherever a requirement needs a sharper test.

1. *The delayed cycle.* A couple can conceive this month, with a high chance of a child who will have a painful early-onset condition but a life that child will on balance be glad to have, or wait one cycle and conceive a different child who will very likely not. One person either way; different person.
2. *The replacement.* Terminate at twelve weeks and re-conceive. The first child's life would have been worth living, but clearly worse than the second's.
3. *The last embryo.* The only viable embryo will produce a child whose life is predicted to be barely worth living. The alternatives are no child, or adopting a child who already exists.
4. *The coin flip.* Conceiving now yields, with equal probability, a life of unusual flourishing or a life of severe suffering ending early. On any additive reckoning the expectation is comfortably positive.
5. *The late-onset result.* A screen shows a condition that will not appear until the person is forty and will then cut short an otherwise good life — a life that the person, once existing, will almost certainly say was worth living.
6. *The grievance.* The grown child of a parent who knew the risk says: you harmed me by creating me. Is there a coherent complaint here, against whom, and what would count as redressing it?
7. *The exit corollary.* A patient already exists in the room. If coming into existence is always a net harm, ceasing to exist looks like a net benefit, and a theory that affirms the first while denying the second owes me the difference in principle rather than by stipulation.
8. *The paused mind.* A person whose existence can be suspended and later resumed — a digital person, or a temporarily suspended one. Here the relevant absence is interruption, not never-having-been. Does the asymmetry between the absence of suffering and the absence of enjoyment survive when non-existence is reversible? This case should force a theory to say whether it is fundamentally about lives, about subjects with futures, or about states of the world.
9. *The roll-up.* If each act of creation is a harm of modest size, the sum over everyone now alive is enormous and the end of sentient life would be an enormous improvement. Relatedly: a vast number of barely-worth-living lives against a small number of excellent ones, and a slow decline in quality of life across generations that no individual ever notices.

Some of what people demand in this area is a genuine desideratum and some is the prejudice of a camp. Separate them. In particular, tell me the status of each of the following: that a theory must not imply extinction is a great improvement; that it must not imply any existing person's death is a benefit to them; that it must respect the person-affecting thought that morality concerns improving the lives of people who will exist rather than adding good lives; that it must still be able to condemn creating a life not worth living; that it must still permit creating the lives people ordinarily do create; that it must handle uncertainty, and must say whose standard of worth-living governs — the future person's anticipated own judgment or some external list, with the complication that people often report their own lives as better than observers predict; that it must yield advice without anyone computing total welfare; that its verdicts must not flip on arbitrarily small changes in probability or magnitude; that it must order populations without cycles; that it must be able to hold ordinary pediatric and obstetric care coherent for the practitioners who apply it; and that it must either say something articulate about funding fertility treatment, prenatal screening programs, and pronatalist subsidies, or explain why silence is correct.

Also give me the hardest part. First: a requirement that the asymmetry be explained rather than asserted — state in your own terms what the asymmetry claims about the four cells of presence and absence of suffering and enjoyment, and say what would have to be true for its rationale to generalize to interrupted existence, to non-human animals, and to minds whose entire life we can specify in advance. Second: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for my purpose and which are negotiable. Third: identify any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why — if the list is itself a result, I want that stated plainly. Fourth: tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise popular candidates, and, if nothing on offer satisfies the non-negotiable core, what the guidance should say instead.

Close with a brief scorecard — a few lines each, no more — for: the asymmetry itself; the person-affecting restriction; straightforwardly additive total views; threshold or critical-level views; and the principle that one ought to select, among possible children, the one with the best expected life. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring, and argue from the cases rather than from what anyone has published: assume no library.
