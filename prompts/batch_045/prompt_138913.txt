I write novels, and the book I'm drafting turns on a mind in which, as far as I can tell, a great deal is going on but which has no way of saying so. To get clearer about it I've been reading the access-consciousness versus phenomenal-consciousness distinction and the global workspace literature alongside Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, which I've agreed to introduce for a book club next month. I'd like a reading of the novel through that material, and I'd like it to be a reading rather than an illustration.

What draws me is how thoroughly the book stages the question from the outside. Madame's gallery collects the students' art in order to find in what they make some evidence of an inner life; the rumor of the deferrals asks whether a couple who can demonstrate love convincingly enough can be granted a future. Both are tests on output, and in both the thing everyone is straining to see is exactly what the output will not settle — and this against a first-person narration, Kathy's, which records memory and detail with immense care and introspects almost not at all.

Three things I want sorted out. First, whether what the characters call souls is phenomenal consciousness at all, or something the philosophy has no category for — moral status, or the possession of a future — and whether it matters to the novel that Ishiguro leaves the words he uses (soul, inner life) deliberately vague. Second, what the book does to the global workspace account: on that view the students are conscious if anyone is, being fluent, planning, remembering, mutually responsive and able to report, so if a residue remains at the end, is that a refutation, or a sign that the theory was never answering the question the book is asking? Third, whether Kathy's withholding voice is Ishiguro making an argument about the inaccessibility of experience, or simply what that kind of retrospective narrator sounds like — I don't want to rest a reading on a stylistic fact that has a duller explanation.

I'd also value being told where the reading fails: which scenes resist the consciousness framing, and what, if anything, the novel knows about experience that the philosophy of mind does not.
