I'm a PhD student in philosophy (Kant-ish, though animals aren't my area) and I somehow ended up on my university's animal ethics committee. We review about fifteen protocols a month, half an hour each, me plus a vet and a lay member, our decisions get audited, and the university's lawyers have told us in writing that we can't justify anything on animal-rights grounds -- so the reasoning in our minutes has to be indirect-duty-shaped: reasons about us and our characters, not about what is owed to the animal. In practice none of us can actually run that view and we fall back on a harm-benefit calcuation, which isn't the job we were given.

Can you turn the indirect-duty view into a procedure we could actually run on a protocol? Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns a flat no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is about values rather than facts. Three things it has to survive: the character-deformation rationale seems to imply that suffering no one ever witnesses is fine, which everyone on the committee thinks is the wrong answer and which I don't think Kant accepts either, so I need a way of handling that which doesn't just smuggle in duties to the animal by the back door; there's the distinction between cruelty and torment that isn't cruel, which we have no grip on; and there's the case of animals who can no longer be of any use -- the old animal put down because it can't work any more -- where the rationale seems to shift from what the act does to the agent to something about the act itself. It also needs a real test for whether an end is the kind of end that licenses pain at all, because 'valid scientific purpose' in our regulations is doing none of that work.

Then run it on three cases of the shapes we actually see: a chronic-pain model in mice with analgesia withheld because it would ruin the data, with a plausible translational payoff; culling a few hundred healthy surplus animals that have no use and nowhere to go; and a field study using unmonitored traps in which animals will suffer and die unseen, where the data is likely to be filed and never published. I want at least one case where the procedure says yes to something I'd rather it said no to, and I want to know plainly what the procedure cannot do.
