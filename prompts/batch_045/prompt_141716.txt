I am putting together a short sequence of fixed-form poems on the limits of self-knowledge, for the closing session of a seminar, and I want one of them to be a villanelle about the failure of luminosity and of the principle that knowing something puts you in a position to know that you know it. Nineteen lines: five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains alternating through the tercets and arriving together in the final two lines. The speaker is lying awake before dawn, cold at the outset, and stays still while the room warms by steps too small to feel.

1. One stanza, one case. Each tercet should be a single case in a series that begins in a clear case of the condition and ends in a clear case of its absence, with consecutive cases differing by less than the speaker could notice. No stanza may report a threshold reached, a discovery made, or a change of mind: what changes is the case, never the speaker's experience of it.

2. The two refrains are a first-order knowledge claim and its iteration — roughly, *I know I'm cold* and *I know I know I'm cold*. They should be metrically identical and differ by the smallest addition you can manage, so that the ear cannot tell which is which without counting, just as the subject cannot tell adjacent cases apart. Neither refrain may alter a word anywhere in the poem; what changes is what they are set against.

3. No jargon in the poem itself — not luminosity, not margin for error, not sorites, not iteration. The machinery should be visible in what happens rather than named: that a claim cannot count as knowledge if it would be false in a case the speaker could not distinguish from the one she is actually in, and that this requirement transmits from case to case along the series, so that by the last tercet she is still saying something she is no longer entitled to say although nothing in her experience has changed.

4. The two refrains must not fail together, and the poem should make it legible which fails first. The reason I ask: the second-order claim inherits the first-order claim's need for room to be wrong and adds a need of its own, so it should run out earlier — there should be a stretch of the poem in which the first refrain still holds and the second has begun to sound hollow. Mark that stretch in a way a careful reader can find, and then, in two or three sentences of prose after the poem, say which stanza you took as the turning point and what signal you planted there.

5. The closing quatrain should read as the contradiction, not as a resolution. After five tercets of alternation, the two refrains sitting side by side in the last two lines should be the place where the reader sees that something has to be surrendered: either the speaker's authority over her own condition, or the thought that knowledge must leave itself room to be wrong. The poem should not argue for either; it should make one of those two losses feel like the one it has earned, and the last line should land there.

If it can be done without straining the sense, let the recurring rhyme words themselves drift — each a half-step warmer than the last — so that the form's compulsion to return to the opening sound at the end does the same work as the return of the refrain. It should be readable aloud and intelligible to someone who has never met the argument: the epistemology belongs in the structure, not in the vocabulary.
