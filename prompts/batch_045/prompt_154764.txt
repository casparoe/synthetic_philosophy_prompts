I'm the lay member on the animal welfare and ethics committee where I work — I've got no academic background in anything like this, I'm in estates management really, and I've just been reading about animal consciousness on my own in the evenings. Which probably shows. I've picked up that "can it feel pain" and "is it conscious" might be two or three different questions stacked on top of each other, and that reacting to a harmful stimulus isn't the same as minding about it — but I'm not confident I've got that right, so correct me if I've garbled it.

The problem is practical. We meet once a month, get through eight or nine protocols in an afternoon, and for each one we have to (a) assign a severity band — roughly, nothing worth counting / mild / moderate / severe — and (b) decide how much scrutiny the thing gets: fast-track it, discuss it properly, or send it back. There's me, a vet, a couple of scientists and a chair. Nobody on the committee is a specialist in whatever species is on the table that week, and we can't go and read neuroscience papers between meetings. Whatever we decide goes on a form afterwards and has to be defensible if anyone asks us about it.

What I want is a procedure, not a position paper. Something we can actually run on each protocol in the twenty minutes we've got: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values question rather than a facts one.

The things I need it to handle, because these are the ones we keep arguing about:

- The evidence is nearly always thin or missing. For most of what comes to us — larval zebrafish, fruit flies, some beetle nobody has ever tested, the occasional cephlopods — we've no direct evidence either way. I need the procedure to say what to do with an absence of evidence without collapsing into "assume everything is fully conscious", because then everything is equally urgent and we can't prioritise anything at all.

- Numbers. One macaque is not ten thousand larvae. But I dont know how to think about it: if I'm maybe 10% confident a fly larva is sentient and there are fifty thousand of them, does that beat one monkey I'm 95% sure about? I've been toying with just multiplying credence × intensity × number and ranking by that, and it seems to give answers I don't like, so I can't tell whether the arithmetic is wrong or the dislike is.

- A consistency rule I half-invented: if you'd accept a bit of pain behaviour as enough to count a pig as sentient, you have to say what's doing the work when you reject the same kind of evidence for a crab. I like it, but I can't tell if it's circular or if it just sneaks in a bias towards things with backbones under a procedural disguise. Tell me if it's sound, and if it isn't what to use instead.

- Where it has to stop. I think there are places where no amount of evidence settles it — whether death is a harm to an animal in the first place, or how a small amount of pain across a very large number of animals is meant to weigh against a modest scientific benefit — and I'd much rather the procedure said "this bit is a decision, not a finding" than pretend otherwise.

Then please run it on three cases shaped like ones from our last few meetings: (1) about fifty thousand fruit fly larvae in a heat-avoidance learning assay, killed at the end; (2) a single rhesus macaque, already trained, in a long-term brain recording study under a severe protocol; (3) larval zebrafish at five days old in a drug screen, where I genuinely don't know whether they can feel anything yet. I want at least one of them to come out with an answer I won't like — my instinct is that the fly one is a paperwork exercise and the monkey is the real ethical problem, and I suspect the procedure says something closer to the opposite, or at least won't let me say that so quickly.
