I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session, whiteboard only and no handouts, for about fifteen volunteers who maintain a fairly large open-source project and are rewriting the rules by which the project elects its five-seat technical steering committee from a ranked ballot. Several of them are engineers with a taste for mechanism design; none of them has formal economics or social choice theory. They have asked, in so many words, whether there is a voting system that cannot be gamed, and my aim is narrower than answering that: I want them to be able to state the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result, see why each of its conditions is load-bearing, and tell a real escape route from an imaginary one. So I don't want the explanation; I want a plan for it.

Give me the order in which the components have to arrive, and what breaks if they arrive later: the idea of a rule as a function from profiles of rankings to a single outcome, the definition of manipulability as the existence of a profile in which some voter does strictly better by reporting something other than her true ranking, the two conditions the audience will mistake for technicalities (that at least three outcomes are actually attainable, and that no voter is a dictator in the technical sense of always getting her favourite among the attainable ones), and only then the statement itself. I want one small election to carry the whole session — my instinct is three voters and three options, with one voter who can promote her second choice to first by demoting her true top choice — and I want you to say what that example must have and must lack: why it needs three attainable outcomes rather than two, why the manipulation has to succeed rather than merely change the winner, why the deviating voter should be able to see the other ballots, and why the example should have no intensities, no ties, no uncertainty and no moral vocabulary about cheating. Then give me the misconception map with a place in the sequence for each: that this is Arrow's theorem again; that it shows democracy to be impossible or all systems equally bad; that five seats somehow exempt them, since the clean statement is for a single winner; that approval or score ballots evade it, since I believe the general version covers non-ordinal inputs; that a second-price or Vickrey-style trick should work here, and why it doesn't fall under the theorem at all; and that it claims people are constantly manipulating real elections rather than that some manipulable profile always exists. I also have a hazy memory that letting the rule randomise buys you nothing better than something like a random dictatorship, but I couldn't reconstruct the argument in front of them, so tell me whether that belongs in the session and where.

Finally: what should I leave out at this level and say aloud that I am leaving out — the proof, I assume, or only its pivotal-voter skeleton — and what should I put on the board in the last ten minutes as the check that it has landed? I had drafted one myself: hand them a rule I invented on the spot, something like plurality with a run-off between the top two whenever they are within one vote of each other, and ask them to find me a manipulation in a profile of their own choosing. Tell me whether that is the right kind of test, what a better one would look like, and what single sentence, if a volunteer can produce it the next day, would convince me the session worked.
