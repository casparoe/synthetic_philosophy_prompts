Next week I am defending, in a job-market talk, a thesis I call the variety principle, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet for the question period rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis: the moral significance of a system's valenced states is fixed by how many functionally distinct valenced computations the system performs, and is insensitive to how many times each of those computations is physically implemented. Distinctness is cashed out as counterfactual independence: two valenced computations count as two only if some nomologically possible input would drive one into its aversive or appetitive state while leaving the other in its neutral state. The guiding thought is that what matters is which distinctions the system itself can make, and that replicating one computation across causally isolated hardware adds no distinction the system can make, whereas adding a genuinely new aversive channel does. I apply this to three cases: insect nociception is not multiplied by the number of ganglia or microcircuits carrying the same aversive signal; scaling a neural network's reward-prediction machinery across more layers and more copies does not by itself scale that system's moral status; and the approach is less exposed than it looks to the familiar indeterminacy worry that any complex open physical system implements arbitrarily many abstract automata.

The room: about fifteen people. Roughly ten are non-reductive functionalists and representationalists who are entirely happy to describe cognition computationally but suspect that questions about which computations matter are verbal disputes in disguise, and who think that whatever is left over is an empirical question for neuroscience. One is a computational neuroscientist who works on insect nociception and believes that computation is individuated too loosely to bear moral weight. Two work on the moral status of AI systems: they are sympathetic to the project but worry about any criterion that hands out moral status either too cheaply or too expensively. Nobody in the room is a consequentialist population ethicist, and nobody works on animal ethics.

Give me the prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would put it rather than in my vocabulary; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is softest; what I should concede in my opening two minutes and what I have to hold; and, separately, the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
