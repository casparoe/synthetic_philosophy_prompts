I'm a journalist — I cover AI and the ideas-ish end of science — and I'm mid-draft on a feature about why "build systems that do what a human would approve of, rather than systems that pursue a goal" keeps getting pitched as a *different shape of agency* rather than just a different objective. I keep writing some version of the sentence "a goal-directed agent is graded on how the world ends up, an approval-directed agent is graded on whether a person would sign off on what it did," and every time I reread it it looks either trivial or false, and I can't tell which. My editor won't let the paragraph go through until I can state, in two or three sentences a smart reader wouldn't roll their eyes at, what the actual disagreement is.

Here's roughly where I've got to, and I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong. It seems to me there are really three different things people might mean by approval-directed, and they behave quite differently: (a) *predicted* approval, where the agent consults some internal model of what an overseer would say and then just acts; (b) *ratified* approval, where an actual human signs off on the action before or around the time it happens, so the approval signal is an event in the world that the agent can in principle influence; and (c) *counterfactual* approval, what a suitably idealized, well-informed and un-manipulated version of the overseer would approve of, which the agent only ever estimates. I've started calling (b) the "live wire" case because the thing being optimized is causally downstream of the agent's own behavior, and I suspect that's where most of the conceptual action is. Separately: at a conference dinner someone told me that approval-directed systems "aren't agents at all, they're just a loss function with a person inside it," which sounded clever but I couldn't get them to cash it out.

So the thing I actually need explained is the distinction itself, not the training methods — please don't spend the answer on RLHF-style mechanics, I've already got 600 words on that and what I'm missing is the conceptual claim underneath. Concretely: if I'm willing to grant that both kinds of system can be described as maximizing an expected utility, then approval-directedness looks like goal-directedness with a strange, conveniently human-shaped utility function defined over a person's mental state rather than over flights booked or bridges built. Is that deflationary way of putting it correct, or does something survive it — say, a difference in what the two systems have *instrumental reason* to do (grabbing control of the physical world versus grabbing control of the approval channel), or a difference in how the policy generalizes once you're off the distribution where the overseer was reliable, or a difference in time-horizon (approval taken per-action versus over whole trajectories)? And then, for the reader who wants to know why anyone would care: what would have to be true about the world for the difference between these two to be the difference between a system that's fine and one that isn't? I can't run equations in the piece, but I can handle the expected-utility framing if you keep it in words, and if there's a clean example — a personal assistant booking something and it going wrong is the one I keep reaching for — that would help.
