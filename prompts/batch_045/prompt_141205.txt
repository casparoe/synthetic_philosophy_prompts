I'm a high school student who programs, and I keep meeting the same package of claims in programming culture without being able to tell whether they hold together. We're told that every general-purpose language can do what every other can, because they're all Turing complete — and also that this isn't a proved theorem but a thesis, one that can't be formally proved. The same people will state flatly that a quantum computer can't solve any problem a normal computer can't, only solve some faster, as though that were settled physics. And P and NP are defined using Turing machines but then treated as if they were about computation itself, so that calling a problem NP-complete is supposed to tell you something that doesn't depend on which machine you own.

Please reconstruct what this package of practices presupposes, kept apart as separate claims rather than merged: the identification of our informal notion of an effective procedure with Turing-machine computability, the claim that nothing physically buildable computes anything a Turing machine cannot, and the stronger claim that any physically reasonable machine can be simulated by a Turing machine with only polynomial overhead. What kind of claim is each — definition, empirical hypothesis, something else — and which of the three does each of my practices strictly need, as opposed to merely sit comfortably with?

Two cases I want addressed directly. First: suppose someone built a machine that ran its first step in one second, its second in half a second, its third in a quarter, and so on, finishing an infinite computation in two seconds. Would that refute what everyone is relying on, or would programmers just say it isn't a computer — and would that reply be a dodge? Second: if the polynomial-overhead claim were false, would NP-completeness results become facts about a machine design rather than about problems, and would Turing completeness stop being what language designers aim for? I'm at least as interested in where the practice is underdetermined — where several rival views would produce exactly this programming culture — as in where it commits us.
