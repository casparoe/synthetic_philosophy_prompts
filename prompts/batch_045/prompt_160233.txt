A friend and I argued late into last night about truth commissions, and I came away unable to say what the institution actually commits you to. His challenge was blunt: either the perpetrator deserved punishment, in which case telling the truth cannot be a way of buying his way out of it, or he did not deserve it, in which case he should have been released unconditionally rather than made to earn his release by confessing. Either way, he said, the trade has no rationale. I replied that the trade only looks incoherent if punishment is assumed to be either a debt that must be discharged or nothing at all, and that the real question is what punishment, truth, and the victim would each have to be for the exchange to make sense. That is what I want from you.

The practice, as I understand it, takes three shapes that pull in different directions.

In South Africa, amnesty was conditional: an applicant who made full disclosure of an act with a political objective could be freed of criminal and civil liability for it. The families of well-known victims objected and were, so far as I can tell, entitled to object but not to veto; the decision belonged to the committee. Applicants were not, as far as I can make out, required to apologise or to express remorse, and some gave self-serving accounts of what they had done and were granted amnesty anyway. The currency was disclosure, not contrition.

In Argentina the order was reversed. Amnesty laws closed off punishment, and proceedings were then held whose stated purpose was to establish what had happened to the disappeared rather than to convict anyone; later the amnesty laws were themselves annulled and prosecutions resumed. Whatever one thinks of that history, it displays a striking priority: punishment was blocked, and the response was not to give up on the past but to pursue a separate good that punishment had been carrying.

In Rwanda, confession before the gacaca courts, as I recall it, bought a substantially reduced sentence rather than a waiver, with part of the reduced sentence served in communal labour, and the proceedings were run by elected local judges in front of the survivors and neighbours of the people who had been killed. Truth was priced, not traded.

Here is what I cannot reconstruct. First, punishment is treated as waivable in South Africa but not in Rwanda, and in Argentina the waiver itself was later declared unlawful: so waivability seems to depend on something other than what the perpetrator deserves. Second, truth is never waivable. Even where no one can be punished, the record is still to be established, and a perpetrator who withholds what he knows is not merely unprotected but, in a sense, still liable. Third, and this is my friend's strongest point, a blanket amnesty that an outgoing regime grants to itself produces exactly the outcome that conditional amnesty produces, namely no prison, yet everyone involved classifies one as impunity and the other as justice of a sort. So the difference cannot lie in the outcomes.

I have a tentative reading that I want tested rather than confirmed. Conditional amnesty looks like a doctrine of substituted performance: there is something the perpetrator owes, hard treatment is one way to discharge it, and full public disclosure is another, and the creditor who may accept the substitute is the political community rather than the victim. But if that is right, three things follow that the practice does not seem to accept. The creditor would be trading away a good it does not own, since the person wronged is the victim and she has no veto. The substitute would be owed to whoever holds the claim, which is why self-granted amnesty is impunity: the debtor is purporting to release himself. And there would have to be a part of the debt that admits no substitute at all, which would explain why the record is never waivable even when everything else is. My friend's alternative is that the non-waivable element is not the information but the community's official condemnation, or else the perpetrator's own acknowledgment of it; the difficulty for him, I think, is that applicants were not required to show remorse, and that a great deal of the disclosure went into archives and reports rather than into any encounter with the people they had harmed.

So: what does this practice presuppose? I want the implicit theory stated precisely enough to see which clause is doing the work in each of the three cases, and on the following points in particular. What view of punishment makes it the sort of thing that can be waived, commuted, or priced at all, given that waiving a duty looks incoherent while waiving a discretionary tool does not? What is the status of truth here: a distinct entitlement of victims and of generations who did not yet exist, an instrument of reconciliation and of resistance to denialism, a substitute for punishment, or the form that condemnation takes when hard treatment is remitted? Who is taken to hold the claim that is being waived, and what does that imply about what the wrong consisted in and about whether victims should have had the veto they were denied? Is a self-granted amnesty really the same outcome as a conditional one, or does the source of the waiver change what it is?

Then two things I care about more than the reconstruction itself. First, tell me which of these commitments the practice strictly requires and which it merely sits well with, because I suspect a thoroughgoing consequentialist who cared only about stability, deterrence, and the avoidance of renewed violence could license almost all of it, and I want to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined rather than expressive of one theory. Second, tell me what would have to change in the institution if the presupposed view were replaced: if punishment were understood as purely forward-looking, with no element of desert at all, what would be left of the truth requirement, and would the commission become something more like an intelligence exercise or a public-health one? And if the right to truth were only instrumental, would a commission be permitted to drop public hearings, or to waive disclosure in the cases where it helps no one, and would what remained still be distinguishable from impunity?

Finally, the case my friend and I could not settle and that I would like the reconstructed theory to decide: a perpetrator who discloses everything, accurately, in a sworn statement to the commission, but refuses to say any of it in public, and whose disclosures chiefly confirm what the survivors already knew. Has he done what the practice demands of him?
