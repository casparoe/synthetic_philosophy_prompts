I'm TAing a metaphysics seminar this term and the professor has been handing down verdicts on causation-by-omission cases at the end of most sessions — just out loud, no handout — and I've been writing them down so I can predict her. I have to present next week and I picked a case she hasn't seen, so I'd like to know what she'll say before I put it on the board. Her verdicts so far, on the question "did that omission cause that outcome?":

1. Billy promises to water Suzy's plant, doesn't, plant dies of drought. Yes.
2. Billy and Suzy both forget to water it; either one of them watering would have saved it; neither does. Yes — both omissions caused the death.
3. A total stranger, no promise, no relationship to anyone, happens to walk past the greenhouse, could have watered it easily, doesn't. Yes, though she added that you'd never actually mention it in conversation.
4. The gardener was locked in the shed by the owner and couldn't get to the plant. No — her idea is that where you couldn't have acted, there's no omission there to be a cause.
5. It doesn't rain for six weeks, and everyone agrees the plant would have lived if it had rained. No: that's a background condition, not a cause.
6. A guard takes a bribe and leaves the loading gate open; thieves walk in and take the stock. Yes, his leaving it open caused the theft.
7. A paramedic is delayed and never reaches the patient; the patient would have died an hour later anyway. Yes, but only for the death at that time: it caused him to die at 3 rather than at 4, not to die rather than live.

Number 7 is the one I cant tell what to make of — I dont know whether that's a real distinction she's making or just a way of hedging.

My case: a clinic loses mains power during a storm at 1am, the ventilator stops, the nurse is supposed to hand-pump it and doesn't, and the patient dies of asphyxiation at 3am. Two wrinkles. First, the hand-pump had been taken off the wall for repair three days earlier and nobody told her, so pumping was not something she could have done; what she could have done was notice the ventilator had stopped, put him in her car, and drive him two miles to the hospital — the roads were clear, she had her keys — and he'd have survived the night. Second, he had an untreatable condition and was going to die inside a week whatever anybody did.

So: does she say the nurse's failure to hand-pump caused the death? What's the rule she seems to be running across 1 through 7, and what other rules fit those seven just as well even though they'd come apart somewhere? What does each of those rules say about my case — both about the pump-being-missing bit and about the he'd-have-died-this-week bit? Give me your best guess with the reason for preferring it over the alternatives. And then the thing I really want: one further scenario I could put to her that would separate whichever rules are still standing after my case.
