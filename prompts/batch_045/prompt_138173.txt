I write a long-form column on language and mind, and I am working on a piece about a very small thing that I keep running into in transcripts: perfectly fluent speakers can say things they know to be false, can lie, can recite lines they reject, can hedge themselves into vapor — and yet there is one shape of sentence that nobody can pull off. You cannot, in one breath and without special stage directions, tell me that it is raining and deny that you believe it. I have spent a few months collecting the cases where the oddity does and does not show up, and my verdicts will not line up with anything I have managed to read about the topic. So I would like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work rather than summarise a debate: find the principle that fits, or tell me which of my verdicts have to go.

My own stab, which I do not trust, is that the oddity appears whenever a speaker does two things at once — takes responsibility for how things are, and disowns the very state in which taking that responsibility consists. That would explain why the oddity survives when the order is reversed, why it evaporates in the past tense and in the third person, and why it reappears for commands and intentions. But it does not explain why hoping is immune, or why the neurological case below improves, or why the knowledge version sits uneasily in between.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts as I actually have them.

1. *It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining.* Unsayable straight. This is the baseline.

2. The same sentence on a stage, delivered by an actor playing a man in denial; and the same sentence inside a supposition — suppose it's raining and I don't believe it. No oddity at all, in either.

3. *Yesterday it was raining for the whole drive, and I never once believed it was — I had filed the sound as a loose hubcap.* Perfectly fine.

4. *It's raining, but Marta doesn't believe it is — she thinks that noise is the shower.* Perfectly fine.

5. *I believe it's raining, but it isn't.* Just as bad as case 1. This surprised me, and it is the reason my own stab may be wrong: if the phenomenon were only about the act of asserting, the belief-first version should be fine.

6. *It's raining, but I hope it isn't.* Fine. *I hope it's raining, but it isn't.* Also fine. Hoping seems immune in both directions.

7. *Close the window, but I don't want you to.* Bad, and the same flavour. *I'm closing the window, but I have no intention of closing it.* Also bad. So the phenomenon is not confined to belief and assertion, which sits badly with case 6.

8. A ladder, in ascending order of badness as I hear it: *It's raining, but I might be wrong about that* (fine); *but I'm not certain* (fine); *but I have no idea whether it's raining* (bad, nearly as bad as case 1). And, hovering between the second and the third: *It's raining, but I don't know that it is.* I go back and forth on this one, and I would like it settled.

9. A patient with a diagnosed condition: whenever it rains, a seizure focus leaves him utterly certain that the sky is clear. His neurologist has explained this to him and he accepts the explanation. Looking out at the rain he says (a) *It's raining, but I don't believe it — this is the thing the tumour does*, and (b) *I believe it's sunny, but it's raining, and I know perfectly well why I believe it.* My verdicts: (b) is fine; (a) is much improved over case 1 but still strained, and I cannot say why the two come apart when the belief involved is the same.

10. A spokesperson, reading: *I've been told to tell you that it's raining in Marrakesh. I don't believe it myself.* Fine.

11. A language model emits a forecast sentence while its own confidence score for that sentence is low, and the developer dashboard displays the sentence and the score side by side. My verdict: no oddity whatsoever. But I cannot say what the model lacks that I have.

12. *Either it's raining, or I don't believe it is.* Fine, and it sounds close to trivial. *It's not the case both that it's raining and that I don't believe it is.* Fine — and it sounds like something I could not be wrong about, which suggests case 1 is a contradiction of some kind. But it cannot be a plain logical contradiction, since case 4 is the very same conjunction and is unremarkable.

One more thing, offered as something to be checked rather than relied on. A logician I interviewed told me that in the standard formal treatment of public announcement, a sentence of the form *p, and you don't know that p* is self-defeating: announce it and it becomes false, because the announcement itself leaves everyone knowing that p. If that is right, it treats knowledge exactly like belief, and my case 8 says they are not alike. I may be garbling it; tell me if I am.

Two cases I have not settled and want your principle to decide, not me. First, the institutional one: a committee issues a report saying the drainage system is unsafe, and the chair later says *the system is unsafe, but we don't believe it* — no member believes it, they voted to publish anyway. Second, the machine one, extended: the model asserts p in one exchange and, in a later exchange, asked directly whether it believes p, says no. Is that a case of the phenomenon, and does the answer turn on whether the exchange is the same conversation?

Please write this as a piece I can work from, in the following sections and roughly the following lengths.

- *Candidate principles*, about 350 words: two or three principles stated precisely enough that they disagree about at least one of my cases, not just about vocabulary.
- *The principle I would defend*, about 500 words: stated clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause and a note on what happens to the pattern if the clause is dropped.
- *Run through my list*, about 400 words: all twelve items, in order, with a verdict on each as your principle predicts it, and a flag on every case where the prediction and my verdict diverge. Include a line on whether the logician's claim is right and, if it is, how your principle accounts for the knowledge version being milder than the belief version.
- *What I would have to give up*, about 250 words: the verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, stated plainly, with your reason for thinking they are the ones that should go. I would rather overturn one verdict than accept a principle with a clause that exists only to save it.
- *The cases I have not settled*, about 300 words: the committee and the two-exchange machine, plus at least one further case of your own devising that your principle decides and that I have not thought of.
- *What the phenomenon is evidence for*, about 200 words: whether this is really a fact about the constitution of belief, a fact about self-knowledge, or a fact about the norms of speech — and, since I have to say something true in eight hundred words for a general reader, which of those is the honest headline.

Define any term of art you need the first time you use it, and do not give me a reading list or cite anything; I want the argument, not the sources.
