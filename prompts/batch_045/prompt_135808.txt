I am a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long feature about fakes, forgeries, and why anyone cares about originals, and my editor wants the piece to turn on a real institutional decision rather than the usual autographic/allographic retread. So for eighteen months I sat in on the acquisitions and display committee of a mid-sized art museum I will call the Alderly, which has unusual power: it decides not only what enters the collection but whether an object is hung as the real thing, hung as a document of something else, or refused a wall entirely. Its written policy says the museum displays works, and defines a work as an object whose making is attributable to an identified artistic intention. One curator keeps invoking what she calls the fifty-year substitution test: if object B could stand in for object A in the same frame for fifty years without any viewer, scholar, or conservator noticing, there is no difference worth curating around. Another keeps talking about shadow provenance, the way a documentation chain can outlive the object it documents. Here are their recent rulings, all of them unanimous or near-unanimous. One: a workshop copy of a Titian made in Titian's shop by a documented pupil, never passed off as Titian — hung in a secondary gallery labelled workshop of Titian, not in the Italian rooms. Two: a 1930s Dutch fabrication of a seventeenth-century Vermeer, exposed decades later — refused a wall entirely; a trustee proposed showing it as what it is, a 1930s forgery, and the committee voted that down, saying they do not exhibit lies. Three: a posthumous bronze cast of 1985, pulled from the artist's own 1912 plaster, authorized by the estate, one of an edition of six — displayed as a work by the artist, full attribution, no asterisk. Four: a one-to-one print of a Roman portrait bust made in 2023 in marble dust, never intended to deceive, indistinguishable to the eye and to the hand — study gallery only, behind glass, as a replica. Five: a fifteenth-century fresco of which roughly seventy percent of the visible surface was repainted in the 1850s by a restorer working from surviving traces — displayed as the fifteenth-century original, with a condition note. Six: a large 1971 minimalist fabrication, destroyed in a fire, remade in 2016 to the artist's own specification and under her supervision, entirely by hired fabricators she never touched a tool — displayed as the 1971 work by the artist, with no note that the object is the second fabrication. Seven: an artist's own autograph replica, painted thirty years later from memory, of her own early painting lost in a shipwreck — displayed as a work by the artist, dated to the year of the replica.

The case now before them, which I need your help with. Iris Vane, 1913 to 1994, painted a large canvas called Depot, Night in 1961; it was the Alderly's single most requested work and it burned in a warehouse fire in 1998. Surviving: the original stretcher, badly charred but restorable; the original frame; her preparatory oil sketch; several hundred colour transparencies; her studio notes on what she was trying to do; and a box of her unused pigments. A team proposes a reconstruction: period-equivalent canvas on the original stretcher, in the original frame, painted by hand by a living specialist in Vane's technique working from the transparencies and the sketch, with the gaps filled by a generative model trained on her complete catalogue and on this painting's documentation. The committee is told to assume that the result would be, by expert consensus, indistinguishable from what hung in that room in 1997, and that no one proposes to call it a Vane: the label would read Reconstruction after Iris Vane, original destroyed 1998, and the room would carry a wall text explaining the fire, the method, and the model. Two further facts they have in front of them. Vane's estate has authorized the project and assigned the fee to a scholarship in her name. And in a 1989 interview Vane said she hoped her paintings would never be reproduced, reconstructed, or, her word, resurrected, and that she would rather a burnt painting stayed burnt. The proposal is to hang it in the room where the original hung.

So: what will this committee do, and what should it do if it wants to be consistent with itself? Give me the candidate rules that fit all seven precedents, say what each predicts here and where they come apart, tell me which single precedent is the closest analogue and which is a trap, and give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives. Then name the further case that would actually settle which rule they are running. I have two in mind and I suspect at least one is useless: the same reconstruction but with a signed 1993 letter from Vane authorizing it, and, separately, an honest, labelled, machine-woven tapestry after a Vane the museum still owns, hung for a year beside the original in an experiment where visitors are told they will see one or the other. Tell me whether either of these discriminates between the rules you have identified, or whether there is a cleaner case that does, and what each outcome would tell me about what this museum thinks it is for. I have about four thousand words and a fact-checker, so precision about the reasoning matters more to me than a verdict I can quote.
