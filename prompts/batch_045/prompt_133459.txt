I run an evening discussion class called Art and Propaganda. I have no academic background; I come to this material as someone who has spent years reading and arguing about W. E. B. Du Bois's essay on Black art on my own, and my teaching sticks to that essay and the questions it raises rather than to anything written about it afterwards. So I would like your help judging an essay against what the essay itself supports, without appealing to secondary literature.

The question I set (20 marks: 5 marks each for parts (i) and (ii), 6 for part (iii), 4 for the overall quality of the argument):

In the excerpt we read, Du Bois makes two claims that are often quoted separately. The first is that all art is propaganda, and always has been. The second is that propaganda, to do its work, must also be beautiful and true. He does not treat these as a contradiction, and the question is how they fit together.

(i) Read literally, the first claim says that propaganda is not something a work may or may not be, the way it may or may not be a novel or a song. What is the shift in the debate between "making art" and "making propaganda" that this claim is supposed to bring about? Someone who insists they want to make art and not propaganda is, on this view, in what position?

(ii) If every work is propaganda whatever its maker intends, why is it not pointless to add that propaganda should be beautiful and true? Are Beauty and Truth extra requirements laid on top of propaganda, or are they only conditions of a given work's succeeding at what propaganda does? Say which reading you take, and why.

(iii) Two novels appeared recently, both of them retellings of an old slave narrative. The first is politically unmistakable and urgent, but clumsily written: the characters are types, the scenes exist to make points, and readers who already agree with it are the only ones who finish it. The second is beautifully written, oblique, and politically harder to place; some readers come away from it with the wrong impression entirely. On the reading of Du Bois you have given, which of the two is the finer and more valuable work, and why?

(iv) A third case, which I would like you to use as a test of your answer to (ii). A beautifully written novel offering a degraded and false vision of Black life becomes a bestseller, and large numbers of readers come away thinking slightly worse of Black people than they did before. By Du Bois's criteria, is this successful propaganda or failed propaganda? Does your answer to (ii) still look right once you have said which?

The answer I need you to grade:

(i) Du Bois is not separating works into two kinds, art here and propaganda there. On his view a work is propaganda in virtue of what it does to its audience, not what its maker meant it to do. Anything that shows the world in a certain light — that makes some arrangement look natural, or worth wanting, or not worth commenting on — is doing that work, and a work that looks purely decorative or purely personal is doing it as much as an explicitly political one. So the distinction the aestheticist wants to draw is not available to him: the pursuit of beauty for its own sake is not a way of opting out of propaganda, it is one more position about which vision art should carry, and it supports the arrangement already in place by treating that arrangement as the settled background against which art happens. Once propaganda is inescapable in this sense, the debate between making art and making propaganda is badly posed. The question left is not whether to make propaganda but which propaganda to make — whose vision, and of what.

(ii) The two claims are not in tension; the second follows from the first. Once we see that every work is propaganda in virtue of the vision it carries, the question of what makes for good propaganda and bad propaganda cannot be avoided, and Beauty and Truth are his answer to it. They are not a second and separate test that a work has to pass in addition to being propaganda; their whole point is to make propaganda better at what it does. A work that preaches, or that lies about the people it claims to speak for, is not deficient because it has failed some standard of disinterested aesthetic excellence that has nothing to do with its politics. It is deficient because Beauty and Truth are the criteria of whether the propaganda works: a work that is ugly or false does not change how people see, and so does not do what propaganda is for. Ugly propaganda therefore fails twice over, as propaganda and as art.

(iii) The second novel is the finer and more valuable work. The first one is urgent, but urgency is not the point; on the criteria he actually gives, a work that only reaches those who already agree with it has not changed anyone's mind, and a work whose characters are types has not told the truth about the people it is about. It is propaganda that fails at being propaganda. The second novel is harder to place and will sometimes be misread, but that is a risk any work that is genuinely beautiful runs, and a work that is beautiful and true will do more in the long run for the vision it carries than one that is merely emphatic.

(iv) This is failed propaganda, and badly failed. It is beautiful, so it passes one of the criteria, but it is false, and a work that is false fails the criterion of Truth no matter how many people read it or how much it moves them. Its beauty is what makes it dangerous: it is precisely because the work is well made that the lie gets as far as it does. So it is not successful propaganda that happens to have a bad politics; it is a failure, and the reading I gave in (ii) is what allows us to say so rather than having to concede that it succeeded.

Could you give this a mark out of 20 with a letter grade, and set out the comments that justify it? I would like the substance of your justification to be specific: where the reading holds up, where it is shaky or does not follow from the claims the answer itself makes, where the essay's own terms quietly drift, and whether each of the four parts actually gives me what I asked for. I am not looking for a general impression of whether the essay is good, and I have deliberately not said what I think of it.
