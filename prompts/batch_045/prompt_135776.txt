My sister's taking a philosophy class and she tried to explain this guy Grice to me: cooperative principle, the maxims, the difference between what a sentence literally says and what the speaker "implicates," and that implicatures can be cancelled — like if you say "I'm not saying X, I'm just saying Y" then supposedly you can't be accused of having said X. I have no idea if she got him right, but it made me realize our conduct code seems to be doing philosophy the whole time without admitting it.

Here's the practice as far as I can tell from the handbook and from sitting in on hearings. The code says you can be disciplined for speech that "a reasonable member of the targeted group would understand as a slur or a threat," even when the actual words are harmless. But: (a) at the hearing the dean has to "show the work" — literally walk through how you get from the words on the screen to the insulting reading, step by step — and if he can't lay out the steps the charge gets dropped; (b) if you say something genuinely awful but convince the board you were being ironic, i.e. mocking people who talk like that, you're usually fine; (c) if you say something whose literal content is completely innocent but the people it's about obviously get it, you're usually not fine, even when you add "not talking about anyone here lol"; (d) the board rejects "I'm sorry if anyone was offended" as a non-apology and makes you write "I'm sorry for what I said"; and (e) intent seems to be everything in the irony cases and nothing in the other cases, which is the bit I keep going in circles on.

Two cases that broke my brain. Case 1: a kid posted a screenshot of a news article about grade inflation in our class groupchat with the caption "crazy how its always the kids who got into [State] who are suddenly so worried about this." The sentence is literally just about an article, the four people who got into State all instantly understood it as calling them cheaters, and he says "im not saying anyone cheated, im literally quoting the headline." I've started calling this double-audience speech: the speaker wants the target to get it and wants the adult adjudicating it to only see the innocent reading. Case 2: somebody posted a meme where three different groups each read the joke as being about them in three different insulting ways, and nobody could pin down which reading was "the" meaning — the board basically gave up. I call that one a shotgun implicature. My sister says Grice's setup struggles with both, something about an implicature having to be worked out from shared assumptions, but she was also half asleep.
