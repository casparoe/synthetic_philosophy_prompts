I'm running a 90-minute session for about 14 undergrads in a reading group that's doing idealized preferences and coherent extrapolated volition, and I want an activity, not a lecture. The thing I actually want them to feel is the gap between *cleaning up* someone's preferences (more information, less noise, no self-deception, no akrasia) and *improving* them — and the fact that the cleaned-up version and the improved version can come apart, and that from inside the procedure you often can't tell which one you just did.

So: give me rules, roles, materials and a timeline. Participants should do the extrapolating *for someone else*, from a dossier of messy, partly contradictory preferences — give me two or three concrete dossiers (someone with a strong religious or political identity, someone with an addiction, someone whose whole life is organised around something they'd probably drop if they knew more). Then hit them with two twists: the swap test, where the same procedure gets run on a dossier whose values the group finds repellent; and the ratchet, where the extrapolated version of the person gets to run the procedure once more on itself.

I want them to notice on their own the three ways this kind of thing fails — the output just collapses back into the person's actual preferences, or it converges suspiciously across very different people because the group smuggled its own ideals in, or it produces something the person would disown as not theirs. Don't name the view until after the debrief, and the debrief should map the moments in the room onto those failure modes.

Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong (I expect at least one group to just "educate" their subject into a nicer version of themselves) and which part of the idea the activity simply can't stage, so I know what I have to say out loud myself.
