Hey — hoping you can help me turn some of the epistemic injustice literature into something my team can actually run. Quick context: I work in the patient experience office at a large safety-net hospital, and a growing share of what lands in our queue isn't really a complaint about wait times or billing — it's people saying, in one way or another, that nobody took them seriously. We close about 40–50 of these a month, I've got maybe 25 minutes per case, the reviewers are retired nurses and social workers with no philosophy background, every letter goes out under my director's name, and anything that hints at a clinical mistake has to be cleared by legal first. I did a medical humanities reading group on this stuff a couple years ago so I know the basic vocabulary — credibility deficits from identity prejudice on the testimonial side, gaps in shared interpretive resources on the hermeneutical side, and the mirror-image case where someone gets *more* credibility than their evidence warrants — but I don't want a literature recap or a reading list, I want the operational version.

So: give me a decision procedure, in the order we'd actually run it. Start with a triage question that peels off complaints that are really about something else (a genuine clinical error, a broken process, a request for money) so we don't epistemologize everything. Then the steps for deciding whether what happened is an epistemic failure at all, and if so which kind, and what kind of repair is owed — where "repair" might mean re-authoring part of the medical record, entering the patient's own words rather than the clinician's summary, giving feedback to a specific clinician, changing an intake form, or just an honest letter. For each step I want to know what counts as a *sufficient* answer, where the procedure should stop and say no repair is owed, and where it has to hand off to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Three wrinkles I keep hitting that I want the procedure to handle rather than dodge: (1) what I've started calling credibility laundering — the report gets believed only once a clinician restates it in clinical register, so the patient is effectively believed but accumulates no track record, and the chart can't support them next time; (2) the version where the speaker isn't the patient at all but a family member or informal interpreter, and the uptake failure and the repair owed arguably belong to two different people; (3) the mixed case where a report was discounted partly for illegitimate reasons and partly because it was clinically unlikely, where I don't want the procedure to just split the difference. If any of those three collapse into distinctions the literature already makes, say so conceptually — I don't need to know who named what.

Then run it on three cases and show your work. (a) A woman in her forties with a history of fibromyalgia goes to the ED reporting what she describes as her nerves being on fire; the resident charts "chronic pain, drug-seeking r/o," her own phrasing never enters the record, and a scan three weeks later finds something real. (b) A daughter interpreting for her father, who has limited English and a dementia diagnosis, reports that he'd been saying his hip hurt for days before a fall; the team charts "baseline confusion" and treats the daughter as the sole authority, and later no one will credit either of them. (c) The one I expect to argue with you about: a fluent, well-insured, locally prominent man complains that a junior resident wouldn't accept his self-diagnosis and demands a letter acknowledging that he was epistemically wronged and that his preferred diagnosis be recorded; the chart shows a thorough and correct workup that ruled it out. I want at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and I want you to say plainly where the rubric is really a judgment call dressed up as a procedure. Finally, give me a cheap audit we could run on a quarter's worth of closed complaints to tell whether using this is actually changing outcomes or just producing more nicely worded letters. Please write the whole thing in American English, and don't worry if it runs long.
