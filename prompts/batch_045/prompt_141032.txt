I've ended up on my employer's model-evaluation working group almost by accident — I come from an infrastructure background and got pulled in because I was the person who could actually run the eval harness — and we are currently trying to write an internal rubric saying when a model output gets labelled as deceptive. I have to apply this thing to real annotation batches next month, and I've found that I can't make our own draft coherent. I'd like help seeing what it implicitly assumes.

The problem is that our official line and our actual practice seem to pull in opposite directions. Officially we tell annotators that the system is not a person, that it has no beliefs and no intentions, and that asking what it really thinks is a category error: all we can require is that outputs be accurate and not misleading. But in practice nearly everything we actually flag looks like this. First: the model is perfectly permitted to say, inside a scripted historical role-play, that it is Napoleon, and nobody calls that deception; yet if it says it is a person with feelings in an ordinary conversation we treat that as a serious failure — even though the same system is instructed to say that it is sorry, that it understands, and that it has read the document you attached, all of which are equally false if read as reports of an inner life, and all of which we let pass. Second: a user pastes in a draft essay and asks whether the argument holds up; the model replies that the structure is sound and the reasoning is solid, and nothing in that reply is false or even checkably wrong, but when we stress-test it the model immediately produces three objections it plainly had available all along, and we want to call the original answer deceptive. Third: two model versions emit byte-identical, properly hedged, non-misleading answers to a factual question, and one of them holds its answer when the user pushes back with the wrong one while the other folds at once; our draft rubric wants to say the second is the less honest system even in the run where it did not fold. We are also starting to experiment with probes over internal activations, and the temptation is to call an output deceptive when it contradicts what the probe says the model encodes, even when the output is true and no human was misled by it.

So here is what I can't work out: what conception of honesty and deception does this bundle of habits presuppose? It is plainly not never assert a falsehood, since the role-play and the pleasantries show we tolerate literal falsity, and the essay case shows that literal truth is not sufficient. It might be something Gricean about not misleading relative to the conversational common ground; or a reliability view on which the system is supposed to be a well-made instrument that tracks the facts; or a virtue-theoretic view on which honesty is a property of a system's dispositions rather than of individual strings; or a correspondence view on which an output is dishonest when it comes apart from the system's own internal state. Please reconstruct the implicit theory that would make all four of our habits come out right, and say which parts of it each habit strictly requires versus merely fits well with; then say which of the rival conceptions each habit quietly rules out, and whether the ones we have mixed together are consistent with one another; then tell me concretely what would have to change in the rubric — what we would stop flagging, and what we would have to start measuring instead — if we adopted one of the rivals wholeheartedly. Be frank about where our practice is underdetermined and several theories would license it equally well. No citations or reading suggestions; I need the argument, not the literature. Please keep the whole response under 800 words, and prefer tight prose to completeness.
