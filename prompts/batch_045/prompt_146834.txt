I am working through the dispute between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, and I keep losing the thread at one particular point, so I would like an explanation rather than a survey.

Both views are usually introduced with the same kind of example: two subjects with identical evidence for the same proposition, one facing trivial stakes and one facing serious ones, where we are inclined to credit knowledge to the first and withhold it from the second. And both views are then often summarized in a way that makes them sound like notational variants: contextualism says the truth of "S knows that p" varies with the attributor's context, encroachment says it varies with the subject's stakes, and the choice between them looks like a choice about where to attach a single parameter. Please explain why that summary is misleading. I take it the key is that the two theses are claims about different things -- one about which proposition a knowledge sentence expresses, the other about which facts fix the truth value of a given proposition -- but I would like that difference made vivid, including why it means the two views can conflict rather than merely redescribe the same phenomenon, and why a contextualist can often simulate encroachment by letting the attributor's context track the subject's stakes.

I would then like you to work the difference out on cases where subject-stakes and attributor-context come apart. Let p be the claim that the bridge on the coast road is open to traffic this afternoon, and suppose two drivers have exactly the same evidence for p -- the same municipal sign, read an hour ago, equally reliable in both cases. One is driving to a picnic and does not care much; the other faces something that matters a great deal. Now consider:

1. The high-stakes driver is asked whether the low-stakes driver knows that p. What does each view predict about the truth of her denial?
2. A third party, who has no stake in p and no intention of acting on it, hazily forms the belief that p; unbeknownst to her, someone else will act on her say-so in a matter of life and death. Are those the third party's stakes, the attributor's, or nobody's? What does each view say, and what would a view have to say if it wanted knowledge to be the thing that makes a proposition strong enough to serve as a premise in deliberation?
3. A subject's stakes appear trivial but are in fact catastrophic, because someone has quietly arranged for error to be disastrous; the subject does not know this. Do actual stakes or appreciated stakes do the encroaching? What is at stake in that choice?

Finally, two structural questions that I think bear on which view is even coherent. First, disagreement: when the low-stakes speaker says "she knows" and the high-stakes subject says "I do not," does either view let the two of them be genuinely contradicting each other, and how should we think about the retrospective report -- "she knew all along, even though at the time we would have denied it" -- given that past-tense attributions seem to let a present speaker's standards govern an earlier subject's epistemic position? Second, direction: encroachment as usually stated is one-directional, in that raising the stakes can only cost you knowledge. Is there any case in which higher stakes make knowledge easier to come by -- say, where the cost of further inquiry is itself enormous, or where acting on p is the only route out of a disaster -- and if not, what in the view explains the asymmetry?

I care less about being told which view wins than about being able to say where the load-bearing difference lies and which datum each view handles awkwardly. Please keep the discussion concrete enough that I could reconstruct the arguments from it.
