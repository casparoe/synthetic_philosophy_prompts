I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep coming back to something wierd about how professional mathematics handles proofs that need a computer.

The practice, as best I can tell: a computer-assisted proof gets treated as settling the theorem — journals publish it, people cite the result, textbooks eventually say it is known that — but the field also acts as if something is still owed. People go on hunting for a human-readable proof decades later. The four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture are the standard examples, and now there is the whole proof-assistant world (Lean, Coq) plus machine-learning systems that emit formal proofs. Grad students get told that formalisation is the gold standard and also that a paper which is just a checked file is not a paper.

Here is the bit I cannot square. The complaint against computer proofs is that nobody can survey them: they hand you a certificate without understanding. But the same community happily accepts non-constructive existence proofs that give you no object whatever, and it accepts enormous human arguments — the classification of finite simple groups, any 300-page paper in a top journal — that maybe four people have read end to end, without treating that as a scandal. So it is not constructivism in any straightforward sense; if it were, half of classical analysis would have to go as well. And it is not literally about some human mind having checked it, because in the long cases no mind has.

So: what does the practice actually presuppose about what a proof is? I have been playing with a distinction of my own — proof as certificate (settles whether), proof as warrant (gives the community reasons to believe), proof as equipment (leaves you able to re-derive it, teach it, extend it) — and my guess is the practice really needs the third and only pays lip service to the first two. But I cannot tell whether that is a philosophical claim or just a journalist's excuse for a vibe.

What I want: reconstruct the implicit theory properly; tell me which features of the practice strictly require it and which would be licensed equally well by two or three rival theories; say where the practice is underdetermined. And if I am wrong that this has nothing to do with constructivism — if the discomfort is really a residue of identifying truth with provability-by-creatures-like-us, or if the fact that classical proofs can be mechanically translated into constructive-looking ones makes the whole worry evaporate — say that too.

Last thing: what would change for working mathematicians if we dropped the presupposition? If a proof is just a certificate, what does an article become, a release note? Would we lose something real, or only a status hierarchy?
