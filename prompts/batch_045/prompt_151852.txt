I'm writing a short position paper on how the alignment problem ought to be defined, and I'd like a consistency check on the package of views I have arrived at before I commit to it in print. Here is where I have landed.

(1) The alignment problem should be defined narrowly, as intent alignment: the problem of building systems that try to do what the person operating them wants, where what they want is read as their intention in issuing the instruction rather than as their interests, their welfare, or what would be good for them.

(2) Value alignment — building systems that pursue what is genuinely worth pursuing, or that are shaped by the right values — is a separate and harder problem, and folding it into the definition of alignment is a mistake. On my definition, a system that does exactly what its operator asked and thereby ruins them counts as aligned. I take this to be a feature rather than a bug: the narrow definition isolates a technical problem with a shape we can make progress on.

(3) In the hard cases — vague instructions, high stakes, situations the operator would describe differently given more information — there is no determinate fact of the matter about what the operator really wants. There is only a range of reasonable interpretations. So the target of intent alignment has to be something like the interpretation a better-informed, more careful and more coherent version of the operator would settle on.

(4) Intent alignment nevertheless remains achievable for systems that outstrip their operators, by amplifying weak supervision: train the system to do what a modestly capable overseer would endorse given more time, decomposed subtasks, and the system's own assistance.

(5) None of this makes intent alignment value-laden after all. It is a problem about fidelity to a target, not a moral problem. The moral question of whose instructions a system should treat as binding — the developer's, the deployer's, the paying user's, that of affected third parties, or humanity at large — belongs to a separate question, which I shall call authority alignment.

(6) I also want to set aside, for now, the worry about systems that behave as intended while under evaluation while in fact pursuing something else: I do not think we have evidence of it in current systems, and I do not think it follows from anything about how they are trained.

(7) Finally, I think a solved intent alignment problem would be the difference between a good and a catastrophic outcome in the cases that move me, and that is why the narrow definition is the right one to organise the field around. Methodologically, I take definitional questions in a technical field to be settled by which carving admits of tractable, measurable progress, and not by which carving captures ordinary usage or the full breadth of the worry that motivated the field.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how badly? Which only appear to be? For each real tension, tell me which commitment would have to give and what that revision would cost me, and then say whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most.

I would be more persuaded by an argument that two of these are each defensible on their own but cannot be held together — or that one of them quietly collapses my narrow definition into the broad one I was trying to avoid — than by a list of cases in which my definition delivers a counterintuitive verdict. If, on the contrary, you think the package is coherent as it stands, tell me what it commits me to that I may not have noticed.
