"Right but early" is not a property a theory can have

The move goes like this. Someone makes a long-run prediction. The date arrives, nothing happens. They say: the mechanism is right, I was just early. In software this is basically house style. Every few years someone announces that scaling is about to hit a wall, or that in N years nobody will write code by hand, and when N passes they say the trend was right and the timeline was optimistic.

Here's my claim. "Early" is a property of a run, not of a theory, and treating it as a property of a theory is what lets a prediction survive every possible outcome. A theory only gets to be early relative to a clock, and the clock is almost never part of the theory — it's borrowed from background conditions. When the clock expires and nothing has happened, "right but early" quietly replaces the background conditions while keeping the mechanism, and books the swap as a small calendar correction.

So split a long-run prediction into three parts.

1. The mechanism. The causal story that is supposed to keep running: mouths multiply, the food curve doesn't, the gap closes, misery follows.
2. The trigger conditions. What has to hold for that mechanism to be the thing that actually sets the outcome, rather than one input among several.
3. The clock. The timescale on which the mechanism is supposed to become visible.

The mechanism is the part people defend. The clock is the part that fails. And the clock is nearly always imported — from a doubling rate, a trend line, a guess about how fast technology moves — which means it was never a commitment the theory made. It was a loan.

That gives three different things "it hasn't happened yet" can mean, and I think they get collapsed into each other constantly.

Deferred. The mechanism is running, its effect is accumulating somewhere you can't see, and a buffer is soaking it up. A reservoir draining. The prediction is still live, and live in a checkable way: you should be able to point at the buffer and watch it go down.

Defeated. A counter-mechanism of comparable or greater strength turned up and permanently offsets the first one. Not delayed — outcompeted. Pulling nitrogen out of the air to make fertilizer is the obvious candidate in the Malthus case. The mechanism is as true as it ever was, it's just no longer load-bearing for the outcome, because the ceiling started rising faster than the thing pressing against it.

Dissolved. The outcome variable stopped measuring what it used to measure, or stopped mattering in the way the prediction cared about. The question the prediction answered is no longer the question anyone is asking.

I want a test that separates these without waiting another century. Here's my attempt: the rescue ledger. For every year the prediction fails to arrive, something has to explain the gap. Ask two things about that something. First, is it a stock or a flow? A one-time offset — a discovery, a new input, a one-off windfall — is a stock, and stocks are finite, so the deferral story has teeth: you can ask how much is left. A recurring offset that has to get bigger each year is a flow, and a growing flow is doing the work the mechanism was supposed to do. Second, does the rescue predict anything you weren't already going to look at? A deferral story names the buffer and says it drains. A story that only retrodicts — that explains the gap after the fact, in whatever vocabulary is lying around — is an escape hatch with an invoice attached.

Call the failure mode rescue inflation: the offset you have to posit grows about as fast as the mechanism's own effect, forever, with no buffer named and no end in sight. At that point you don't have a theory that was early. You have a different theory wearing the old theory's name, and the name is doing rhetorical work — it's spending credibility the new theory never earned.

Last bit, the part I actually care about. Even in the charitable case, where the mechanism really was right and only the clock was wrong, nearly all the practical content of a prediction lives in the clock. Policy, prices, careers, data centers — all of it gets built on "soon." If soon was wrong by a century, then the mechanism being right is true and close to worthless. "I was right about the mechanism" isn't the defense it sounds like. It's an answer to a question nobody acted on.
