At a local EA-adjacent meetup last month, someone closed an argument about cause prioritization with the line "the arc of history is long, but it bends toward justice." The work it was made to do was load-bearing: if moral improvement is the default trajectory, then the long-run future is good-by-default, which weakens the case for spending on existential-risk mitigation rather than on near-term global-poverty work, and which licenses treating current democratic backsliding as noise rather than evidence.

Treat it as the unattributed slogan it has become, and precisify it into a menu of distinct theses I could actually evaluate. I'd like at least the following separated out:

- What "bends" commits you to geometrically: only that the endpoint is higher than the start, or that the slope is positive on average, or that the slope is positive throughout, or that curvature is positive so improvement is accelerating. Note explicitly that a curve can bend toward a target and still asymptote short of it, and can bend toward it while its current slope is negative.
- Whether "justice" is a target specified independently of where history goes, or a label we attach post hoc to wherever it ended up — the whig-history reading — and whether it means distributive justice, a widening circle of moral concern, reduced cruelty, or something else that could come apart from the others.
- Whether the claim describes the shape of the recorded past, predicts the future, or states an expected value conditional on there still being evaluators around to register it, and what baseline or counterfactual the improvement is measured against — given that much of what gets counted as moral progress may be rising state capacity and material surplus rather than rising virtue.
- Whether it is offered as a prediction at all, or as a commitment device or rallying cry whose utterance is supposed to help produce the outcome it describes.
- Whether "long" is doing any work beyond insulating the claim from falsification within any window anyone alive can check.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and contested claim, or straightforwardly false. Then tell me which readings would actually support the inference my interlocutor wanted — that the long-run default is good and that present backsliding carries little evidential weight — and which merely license "things were worse earlier," which is perfectly compatible with a default future that is very bad. My suspicion is that almost none of them do the work he needed, and that the ones which come closest are also the least likely to be true; tell me whether that is right, and if so which single reading would do the most for him if he could establish it.
