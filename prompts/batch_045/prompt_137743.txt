I'm finishing a novel narrated in the present tense by someone who is speaking from after the events, so that every sentence is at once a report from a person who knows how it ends and the experience of a person who doesn't. I want the book to end with a single short poem, no more than a page, that turns that double position into a question about assertion rather than a trick of tense — and I want the philosophy done by the poem's structure rather than stated in it.

The form: roughly a rondeau. Fifteen lines, two rhymes, and a refrain consisting of one plain declarative sentence, flat and unhedged, the kind of sentence a novel produces without thinking — "The house was empty when he got there," or something of the sort. The refrain returns three times in the same words; the last time it closes the poem, broken off or hedged, as if the speaker were no longer willing to put their weight on it.

What changes between the returns is not the sentence but the position of whoever is saying it. Once, by someone who knows it. Once, by someone with grounds that fully justify the belief and that are nonetheless not knowledge — the lottery case, where the odds overwhelm and the belief is still a variety of luck — and, as the reader later discovers, the sentence is true. Once, by someone with no warrant at all, for whom it is true because they are making the world in which it is true: my narrator's case, and the one I cannot settle, since if what narrators do is not assertion but something beside it — showing, or inviting make-believe — then no norm of assertion reaches it, and a novel may say anything.

I keep going back and forth between the three answers this literature offers: that one may assert only what one knows, only what is true, or only what it is reasonable to believe. I'd like a reader who knows that argument to be able to say of each return which answer it is meant to embarrass, and a reader who doesn't to feel the same sentence getting harder to say. Keep the sentence flat even where the saying is wrong, so that the defect lies in the act and not in the wording; and let at least one return go wrong while being true, so that the wrongness cannot be a matter of falsity. If you can also give the truth norm its own trouble somewhere in a surrounding line — a claim everyone was entitled to, and that was false — do. No apparatus: at most two lines after the poem telling me which return is which.
