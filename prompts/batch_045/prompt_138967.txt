I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on Aristotle on exchange, and I'm running a graduate reading group this term on the treatment of money and exchange in Nicomachean Ethics V and Politics I: proportional requital as what holds a community together, the shrine of the Graces set up in a public place, money as a conventional surety of future exchange rather than a good in itself, the contrast between household management, which aims at something and therefore has a natural limit, and wealth-getting that has none, and the claim that money is barren, so that a coin fathering a coin is the most unnatural way of acquiring anything. We meet for the last time in three weeks, and I'd like to end with something lighter than another commentary.

What I want is a parody artifact: the white paper for a fictional cryptocurrency, written in complete sincerity by founders who take themselves to be rigorous Aristotelians. Call the token something like Nomisma. The protocol should try to implement, in smart-contract form, exactly the doctrine the group has been reading — commensuration by reference to need, money as a pledge of later exchange, exchange as the bond of a political community. And it must, like any token, offer holders a return: a yield schedule, a distribution, a staking mechanism — because otherwise no one would hold it. That collision is where I want the comedy to come from: the founders reasoning in good faith from the texts to a yield-bearing instrument, relabelling the return in Aristotelian vocabulary (proportional requital, a reciprocity distribution, a pledge that pays) so that it does not count as the thing Politics I says is most contrary to nature, with a footnote apparatus that tries and fails to close the gap.

Constraints:

- The register and structure of a real white paper — abstract, problem statement, tokenomics with a diagram described in words, a governance section, risk disclosures or an FAQ — about 800–900 words, plus the postscript below.
- Deadpan throughout, and legible to a reader who has not done the reading. No character may ever say that Aristotle would object; the reader should notice unaided, and I don't want the narrator to wink in the final paragraph.
- At least two jokes that only land for someone who has done the reading. Two I'd especially like to see used: the point that money exists by convention rather than by nature and can be rendered worthless by changing the convention, sitting in the same document as the usual "sound money" claim, with the founders unaware of any tension; and the story Aristotle tells about Midas, worked in as a feature, a warning, or a mode of the treasury.
- Every exaggeration should be of something Aristotle actually holds. No invented puritanism: he is not against markets, not against money, and not against all exchange — the satire should be fair to what he does say.
- One invented epigraph or cover blurb from a fictional Aristotelian or fictional reviewer, clearly fictional and not attributed to any real person. Please put nothing in quotation marks from any actual text and cite nothing; I want all of it in your own words.

Finally, a postscript of 100–150 words, which I'll circulate with the piece: the two or three places where the parody depends on a contested reading of the passages — how sharp the natural/unnatural distinction really is, what exactly the complaint about interest is a complaint about, whether commensuration runs through need or through money as a measure — given as the alternatives a specialist would press; and the single joke you would cut if a Hellenist told you it was unfair, together with what would have to be true of the texts for you to keep it.
