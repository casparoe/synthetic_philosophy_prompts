I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a graduate seminar on the ethics of existential risk and the far future. We opened with Samuel Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* (Oxford University Press, 2013), and the seminar spent almost all of its two weeks on it arguing about whether the afterlife conjecture is *true*: the claim, roughly, that a great deal of what we now value would lose its point for us if we learned that humanity would end very soon after our own deaths — not just the obviously future-directed projects like curing a disease or raising a child, but also things with no connection to posterity at all, like finishing a paper or listening to a quartet. Scheffler's two stock scenarios — one in which everyone alive dies shortly after I do, and one in which no further children are ever born although the present population lives out its natural lives — did most of the work in that discussion.

I have a final paper to write: about twenty-five pages, one semester, no empirical work, and I get to choose the topic. I've decided I don't want to spend it re-litigating whether the conjecture is true, which is what everyone in the seminar will write about. I want to spend it on whatever *follows* from it — or on whatever failure of a follow-through is most worth exposing. So what I'd like from you is an agenda, not a survey: among the open questions the conjecture raises, which one or two deserve twenty-five pages from someone in my position, and why those rather than the others? Please be explicit about the criteria you're using and about the trade-offs between them — how much turns on the answer downstream, how tractable it is with the tools a graduate student actually has in a semester (no new empirical data, no formal modelling that would take a year to build), how neglected it is relative to how much it's cited, and how likely the answer is to change anyone's mind about anything they care about. I'd also like you to say what would move a question up or down your ranking: what would I have to discover in week three to make me abandon the topic you've picked?

Three things I'd like you to work into the ranking, because they're where I suspect the real action is. First, a size question: does the conjecture require a *vast* future, or does it require only that there be *some* future? If the latter, then the conjecture may give longtermists much less than they think — it would secure the badness of extinction while saying nothing about the badness of a merely *smaller* future, and it would give no support at all to the huge numbers that usually motivate concern about existential risk. I've been thinking about this through a case I'll call the Floor case: suppose humanity continues for ten thousand years but, because of a permanent fertility collapse, exactly one million people in total ever live after us, and then we end. Does the conjecture's subject feel the same loss of confidence in the Floor case as in the doomsday case? Second, a question about what the afterlife has to consist in: must future people know about us, remember us, or share our concerns, or is mere biological succession enough? Call this the Amnesia case — two hundred generations live on, each raised from scratch by machines with no records, no history, and no knowledge of any earlier generation. If the Amnesia case still provides an afterlife, the conjecture looks like a claim about the continuation of a *kind of activity* rather than about being witnessed or remembered; if it doesn't, it looks much more like a claim about audience. That difference, it seems to me, decides what the conjecture could possibly be an argument for. Third, whether the loss the conjecture describes is a loss in what is *actually* valuable or only a loss in our confidence and motivation. If it's the latter, it's not obvious the conjecture shows extinction to be worse at all — only that we would experience it as worse — and a friend of mine who works on cause prioritization recently insisted that it gives an argument for reducing extinction risk that is independent of how many future lives are lost. I don't believe her, but I can't yet say exactly where the inference fails, and I'd like the paper to say it.

There are also a few alleged implications I'm inclined to set aside as overrated or simply invalid, and I'd like you to tell me whether I'm right to set them aside or whether I'm dismissing something that actually follows: that the conjecture gives anyone a new reason to have children; that it shows the badness of extinction to be astronomically large rather than merely structural; that it explains why we should care about the far future even if we reject person-affecting views in population ethics; and that it tells us something distinctive about extinction as opposed to the collapse or radical transformation of everything we value under a successor civilization that shares none of our concerns. Please don't give me a bibliography or pointers to the literature — I have a reading list already and the library is across the street. I want the argumentative shape of the paper.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order: **Assumptions about the conjecture** (about 200 words: state, briefly and in your own terms, what you take the conjecture to be claiming, so I can see what your ranking rests on); **Criteria and trade-offs** (about 250 words); **The ranked shortlist** (about 600 words: the questions in priority order, with the reasoning for the ordering, and an explicit statement of what each is upstream of); **The Floor and Amnesia cases adjudicated** (about 400 words: what the conjecture implies in each, and why the answer matters); **Implications I should drop** (about 300 words: which of the alleged implications in my last paragraph fail, and why); **What I should actually write** (about 200 words: the single narrowest version of the topic you'd have me commit to, and the first objection I should expect).
