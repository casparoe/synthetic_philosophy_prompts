So I've settled into a position on hypocrisy and standing to blame and I can't shake it, and I'd rather someone tried harder to shake it than I have.

The position, roughly: hypocrisy never costs you standing *directly*. It costs standing only the way any credibility-undermining fact does — by giving the target and the bystanders reason to discount your blame as cheap talk. If the blamer has already done something that makes it credible they really do accept the norm — owned up to their own violation in public, taken the sanction, paid the fine, told the person they're blaming about it — then standing is back in full, and whatever bad feeling is left over is just dislike of hypocrites, not a normative deficit.

Because I want to be pinned down, here's the version with clauses. You forfeit standing to blame N for violating norm V only if: (a) you've violated V yourself, (b) N knows or could reasonably find out, (c) you haven't been sanctioned for it or acknowledged it to N, and (d) your own violation isn't ancient history. Call it the ledger condition. I like it because it explains the stuff that actually moves me: the stranger on the bus has as much standing as anyone, the department chair who quietly pads her own expense reports and then tears into a colleague for doing exactly that is the paradigm of someone who shouldn't, and the reformed alcoholic who tells you straight out "I drank, and you shouldn't" is fine.

But here's the thing that bugs me and that I think is the live thread: my view says standing tracks your *record*, and what intuitively seems to matter is your *commitment*. So take the lucky abstainer — someone who never violated V, but only because the temptation never came, and who knows perfectly well they'd have done it. On my account they have full standing; on a commitment account they're in the same boat as the hypocrite. I find that both a prediction I'm stuck with and a reason to doubt the whole setup.

So: what would count against this? I don't want the generic "but the hypocrite lacks authority / can't address me as a moral equal" gesture unless you cash it out. What I want is:

- Which of (a)-(d) is doing the real work, and what specifically would kill each one. Is there a case where someone satisfies all four and still clearly shouldn't blame, or fails one and clearly can?
- Which intuitions and which possible cases I'd have to give up, and which ones a defender of my view would concede as counting against them versus dismiss as noise. Please separate the two — I'm more interested in what my own side would admit than in what the other side shouts.
- What my view stands or falls with in neighbouring territory: apology and forgiveness (does repair really restore a moral relationship, or only offer one?), promising, the norms of assertion and testimony, legitimate authority, punishment and who may punish. If any of those areas has a settled-ish result that my account can't fit, that's a defeater for me.
- Whether the view quietly collapses into one of its rivals — either into plain "hypocrisy never affects standing at all," or into the relational second-personal view that says standing is about the relationship you're in, not about what you've done. If the ledger is just a delivery mechanism for the relational view, I'd rather know.
- Whether it's insulated. If every apparent counterexample gets absorbed by "that's just the evidential route again," is that a feature or has the thing gone unfalsifiable — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or fine for a claim in ethics.

Personally, the thing that would move me most is a case where the blamer has fully owned and been sanctioned for their violation, the target knows all of it, and it still seems wrong for them to blame — or an explanation of why my feeling there isn't tracking standing at all.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back at me. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward while you're at it.
