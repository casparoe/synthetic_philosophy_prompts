Something came up in our department's epistemology reading group yesterday and I've been chewing on it since, and I'd like a number rather than a survey of the literature. The question I care about is this: is reductionism about testimonial warrant false? Specifically: a hearer's justification for believing what a speaker tells her cannot, in the end, be fully traced back to that same hearer's own non-testimonial sources — perception, memory, inference from what she already had. I'm only asking about the warrant version, not about whether the *content* we get from testimony could in principle have been acquired without it, and not about the psychological question of whether testimonial belief is inference-like; I think those three get run together constantly and I want them separated. My own credence that reductionism so understood is false is around 0.4, and my supervisor's is around 0.8, and I suspect that gap is mostly not about the epistemology at all but about something structural. Two things make me think that. First, there's a further split inside reductionism that I don't think is doing the work people assume: the local version, where the hearer needs reasons bearing on this speaker or this claim, versus the global version, where general reasons about people-like-this being mostly reliable are supposed to be enough. Once you're at the global version, I lose my grip on what distinguishes it from the default-entitlement picture on which a hearer is entitled to trust absent defeaters and owes no reasons at all — and if that difference is only verbal, then a big chunk of the debate is a pseudo-dispute. Second, the case that actually moved me was a testimonial loop: two researchers, Aya and Bo, are the only people with any access to a small, closed body of data; Aya's beliefs about it come entirely from Bo, Bo's come mostly from Aya plus one inherited notebook, and the notebook turns out to have been copied from Bo, who copied it from Aya. Local reductionism seems to say nobody there is justified in anything; the default view seems to say they're fine unless something specific goes wrong; and I genuinely can't tell which answer to trust, or how much weight the intuition carries outside the case. There's a related wrinkle I'd like you to handle: suppose a hearer has excellent reasons for thinking a speaker reliable in domain A, and the speaker then asserts something in domain B, with no overlap that the hearer can see. Does global reductionism let that through? I keep going back and forth on whether the answer reveals that the view was never really about reasons at all.

Give me your credence that reductionism about testimonial warrant is false, plus a second number: your probability that thirty years from now this debate has been *resolved* — meaning a clear majority of epistemologists accept one account and it's actually doing work elsewhere, e.g. in how social epistemology treats trust in experts and institutions — as opposed to *dissolved*, meaning people conclude the central question was ill-posed, say because "justification" isn't the right unit or because the local/global/default taxonomy doesn't cut at the joints.
