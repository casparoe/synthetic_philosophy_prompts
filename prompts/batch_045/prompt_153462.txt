I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about moral luck, and I would like to stop arguing with him in prose. He knows this material far better than I do, and I would rather send him a poem.

The argument went roughly like this. We began, predictably, with Nagel's two drivers: same recklessness, same bend taken too fast, one of them met by a child and the other by an empty road. I said the difference in our blame is evidential rather than paradoxical — the outcome is noisy evidence of how badly he was driving, and if you imagine the same man taking the same bend for thirty years, whether someone dies stops being luck and becomes a fairly reliable signal. He replied that we do not blame him for his lifetime average; we blame him for Tuesday, and my long run quietly substitutes a hypothetical population of acts for the one act he performed. I then said the near miss shows the outcome is doing less work than the example implies: we blame almost as much when the ball stops at the kerb, so what carries the blame is the risk rather than the death. He said that only relocates the luck one step back, since whether there was a child on that road at all is beyond the driver's control too; that the case he actually cares about is the careful driver, the one who did everything right and still killed, whom we excuse and do not excuse; and that credit runs on the same machinery as blame, since the bystander we thank for pulling the child back was as much at the mercy of the accident as the driver is. I could not answer any of it, and I have been turning it over since.

What I want is a pantoum, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than illustrate it. The rule, in case it is useful to have it stated: the poem is four quatrains and has exactly eight lines; the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third lines of the next; and the closing quatrain introduces nothing new, its second and fourth lines being the poem's third and first lines. So every line is heard exactly twice, and the last line of the poem is its first line. Beyond that:

- Let the first quatrain be entirely his — the speed, the decision, the hands, the thought he did not have — and nothing that happens to him.
- Let the second quatrain's two new lines be the only place the world speaks: one for the child in the road, one for the near miss, the ball stopped at the kerb, the blame that arrives anyway.
- Let the third quatrain's two new lines be what we say: the word we have for him, and the word we have for the person we thank instead.
- Let the last quatrain add no fact at all, only rearrange the same eight lines so that one or two of his own sentences, unchanged, come back as a verdict.

Unrhymed, roughly four stresses to the line, no line much over twelve syllables, no abstract nouns of the trade and no philosopher's name anywhere in it. It should work as a poem for someone who has never heard of the problem.

After it, one paragraph of your own, and please make it committed rather than provisional: tell me which of the following you have written the poem as taking to be true — that our sensitivity to outcomes is an unjustified residue we ought to train out of ourselves, that it is justified because the outcome is evidence of the risk run, or that it is not blame at all but something else we owe — and give your reason for choosing it. Then, in a sentence or two, say what the form let you get away with that an argument could not, and which single line you think my friend would call question-begging.
