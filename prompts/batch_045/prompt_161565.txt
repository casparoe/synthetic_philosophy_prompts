Write a dialogue between two members of a committee that is redesigning the decision rule of a large grant-funding body. Every proposal is currently decided by simple majority vote among n reviewers, all of whom read the same two-page summary written by a program officer.

One member argues for enlarging the panels, invoking the Condorcet jury theorem: if each reviewer is more likely than not to judge a proposal correctly, and their errors are independent, majority accuracy approaches one as the panel grows, so the cheapest available reform is to add reviewers.

The other member replies that independence is doing all the work, and proposes a concrete model of the dependence: each reviewer either, with probability λ, arrives at an independent judgment that is correct with probability c greater than one half, or, with probability one minus λ, defers to a lead reviewer whose judgment is correct with probability d. They claim that on this model majority accuracy is capped at d whenever λ is at most one half, however large n becomes, because the deferrers vote as a single bloc that the independent reviewers can never outvote; the lever that matters is raising λ, not adding bodies.

Write the exchange so that both speakers are equally competent and actually answer each other. In particular: the aggregator should grant whatever genuinely follows from the model and then attack what it leaves out, namely that λ is not a fixed constant but depends on whom you add, that real correlation is graded and multi-centered rather than a single common coin flip, and that deference to a lead reviewer transmits genuine expertise rather than mere noise; the skeptic should concede the real costs of de-correlation, such as that splitting reviewers into subgroups that never talk sacrifices the gains from pooled evidence, and that reviewers exert less effort per proposal as their vote becomes less pivotal, which drags c down as n rises. Both should also engage the competence side of the theorem, not just the independence side: on the hardest proposals the reviewers may be systematically worse than chance, in which case majority voting amplifies error, and the easy cases are mostly filtered out before they ever reach a vote.

Let the dialogue end not in agreement but with each side naming the one empirical quantity they would need to measure on the body's own past decisions to settle the dispute, and why that quantity is hard to measure.
