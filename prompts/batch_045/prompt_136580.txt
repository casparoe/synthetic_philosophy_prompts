My little brother (he's 11) was born with cataracts in both eyes, in a village where nobody caught it for years, and a charity surgical program is finally going to operate next month — so I've been reading everything I can about what happens *after* the bandages come off, and something about the whole setup is bugging me. The program's materials are very insistent that earlier is better, that there's a kind of window and if you wait too long the brain never properly learns to see; and they describe a whole post-surgery "visual rehabilitation" where he gets shown objects and told what they are, practices picking things out of a lineup, learns to get around obstacles. The eye itself will be fine, optically. The problem is supposed to be in what he does with the input. But at the same time, at the school for blind children where I've been volunteering this year (I'm supposed to be applying to nursing programs and instead I've fallen down this rabbit hole), nobody acts as though he's missing anything about *shape* or *space*. They teach him geometry with raised-line drawings, give him a tactile map of the school, describe rooms and routes to him in words, expect him to navigate with a cane, and treat him as knowing exactly the same layout and the same objects that everyone else knows — a ball is round, a doorway is a gap, the classroom is the third door on the left. So the very same people who will say afterwards that he'll have to learn which visual thing is the cube and which is the sphere he's known by touch for years are, right now, perfectly happy calling his touch-and-hearing knowledge knowledge *of the same world*. Someone online pointed me at an old version of this question — apparently a philosopher asked it 300-odd years ago about a man born blind who could tell a cube from a globe by touch, whether he'd know which was which the moment he was made to see — and most of the answers I found were no, with a couple of yeses and some in-between ones.

I don't care that much about the old question for its own sake; I care about the package of practices around my brother, because it seems to be implicitly committed to something, and I can't tell what. Is the urgency-of-surgery part actually a separate claim about neural plasticity that no view about perceptual content is committed to, so that a program could rush the operation and still be agnostic? Is "he'll have to learn to see" genuinely incompatible with the school's assumption that he already knows the shapes and the layout, or is there a single view — one space, one set of shape properties, but each sense has to be paired up with them by experience — that makes both halves perfectly consistent, and is the rehab then just calibration rather than acquiring a concept? And what's the thing he's supposedly missing, exactly: a concept, a learned correlation between how things look and how they feel, a wiring pattern in visual cortex, or something about what it's like for him? My friend (who is a philosophy major and will not shut up) says we're conflating two questions: whether he'd *identify* the object at first sight, and whether he'd see a world of objects and surfaces at all rather than a confusion of colour and light. She says the second is the real one and the rehab people only ever measure the first, and she's invented a test she likes better than the old cube-and-sphere one: don't ask him to name anything, just hand him a pile of things he knows well by touch and see whether by sight alone he can sort them into the right two groups, or match a felt object to a seen one, better than chance. I think her test is rigged too, but I can't say why. Anyway: reconstruct the theory of perception this whole setup presupposes, tell me which parts of the practice strictly require it and which parts several rival theories would license just as well, and then tell me concretely what the rehab program would stop doing and start doing if the yes answer were the right one.
