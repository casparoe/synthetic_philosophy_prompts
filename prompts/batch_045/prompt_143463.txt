I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory (I do most of my work on deliberative democracy and algorithmic governance, though I've been reading way to much of the AI alignment literature lately and it's starting to show up in my chapters). Next month I'm giving a 20-minute talk with 25 minutes of Q&A at a small workshop on AI and democratic legitimacy, and I want to walk in with a real prep sheet rather than vibes. The room is about twenty people: mostly political theorists of a delibrative-democracy bent (the Habermas / Gutmann-and-Thompson types, plus a couple of agonist types who think consensus is just coercion with better manners), two formal social-choice people who work on epistemic democracy and jury theorems and who will want to see a stated mechanism with an equilibrium, one STS scholar who studies algorithmic decision systems in welfare administration and who is extremely allergic to anything that sounds like automating legitimacy, one evidence-law scholar who writes about cross-examination, and one ML postdoc who thinks the whole scalable-oversight literature is elegant and completely untested at the scale that matters. Nobody else in the room works on AI safety, so I'd rather get the objections this audience will actually make than the ones I'd get at an alignment workshop.

The thesis I'm defending is that debate should be sold less as an accuracy-scaling technique and more as a *sorting* device, and that its real political payoff is legitimacy rather than correctness. Setup I'll sketch: the 2018 OpenAI debate paper (Irving and co-authors) has two models take short turns on a question and a human judge picks whoever gave the more true and useful information; the headline complexity result is that with optimal play and polynomial-time judges you get something like PSPACE rather than the NP you'd get from judging directly, which I'm going to explicitly downplay — my ML postdoc will point out that it assumes optimal play and says nothing about what gradient descent on a real model does, and I want to concede that up front. My actual argument is in three steps: (1) debate is well-suited to questions where there's a discoverable answer that's just expensive to verify, and actively bad on irreducibly value-laden questions, where it doesn't approximate truth but just selects for whatever asymmetry in rhetorical or computational power happens to exist between the debaters; (2) worse, in the policy context it *launders* that selection — the output arrives stamped as "what an adversarial procedure found," hiding a value judgment inside a fact-finding ritual; (3) my proposed fix, which is the part I most need stress-tested: after the judged debate, run a second "swap round" where each debater is required to make the strongest possible case for the other side's answer, same judge, same length limits, and then look at the gap between how well each debater argued its own position and how well it argued its opponent's. Where the gap is large I say treat the question as answer-sensitive and trust the debate verdict; where the gap is small — each side can argue the other's case about as well as its own — treat the question as value-laden and route it out of debate entirely into some aggregative or mini-public-style process. The claim I'm making about legitimacy is that the value added isn't accuracy at all, it's that the procedure makes the fact/value routing explicit and auditable: you can show someone exactly which sentences of an AI-drafted policy memo came out of adversarial fact-finding and which are value judgments that got kicked somewhere else.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the seven to nine objections I'll actually get from this specific room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do if I fumbled them, each one phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (the STS person's version of "you've reinventedX" should not sound like the social choice person's version of it), and for each a reply I could give in about two minutes plus an honest note on where my reply is soft or where I'm bluffing. I especially want the objections that come from the deliberative-democracy side rather than the technical side, since I think I can handle the technical ones, and I'd like you to flag anything I should concede in the talk itself rather than during questions so it doesn't look like I got caught out. Obvious candidates I've already half-prepared for: that the swap round is just a sincerity test with no incentive-compatibility argument behind it; that "how well can you argue the other side" may just track how easily statable a position is, which is close to the rhetorical asymmetry I was trying to control for, so my instrument is contaminated by the thing it measures; that the whole framing presupposes a fact/value distinction the constructivist half of the room doesn't accept; and that adversarialism entrenches exactly the inequalities of capability that deliberative theorists got into this business to mitigate. Also, please tell me which question is the one I should be hoping nobody asks — my own guess is that it's something about a sycophantic, RLHF'd model collapsing the swap gap toward zero everywhere and thereby reporting that every question is value-laden, but if you think the real killer is a different one (maybe something about who gets to pick the debaters, or the judge's own competence being the thing I can't test for), tell me that instead and don't be polite about it.
