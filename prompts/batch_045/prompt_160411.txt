I am writing the discussion section of a paper on the badness of pain, and what I need from you is a classification instrument rather than a survey of the literature: a decision tree that a reader can walk from a starting intuition down to a named position about what, if anything, makes pain bad.

The setting that should fix the angle is a clinical one. Imagine a palliative-care team looking at a patient who reports noxious sensation without apparent distress, and who has to answer two questions that I think are constantly run together: first, whether the patient is in pain, in the sense that matters for the chart, the consent form, and the conversation with the family; second, whether the team has a pro tanto reason to treat it, and whose reason that is — the patient's own prudential reason, the clinician's reason, or an impersonal reason that holds regardless of anyone's attitudes. Please keep those two questions, and a third, separate throughout the whole tree: whether, on the view in question, the badness of a pain could in principle be hidden from the subject who has it. Every leaf should therefore come with three verdicts, one on each axis, rather than one verdict. My suspicion is that a good deal of the apparent disagreement in this area is disagreement about which axis is being asked about, and I want the instrument to expose that.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin from the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions in the occupied cells. I want six to eight branching questions and eight to ten leaves.

Constraints on the branching questions. Each node must be a concrete case or a concrete claim about a case, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which position it leads to; nobody should be able to infer the destination from the wording. No node may be phrased directly in terms of identity, essence, or the location of a property — nothing like "is unpleasantness part of the essence of pain?" or "is badness identical to unpleasantness?" If such a distinction matters, it has to be reached through what someone is willing to say about a patient. At least five of the nodes must be drawn from the case-profiles below; you may add up to three further cases of your own if the tree needs them.

The profiles:

A, "the indifferent discriminator." After a stroke the patient detects, localises and intensity-rates noxious stimuli at normal accuracy — pinprick, left index finger, seven out of ten, sharp rather than burning — while reporting no unpleasantness, not withdrawing, not asking for analgesia, declining to press a button that would stop the stimulus, and chatting amiably through the examination.

B, "the unlocatable sufferer." The mirror image. The patient can report no location, intensity or sensory quality, and denies feeling anything sharp, burning or aching, but grows agitated during the examination, says that something awful is happening, begs for pain relief, and is markedly relieved by an analgesic whose administration changes none of the sensory reports.

C, "the unmoved." The patient rates the stimulus as excruciating, says it is awful, says they would prefer it to stop, and is angry that it continues — but reports no urge to do anything about it, does not wince, does not press a button within easy reach, and shows no avoidance learning across repeated trials.

D, "the shrugger." The patient says the sensation feels exactly as their pain felt before the operation, unpleasant feel and all, but sincerely denies that it is bad for them or that it gives anyone any reason to intervene, and declines an analgesic that is free, immediate and side-effect free, while insisting that the sensation itself is unchanged.

E, "the relabeller." With the stimulation held constant, the patient's unpleasantness tracks what they are told about it: a neutral tingling when told the device is harmless and calibrated, an unpleasant and demanding pain when told it is producing micro-damage. The sensory-discriminative reports do not shift at all.

Constraints on the leaves. Name each leaf with the label in general use where there is one, and write "unnamed" where there is not; do not coin labels. Each leaf gets one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, the thing that view has to swallow. At least two leaves must occupy cells of the logical space that nobody actually defends, marked "unnamed," each with a one-sentence diagnosis of why the region isempty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. One leaf must be for a position that is deflationary about the semantic question — no deep fact of the matter about whether the patient is in pain, or a fact only relative to which precisification of "pain" we adopt — while remaining fully substantive about the normative question. The family of views on which A is not a possible case at all, or must be reinterpreted as confabulation, as a deficit in self-report or interoception, or as a different state mis-described as pain, needs its own region of the tree rather than a footnote, and the tree should say what verdicts that family gives on all three axes. Where the tree cannot separate two positions — because they agree on every verdict and differ only in metaphysical packaging — say so explicitly, and either supply the further question that would separate them or argue that nothing could.

Please do not include citations, author names, paper titles or reading suggestions anywhere in the tree; I am handling the bibliography separately and I want the instrument to work on its own.

Close with two things. First, the three questions from your tree that do the most sorting work, in the order I should put them to a seminar, as a ten-minute exercise to be done before anyone reads anything. Second, a short note on which single case most reliably flushes out the difference between views that locate pain's badness in its unpleasantness and views that locate it in what pain represents or demands, and why that case does the work better than the others.
