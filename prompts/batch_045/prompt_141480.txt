Hey — I run a fortnightly reading group (mostly grad students plus a few brave undergrads) and we're doing a session on the wrong kind of reasons problem. I want a one-page handout that isn't just a list of isms: I want a decision tree that sorts people by what they say about cases, and I want the tree built from the logical space first, with the actual views dropped into the cells afterwards.

Roughly what I have in mind:

- Start from the standard case: a very rich stranger (an evil demon if you prefer, but no threat and no trick) offers you a large prize on condition that you admire him. You have a strong prudential consideration in favour of having the admiration; it seems to be the wrong sort of consideration for admiration. Then 4–6 branch questions on the way to 7–9 leaves.
- Every branch node should be phrased as a concrete question someone can answer without knowing which theory it leads to. Two or three answer options each. No node may name a theory, and no node may use the labels 'right kind', 'wrong kind' or 'fitting' — say something like 'the sort of consideration that makes the admiration appropriate to him' instead.
- Leaves: I want at least these on the tree. (1) The scope/ambiguity dissolution: the prize is a reason to bring it about that you admire him, not a reason to admire him. (2) Buck-passing with a fittingness restriction: value is still analysed in terms of reasons, but only some reasons do the analysing. (3) Value-first: the split is explained by which considerations bear on the value of the object, so buck-passing is false. (4) Proprietary-reason pluralism: each attitude has its own proprietary kind of consideration (the funny is what you have reason to be amused by, the fearsome what you have reason to fear), so the prize is a reason belonging to some other attitude's proprietary kind. (5) Weight deflationism: there's no distinction at the bottom; the prize really is a reason for admiration, just a small or easily outweighed one. (6) Something that treats a reason for an attitude as evidence that the attitude is correct. Fill the remaining leaves with whatever you think belongs there, including at least one marked 'unnamed'.
- Two case families I want forced into the nodes, because I don't think the usual taxonomies handle them cleanly. First, retentive reasons. You already admire your old supervisor for her patience with students; you then learn that keeping that admiration — not newly acquiring it — is what will stop you quitting your PhD. Same question: is that the same kind of consideration as her patience, or a different kind? I want a node that forces a view to say whether reasons to acquire an attitude and reasons to retain one can come apart in kind, plus a note on what happens to a view that quietly assumes they can't. Second, long-run self-shaping reasons: you have a consideration in favour of letting yourself grieve — not because grief fits the loss better than some other response, and not because anyone is paying you, but because grieving over the next two years will make you the kind of person who notices other people's losses. Call it a 'functional' consideration if you like. Most taxonomies just file this under 'state-given' and move on; I want it as its own test, and if your tree genuinely can't give it a home, say so at the node rather than tucking it in somewhere.
- Where cells in the logical space come out empty, mark them and give a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate, and say whether some further case could seperate them or whether they're the same view wearing two hats.

At the end add a short key, six or eight lines, of the form 'if you answered (b) at Q2 and (a) at Q4 you end up at leaf 6', so people can locate themselves after the fact.

Don't give me citations or a bibliography — half the group will read this on a train with no signal, and I don't want reading suggestions anyway. Names attached to views in parentheses are fine where you're confident, but I'd rather each leaf be identified by what it says about the cases than by who said it. Wether or not you keep it under about 900 words, it's going on one side of A4, so don't pad the nodes with exposition.
