I use a friend's arbitration awards as a running example in a paper on whether a run of past verdicts can pin down a single decision rule, and I now have to predict her next one. Every case involves two parties, a disagreement point (what each gets if talks break down), and a set of feasible settlements; I write payoffs as (A, B), and the feasible set is always everything on or below the frontier I describe.

1. Disagreement point (0, 0); feasible frontier the straight line from (0, 30) to (10, 0). Award: (5, 15), with a note that an option the parties had floated and then dropped, worth (9, 3), played no part in her reasoning, since she would not have chosen it anyway.
2. Disagreement point (10, 5); feasible frontier the straight line from (70, 5) to (10, 25). Award: (40, 15).
3. Disagreement point (0, 0); feasible frontier kinked, running from (0, 20) to (6, 12) to (10, 0). Award: (6, 12).

She has also said, in a public lecture, that whenever the menu of settlements is enlarged while neither side's best possible payoff changes, no side should end up with less than it would have got from the smaller menu.

The case now before her: disagreement point (0, 0); feasible frontier kinked, running from (0, 10) to (4, 9) to (10, 0) — nearly flat in A's favour up to 4, then falling steeply.

Two rules seem to me to fit all three awards: maximising the product of the two sides' gains over their disagreement payoffs; and taking the largest feasible point on the segment joining the disagreement point to the pair of best possible payoffs, which comes to giving each side the same fraction of the most it could have got. I take these to be the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky rules; correct me if I have them muddled, and tell me whether some third rule fits the record equally well.

Predict her award in the new case under each rule, with the working shown rather than the answer announced; say which of the two you would bet on and why, bearing in mind that her remark about the dropped option in case 1 and her remark about enlargements pull in opposite directions; and name the further case that would most cleanly separate the two rules. My own candidate is a variant of the new case from which every option giving A more than 8 has been struck out (all of which give B less than 3), but say if a different probe is sharper, and what each rule predicts there. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; no preamble, no restating of my question, and British English throughout.
