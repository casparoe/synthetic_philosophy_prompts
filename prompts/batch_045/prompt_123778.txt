I work on the dispatch team at a grocery-delivery company, and two of my colleagues have been arguing about something for weeks that I genuinely can't settle. I should say up front that I have no philosophy background, so please don't assume I know any of the literature on this — I'm after the reasoning, not references.

The background. Our routing system assigns orders to self-employed couriers, shows each of them a live "surge" map, and reprices every job minute by minute. Last quarter a bug caused the surge map to light up over an empty multi-storey car park. Within about ten minutes nearly forty couriers had converged on it, each arrival nudging the system to reprice the surrounding zones, which pulled in the next few. By our own internal metrics it was the most tightly coordinated behaviour the network has ever produced — and it was aimed at nothing whatsoever.

That incident is what set them off. Their exchange, roughly as it happened:

1. **Daniel:** None of this is joint action. Real joint action requires the participants to know what they are doing together. Not one courier could have told you what the group was doing; each saw one job and one price. Individual actions plus a coordination mechanism isn't a "we", it's a market — and the car-park episode proves it. A market converges on a non-existent target just as happily as on a real one, precisely because nobody ever held the target.

2. **Meera:** That's too demanding. A platoon doesn't know the general's plan, and the second violins don't know what the conductor is reading. Division of labour is what joint action looks like at scale, and it's normally taken to *support* rather than undermine the claim that there's a shared intention. Each courier accepts a job on the assumption that others are covering the rest of the network. That expectation is doing exactly the work the violinist's expectation does when she trusts the rest of the section to come in on time.

3. **Daniel:** Two problems. First, in your orchestra someone holds the whole plan and everyone is tracking her; here the plan sits on a server and the couriers are tracking a price, not a plan. Second, joint action needs the right kind of mutual responsiveness: I adjust to you *because we are doing this together*. Our couriers are competing. One courier's good order is another's lost income, and the pricing exists to make them bid against each other. That's the opposite of a team.

4. **Meera:** You keep conflating joint action with harmony. Sales teams run on internal competition for the same commission and are still teams. And mutual responsiveness is a mechanism, not a warm feeling; its job is to keep everyone's sub-plans meshed so the joint activity survives disturbance. In our system that job is done by the repricing loop — drop a courier out and the system reallocates within seconds and the overall pattern holds. Call it the plug-in test: if you can remove any participant and the remainder re-forms around the hole so the pattern is preserved, there is a collective here that is acting, whatever is or isn't in anyone's head.

5. **Daniel:** Your plug-in test can't tell a joint action from a well-tuned control system. A datacentre's autoscaler passes it, and so does a thermostat. What's missing is that participants respond to each other *in order to* keep the shared activity going, guided by their grasp of its point. Try this: subtract the shared goal from each courier's motivation and leave the money exactly as it was. The behaviour is identical. So the shared goal does no work in explaining what they do — which means it isn't what they're acting on.

6. **Meera:** That subtraction move is too powerful. Run it on a rowing crew: stipulate that every rower is in it purely for the win bonus and would row identically in a boat going nowhere, and your test now reports that they never shared an intention. A test that returns "no shared intention" for a textbook joint action is a broken test. Besides, the point of institutional coordination is that nobody needs to hold the goal — that's the feature, not the defect. Insisting that the goal be motivationally present in each participant builds your theory for hunter-gatherer bands and then rules by definition that nothing larger can ever act.

7. **Daniel:** Then be honest about where the intention has gone. On your account the thing with the goal is the company, or the system, or some amalgam of couriers, dispatch code and prices — and if that's an agent, then every market, every traffic jam and every ant colony is an agent too. You've either inflated the number of agents until "we" means nothing, or you've quietly relocated the "we" to the operations committee, which was never the interesting claim. And the car park is still sitting there: whatever your account is, it shouldn't end up crediting that episode to an agent with a purpose.

8. **Meera:** I'll give you the car park — nobody jointly aimed at the car park. But I'd say that shows the description is at the wrong grain, not that there's no joint action anywhere in the system: for those ten minutes the network *was* jointly doing something, just not that. And I think your question should be split in two. "Who shares an intention?" and "who can be held responsible as a group?" can come apart, and the second is probably the one that matters to us, given that we're the ones shipping this thing.

Please adjudicate. Go through the eight moves in order and rate each one — clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, half right, mostly right, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification, and flag any move that reaches the right conclusion for the wrong reason, or that answers a different question from the one it was replying to. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing: the distinction, consideration or reframe that dissolves part of the dispute or shows they were talking past each other. Where an argument rests on an assumption nobody stated, please name it.

Two practical notes: I'd like the answer in British English, and ideally under about 900 words — dense is fine, but I'd rather have the verdict than the preamble.
