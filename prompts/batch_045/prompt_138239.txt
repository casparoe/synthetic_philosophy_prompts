I have recently been promoted to head of supplier relationships at a mid-sized medical-device manufacturer, and I inherited a recurring decision that I am now convinced is a single problem wearing many hats. My team of four buyers deals with roughly sixty standing counterparties — component moulders, a contract sterilisation lab, calibration services, three regional logistics providers — and at least once a week somebody faces the same question in a new guise: do we hold the cooperative line this time (absorb a short-term cost increase, expedite a favour, share forecast data, waive a late-delivery penalty that is technically owed), or do we protect ourselves now on the assumption that they may not reciprocate? My predecessor left no framework; the answer currently depends on which buyer is handling it and whether the relationship feels warm. Two relationships blew up in the last year in ways that cost us real money and, in one case, a delayed product launch.

I would like a decision procedure my team can actually run, and I would like it built out of the theory of the prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation rather than out of folk wisdom about partnerships.

The constraints are these. It has to be runnable in about fifteen minutes by a buyer with no training in game theory, on one page, from the information in a contract file plus a conversation. It has to terminate in one of a small number of stances — cooperate unconditionally, cooperate conditionally, cooperate conditionally with a defined forgiveness rule, reduce exposure and watch, withdraw from cooperation, escalate — together with a note in the file naming which test produced that answer, so that I can audit it. Anything involving withdrawal or escalation comes to me; anything involving contract or competition law goes to Legal and stops there.

The parts I most need the procedure to get right are the following, and I suspect they are where a simplified version will do damage.

First, I want the very first branch to sort out whether the case is a prisoner's dilemma at all. My strong suspicion is that many of what my team calls cooperation problems are not: sometimes our interests simply coincide, so there is nothing to guard against; sometimes each side's best move is to cooperate provided the other does, so the real problem is assurance rather than exploitation. In those cases a reciprocity rule is not merely unnecessary, it may manufacture the defection it was meant to deter. Tell me what observable signs in a commercial file distinguish these from a genuine dilemma, and how confident a buyer can reasonably be from the outside.

Second, the identity of the other player. Our counterparty is a firm, but I deal with a named person who may be in a different job next year, whose bonus may be quarterly, and who may or may not know whether we will still be buying from them in eighteen months. My team has started using a rough in-house notion they call continuity: the chance that the same decision-maker, in the same role, with the same incentives, will face us again next round, and knows it. Refine that or replace it, but give me something a buyer can estimate in a few minutes, and say what to do when it cannot be estimated.

Third, endpoints. Some of our relationships have a known end date — a contract we will not renew, freight we will consolidate after a merger — and some end unpredictably. I am told that this distinction matters a great deal, and that it matters differently depending on whether the end is known to both sides or only to us. We are under instructions not to disclose a planned exit before announcement. I need the procedure to tell me plainly what that does to our position, including what it does to the earlier rounds of the same relationship.

Fourth, ambiguous defections. Most of what my team reports as a defection is ambiguous: a late delivery caused by their sub-tier supplier, a price increase justified by an index clause that reads either way. I need a rule for responding to a suspected defection you cannot verify, and I want the rule to say how the relative cost of being wrong in each direction should shift our tolerance — including the case where we ourselves are the ones who may have defected by accident.

Fifth, reputation and audiences. Several of our suppliers know each other, and one of my live problems involves four distributors drawing on a shared allocation pool. I want to know when the existence of an audience should change the stance, and when it should not.

Please give me the tests in the order they must be applied, with the stop points, the points where the file note must say that a number could not be obtained, and the points where the question is one of values rather than facts and must be handed to a person. Be explicit about which distinctions in the theory have to survive the translation into a one-page form and which can be dropped, and about where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as a rule.

Then run it on three of my live cases:

A. An eleven-year supplier of a sealed packaging tray, sole qualified source, no reliability problems ever. A new account manager, four months in post, has invoked an index clause mid-contract for a six percent increase; our buyer reads the clause as not applying to this resin. We are qualifying a second source, expect validation in about eight months, and have not told them.

B. A regional logistics provider with an excellent service record has asked us to co-fund a shared tracking integration: they carry most of the cost now, both of us benefit over roughly eighteen months. After a merger we will consolidate freight and drop them in about five months. This is confidential until announcement in roughly ten weeks.

C. Four regional distributors drawing on a shared allocation pool. One has been inflating orders by around eight percent for two quarters. Our contract permits an audit; auditing costs a relationship. The four of them know each other, and two of them know each other well.

At least one of the three should turn out, on your own diagnosis, not to be a prisoner's dilemma. At least one should come out with an answer I will not like and will have to defend upward — I would rather have that now than discover it myself in six months.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, against what kind of counterparty it is actively exploitable, and what my team should record each time they run it so that in two years I can tell whether following it actually improved our outcomes rather than just filing better paperwork.
