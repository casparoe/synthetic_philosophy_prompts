I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, and I've been asked to take one ninety-minute session of our graduate reading group (roughly sixteen people: mostly philosophy MPhil and PhD students, three or four from economics, two from psychology). The session falls between the week on the static coherence norms and the week on updating, so I have them at the point where they have read the basic betting set-up but have not yet met the diachronic material. I want them to run into something rather than be told it.

The shape I have in mind — please rework it freely if you can see a better mechanism — is two phases.

Phase one, synchronic. Participants act as market makers rather than bettors. Each is given a sheet listing propositions about a single well-defined chance process that I can actually resolve in the room, and must post a two-sided price for each: the highest price at which they will buy a ticket paying a fixed stake if the proposition is true, and the lowest at which they will sell one. The propositions need to include negations and disjunctions of the same events so that failures of additivity and of the negation rule are catchable. Then a roaming bookie trades against them at their own posted prices, with settlement in points rather than money, converted at the end into something trivially small.

Two things I want the design to carry, and this is where I most need help.

First, the spread. Because they post two-sided prices, most will post a bid–ask spread, and many will read the spread as expressing uncertainty about their own credence — a way of saying they are not really sure — rather than as a transaction cost or as the expression of an interval-valued credence. The sure-loss construction only bites if they will trade at both endpoints. I would like the rules arranged so that this distinction surfaces on its own rather than being announced by me: some participants should end up having posted genuinely imprecise prices and getting arbitraged, and others should have posted gaps that are mere noise and are, I think, being caught unfairly. How should the posting rule, the settlement rule and the bookie's discretion be set so that this comes out? And what do I do about the participants who are simply averse to betting and so post very conservative prices? I take that to be the risk-attitude and diminishing-marginal-utility confound, and I would rather the activity did not rest on the assumption that betting prices transparently reveal credences, since I do not think they do.

Second, phase two, diachronic. I want them to feel the pull of the claim that a non-conditionalising update plan is exploitable, without anyone in the room saying the word "conditionalisation". Before I reveal the outcome of the process, each participant writes down, on a form, their current price for a hypothesis H and the price they would post for H under each of the possible evidence outcomes — that is, they commit themselves in advance to an update plan. Then the evidence is revealed and the bookie trades at their own committed prices. The predictable thing is that a good number will refuse to honour the plan once the evidence is in, on the grounds that they have thought again. I want that refusal, and the argument about it, to be the centre of the session rather than a glitch. My thought is to assign some participants at random a genuinely binding commitment — their form is executed mechanically by a third party whether or not they now like it — and others a non-binding one, so that the room splits into people who were exploited and people who escaped, and the argument about whether escaping is legitimate, or whether it merely shows that the argument was never addressed to them, has to be had by them. Is that the right mechanism? Does randomising bindingness create a problem I am not seeing — for instance, does the exploitability of the bound group establish anything about the unbound group?

What I want back from you:

A minute-by-minute allocation of the ninety minutes, including what has to be printed and what has to be resolved in the room, with a specified chance process that generates enough distinct events for additivity and negation violations to be detectable without anyone needing a calculator.

A mapping from specific, predictable moments in the activity — the participant whose bid exceeds their ask; the participant who posts 0.6 for H and 0.6 for its negation; the participant who is arbitraged only because they were in the binding condition; the participant who refuses to trade at all — onto the elements of the argument they are supposed to be encountering: the synchronic sure-loss construction, the converse direction, the step from exploitability of a plan to the updating rule, and the reply that one can always decline the later bets.

An honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run it. I expect at least arithmetic slip-ups, a bookie too slow to clear all the sheets, participants who treat the points either as real money or as nothing whatsoever, and someone who observes that the whole exercise only shows they are exploitable by someone with information they lack. Tell me what to do in the room in each case, and what to say if the exploitation fails to materialise at all.

Which parts of the dialectic the activity cannot stage and which I must therefore supply by hand. My own list: the converse result, that coherence is not merely necessary but sufficient for immunity to sure loss; the point that a sure-loss argument is at best pragmatic and does not by itself show incoherent credences to be epistemically defective in the way an accuracy-based argument tries to; and the point that the diachronic construction has to assume something about which bets the bookie can offer later and on what terms. Add anything else that belongs on that list.

One request that applies to the whole answer. Please keep clearly separated what is settled among people who work on these questions and what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested claim come through as the orthodox one. In particular, I do not want to come out of the session having implicitly taught the group that interval-valued or imprecise credences are refuted by sure-loss arguments, or that the diachronic argument settles the updating rule, if specialists are in fact divided on those. Where you think my own framing above already smuggles in a contested assumption, say so.
