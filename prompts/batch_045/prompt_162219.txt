Two people in my department got into it over lunch last week about whether a constitution is really a Ulysses contract, and I was stupid enough to say I'd referee. Having thought about it I honestly can't tell who wins, so I'm handing it to you. Please go through the exchange move by move — who is right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.

Nora (constitutional theory): The whole point of the apparatus — entrenchment, supermajority amendment rules, staggered terms, an independent central bank, debt clauses — is that a polity binds itself against what it will later want. The framers tie the hands of the future majority the way Ulysses ties himself to the mast. That isn't a decorative metaphor, it's the same structure: an agent at t1 imposes a constraint on itself at t2 because it expects t2's preferences to be worse than t1's.

Sam: It's the same structure except for the part that makes it work. Ulysses binds Ulysses; the crew is only executing a prior instruction he gave. A constitution is written by people who are dead and binds people who never agreed to anything. One agent restraining a different agent isn't self-binding, it's just restraint — the dead hand, with better PR. If I tie you up I haven't performed a Ulysses contract, I've done something else entirely.

Nora: Two replies. First, "the people" is a corporate agent that persists while its members turn over; a club that binds new members who joined knowing the rules isn't a dead hand, it's a club. Second, look at what the ropes buy: credible commitment. A polity that can promise creditors, minorities and its own central bank that it won't raid them can do things an unroped polity can't. Later generations get that benefit whether or not they consented, and consent was always just a proxy for benefit.

Sam: Grant the persistence point, it's the strongest version of your view. The mechanism is still wrong. Ulysses' ropes were causally efficacious — he struggled and could not get free, and the crew's ears were stopped so they literally could not hear the counter-order. A constitution's ropes hold only because later officials choose to treat them as holding. Something that constrains by being honored isn't a constraint on the will, it's a request the will usually grants. So the analogy is either false or empty — it collapses into "law is law."

Nora: You're testing for the wrong thing. Ropes don't delete a desire, they price it: more actors who have to collude, more delay, more publicity, more reputational and electoral exposure. Ulysses didn't need a cage, he needed to get through one afternoon.

Sam: Fine, but here's a harder one: foresight. Ulysses knew exactly which passion was coming, when, and from which direction — he'd been warned. Constitutional framers are binding against a future they can't see: new technologies, new party alignments, new moral majorities. Call it the calibration problem — a precommitment whose target can't be named in advance is as likely to block the good future act as the bad one. And notice which real clauses actually look Ulyssean: debt limits, central bank independence, term limits. Those guard against a temptation you can name. Broad rights clauses don't; they're invitations to argue, not ropes.

Nora: There's a second species you're ignoring. Drift-precommitment: not a guard against one identifiable passion but against slow erosion, where the danger is precisely that at no single moment does anyone have a strong reason to object — individually reasonable accommodations compounding into something nobody would have chosen. That needs no foresight of content, only of the mechanism. And calibration cuts both ways: framers can't foresee their own blindness either, so your principle would have licensed tying the ropes in the direction of the settled moral assumptions of the founding generation.

Sam: Last thing, and this is where I think it really breaks. Ulysses contract with an escape hatch isn't one. The scream is the symptom, not evidence of a change of mind. So an amendment rule that lets the bound majority reopen anything is self-defeating, and the only coherent version is total deafness — an interpreter who cannot be moved by the majority's arguments. Which is to say: the crew has to be a court nobody elected.

Nora: Then no long-lived precommitment could ever be justified and the device doesn't generalize past a single afternoon, which is the only case we actually need it for. Better: keep a channel, but make it procedural — supermajorities, delay, conventions — so you distinguish passion from considered change by the size and slowness of the coalition rather than by content, since from inside the passion you can't test content at all.
