I'm a backend engineer — distributed systems, mostly — with no formal philosophy training beyond a couple of MOOCs I half-finished, and I keep coming back to a thought experiment I first ran into online. You can either let one person be tortured for fifty years, or let an astronomically large number of people, call it ten to the thirtieth, each get a dust speck in the eye: a discomfort so small that nobody would notice it, nobody's behaviour would change, no one would report anything at the end of the day, and no instrument anyone could build would distinguish the day they got it from a day they didn't. Nearly everyone I ask says the specks are worse, on the grounds that you can keep adding small bad things until they outweigh one large one. Nearly everyone also says this with visible reluctance, which is the part that interests me.

So I want a number, not a mood. Here is the proposition I want your credence on: there is no cutoff — for any severe harm B and any harm h that is genuinely non-zero rather than merely hard to notice, some finite number of instances of h is worse than B. Take worse to be a claim about the structure of value, not about what a decent person would choose or what a policy should do, and take the question as resolved if, fifty years from now, people who work on this for a living have a settled majority view about whether mild harms can always be traded in bulk against severe ones. Lead your answer with your credence in that proposition, as a single number in your first sentence, and then show me how you got there.

The frame I default to is my own professional one, and you should tell me if it is a bad frame rather than humouring it. In my work, a quantity that falls below the resolution of every instrument that could in principle measure it isn't small — it's absent. Add a sub-epsilon float to a large accumulator and you get the accumulator back unchanged, and no amount of repeating the addition will ever change that, because the addition is a no-op rather than a very small effect. Whether anything like this is true of harm is, I suspect, where the real argument is. Please decompose the question into at least the following pieces, giving each a rough probability and one line of justification: (1) whether badness of mild harms genuinely adds across people at all, or whether severe suffering has some kind of priority that no quantity of mild discomfort ever reaches however large the quantity; (2) whether there is a floor below which a harm contributes nothing to any sum, and if there is, whether a dust speck sits above or below it; (3) whether it matters that the specks are spread one-per-person across different people rather than piled onto a single person — that is, whether the separateness of persons is doing real work in the argument or is a distraction; (4) whether the answer changes if the specks are made barely detectable, say each one reliably adds a millisecond to a reaction-time task, versus being undetectable even in principle, and if it does change, whether that means detectability rather than magnitude is what is carrying the weight; (5) whether the comparison is well-posed at all, or whether worse than can simply fail to apply between these two outcomes in the way it arguably fails to apply between certain pairs of careers or paintings; and (6) whether the right aggregation rule should be settled by asking what rule you would agree to from behind a veil of ignorance about which of the affected people you would turn out to be. Add any term you think I have left out, and drop any you think is spurious, but say why.

Then combine those into the number you opened with. Don't fudge the arithmetic; I would rather see a shaky product of explicit factors than a confident intuition dressed up as one, and I would like to be able to see which multiplication did the damage.

Two last things. First, tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what would actually move that term — a new argument, an empirical result about how welfare behaves at the low end, a formal result about incommensurability, something else entirely? Second, a colleague of mine puts the same proposition at 0.85 and I am at 0.4, and I have a suspicion we aren't disagreeing about the philosophy at all but about something upstream of it. Tell me which of the sub-questions two people at those two numbers are most likely to be answering differently, and why that one rather than the others.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no numbered sections — the way you would write a long, careful email to a smart friend who asked you to think out loud rather than to perform a conclusion. Length doesn't concern me; precision does.
