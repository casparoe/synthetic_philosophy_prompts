I am finishing a thesis chapter on scientific progress, and the literature I am writing about seems, as far as I can tell, simply to have stopped. The question is whether one theory can be closer to the truth than another: closer not merely in the coarse sense of true versus false, but in the finer-grained sense in which the claim that the solar system has nine planets beats the claim that it has nine billion, a precise truth beats a bare tautology, and a precise falsehood arguably beats a hedged truth. Popper's original proposal (truth-content minus falsity-content) collapsed under the now-familiar results showing that on that definition every falsehood ends up equally far from the truth. Since then there have been three broad families of response, and they have not converged: a content-and-consequence camp that keeps trying to repair or restrict the definition; a likeness camp that measures truthlikeness as distance in a space of possibilities, which restores the intuitive orderings but leaves the numbers sensitive to how the space is carved up and to whether one averages or sums; and a camp that treats truthlikeness as something epistemically accessible to an inquirer, or as a cognitive value, rather than as a logical relation between contents. Each family satisfies some adequacy conditions and violates others, nobody agrees on a meta-criterion for choosing between them, and my supervisor's view is that the whole thing is a pseudo-dispute generated by asking one word to do several jobs.

My own hypothesis is slightly different: I think the real dispute is about whether truthlikeness is a comparative relation between theories at all, or a relation between a theory and a body of evidence or a practical question, and that the collapse results are symptoms of that rather than obstacles to be engineered around. To test this I have been playing with a construction. Fix a finite partition of the space of possible states into cells, chosen as the cells that matter for some question, and score a theory cell by cell: it earns credit in each cell in which what it says holds, and its score is the weighted share of cells in which it is right. This immediately avoids the collapse result, because a false theory can outrank another false theory on a partition fine enough to tell them apart, and it is invariant under re-expressing the theory in another vocabulary or with different logical strength, since credit is assigned locally rather than by counting consequences. The cost is that truthlikeness stops being a property of theories alone: the same theory gets different scores under different partitions, and rankings can reverse when a partition is refined.

I would like a diagnosis of the stalled debate rather than a survey of the positions. Is it verbal, substantive, or two questions under one name? Is some hidden formal question doing the work? And does my construction dissolve the old dispute, or merely relaunch it one level up?

Reply in British English as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) your verdict on what kind of disagreement this is; (2) the evidence for that verdict from what each of the three families is in fact trying to do; (3) whether a hidden formal or empirical question is doing the work; (4) an assessment of both my hypothesis and my partition-relative construction, including what the construction gains and what it silently gives up; (5) what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress here, or why nothing should be expected to.
