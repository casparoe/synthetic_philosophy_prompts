I'm putting together a first-year seminar where nearly every reading is a thought experiment, and I want a spec before I turn students loose on them. What would a theory of thought experiments have to deliver to be worth teaching — good enough that someone can pick up a scenario they've never seen and tell wether it's doing real work or just pumping? Give me the desiderata: the cases it has to handle (Gettier, the violinist, Twin Earth, the Chinese Room are all fair game), what has to be true of a scenario for its verdict to count at all, and the distinctions it can't collapse — persuasive vs probative, load-bearing detail vs set dressing, drawing out an intuition we already had vs installing a new one. Two of my own to include: the pumpmaker's regress (you can't say which features of a case are doing the work without already knowing the conclusion it was built to support, so the theory has to say where that stops), and twin pumps — same structure, valence swapped, verdict flips, as with the violinist against its people-seeds twin — what is a theory meant to do when both twins feel right? For each requirement say why it's a requirement and not just a taste of one camp, mark the non-negotiable ones, and flag any pair that can't be jointly met: I suspect "the verdict must be invariant under irrelevant variation" and "pumps have to be able to overturn commitments we already hold" are quietly at war, and I want to know which one to drop. One closing sentence on whether "a pump is just an argument with its premises hidden" survives the list, no more than that. Table's fine, eight to ten rows, 600 words max.
