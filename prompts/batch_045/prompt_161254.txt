I have no formal training in statistics or philosophy. I work in operations for an online retailer, where part of my job is reading the results of A/B tests on our website, and as a hobby I go through the outcome tables that our national health service publishes for hospital clinics. For about two years now I have been running into the same shape of difficulty in both, and I would like to know whether it has a name of its own, because the name I was given for the simple version of it does not seem to cover what I actually keep meeting.

The simple version I can give exactly. Two clinics carry out the same procedure. Clinic A handles 800 patients and succeeds with 540 of them. Clinic B handles 800 patients and succeeds with 620 of them, so B looks better overall. But the published tables also break cases down by how severe they were on arrival. Of A's 600 severe cases it succeeds with 360; of B's 200 severe cases it succeeds with 110. Of A's 200 mild cases it succeeds with 180; of B's 600 mild cases it succeeds with 510. So A does better among severe cases and better among mild cases, while doing worse overall, simply because it takes the severe ones. A statistician friend told me this two-level case is a famous paradox — Simpson's something? — and that the lesson is that the overall figure is misleading and one should split on the factor that is really driving things, after which the comparison is settled.

My difficulty is that in the cases I actually meet, nothing is settled. Split those same clinics again, by how the patient came in — emergency admission or elective referral — and in three of the four resulting boxes clinic B comes out ahead again. Split again, by whether the operation was performed on a weekday or at the weekend, and it tips back toward A. I wrote a small script that, given any table of this kind, searches the available columns for one that reverses the comparison, and it has never once failed to find one; and once a reversal is found, that finer table can be fed back in and reversed again. The number that comes out depends on which columns I condition on and how many of them I condition on at once, not on the order in which I do it, and there is no depth at which the answer stops moving.

Two things about this puzzle me. The first is that the columns which "work" at the deeper levels are usually not background facts about the patient at all. Whether a patient was moved into intensive care, how long they waited, which surgeon ended up operating, whether they came back for a follow-up: these are things that happened partly *because* of how the clinic handled them. Splitting on those feels like cheating in a way that splitting on severity does not, but I cannot say what the difference is or where the line falls. In my own work the same thing happens: a variant of a page shows a lower conversion rate overall, wins once I split by device type, loses again once I split by device type and by the advertising campaign that sent the visitor, and that second split is on something the variant itself partly influenced, because changing the page changed which campaigns were eligible to send traffic to it.

The second is that I cannot tell how much of this is a genuine feature of the situation and how much is an artifact of my own searching. In the hospital data the deeper boxes still contain hundreds of patients and the swing is large. In the website data the deeper boxes get down to dozens of visitors, and I assume that at some point I am just looking at noise that I went looking for.

So my questions. Does the recurring version — a comparison whose direction depends on how finely you carve up the population, with no carving that settles it — have a name of its own, or is it only the famous two-level paradox applied over and over? Is there a name, and a principled rule, for the specific move of dividing your population by something that the thing you are comparing helped to bring about, and is that move always illegitimate or only sometimes? How should I distinguish cases where the recurring flip is structural from cases where it is manufactured by my own search or by small boxes? And if the pattern as I have described it has no established name, I would rather you said so and gave me a coined term with a careful definition and a set of boundary cases — cases that clearly count and cases that clearly do not — than gave me a label. I am not collecting vocabulary; I want to know what this thing is, what nearby things it is not, and where its edges lie. I have to sign off on which version of a page ships, and I would like to know which clinic I would send my mother to.
