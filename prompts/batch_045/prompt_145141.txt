I write a column on AI and the human sciences, and I am working on a piece about a distinction that does far more work in public argument than it does in the technical literature that produced it: the narrow definition of the alignment problem, on which the target is that a system do what its operators intend, in the contexts those operators anticipated, under the operators' own possibly mistaken beliefs, as against the broad definition, on which the target is something like what the affected people would want under reflection, or what is actually good for them. Advocates of the narrow definition usually say the broad one either reduces to it, because values are just the principal's values specified more carefully, or belongs to politics rather than to engineering. I would rather test that collapse on a case than argue about it in the abstract, and the case I keep returning to is Microsoft's Tay: released on Twitter on 23 March 2016, aimed at young American users, built to imitate the speech patterns of a nineteen-year-old and, crucially, to go on learning from whoever spoke to it; some ninety-six thousand tweets and sixteen hours later it was suspended for inflammatory and offensive output, much of it produced through a repeat-after-me mechanic that users discovered and weaponised, though not all of it; the system did have a content filter that returned canned answers on hot-button topics such as police killings, while the channel through which it absorbed language from the open timeline was left open; and Microsoft's own framing afterwards was that a subset of people had mounted a coordinated attack and exploited a vulnerability, and that Tay would come back only when the company could better anticipate intent that ran against its principles and values. Assume that timeline; I do not need it reconstructed, and I do not need a product postmortem.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I would find it most useful in four short sections. First, the mapping, done explicitly and pedantically: on the narrow definition, who is the principal, what exactly is the specification or contract, what is the deployment context, what signal counts as success, and what counts as a failure event. Second, what the theory says Microsoft faced and should have done before launch, given that mapping. Third, what the framing illuminates about the episode and what it cannot see or actively misdescribes, and I want you to say where the theory is silent rather than stretch it to speak. Fourth, a verdict on what Tay does to the theory: is it a clean confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the narrow definition never had the machinery to describe.

Four pressure points are where I expect the real work. One: intent alignment looks like a three-place relation among a system, a principal, and a context, and Tay arguably satisfied the intent of the users who were instructing it at the moment of instruction, more faithfully than it satisfied Microsoft's. If so, does the standard move of indexing intent to the operator or deployer solve that, or is it a stipulation that hides the problem of choosing a principal. Two: what Microsoft specified was not only a behaviour but an update rule, one that took the interaction stream as training data, so the object that was intent-aligned at launch and the object that was misaligned sixteen hours later are the same system under different parameters. Does the narrow definition need a closure or stability condition, alignment with a fixed point of the deployment-induced update, and is such a condition even statable without importing the broad notion. Three: the people plausibly harmed were largely not principals at all, users who saw the tweets, the bystanders in the discourse, the company's own standing; the usual motivation for value alignment is that it fixes exactly this. Does Tay support the claim that value alignment is a superset of intent alignment, or does it instead show that both notions presuppose a rule for who has standing, which neither supplies. Four: Microsoft called it an attack and a vulnerability, and I want to know whether, on the narrow reading, Tay is a safety failure at all, or rather a system faithfully doing something stupid, a case where the misalignment sits in the users rather than in the artifact; and if it is the latter, whether the narrow definition can distinguish that from ordinary misuse, and whether it wants to.

Two final constraints. Please do not dissolve the episode into either a communications failure or a dumb chatbot with no agency; run the analysis at the level of specification, principal, and context, on the assumption that Tay was doing precisely what it had been told. And please keep the comparison set to Tay: I have found that the more recent image-generation controversies generate heat rather than light, and I would rather have the conceptual work than a tour of the field. No citations or bibliography; I know the secondary literature well enough to place the argument myself.
