I'm taking a grad seminar on well-being this term and somehow got talked into presenting on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism next week. I've written up some notes and I'm fairly unhappy with them but can't put my finger on why, so I wanted a second pair of eyes. Quick version of where I land: I don't think the standard move of positing a shared phenomenal quality — some thin "hedonic tone" laid over the top of otherwise different experiences — actually works, because when I introspect the pleasure of finishing a hard crossword, the pleasure of cold water after a long run in the heat, the pleasure of scratching a mosquito bite, and the pleasure of a huge orchestral crescendo, I can't find any felt quality they share beyond the bare fact that I don't want them to stop, and that sounds like it collapses into a desire-ish theory, which is the thing I was trying to get away from. So I've been playing with something else: what pleasures have in common isn't how the experience feels at an instant but how it's *moving*. Call it the Derivative View — an experience is pleasant insofar as its phenomenology presents something as improving, or as having just improved, along some dimension the subject is already tracking. That's supposed to explain why relief is a pleasure even though it starts out feeling bad, why itch-scratching is a pleasure even though it's tangled up with something aversive, and why a long flat stretch of mild contentment is the hard case rather than the easy one. My biggest worry is that it hands out pleasures too cheaply to people with warped concerns: if pleasure is just perceived improvement along a dimension you happen to be tracking, then the person who delights in someone else's humiliation gets a real pleasure and my view has no way of saying there's anything defective about it as a pleasure, as opposed to just being one the rest of us would rather not have around. Second worry: I may have sneakily put the unity into the subject's tracking machinery instead of into the experience, which would make this the desire theory wearing a hat. Notes below, then some specific questions.

--- notes ---

The setup: hedonism about well-being says a life goes well for you in proportion to its pleasant experience. The heterogeneity problem asks what makes an experience pleasant in the first place. Standard versions use cases like the ones I mentioned to argue that there's no single felt quality running through the class, and that if there isn't, then "pleasant experience" is either a gerrymandered family-resemblance class (in which case hedonism is stuck summing a category that doesn't carve at a joint) or it isn't a genuine natural-ish kind at all.

Three ways out, as I see them.

(i) Tone. There is a distinctive quality of pleasantness, thin and hard to attend to, that rides on top of the experience of the water, the music, the joke. Trouble is twofold. Introspectively I just don't find it, and I don't think appealing to "it's too subtle to notice" is much better than admitting defeat. Structurally: if tone is a separable component, you should be able to hold the underlying experience fixed and vary the tone, and it's opaque what work the tone is doing there — it starts to look like a label for the very thing we wanted explained. Also, relief doesn't feel like a positive sheen added to something; it feels like a subtraction.

(ii) Attitude. Pleasant = such that in having it you're disposed to want it to continue. This handles the heterogeneity beautifully and I think that's exactly why I distrust it. Problems: you can be pleased by something you simultaneously want to end (scratching until it hurts, watching something frightening on purpose), which means the disposition is at best typical, not constitutive. And the theory puts the unity outside the experience, in a second-order state that can in principle come apart from it.

(iii) My candidate. Pleasantness is a matter of the experience's presenting an improvement — a positive first derivative, so to speak — along a dimension internal to what the experience is of. Not a level: a change. A few cases:

- Relief: level is negative or neutral, derivative positive. Clean win for (iii) over (i).
- Itch-scratching: the aversive element and the pleasant element aren't two beads on a string; the pleasantness *is* the collapse of the itch. That's exactly what a derivative view predicts and what a tone view has to tell an awkward story about.
- Puzzle-solving: the improving dimension isn't sensory at all, it's something like grip or coherence — something unresolved becoming resolved. Good.
- Music: this is where I had to broaden. A crescendo isn't an improvement in *my* condition, it's an improvement in the unfolding object. So the dimension has to be allowed to be internal to the experience's own object: the thing the experience is of is going better by standards that the experience itself has set up. I think that broadening is correct but I also think it's where the view starts to leak.
- Contentment: flat for hours. No improvement anywhere. On my view this isn't strictly a pleasure, and I'm genuinely unsure whether that's a counterexample or whether it shows the question I was handed was wrongly framed — maybe pleasure was never the basic hedonic category and hedonism should have been built on something else all along.
- Malicious pleasure: humiliation of another registers as improvement along a dimension the subject really is tracking. So (iii) returns a verdict of "real pleasure." I can either swallow that, or say the tracked dimension has to be one the subject has *reason* to care about — but then I've imported a normative condition into an experiential theory, which is a big deal and maybe fatal.

Last thing: there's a deflationary option I keep coming back to and rejecting. Maybe we should stop demanding a shared property and give a family-resemblance or cluster answer. But then the class is gerrymandered, and hedonism inherits the arbitrariness: it would be a coincidence that what's good for you tracks a category assembled for other reasons. I'm not sure I have a knockdown reply to this beyond "it feels unsatisfying," which isn't an argument.
