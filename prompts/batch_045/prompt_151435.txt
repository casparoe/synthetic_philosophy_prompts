I sit on a small committee that signs off on delivery forecasts for internal software projects, and before we write our method into a guidance note I want one part of it checked. For every project we now produce two figures: a reference-class forecast read off the distribution of completion times for the projects we judge relevantly similar, and a bottom-up figure built from the task-level plan for this particular project. Where the two disagree we report the arithmetic mean. The reasoning in our draft note is that we have no principled basis for preferring either view, that the reference-class number is best regarded as the judgment of one more forecaster whose reliability we have no special reason to doubt, and that splitting the difference is therefore the epistemically modest thing to do rather than the arrogant one.

The case now in front of me makes me doubt that reasoning. The reference class is tight: a median near thirty months, with nearly all comparable projects finishing between twenty-seven and thirty-three and essentially none below twenty-four. Our own plan says eighteen months, and I believe we have genuine case-specific grounds for it, since the three components that historically generate the long tail have already been built twice by this team, on schedule. The rule hands us twenty-four months, and I cannot say what that number asserts. Could you explain whether averaging is really the modest option here, or whether it is doing something we have not noticed, and, if so, how the two figures ought to be combined instead? Please argue from the case itself rather than from the literature: we have no library access, and a reading list would be of no use to us. I would also ask that you reply in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and in under six hundred words.
