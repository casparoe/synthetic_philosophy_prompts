Hey — I need a hand turning a half-formed idea into a two-page proposal for a one-semester independent study (meeting my supervisor every other week, ~6000-word paper at the end, philosophy department but my supervisor is happy for me to lean on econ stuff). Please don't put in any citations or a reading list, I'll fill that side in myself.

Here's how I got into it. My brother is in an econ PhD program and at Thanksgiving he told me that rational people with the same information can't disagree, by which he meant the standard result that agents with a common prior can't have common knowledge of differing posteriors. I pushed back and said fine, but that result only has content if the space of possibilities is fixed in advance, and in practice the modeler chooses it. My actual suspicion, which I can't yet state cleanly let alone prove: given any finite pattern of disagreement between two agents, you can inflate the underlying space — add possibilities neither agent has ever entertained, split old ones, let some of the new ones carry zero or vanishing probability — and then find a common prior plus information partitions on the inflated space that reproduces the observed disagreement exactly. If that's right, then the common prior assumption, read as a claim about real disagreements, is close to unfalsifiable: it's a modeling convention dressed up as a constraint on rationality. And the usual Harsanyi-flavoured argument (priors are where pure logic speaks, so rational agents shouldn't differ there) would lose its bite too, because any difference could always be relocated into the extra structure rather than into the priors.

The problem is that 'you can always gerrymander the space' is only a complaint, and complaints don't make a paper. So I want the proposal built around a principled restriction instead. My candidate is a minimal-space requirement: the state space may only distinguish possibilities that the agents' own disagreement vocabulary can express, so if two people are arguing about inflation, the space can't contain a silent extra world whose only job is to rescue a common prior. The questions I'd want stated precisely: on such a minimal space, is the existence of a common prior still equivalent to something with real content (I'm thinking of the characterizations in terms of there being no bet both parties would accept), or does even the minimal space collapse into triviality? Does the answer change when the disagreement is about which possibilities there are at all — a theist and an atheist, or someone who thinks an entire style of explanation is bogus — rather than about probabilities across a shared menu? And most importantly, is the common prior assumption doing two different jobs that I should pull apart: telling us when a difference in belief is somebody's fault, versus telling us what a complete explanation of a difference in belief has to look like? I'd also want the proposal to say why this is a philosopher's project and not just a technical exercise in interactive epistemology, what I'd actually do month by month (including where I'd go looking for a clean version of the inflation result and what I'd settle for if it turns out to be messy), and honest failure conditions — my guess is that if the no-bet style conditions turn out to be invariant under the kind of space inflation I have in mind, my main suspicion is just false and there's no paper here, only a footnote in someone else's.
