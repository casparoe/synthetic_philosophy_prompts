I'm a PhD student in CS — I work on RL for urban search-and-rescue, mostly multi-agent drone stuff for post-earthquake triage, and I've somehow ended up being the person on the project who has to write the ethical-objective section of our paper, which nobody else wanted. I went down a rabbit hole this week and found Taurek's old should-the-numbers-count paper and the aggregating replies to it, and I cant tell whether the disagreement is real or whether it's mostly notational. So I want you to translate it into the vocabulary I actually use, and then tell me honestly what got lost.

My vocabulary: reward functions and scalarization, expected return over an episode, discounting, per-person indicator rewards versus one summed team reward, constrained MDPs and chance constraints, risk-sensitive and distributional objectives (worst-case, CVaR), credit assignment across agents, partial observability and belief states, stochastic policies and where the randomness gets drawn (sampled per step, sampled once per deployment, or sampled once at training time when we pick the objective), policy evaluation and regret under distribution shift. Assume I'm fluent in all that and that I know roughly zero moral philosophy beyond what I picked up this week.

The case as it actually shows up for us: a drone arrives at a collapsed structure. It has a tracked identity on one victim — face and ID, we've been watching that person for twenty minutes — and a count of five more from a thermal/audio sensor in the north wing. We think there are five in there but we have no identities and not even a hard number; the belief is about 4.6 expected. It can only stabilize one location before the battery and the window close. So: one identified person versus an anonymous, slightly uncertain many.

Please restate, in that vocabulary and as faithfully as you can, three positions: (1) Taurek's — the numbers don't count, nobody loses more than everything, there's no one who suffers the aggregate loss, each person has an equal claim, so a coin flip is the natural impartial answer and saving the one you happen to care about isn't a moral mistake; (2) the aggregating reply — five deaths are worse than one, and impartiality plus everyone counting once gets you the greater number; (3) the equal-chance / lottery position that seems to sit between them. For each, say what translates cleanly, and flag every place where you had to make a choice that is really a moral assumption wearing a technical hat. I already suspect that writing the reward as a sum of per-person indicators is just the aggregator's conclusion smuggled in as a definition, and that picking a risk measure is already a distributive decision — tell me if I'm wrong about where the smuggling happens.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. My guesses at what has no counterpart: the claim that nobody is five times worse off; the difference between what's permitted and what's required; the idea of a claim being owed *to* someone; partiality toward someone you have a relationship with, since a drone has no relationships; and the fact that choosing an objective at training time is a different kind of act from choosing an action at deployment time. Correct me where my guesses are bad.

And four specific things I want chewed on:

(a) Identified versus anonymous. Taurek's cases seem to have everybody identified. Does his argument even have a grip when the five are an expected count in a belief state rather than five someones? Once the many are statistical, does saving them just collapse into expected-value maximization, so that the aggregator wins by default and nobody notices?

(b) Where the randomness lives. Sampling an action at deployment; randomly committing to a deterministic policy per deployment; randomizing once at training over which objective to train; or training on the aggregate and deploying deterministically. Are these genuinely different translations of giving each person an equal chance, or is only one of them a lottery in the sense that matters? Does the coin flip map onto a stochastic policy at all, or onto something about the training procedure?

(c) The sequential version. My system makes thousands of these calls across a deployment lifetime. Is equal chance a per-decision constraint or a lifetime-level distributional constraint, does the one-shot argument generalize, and if it doesn't, is that a flaw in the argument or an artifact of forcing it into a policy?

(d) Under team-reward MARL, everyone shares one return and per-agent losses aren't even representable. Is the whole numbers debate invisible from inside that setup, and is that a fact about how I've specified the problem or a fact about aggregation being baked in from the start?

Finish by giving me your view on whether the residue means my framework is missing something representable — per-agent claims as hard constraints, some non-aggregative objective I've never heard of, whatever — or whether the philosophers' disagreement is thinner than it looks once you force it into something you'd actually have to implement. Be blunt if you think one side only survives on a slide between five deaths being worse and you being required to save the five.
