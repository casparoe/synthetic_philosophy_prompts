The setup, in my own terms, so that we need not agree on any canonical formulation. A judge of limited capability has to adjudicate a question it could not answer unaided: whether a long-horizon plan is safe, whether a scientific hypothesis is supported by a messy body of evidence, whether a piece of reasoning contains an error. Two debaters take fixed opposing sides and produce a branching structure of claims, challenges and concessions; the judge picks a winner and that verdict is the training signal. The hope is that the protocol converts one question the judge cannot settle into many questions it can: a falsehood is harder to maintain than the truth is to defend, because the liar must fabricate an entire consistent story while the truthful debater can point at the place where the story breaks.

What I want explained is a cluster of worries that I think get run together in discussion, and I suspect only one or two of them are doing real work. Please say which.

First, the proof-checking analogy and where it frays. Formal proof checking lets the verifier be weak because the medium is rigid: each step is mechanically checkable and no unstated premise has to be supplied. Debate aspires to the same division of labour but is conducted in natural language, so accepting a step requires the judge to supply the bridge. Call the unstated inferential work the judge must do to accept a single step the bridging cost, and call the number of steps whose acceptance is needed for a verdict the verification depth. Both are, in part, chosen by the debaters. An honest debater can try to compress its case into many cheap, shallow steps; a dishonest one can try to route the judge through steps that are individually easy to accept but jointly misleading. How should we think about the judge's required competence here? Is the claim that the judge needs almost no domain knowledge, or only that it needs the kind of domain knowledge it can acquire inside the debate? What stops a debater from exploiting bridging cost?

Second, the localizability of defeaters. The optimistic case seems to presuppose that any false answer has a locally checkable flaw: there is a node in the tree where the honest debater can point and produce a claim the judge is able to check. In several of the domains we actually care about the defect looks distributed instead: the plan fails because of how fifty individually innocuous steps interact; the hypothesis is unsupported because of a diffuse pattern of selective attention to evidence. Call a defeater non-local when it can only be exhibited by displaying a large object and asking the judge to assess it holistically, which reproduces the original hard question. Is non-localizability fatal, or does cross-examination rescue the scheme by forcing the dishonest debater to commit to a detailed model that can then be probed for internal inconsistency, thereby converting a distributed defect into a local one? And what is the right way to think about commitment here, given that debaters are trained policies rather than persons and can in principle be shaped never to make a checkable commitment at all?

Third, the gap between statements about the game and statements about the training process. The natural way to state the guarantee is as a fixed-point claim about the game: under some idealization, truth-telling wins, or the honest debater has a strategy that beats any liar. But in the intended use we never observe the fixed point. We run reinforcement learning against a reward that just is the judge's verdict, and the debaters, and in some variants the judge, co-adapt. So there are two quite different claims that might be meant by saying that debate is truth-conducive. I have been toying with the idea that the relevant quantity is not the judge's accuracy on the debates it actually sees but its accuracy on counterfactual debates involving a competent liar it has never faced: a robustness-under-adversarial-perturbation notion, or off-distribution generalization, rather than on-distribution accuracy. Tell me whether that captures what is needed, and what it leaves out.

Fourth, the scaling claim itself and a tension inside it. If the protocol is doing work, the judge's verdict must be tracking something other than the debaters' persuasive power; yet the training signal consists in that very verdict, so effective training makes debaters better at persuading this judge. The more the method succeeds at producing skilled debaters, the more the judge's verdict reflects skill at persuasion. Is this an instance of a familiar structure, such as Goodhart-style degeneration or the way a measure used for selection stops measuring what it measured, or is it structurally new? In particular, from the judge's own position, is there any difference between being convinced by an argument that is in fact sound and being convinced by one that merely looks sound to you?
