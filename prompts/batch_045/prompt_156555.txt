Hey — I'm in a final-year reading group on evolution and mind (philosophy and cog-sci students, plus one physics PhD who tags along and relises everything into a foundations-of-physics argument), and we keep hitting the same pair of slogans in a way that feels like a stalemate rather than a disagreement.

Last week someone argued that personal-level explanations — she crossed the road because she believed the bus was coming and wanted to catch it — are genuinely causal, not just a stopgap we use until neuroscience fills in the details. The physics guy said something like "that's a skyhook, there are no skyhooks, cranes all the way down" and treated the question as closed. Somebody else shot back that this is just greedy reductionism wearing a different hat. The line on our handout is Dennett's: "Good reductionists suppose that all Design can be explained without skyhooks; greedy reductionists suppose it can all be explained without cranes" (I think from the universal-acid chapter of Darwin's Dangerous Idea). I'm less interested in what Dennett meant than in what these sentences can be made to mean, because I suspect the physics guy was leaning on a reading he'd never defend if it were stated flatly.

So: could you give me a menu of the distinct theses someone might be asserting when they say (a) "there are no skyhooks", (b) "cranes all the way down", and (c) "that's greedy reductionism"? I tried sorting them myself into three buckets — claims about where explanatory lift comes from, claims about whether a given level of description is dispensable, and claims about history (how the design got there) versus claims about what explains what right now — but I keep merging things that should stay apart, and "all the way down" in particular seems to pull in the opposite direction from "a crane has to stand on the ground". For each reading say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or actually false, and then the bit I actually care about: which reading would have to be true for the skyhook charge to settle the argument about personal-level causation, whether that reading is the one being invoked, and whether it's in tension with the greedy/good distinction the same person would appeal to five minutes later.

One more wrinkle I want your take on, since I've been chewing on it and can't tell if it's a real third option or just cranes in a costume. Call it buoyancy. A crane gets its lift from a structure built up from below; a skyhook gets it from nowhere, dangling from above. But something could get its lift from the medium it floats in: a higher-level posit that isn't derived from the lower-level facts and isn't a free-floating wonder-worker either, because the lower-level facts pin it down completely even though they don't entail it, and it then does explanatory work on the very things that constitute it — the traffic jam is made of cars, is fixed by where the cars are, and is also why each driver slows down. Is that coherent as a third category across the readings on your menu, does it collapse into one of Dennett's two under some of them (and which), and if it survives, does "no skyhooks" still rule it out — and should the person who says "no skyhooks" want it to? Don't bother with reading suggestions, our syllabus is locked; I just want the distinctions clean enough to take back next week, and ideally a flag on which of them I'd be conceding too much by granting.
