I've been arguing with a friend about some interpretability stuff, and I'm pretty sure the disagreement bottoms out in something Dennett-ish, so I want an actual number out of you rather than a survey of positions.

Setup first, so you don't need any ML background: take a big trained neural network. At some middle layer, the network's state is a long vector of numbers. You train a small auxiliary model to rewrite each such vector as a sparse sum — a handful of directions picked out of a fixed learned menu of maybe 100k candidate directions (this is what people call dictionary learning / sparse autoencoders; that's the only jargon I'll use). Now, two things reliably happen in this kind of work:

1. The rewrite is decent, the directions in the menu are semi-interpretable (some of them seem to correspond to things like "the text is in French" or "the speaker is stating something false"), and using them you can predict the network's later behavior a bit better than you can from the raw numbers.
2. Change the random seed, or the menu size (50k instead of 100k), or how hard you push on sparsity, and you get a *different* menu. Same reconstruction quality, same downstream predictive performance, but the two menus carve the activation space differently. Some directions from run A have near-duplicates in run B — call it 20-30% of them, the "seed-robust" ones. The rest have no counterpart in the other run, and the two menus' descriptions of the same activation partly conflict.

My friend says: so there's no fact of the matter about which menu is the network's real decomposition. These are useful fictions, like centers of gravity. I say: no, the seed-robust ~25% are onto something — independent runs converging on the same directions is evidence of a real pattern, and the rest is an artifact of our compressor. He says convergence across seeds tells you about our training procedure and our aesthetic preferences, not about the network.

So: what's your credence that there *is* a fact of the matter about which decomposition of that layer is the real one — not "which is most useful for us," but which carving the network actually has? Give me a number and decompose it. The terms I want to see, at minimum:

- how much rides on the compressibility analysis of patternhood being correct (Dennett's move, as I understand it: a pattern is real just in case there's a description of the data that's shorter than the raw bit-level description, and the physical, design and intentional stances are all just schemes for finding such compressions, none of them metaphysically privileged — correct me if I'm mangling it)
- how much rides on whether that analysis can even distinguish my two menus, given that they compress equally well: is the tie a genuine indeterminacy in the world, or just a gap in the criterion
- how much rides on whether the physical stance is privileged after all (the raw floats are the one description that's always available and always exact), since if it is, the question is settled in favor of "no real carving above the raw level" and the whole debate is over before it starts
- whether cross-seed convergence is evidence of mind-independent structure or only of shared inductive bias in our learners, and how strong that evidence is, conditional on each of the above
