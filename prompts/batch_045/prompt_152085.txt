I write for the science-and-ideas desk at a national magazine, and I'm turning a story about who gets to vote into an interactive graphic for our website: readers answer a short series of questions and get sorted into a position on the boundary problem — the puzzle of who constitutes the demos, which no voting rule can settle on its own, since the rule presupposes an answer to it.

I don't want a list of theories with names attached. I want the tree built from the logical space first, and the labels placed afterward. Give me six branching questions, each phrased as a concrete case a reader with no background in political theory can answer, and never as the doctrine it tests: don't ask whether subjection to coercion is the proper criterion, ask about the township that gets the noise and the water draw from a data center it has no vote on. Sort toward seven to nine leaves, each carrying the ordinary label where one exists — all-affected, all-subjected, membership-based, competence-based, and so on — or marked unnamed, plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment that leaf carries.

The cases I want the tree to use, at minimum: a long-term resident without citizenship voting in a municipal election; a sixteen-year-old in a school board race; an undocumented farmworker; an out-of-state commuter who works but does not sleep in the district; the generation not yet born that inherits a forty-year infrastructure decision; a corporation that owns land in the district; and a resident of a territory whose laws are made elsewhere.

Also: mark the combinations of answers that no actual position occupies, and say for each whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate rather than forcing a split between them; and name the single node that does the most sorting work.

Be committed rather than even-handed: defend one criterion as the right one, say which leaf you would stand behind, and give your reasons — with a note on how confident you are and what would change your mind.

Return a single JSON object with these fields: nodes (id, question_text, case, yes_target, no_target, why_this_split), leaves (id, label_or_unnamed, one_sentence_awkward_commitment, who_typically_lands_here), empty_cells (answer_path, diagnosis, note), inseparable_pairs (leaf_ids, why), load_bearing_node (id, why), and verdict (criterion_defended, leaf_id, justification, confidence, what_would_change_it). American English throughout.
