Hey — I'm in a seminar on consciousness and well-being this term and we just spent two weeks on the badness of pain, and I came out of it realising my gut verdicts don't add up to anything clean. My roommate, who's in the class too, says it's obvious: pain is bad exactly to the degree you dislike it, nothing else matters, and asymbolia patients just aren't in pain in the sense that counts. That feels too quick to me but I can't say why. So here's where I land, case by case. Classic pain asymbolia — sensation and intensity reporting fully intact, patient can tell a pinprick from a burn and rate it accurately, but reports no unpleasantness, isn't bothered, doesn't pull away, sometimes laughs: I want to say something bad is still going on, maybe 1 out of 10 bad, definitely not zero. A distance runner in real pain at mile 20 who hates it in the moment, is glad afterwards, and would refuse an analgesic if offered: bad, but a bad she'd choose to keep — and I don't want to say it stops being bad just because she wants it. A masochist who genuinely enjoys it, seeks it out, is disappointed when it's too mild: I want to say it isn't bad at all for them, maybe positively good, even though I also want to say it still hurts. A headache you don't notice at all during a three-hour exam and then feel throbbing the second you hand the paper in: zero badness during the exam, not even unnoticed badness — and I hate the implication that it became bad at the instant I noticed it. A chronic pain patient ten years in who still rates it 7/10, notices it constantly, but says it doesn't really bother her anymore: still bad, basically as bad as ever. Surgery under full anaesthesia with nociception but no experience whatsoever: obviously not bad at all. Scratching a poison-ivy itch, or eating food so spicy it genuinely hurts: I go back and forth — half the time I say a bit bad but worth it, half the time I say not bad at all, and part of me wants it to stop while part of me wants more.

Here are two cases I made up myself, so they aren't from the readings: in the first, the sensory pain is fully intact and the person has an overwhelming urge to make it stop — they pull away, they'd pay to end it, they call it horrible — but on careful introspection they insist that nothing about how it actually feels is unpleasant; the urge is more like a reflex demand, like needing to blink or scratch. I want to say that's still substantially bad. In the second, the feeling is thoroughly nasty, exactly as unpleasant as a bad burn, but the person has no urge to stop it, no aversion at all, is entirely unbothered and happy to keep going for hours. I also want to say that's bad. My tentative principle, which I think is roughly what I've been running, is: pain is bad for you to the extent that you want it to stop, where the wanting is not the same thing as the intensity of the sensation and not the same thing as how unpleasant it feels. That gets the masochist and the exam headache right, but it seems to say the asymbolia case, the adapted chronic pain patient and my second made-up case aren't bad at all, and I don't accept that.

So: can you state precisely what principle or principles actually fit my verdicts, and tell me which clause of each is doing the work on which case? I care about bad for the person having it, not badness in general or badness for bystanders. Do I need a disjunctive thing — bad if either unpleasant or unwanted — and is that just a gerrymandered list, or is there something less ad hoc that gives the same pattern by giving up one or two of my verdicts? If something has to go, tell me which verdict is the weakest and why. Then run whichever account you like best on three cases I haven't listed: pain you know is coming tomorrow and spend all week dreading; pain that hurt less at the time than it does in your memory of it afterwards; and an animal or infant that has the pain but no concept of pain and never reflects on it. Also please say how confident you are in the account you're giving me overall, and single out the one part of it you're least sure about.
