Hey — I write novels, not philosophy papers, so please forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary a bit. I'm finishing a campus novel, and one chapter is basically just my protagonist's final exam, printed in full. She's Dr. Ines Vasa, a visiting lecturer, tired, slightly disillusioned, teaching an upper-level seminar called "Intuition, Culture and the Limits of the Armchair." So I need the exam to read like something a real overworked academic would actually set — a bit provocative, a bit deflaty — and not like a parody of philosophy. But it also has to be *real*, in the sense that a philosopher who picked up my book wouldn't roll their eyes.

What I need from you: one 90-minute in-class essay question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points (4 marks each), with a one-line note per point on how candidates usually mess it up.

Here's the material I want you to work with, some of which I've made up.

The set-up my character gives the students: a Kripke-flavoured reference case — call it the Kalu case. In a village, everyone has learned the name "Kalu" from a single storyteller. The storyteller's tales are all true of a man called Kofi, who died twenty years ago in another country and of whom nobody in the village has ever heard. Villagers say things like "Kalu was wise" and "Kalu was unlucky." The question put to respondents: when a villager says "Kalu was unlucky," is she saying something about Kofi? Suppose a survey finds that one population mostly answers yes (it's the man the stories are actually about) and another mostly answers no (she isn't saying anything about Kofi; or there's no one she's saying it about). Alongside it, a "control" case where both populations agree — something flat and perceptual, like: Ama puts her keys in the drawer at noon, says "my keys are in the drawer," and at three o'clock, nobody having entered the room, we ask whether she knew where the keys were. Both groups say yes.

Then the bit I invented. Vasa calls it the *bridle*: whatever mechanism you use to explain away the divergence in the Kalu case — miscomprehension, a different reading of the question, pragmatic politeness, not "going along with" a weird story — has to be one that wouldn't equally predict divergence on the control case. You can't wave off disagreement with "they didn't really get the story" unless you can say why they got the other story fine.

So the question should force a stand: grant the bridle. Does the divergence then give us any reason at all to revise what we think about reference (or about knowledge attributions)? Or is the whole programme — surveying people's intuitions and treating the results as philosophical evidence — asking a question it can't answer? Either way, the answer has to say what would have to be true for her to change her mind.

Three constraints, please.

1. No citations, no bibliography, no "for further reading." My character is grading these on a train with no signal, and I don't want the question to depend on anyone having memorised who ran which study. Refer to the literature generically if you must — "the usual joke that student samples are Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic," that sort of thing — but nothing that needs a library to check.
2. The model answer must not get away with the two lazy moves: "the samples are unrepresentative, therefore" and "only expert intuitions count, therefore." If either move appears, it has to be actually cashed out — what exactly is being claimed about experts, and what makes their verdicts evidence.
3. Have the model answer mark, as it goes (a short closing paragraph is fine), which of its claims are broadly settled among people who work on this and which are live fights — including whether the empirical variation itself is robust, and whether expert-filtering is legitimate. My protagonist is the sort of marker who takes points off for presenting a contested reading as if it were consensus, and I'd like the scheme to reward that too.

Also, teh question itself should be short — half a page at most, the kind of thing a student can read twice and still be unsure which way to jump. And it can be a little deflaty, but not cute.
