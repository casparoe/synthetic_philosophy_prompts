I read a good deal of popular history, and there is one move I keep running into that I don't have a word for. The pattern is this: a book builds its story around a hinge — a battle that could have gone the other way, an appointment that went to one candidate rather than another, a technical standard adopted on a close vote — and someone objects that the hinge isn't really load-bearing, because if that particular thing hadn't happened, the same end result would have arrived anyway by some other route in roughly the same period. I've seen this said about the spread of printing in Europe, and I've seen it said about the discovery of penicillin. In both cases the claim was not "it was a small event" but "it was a replaceable one."

What interests me is that the move seems to come in at least three grades, and I can't tell whether they are one claim or three:

1. A different person or event would have produced the same result within a few years, and we can point to rivals who were close behind.
2. The result would have arrived eventually, on no fixed timescale — decades later, or centuries later, it doesn't matter which.
3. Not that exact result, but something recognisably of that kind was bound to happen: the pressure was building, only the shape it took was optional.

Grade 1 looks checkable and is sometimes true. Grade 2 looks as though it cannot be wrong, since there is always more time available for it to be right about. Grade 3 seems to change the subject, because "something of that kind" can be stretched until almost anything counts. And there is a fourth thing I would want pulled out separately: we only ever seem to say this about outcomes that actually occurred. Nobody says of some reform that was defeated two centuries ago that it was bound to be enacted eventually, even though by the same reasoning it should have been.

A friend told me the term for this is "path dependence," but from what I can make out that names something close to the opposite — the idea that early, small events get locked in and really do matter. So: does the move I've described have an established name in history, in philosophy of history, or somewhere else, and does anything in the literature separate its grades the way I've tried to? If there are several nearby notions — one for replaceable causes, one for the same end reached by different routes, one for the impression created by only asking the question about outcomes we already know about — I'd like them set out separately and told apart from each other. I would rather be told that nothing established fits and be given a coined term with a definition and a couple of borderline cases than be handed a label that covers only half of it.

Please also say how confident you are in each part of what you tell me, and single out the one claim you are least sure of.
