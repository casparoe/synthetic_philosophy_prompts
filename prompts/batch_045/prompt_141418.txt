I am a novelist, and I am trying to stage an argument honestly rather than to win it.

The sentence I keep coming back to is one my own drawing teacher used to say while erasing a page of mine: there is no innocent eye. I have repeated it for years without being able to say what it means, and now it has ended up in a novel, where it has to bear weight.

My protagonist is a painter. At nineteen she spent a summer painting a valley obsessively, in a bad, earnest, untrained way. At forty-four, after two decades of a brutal atelier education, she goes back, and she insists that the valley itself now looks different to her — not that she notices more, not that she describes it better, but that it looks different. Her childhood friend, a vision scientist, denies this. He says the sentence means nothing more alarming than that you cannot report what you see without concepts, or that your eye movements are guided by what you know, or that you cannot say what you saw without thereby interpreting it; and that none of this is a change in how the valley looks. She says it is. I need to know what the disagreement is actually about, because at the moment I suspect both of them are using the slogan to avoid stating a thesis.

So: please set out, as a menu rather than as a single preferred reading, the distinct precise claims "there is no innocent eye" could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical. For each, say what the world would have to be like for it to be true; label it as a platitude, as a substantive and defensible empirical claim, as substantive but probably false, or as straightforwardly false; and — this is what I most need — say whether it would support her claim that the valley now looks different in a way his account denies, or whether it collapses into something he could happily grant.

I have been trying to sort this out with a rough taxonomy of my own, and I would also like to know whether the taxonomy has the right shape and what it is missing. First, the difference between changes in what gets selected — which objects, features and properties are picked out of a scene for attention — and changes in how the selected things look; I have been calling these selection effects and aspect effects, my own labels, and I suspect the fight is really about whether anything of the second kind exists. Second, within aspect effects, whether the changed look is available to her as a look, something she can attend to and cannot unsee, or only inferable from her discriminations, her brush, her reaction times. Third, whether the change survives her knowing its cause: she knows exactly what her training did to her, she can name the mechanism, and the cheek still looks like a plane turning over bone, the far ridge still looks further away than it did, the hillside still looks structured once she knows the strata. Does that persistence count as evidence for her, or is it exactly what his account predicts?

There is a related worry I want your view on. Her old paintings of the valley survive, and so do her father's photographs of it, and both now look wrong to her — the paintings too flat, the photographs as if they had been taken of a different place. He says this shows the change is in her judgment, not in her seeing. She says it shows her seeing has changed and the record has not. Is there a distinction here that settles it, or does the availability of a fixed record do no work at all?

Finally, the thing that makes me suspicious of the strong readings: if her seeing has been taken over by what she knows, then the very looking she would use to check whether her new way of seeing the land is correct has already been captured by it, and there is no neutral tribunal left — the claim, on those readings, seems to go unfalsifiable, or to consume the evidence offered for it. Does that objection land, and is there a reading of the sentence that is genuinely nontrivial and true while escaping it?

Tell me, as well, which reading people who repeat this line are most likely to mean, and what each of my two characters would have to concede for the argument to be a real one rather than a quibble. I do not want a reading list or a set of references; I want the menu, the verdicts, and the verdicts on my taxonomy.
