My uncle was a hard atheist for forty years, had a health scare last spring, and now he's at mass most Sundays. I asked him wether he actually believes any of it and he said, more or less: it costs me an hour a week, and if it happens to be true it's the best trade anyone ever made — what's the downside? Thing is, he also thinks a God who'd reward that kind of calculation would be contemptible, and in every other part of his life he won't act on a 2 percent chance of anything.

It's clearly not just him: people who don't believe get married in churches and baptize kids "just in case," pray when the plane drops, and there's a whole institutional thing around deathbed conversion. So what does that practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that belief is something you can decide to have, or only that going through the motions is enough to collect? Does it presuppose the payoff hangs on belief itself rather than on a decent life, or on not being a hypocrite? Does it quietly assume there's basically one candidate worth hedging against, given that nobody ever hedges against a god who punishes church-goers? And what about the mismatch with how these same people treat every other tiny-probability, huge-payoff gamble, which they'd normally call negligible? I've heard there's a famous old argument that sounds like his, but as I understand it that one is about getting yourself to actually believe, not about hedging with ritual — so I'm not sure it even covers what he's doing. Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me which bits several different theories could equally license, and say what would have to change if it turns out belief isn't up to us at all.
