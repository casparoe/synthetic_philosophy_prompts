Hey — I'm in a tiny reading group on philosophy of mathematics (two logicians, an astrophysicist, and me, a maths PhD student who mostly works in analytic number theory), and we each have to circulate a one-page statement of where we stand so the others can pull it apart. I've been going back and forth on mine for weeks and it's making me suspicious that the pieces don't fit, but every time I start from a different commitment I get a different answer about what the problem is, which usually means there's more than one. So please audit the set rather than tell me what to believe.

Where I've landed: (1) Platonism — there are mind-independent abstract objects, and mathematical claims are determinately true or false of them, not merely assertable or useful. (2) Benacerraf's challenge is the *real* problem for that view: the job is to explain how our mathematical beliefs could be reliable given that abstract objects, if they exist, are causally and spatiotemporally inert, and I don't think anyone gets to just stipulate a faculty of mathematical intuition. (3) I don't accept the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument: the fact that our best science quantifies over mathematical entities doesn't by itself commit us to them, and I'm sympathetic to the thought that a Field-style nominalization is always available in principle, or that the quantification can be read as in some sense non-committing. (4) My positive proposal, which I made up and am attached to: I distinguish two grades of reliability for a mathematical method — call them output-stability (feed the method slightly different but equivalent formulations inside a successful scientific model and it doesn't flip verdicts; it isn't fragile to the auxiliaries) and source-fidelity (its verdicts match how the abstract objects actually are). My claim is that empirical success calibrates the method for output-stability, that we can factor the mathematical contribution out of the confirmation of the total package because a piece of mathematics typically recurs across theories in unrelated domains — I've been calling this cross-theory invariance — and that output-stability then supports source-fidelity by a no-miracles-style inference: the best explanation of a method's stable success across domains is that it's tracking something. (5) Mathematical knowledge is nonetheless a priori: what justifies a working mathematician's belief is the proof, not any experiment. (6) I'm a methodological naturalist — no first philosophy, no tribunal outside science. (7) Some verdicts I don't want to give up: we know the Gödel sentence for PA is true; the search for new axioms settling the continuum hypothesis (large cardinals and so on) is a discovery project, not legislation, so its outcome is the kind of thing we could come to know; and I think the community's pre-proof confidence in something like Goldbach, grounded in density heuristics, is genuine evidence rather than just psychology.

What I'd like: work through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, ordered by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad; for each real tension say precisely what would have to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the set; and flag the ones that only look like they conflict and say why they don't, because I'd rather not throw out something that was fine. Finish with the single revision that cleans up the most. Several people in the group keep telling me the obvious move is to drop (1) and become a fictionalist, and I'd rather not do that on the cheap — so if that really is where the pressure lands, say so plainly and tell me what I'd lose.
