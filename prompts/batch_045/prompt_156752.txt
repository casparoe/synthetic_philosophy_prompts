I have to give a single seventy-five-minute seminar on Dewey's experimentalism to a reading group of software engineers: people who run A/B tests for a living, have no philosophy background, and will arrive assuming democracy is a mechanism for aggregating pre-existing preferences. Plan the session; don't write it.

I need the dependency structure first. My instinct is that the claim that inquiry is triggered by an indeterminate situation — a disruption that can't be resolved by applying an existing habit — has to be on the table before I say anything about voting, deliberation, or democracy, and that only then can the analogy between experimental revision of a hypothesis and experimental revision of a policy do any work. Confirm or correct that order, and say what each step is setting up.

Then the carrying example. I want to build the session around a hospital morbidity-and-mortality conference: a regular, non-punitive review of adverse outcomes whose whole point is to revise protocol rather than to assign blame. My worry is that it carries inquiry perfectly and democracy not at all, since it's expert-run and nobody outside the room is implicated by the decision. Should I run it as a case that gets deliberately broken and extended partway through, or drop it for something else? Tell me what a single example must contain to do both jobs, and what mine necessarily lacks.

The misconceptions I expect: that experimentalism is just randomized controlled trials scaled up, that calling a policy an experiment means trying it and watching the polls, and that fallibilism means no course of action can be criticized. Give me the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, not just a rebuttal to each.

Finally: one closing question that separates someone who has the idea from someone who has only picked up the vocabulary, and one thing I should explicitly announce I am not going to cover in seventy-five minutes.
