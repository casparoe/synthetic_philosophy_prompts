My friend and I got into a huge argument after our ethics seminar last night and I've been chewing on it ever since. We were arguing about whether hard choices — the kind where you deliberate for weeks, can't say that either option is the better one, and then just commit without thinking you got it wrong — really show that the options are incommensurable. He defends the parity story and I went in wanting to defend it too, but I built the following argument on the walk home and now I think it kills the view, which I definately don't want. Let A be staying on as a lecturer in the city where your partner lives and B be a much better paid consulting job abroad, and let the improvement I keep adding be salary, since salary is something you can nudge by a cent at a time:

(1) Some choices are hard in the demanding sense: it isn't settled which option is better, but it also isn't that you're merely unsure which is better — you can deliberate well, still find no verdict, and then rationally commit to either one. (A versus B is supposed to be like this.)
(2) If a choice is hard in that sense, then A and B are not related by better than, worse than, or equally good; they are on a par, i.e. incommensurable. If one of those three relations held, the choice would be easy: take the better option, or treat equals as indifferent.
(3) Parity is stable under small improvements: if A and B are on a par, then A plus a tiny raise is still on a par with B. My friend insists on this, and I think he has to — if a twenty-buck raise settled the choice, then the original choice was never hard in the sense of (1), it was just a close call about which option was better, and the raise merely broke the tie.
(4) Parity is not stable under every improvement: a large enough raise makes the improved option clearly better and the choice easy. Surely at some point the money settles it.
(5) So there is some amount x such that A plus x is clearly better than B, while A plus (x minus one cent) is still merely on a par with B.
(6) What flips at that boundary cannot be the extra cent: not its size, not what it buys, not any difference in the reasons it gives you. So whether two options are comparable is not sensitive to a one-cent difference.
(7) Take the clear case from (4) and step down one cent at a time. By (6), each step preserves comparability. Step down far enough and A itself is comparable with B.
(8) So A and B are comparable after all, (2) is false, and hard choices have to be explained without incommensurability — as ignorance, as ties broken by tiny gains, or as something about the will.

The conclusion I can't swallow is (8), because I don't want to say that a hard choice is just a close call with a tie-breaker, and I don't want to say it's ignorance either. When I pushed him, my friend offered a rescue: the cutoff in (5) is just where the raise becomes noticeable to you, so being on a par means being inside your just-noticeable-difference band. I said that makes comparability depend on how attentive or caffeinated you are, and he said that's fine because reasons are agent-relative anyway, which struck me as a bigger bullet than (2). Then I pointed out that the same shape of argument would give sharp cutoffs for heaps and baldness, and he said those are descriptive and can happily be vague whereas a normative relation can't be, because otherwise there's no fact of the matter about what you ought to do — and that sounded like yet another bullet, but I couldn't say why. If something has to go I'd rather give up (4) than (2), except I can't see how (4) could possibly be false, and I'm loosing track of where the real problem is.
