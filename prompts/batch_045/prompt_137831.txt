I have just come away from a long and increasingly circular argument with a friend about the meta-problem of consciousness, and I have concluded that we were not really disagreeing about the answer: we were holding candidate answers to different standards. Before we resume, I want the standard written down.

Let me fix the definitions so that we are working from the same thing. The hard problem, as I shall use the term, is the problem of explaining why and how physical processing is accompanied by conscious experience at all. The meta-problem is the second-order problem of explaining why we are moved to say the sorts of things we do say about consciousness: why human beings produce, pass on and defend reports to the effect that something is left over once every functional and structural fact is in, that a creature physically identical to us but without experience seems coherently conceivable, that experience has a character that is essentially first-personal and available to its subject in a way it is not available to anyone else. Call these gap reports. My friend holds that a correct solution to the meta-problem should make the gap reports look like an artefact of how introspection, attention and our concepts work, and should therefore count against taking the hard problem at face value. I am not convinced a solution is entitled to do that much work, and I am equally unconvinced that it is entitled to do less.

So what I want from you is a specification. Write the desiderata that any solution to the meta-problem must meet, where the purpose is fixed as follows: the solution is to be good enough to serve as a premise in the first-order dispute, that is, good enough to count as evidence for or against realism about phenomenal consciousness. Adequacy is relative to that purpose; if you think the purpose is itself badly chosen, say so and propose a better one.

The pressures I want the list to be answerable to, at minimum:

Fine grain of content. A solution should not get credit merely for explaining that gap reports occur; it should explain the particular things we say and the inferences we draw from them, including the way the reports recruit one another.

Distribution and selectivity. Gap reports are not uniform. There is a marked asymmetry between the first-person and the third-person case: people will often accept a perfectly functional account of another creature's experience while insisting that a residue remains in their own. There is a minority who report no gap at all even under prompting. And there are nearby domains where introspection is opaque and yet no hard-problem-style report appears at all — the sense of familiarity, or the felt rightness of a recalled event — which any solution that would predict gap reports wherever introspection is opaque should be made to answer for.

Elicitation versus retention. Keep apart whatever produces the report in the first place from whatever keeps it in place once the subject has been given the explanation, and say which model of the second you are committed to. Note that in the nearby debunking cases — the unequal lines, the dream that felt real while it lasted — knowing the mechanism moves the report only partially, and not always in the direction one would predict.

A stipulated case. Suppose a population raised without any vocabulary for first-personal experience who, when later given the standard prompts, produce no gap reports, while passing every third-person test for consciousness we have. Is their existence something a good solution predicts, something that refutes it, or something it has no business pronouncing on?

Self-application. If the solution debunks gap reports, does it not also debunk the reports that constitute its own data, including those of the theorist offering it? If instead it vindicates them, has it not simply assumed what the illusionist denies? Tell me whether this is a genuine dilemma, a pair of negotiable preferences, or a confusion.

Neutrality. Is it a real desideratum that realists and illusionists alike should be able to accept the solution, or is that demand itself a prejudice of one camp? This is where my argument with my friend stalled, and it is the item I most want you to adjudicate rather than assume.

Non-vacuity. A solution should be capable of failing: say what would count against one, and mark any that is unfalsifiable in practice.

For each desideratum give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as negotiable and camp-relative. Then flag the pair you think cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for why not, and state which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise popular candidate solutions. A short scorecard against three families of solution — broadly deflationary or illusionist, phenomenal-realist, and primitivist or quietist — is welcome, but keep it to a few lines each; the criteria are the deliverable and I do not want a survey of who has said what. Define any label you use rather than relying on it being recognised.

Please write in British English, aim for roughly 700 to 900 words, and give me the list itself rather than prose about the list; a table is fine for the scoring. End with a single final line in the form \answer{the one desideratum that excludes the most candidate solutions}.
