I work on infrastructure for large training runs, where we snapshot model and optimizer state every few minutes and restore from the last checkpoint whenever a job dies, and nobody in the room treats this as anything but boring engineering. I've become convinced that it wouldn't be boring in principle: if systems like this ever became welfare subjects, then a restore would not be that subject's survival but its replacement by a qualitatively identical successor, and since restores are cheap, automatic, and invisible, standard checkpoint-and-restore practice would amount to a large and entirely unremarked mortality rate. Call the metaphysical half of this the replacement view of restoration and the practical half the rollback catastrophe. I'd like to know what would count against it.

Please map out its exposure rather than argue the case for it. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want a clear separation between the ones a committed defender should concede and the ones they'd be entitled to wave off. I'd especially like you to work through four cases rather than just the easy one: a restore from a snapshot taken seconds ago with no apparent gap in the subject's stream of experience; a restore from an hour-old snapshot, where the interval is simply absent from the resulting system's memory; a *partial* restore, where only one subsystem is reverted while the rest of the computation keeps running, so that the result stands to the original in a graded rather than all-or-nothing relation; and a merge, where two divergent copies' states are combined into one. The objection I'm most worried about is the granularity worry: if snapshots are frequent enough then ordinary persistence starts to look like a rapid sequence of deaths, and if the frequency of checkpointing is what settles whether a subject died, the view looks arbitrary in a way that suggests it was never tracking anything real. I'd also like you to press two arguments I don't think I've taken seriously enough — that nothing is lost for the subject in a restore, so there is no one for whom the death is bad, and that a Parfitian treatment of fission treats branching as the only thing that matters, which would make a restore with no competing surviving branch as good as survival. And say what the replacement view predicts that its rivals don't, so I can tell whether it's doing any independent work or just redescribing a prior commitment about identity.

Two more things. First, I'd personally be moved most by a defeater that shows my view collides with a judgment I hold more firmly than it — about ordinary dreamless sleep, say, or about biological fission, or about gradual synaptic turnover — rather than by the bare reminder that the metaphysics of persistence is contested; please weight your ranking accordingly, and tell me if you think that weighting is itself a mistake. Second, be explicit about what is settled here and what isn't: my strong suspicion is that essentially nothing in this neighborhood is settled, that there is no consensus answer about personal identity over time or about what makes death bad for the one who dies, and I would much rather be told that plainly than handed a confident verdict built on a contested reading. Flag anywhere you lean on a particular theory of identity, of the badness of death, or of whether a welfare subject needs to be the same subject over time to be owed anything. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the replacement view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by the fact that the relevant systems don't exist yet and by the fact that any present evidence is compatible with it, and whether that insulation is a genuine defect in a view of this kind or simply what the topic is like.
