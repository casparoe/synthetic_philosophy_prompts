Hey — I'm a TA for a first-year seminar and I've got one 75-minute slot on Hume's argument against miracles. I want to do something other than the usual slides, so I'm thinking of screening The Green Mile (Frank Darabont, 1999) — death row at Cold Mountain in 1935, Paul Edgecomb and his guards, John Coffey convicted of killing two girls, cures Paul's infection, brings Del's crushed mouse Mr. Jingles back to life, heals Warden Hal Moores' dying wife Melinda in her bedroom, then gets executed anyway. I've got maybe forty minutes of film to show and I want an actual reading of it I can hand to students, not a plot summary or a list of themes.

What I keep going in circles on is a distinction I made up and I can't tell if its real or if it's a cop-out: call it sign-miracles vs gift-miracles. A sign-miracle happens in order to be evidence — it's public, it has an audience, it's meant to found or confirm a religion. A gift-miracle just helps somebody and would work exactly the same if nobody ever found out. Hume's target looks like the first kind: his checks on testimony (are the witnesses numerous enough, of good sense and education, of unimpeachable reputation, in a learned age, in public, with something considerable at stake) and his big move — that no testimony establishes a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it supports — all seem to presume the miracle is being offered as a proof. Coffey's healings look like the second kind: he doesn't start a church, doesn't tell anyone, absorbs the illness and it wrecks him, and the healing of Melinda is basically a favour to the guards. But then the film is also completely clear that nobody in authority is ever brought around: Paul believes, the warden half-believes for about a night, and Coffey dies. So maybe the film grants Hume the whole thing about sign-miracles never being properly attested, and then asks whether that's a fact about God or a fact about us.

So read it through that. Does the sign/gift distinction actually get the film's miracles out of Hume's reach, or is it just moving the goalposts — isn't a gift-miracle still a violation of a law of nature, which is his actual definition, and does he care why it happened? How do the film's witnesses stack up against the criteria: five guards, a warden, a dying woman who recovers, a mouse, all of it happening in 1935 Louisiana rather than ancient somewhere, and all of it deliberately kept out of any courtroom or newspaper? Does that satisfy the falsehood-more-miraculous clause, or is Hume's argument rigged so nothing could ever satisfy it, and does the film know that? And the bit I find weirdly unexplored: Paul gets Wharton's guilt by supernatural transfer — he literally is shown it, he knows — and that knowledge is worth nothing to anyone else. Hume is an argument about what we can hand each other; the film gives its hero certainty he can't transmit. Does that do work, or am I overreading?

Also: the frame puts us in two epistemic seats at once. We watch the healings, so we're witnesses not hearers, but we also get them as a story a 108-year-old man is telling one woman in a nursing home in 1999, and the evidence he offers her is a mouse in a cigar box and his own age. Is that duration-as-evidence thing the film's answer to Hume, or is it just a magical realist premise the movie is quietly cheating on?
