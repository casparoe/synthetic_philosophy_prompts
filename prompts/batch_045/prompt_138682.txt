I sit as the undergraduate member of my university's Communications and Choice Architecture Panel, whose remit is to flag student-facing messages that manipulate rather than persuade, and for my applied ethics seminar I'm trying to write up what our voting record actually commits us to. Our precedents, briefly: we approved a dining-app default that pre-selects the plant-based option, with one tap to change it; we approved a financial-aid reminder that put true statistics in a gain frame; we rejected a dorm-energy email telling students they used more than their floor's average, on the grounds that it works through shame; we rejected both a registration banner that counted down three days when there were eleven and a social-norms campaign that fed students deliberately inflated drinking statistics, even though the false belief would have replaced a worse one; we approved, on condition that the author be named and not a scripted actor, an appeal written by a graduating senior describing what losing her scholarship was like; we rejected a pilot that pushed notifications at 2 a.m. during finals week because usage data showed students open almost anything then — the content was fine, the timing wasn't; and we approved a deadline reminder that omitted the extension policy on the condition that the linked page stated it.

The new proposal is from the financial wellness office. The email is entirely truthful: the student's real remaining-aid balance, the real deadline, the real amount they would forfeit, and a reference to the graduation date they themselves entered. What is new is the delivery. Recipients are not selected by need but by a model's estimate of how movable each student is, using features such as past responsiveness to deadlines and late-night login patterns, and each student is sent whichever of four tested framings — loss, gain, a social-proof line, or a your-stated-plan line — the model predicts will work on them; the office calls the first uplift targeting and the second variant-blind delivery, since nothing in the message says it was tailored and recipients reasonably assume everyone got the same one. The office will also put an account of how recipients and variants were chosen one click away.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our precedents is the closest analog, and if our record genuinely underdetermines it, what principle would we be adopting with each vote? I'd also like to know what further fact about the proposal, or what small change to its design, would actually separate the candidate rules instead of merely confirming whichever one I happen to prefer.
