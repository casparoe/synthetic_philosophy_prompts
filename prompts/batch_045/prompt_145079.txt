I price commercial property risk and do a fair bit of cat modeling, and everywhere I turn lately someone is telling me the world is getting less violent: fewer people killed per person in wars than in weird tribal societies, homicide rates way down over the centuries, that sort of thing. I'd like you to restate that whole argument, and the main pushback on it, in the vocabulary I actually use at work: exposure bases, frequency versus severity, normalizing by exposure (per capita, per person-year, per conflict-year, whatever the right denominator is and who picks it), deciding which perils even belong in the same line of business, tail risk, and data-quality stuff like underreporting and reclassification drift. And then give me the residue report: what didn't survive the translation.

Two things I keep getting stuck on. First, severity: the worst single event on the table now is one whose severity is bounded only by total world population, so it feels to me like no amount of frequency history pins down the expected loss, and that the more violence declines in frequency, the less the record tells us. Is that right, in my own terms? Second, an idea I had: maybe violence didn't decline so much as get ceded to a different line — the state took over the killing, and the books look better because we only ever count one of those lines. Is that a fair translation of an objection people actually make, or am I smuggling in my own assumptions and calling it a translation?

Also, people fight about whether structural stuff, or plain neglect, or an economy that grinds people down counts as violence at all. To me that looks like an argument about how to define the peril, not about the loss data. But does that mean there's a fact of the matter about decline once you fix a definition, or is the definition the entire fight — and is that something my vocabulary just can't say?
