I am giving a one-hour presentation next month to a constitutional-reform working group, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet written for that specific room rather than for the sortition literature in general.

The proposal I am defending is a second chamber constituted by lot. My central claim is deliberately not the familiar one. I do not rest the case on descriptive representation, and I do not rest it on the claim that randomly selected bodies deliberate better than elected ones; the deliberative-democracy literature has argued that point at length and I want to avoid depending on it. I rest the case on what I call agenda independence: elected legislators have a structural incentive to leave alone issues whose benefits are long-term and diffuse while their costs are concentrated and immediate, and a body whose members cannot stand for anything and cannot be re-selected does not have that incentive. The chamber's power should therefore be a trigger rather than a veto: it can compel the elected chamber to hold a recorded floor vote on a specified measure within a fixed number of sitting days, but it cannot legislate, amend, or block.

Three design features carry the argument, and I expect all three to be attacked. First, continuous rotation in overlapping thirds, each cohort serving three years, so that the chamber is never wholly new and never wholly lame. Second, the subject of any trigger is selected by lot from a long-list that the chamber itself has adopted by ordinary majority vote; I call this agenda-by-lot. It is meant both to defeat capture of the chamber by a durable internal faction and to hold the chamber's workload down to what it can actually deliberate well. Third, before voting on a trigger the chamber must receive two reports, not one: the second commissioned from a panel of outside experts drawn by stratified lot from a standing registry, so that no single briefing frames the question.

The room will be about twelve people. Two are deliberative democrats in the Habermasian tradition, who think legitimacy is a function of the quality of the deliberative process and who are sympathetic to sortition but suspicious of any design that seems to short-circuit deliberation. One is a political economist who works on electoral systems and institutional design, committed to competence and accountability as the metrics that matter, and inclined to think expertise is being thrown away. One is a theorist of descriptive representation, who cares about who is in the room and about what sampling and non-response do to a claim of representativeness. There is also a practitioner who has run several real citizens' assemblies and knows what goes wrong logistically, and a civil-service secretariat member who will ask what the enabling legislation would have to say and what it would cost.

Please give me the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each of these people would put them rather than in the vocabulary of the sortition literature. For each one: a reply I could give in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which one or two objections I should concede in the body of the talk rather than wait to be asked, and what the shortest honest version of that concession is. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it is the one that asks why the randomness is doing any work at all, given that a chamber of fixed-term appointed members would be equally insulated from electoral incentive; but tell me if you think the more dangerous unasked question is a different one.
