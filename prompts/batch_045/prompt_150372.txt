I'm a novelist, three drafts into a book whose protagonist actually does it: gives away most of what she earns, moves where she's useful, abandons her own work whenever the arithmetic says someone else's need is greater. My problem is that I cant make her read as a person, and I've started to suspect that isn't a craft problem.

So: take the demandingness objection to consequentialism — the whole family of complaints, that it asks too much, that it alienates you from your own deepest projects and from the people you love, that it collapses the merely permissible and the heroic-beyond-duty into obligation, that it wants everyone to be a moral saint and saints are either dull or unnerving — and restate it in the vocabulary I actually work in. I'm fluent, so dont soften it: character, spine, arc, POV, scene vs summary, dramatic stakes, motivation that has to survive being woken at 3am, and what I call the refused scene — the place in a book where a character declines something she plausibly wants and you see, for the only time, who she is. (My rule of thumb: a character with two spines reads as two characters.)

Then the residue report, and be honest about two places I think it'll bunch up:

Impartiality. Multi-POV and omniscient narration are the closest fiction gets to the impersonal standpoint. Is that a translation of the impartial point of view, or just a voice doing an impression of no one in particular?

The saint's life. A life fully organized around one end is, in my terms, simply a strong spine, and plenty of great novels have protagonists like that. So does my vocabulary contain any way of saying what's supposed to be wrong with the demandingness, or only ways of describing it beautifully?

Commit to something at the end: is the residue evidence that novelistic vocabulary is missing a real category the philosophers have, or evidence that the objection was never about the shape of a life at all — that it's about how much it costs and who bears it? Take a side rather than laying out both, and skip the notes on my manuscript.
