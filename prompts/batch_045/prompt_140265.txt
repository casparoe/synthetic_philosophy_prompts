I work as an environmental impact assessment consultant — about twelve years of it, mostly on linear infrastructure and extractives under a NEPA-style regime, more recently on biodiversity net gain metrics — and I got into an argument last week with a friend who's doing a PhD in AI safety about whether a very capable AI system can be made to avoid side effects without anyone first writing down what matters. As he described the setup: you give the system an objective, and you add a penalty on the difference between what actually happens and what would have happened if the system had done nothing from that point on — an inaction baseline — where the difference is measured by some distance-like quantity over world states. Candidates he mentioned for that quantity included plain state distance, measures of how much the system's action changes what other goals remain reachable afterward, and various notions of how much power or option value the system ends up holding. He also flagged three problems that I found more interesting than the setup itself. First, the distance has to weight some changes as large and others as trivial, and those weights are not supplied by the objective; get them wrong and you get a system that satisfies the letter of the penalty while doing something nobody wanted. Second, the penalty has to be traded off against actually achieving the objective, so someone has to decide how many units of impact one unit of objective achievement is worth. Third, when other agents are acting at the same time, it isn't even clear what portion of what happened is attributable to the system and therefore what its baseline comparison should be. His own side's response, as he put it, is that maybe you don't need anything like a full specification of value: maybe a highly structural quantity — roughly, don't leave the world with less future option value than you found it, or don't gain power — gets most of the way there while staying agnostic about what the option value is for, and maybe in any case the honest aspiration is a regularizer that makes bad outcomes less likely rather than a solution to the whole problem.

I told him this sounded like my day job with different nouns: a statement of purpose and need, a no-action alternative, scoping to decide which effects to study at all, a significance determination with significance criteria, a mitigation hierarchy (avoid, minimize, restore, offset), cumulative effects assessment, monitoring with adaptive management, and thresholds below which effects simply aren't significant. I said the answer to his question is obviously no — significance determination is precisely where all the discretion in my field lives, and we don't pretend it is value-free; we source it from scoping, public comment, regulatory precedent, and a legally accountable decision-maker. He said that instinct probably means I'm missing the interesting part of his problem, and I'd like to find out whether he's right before I dig in further.

Please respond as someone who has actually worked on impact measures and low-impact AI and who is willing to tell me when my vocabulary is doing violence to the problem. Before you give me the final version, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: lay out candidate mappings from my vocabulary — no-action alternative, project purpose and need, scoping, valued components, significance and significance criteria, mitigation hierarchy, offsets and no-net-loss, cumulative effects and attribution among multiple actors, monitoring and adaptive management — onto the AI-side notions (the objective, the baseline, the impact measure, the penalty and its units, the gaming failure mode), and explicitly cross out the mappings you think are misleading and say why. Then give me the translation you would actually defend, and then the residue report: which parts of the low-impact problem translate cleanly into impact-assessment terms, which translate only with distortion (and what exactly gets distorted), and which have no expression in my framework at all. I especially want your view on what the residue indicates — whether it is a defect of impact assessment, for instance that the whole practice presupposes a legal order, an affected public, and an institution whose job is to legitimate the value judgments rather than derive them, or whether it is a sign that the AI-side claim that you can buy safety here without solving value alignment was never as clean a claim as it sounds. And please say plainly if my obvious no is correct but shallow.
