I run a small reading group — me and four math grad students, all trained totally classically, picking our way through constructivism for the first time this term. I want to hand out a single page: a "which kind of constructivist are you, going by what you'll actually assert" decision tree. Not a self-report quiz, not a glossary of schools.

What I need is the tree: 6–8 branch questions, each phrased as a concrete claim you either will or won't assert or prove, with each leaf being a named position (or "unnamed, and here's why nobody lives here").

The branch questions can't be "do you accept the law of excluded middle" — that's too on the nose, everyone has heard it and will just parrot their side. Use cases nobody in my group has rehearsed: the claim that there either is or isn't a run of a hundred consecutive 7s in the decimal expansion of pi; whether every function from the unit interval to the reals is continuous; whether "it's been refuted that this search runs forever" is enough to conclude the search terminates; whether from "for each n there is an m" you get a genuine function picking the m; whether the naturals are a finished totality or an indefinitely extensible process; whether a set of reals may be defined by quantifying over all sets of reals; whether it's ok to reason classically about a system while requiring constructivity inside it.

Leaves I want reachable, roughly: Brouwerian intuitionism with choice sequences and bar induction; the Russian/recursive school where everything is an algorithm, plus the principle that gets you from not-not-halts to halts; Bishop-style constructivism; Martin-Löf type theory; predicativism in the Weyl-to-Feferman spirit; the Dummett/Prawitz meaning-theoretic route where the dispute is about what a speaker can manifest in use rather than about what exists; the ecumenical position where classical math is fine because constructive math sits inside it via some translation; and the "I dont want to revise math, I just want the witnesses" extractive/proof-mining position.

Two things I more or less made up, which I want accommodated one way or the other:

- what I call the feasibility requirement: you have to be able to get the witness in something like your lifetime, not merely in principle. I have no idea whether anyone seriously holds this. If it just collapses into strict finitism, say so, and tell me whether it deserves a branch or is a leaf nobody occupies.
- what I think of as the leaky metatheory: you demand constructivity inside the system but cheerfully prove its normalization or soundness theorem classically. Is that a seperate position, or is it just what everybody does and nobody admits — in which case it should be a footnote rather than a branch.

Please build it from the space rather than from the name list. Start from which combinations of answers are consistent at all (I've been told that full choice plus extensionality collapses into excluded middle — check me on that, since it's meant to rule out one whole region of the tree), then locate the actual positions in the regions that survive. For surviving regions nobody occupies, give a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored/boring. And tell me which of your splits are real logical fault lines and which are just accidents of how the schools happened to cluster.

Also flag any pair of leaves your questions genuinely can't pull apart. I suspect the Bishop people and the type-theory people answer several of my questions identically, and I'd rather you admit that than invent a question that secretly just asks "do you like types?".

End with a short note on how to read your own path through the tree, and which single question does the most sorting work, if any does.
