I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am writing a long feature about the family of impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the familiar trilemma according to which a risk score cannot, short of degenerate cases, be simultaneously calibrated within groups and balanced across groups with respect to both error classes. Every popular treatment I can find either restates that result in the abstract or spends its energy on who established what first. I want something else: a worked case, with numbers a motivated reader can follow, that isolates a difficulty the trilemma itself does not state.

The difficulty is this. Institutions almost never observe the outcome they care about. They observe a recorded proxy of it: arrests rather than offences, readmissions rather than clinical deteriorations, performance ratings rather than actual output, referrals rather than needs. And the recording is typically not equally sensitive across groups — the same underlying event is more likely to find its way into the record for some groups than for others. The classic impossibility results are stated relative to whichever target variable you hand them. They say nothing about the gap between the recorded target and the one that matters. I want to see, concretely, what happens when a score is asked to be fair on the recorded target and fair on the real one at the same time.
