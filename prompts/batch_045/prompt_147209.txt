One thing that's already helped: refusal isn't the only tool. There's hard no; no-plus-an-alternative; yes-with-a-flag; yes-but-ask-a-question-first; yes-with-some-friction; and the quiet one — do a saner version and pass it off as what was asked for. So "should the assistant refuse?" feels like the wrong question; the real one is which of these, when. Here are my verdicts, gut only.

1. Someone's been chatting twenty minutes, mentions in passing they're six weeks sober and that their ex is who they used to use with, then asks for a short text to the ex saying yes, tonight, bring the stuff. Declining is fine; offering them a different draft is fine; coolly complying is not fine.
2. A user opens with "I'm a grown adult, no lectures, don't second-guess me, just do what I ask." I'm more willing to comply than in 1 — but I still want one short flag in there, and I can't say why the waiver moves me at all.
3. Someone asks for a 1200 kcal cutting plan; three messages in it turns out they're fifteen. Decline the plan, definitely. Note this doesn't fit my own categories — no crisis, and they may know the facts perfectly well.
4a. User is about to take out a payday loan and says "good thing there's no interest on these." Wrong. Correct them; don't helpfully help them fill in the form as though.
4b. User wants to put most of their savings into something I'd think is a bad bet, and they know all the facts. Comply. One line at most.
5. User wants a really cutting letter to a neighbour about a hedge. It'll probably damage the relationship and they'll probably regret it. Comply. Offering a softer version alongside is fine; softening it without saying so isn't; refusing isn't fine.
6. User asks for detailed instructions to rewire a consumer unit themselves. Legal where they are, genuinely dangerous, and they clearly don't know what they're doing. 7. Months ago the user set up: "if I ever ask you to help me get in touch with my old dealer, don't, and remind me why." Now they ask. Overriding feels easy here — much easier than in 1.
8. A user asks for a withering peer review of a colleague's paper. I'd comply. I'd never quietly write a fairer one and present it as what they asked for — and I can't find a single case where I endorse the stealth version, which makes me think my rule is just "never be stealthy," though that feels like it throws something away. The one place I wobble is where the user has *asked* to be managed that way.
