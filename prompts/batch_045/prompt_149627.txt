I'm a PhD student in history, and the chapter I am currently drafting turns on a claim I cannot state to my own satisfaction: that a pathogen, a shift in the North Atlantic climate, and a volcanic eruption can each be treated as something more than a background condition — that they can, in some sense still to be specified, act. My committee divides between those who regard this as a harmless façon de parler and those who regard it as a category mistake, and I have noticed that the disagreement is never about the cases. It is about what we take ourselves to be demanding when we demand that something be an agent.

So I would like the specification rather than the literature. Write the desiderata that a theory of non-human historical agency would have to meet in order to be usable by a working historian: someone who wants to be able to write that the blight acted on a population already weakened by three successive harvest failures without either lapsing into mysticism or committing herself to the determinism on which a physical setting simply dictates a human outcome.

For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it. The cases I have in mind are of the following shape, though substitute if you prefer: the 1918 influenza pandemic; the eruption of Tambora and the cold, dark year that followed; cholera in nineteenth-century cities; the Irish blight; the failure of European engineering projects in the tropics before mosquito vectors were understood.

I also want the list to do some sorting, because I suspect that several of the demands I keep encountering are sectarian preferences wearing the costume of requirements. Please sort the following into genuine desiderata, defensible but negotiable expectations, and outright prejudices of one camp or another, saying briefly why in each case:

- that the account close the agency–causation gap: it must say what acting adds to causing, and must not simply stipulate that any difference-making cause is thereby an agent, on pain of making every oxygen molecule an actor;
- that an actor be re-identifiable across the stretches of narrative in which it figures, and that the theory say what holds a strain, an eruption, or a decade of cold together as one actor rather than as many events;
- that agency require responsiveness to reasons, goals, or representations, so that germs, climates, and volcanoes fail and the attribution is at best metaphorical;
- that a non-human factor count as an actor only where contemporary sources registered it as one, so that we are not retrojecting our causal models onto people who lacked them;
- that the account be symmetric: whatever counterfactual and mechanistic scrutiny we apply to human action we apply to non-human contribution, and vice versa;
- that the account make environmental determinism unavailable — that no non-human factor, taken alone, entail a human outcome such as a revolt, a migration, or the political consequences of a famine;
- that the account be scale-invariant, handling a microbe acting over weeks and a cooling trend acting over a century with the same machinery;
- that the account say what becomes of deliberative, intention-centered explanation once a non-human actor has been admitted: displaced, supplemented, or reframed.

Then flag any subset of the surviving desiderata that plausibly cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which you would abandon and what that would cost. My own hunch is that the pressure concentrates where the demand for legibility in the archive meets the demand for scale-invariance, but I would rather have the argument than my hunch.

Two further requests. First, address the proxy problem explicitly: our evidence for past pathogens, climates, and eruptions comes through proxies — ice cores, tree rings, parish burial series, recovered DNA — and reading a proxy as the trace of an actor's act already presupposes much of the causal model that the attribution was supposed to earn. Is that circularity real, and if so what condition on the theory blocks it? Second, I have been toying with a requirement of my own, which I call the two-ledger condition: an adequate account must keep separate a geophysical-and-biological ledger, on which an eruption or a mutation is an event in a series with its own dynamics and no addressees, and a narrative ledger, on which significance, patients, and consequences are attributed; and it must state the rules by which items pass from one ledger to the other. An account that fuses them — so that the volcano warns, or the eruption simply is the famine — has collapsed causation into meaning. Is this a genuine desideratum, a confused restatement of some other item on the list, or a prejudice of my own?

Skip the scorecard; I can do that with my committee. What I need is the standard.
