Setting aside the two standard battlegrounds in the laws-of-nature debate — whether laws govern rather than merely describe, and how a best-system account should trade simplicity against informational strength — give me one concretely described case in which a Humean and an anti-Humean would still come apart over what the laws are, or over whether the world has any laws at all.

I'd like the case to turn on a candidate law that is not a universal generalization over local matters of fact: something like a global structural constraint (a law fixing the universe's total charge, or the topology of its spacetime), a one-off prohibition on a unique unrepeatable event, or a law stated over a region or period in which nothing exists. Assume both sides agree about every particular matter of fact, so the disagreement is not empirical. Show exactly where the two accounts diverge, why that divergence is not simply the governing-versus-describing dispute or the simplicity–strength trade-off in another guise, and what the case reveals about the real fault line between the positions.
