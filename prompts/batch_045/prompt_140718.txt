I've been watching a recorded lecture series on paradoxes (aimed at a general audience, but the guy is an actual philosopher) and this is roughly how he summed up the two big theories of vagueness. I'm paraphrasing from memory so I definately won't have his exact wording, but the claims are his:

"Supervaluationism says that a vague sentence like 'Dave is bald' is true if it comes out true on every admissible way of making 'bald' precise, false if it comes out false on every admissible way, and neither true nor false when the precisifications disagree. So supervaluationists give up the law of excluded middle: for a genuine borderline case, 'Dave is bald or Dave is not bald' is not true. What they keep is the rest of classical logic, plus the idea that vagueness is a feature of our language rather than a gap in our knowledge. Epistemicism, the view associated with Timothy Williamson, says the opposite: there really is a sharp cut-off, some exact number of hairs where 'bald' stops applying, but our use of the word doesn't single out any particular cut-off, so there is no way in principle to know where it falls, and vagueness is really just ignorance. The decisive objection to epistemicism is the obvious one: if there were a sharp line, then someone who knew every relevant fact about Dave's scalp and about how English speakers use 'bald' would be able to tell whether Dave is bald, and that is absurd, so the view is refuted. Supervaluationism avoids all that, and unlike its rivals it has no trouble with higher-order vagueness either, since when it is borderline whether something is even a borderline case, that just comes out neither true nor false as well."
