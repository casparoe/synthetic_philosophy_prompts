Hi — I'm giving a ~20 minute talk in a reading group on the problem of universals and I'd love some help pressure-testing an idea. We've already done the basic realism vs. nominalism dialectic, so please don't spend time re-explaining that; assume I know the standard moves.

The idea I've been playing with — I've been calling it the feature view — goes like this. Imagine an ideal compressor: not an actual machine-learning model (please don't turn this into a discussion about neural nets or LLMs), but something like the limit of an ideal science, which gets an enormous amount of data about the world and tries to give the shortest possible description of it. On the feature view, being a property just is being one of the distinctions that the ideal compressor has to draw in its best description: the classes it has to separate in order to compress as much as possible. Properties aren't extra furniture added on top of the objects; they're the joints the ideal description has to cut at. So green, spherical, and having such-and-such mass come out as properties because the ideal description needs those distinctions, while "being either a swan or a bicycle" presumably doesn't. A friend of mine says this is just what people call the sparse view — the elite minority of natural properties that do real causal and nomological work, underwrite genuine resemblance and show up in the laws, as opposed to the abundant, gerrymandered classes — wearing an information-theoretic trenchcoat. I keep going back and forth on whether he's right.

So: compare the two properly, not as a list of bullet-pointed pros and cons. Where do they really come apart, and where are they just saying the same thing in different vocabularies? A few things I'd especially like you to dig into. (i) Grue: does an ideal description collapse onto something like the natural/sparse elite, or does the choice of coding language reintroduce Goodman-style riddles one level down, at the level of features — and which of the two views is in better shape there? (ii) The Russell-style regress: if I say a and b are both green because the ideal description assigns them the same feature, haven't I just moved the bump in the rug? Is there a real stopping point here, or does my view inherit exactly the problem the sparse view was supposed to solve? (iii) Limit cases: a property nothing instantiates (say a charge value no particle ever happens to have), and a property instantiated exactly once — does either view have a principled story about whether those exist, and why? (iv) What if the ideal description turns out to need something disjunctive-ish, a feature that's only well-defined as a disjunction of two more basic states — does how you handle that undercut whatever you said about whether my view is really just the sparse view?

Please push back hard where you think the whole thing is defintely confused or where I'm smuggling in what I need to prove; I'd much rather find that out now than in front of the group. Concrete points I can steal for the talk are worth more than a tidy overview, and I don't need any reading list or citations — just the argument.
