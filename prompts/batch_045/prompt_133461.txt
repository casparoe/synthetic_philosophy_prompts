I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of economics — fifteen students, a single ninety-minute session, one room with a whiteboard and a projector, no budget for cash payments but I can bring in six small real items (snacks and bits of stationery) to serve as stakes. The students have already worked through the standard consistency conditions on choice and the claim that an agent's choices can be represented as maximizing a utility function. What I want next week is for them to feel, before they are told, exactly which part of that claim carries empirical content and which part is bookkeeping.

Please design a runnable activity with the following arc, and nothing in the way of up-front lecturing: the rules and roles should produce the discoveries, and I would rather not name revealed preference, the consistency axioms, or the choice-anomaly literature until the debrief.

The sequence of things they should run into, in this order:

First, they make a series of real, recorded choices from varying menus drawn from the six items, with one round selected at random at the end and each student actually receiving what they chose in it. Before the choices, they privately rank the six items and privately rate how strongly they want each one, on a scale from zero to ten; those sheets stay sealed until the end.

Second, working in small groups from their own choice records, they try to write down a single utility function over the six items that makes every choice they made look like the maximization of something. Some groups should find that no such function exists, because among the menus there are pairs in which the same two options appear together, and then appear again alongside a third option that is clearly worse than one of them but not straightforwardly comparable to the other, so that the choice between the original two flips. I would like a second, independent route to the same failure built in as well, in case the first does not take.

Third, once the failure is on the board, the facilitator permits utility to depend on the menu as well as the item. Fitting now becomes trivial and instant, and I want that contrast to be the thing they notice: the relaxed claim costs nothing to satisfy and therefore says nothing.

Fourth, for the groups whose data was well behaved, the point that the numbers are not unique should come out of the comparison between groups rather than from my saying it — two groups should end up with functions that rank everything identically but disagree about how much better one item is than another, and should be brought to see that nothing in the choice records could settle the disagreement. Then the sealed intensity sheets come out, and each student checks which of the competing functions, if any, matches the strengths of desire they actually wrote down at the start.

Finally, a closing sting: the class is told that only three of the six items can be stocked from now on, and must decide which three maximize total welfare given everything they have reconstructed. The groups should reach opposite recommendations from identical information about everyone's ordering, with no available data able to adjudicate between them.

For the deliverable, please give me the six items with the attribute descriptions that make the manipulation work, the full menu schedule with timings, the record sheets, and a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes including the transitions. Then a debrief that maps each moment onto the specific philosophical point it is meant to land, ordered and phrased so I can actually run it. I also want an honest account of what will predictably go wrong: students who work out what is being manipulated and start performing consistency, students who conclude that the moral is that people are irrational, groups that fit the data by assigning utility to item-menu pairs before I have licensed it, and what I should do if the class turns out to be so consistent that no violation materializes — assume I can pilot the menu schedule on another group beforehand. Close by being explicit about which features of the topic the activity cannot stage at all: the role of risk and the reasons utility has to be cardinal rather than merely ordinal under uncertainty, the difference between preference as a cause of choice and preference as a redescription of it, and the fact that nothing learned from snacks and stationery licenses welfare claims about real goods or real policies. Say plainly which of these I will simply have to assert in the debrief.
