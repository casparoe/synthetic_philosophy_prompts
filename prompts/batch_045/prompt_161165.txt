I'm a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years of spectroscopy, using linear algebra and a bit of group theory every single day without once wondering what kind of thing they were. Now I'v gotten into philosophy in retirement (reading group, mostly other retired scientists, we argue a lot) and I'm trying to get clear on platonism vs nominalism. One thing keeps bugging me: the nominalist is always sold as the parsimonious option, the one who doesn't believe in extra spooky objects. But that seems to only count one column of the ledger, and I want a case that makes the other column visible.

So: build me a thought experiment in which the nominalist ends up with a *bigger physical* ontology than the platonist — i.e. the usual "nominalism is the austere view" direction flips. Concretely, I want the nominalist who paraphrases rather than fictionalizes — the one who tries to rewrite our successful scientific theories so they quantify only over concrete stuff, trading talk of numbers, functions and vectors for talk of spacetime regions and comparative relations between concrete things — to be forced into positing concrete structure that our best physics doesn't obviously contain.

Constraints:

- One paragraph, realistic setting. A lab, a field campaign, an instrument, a measurement programme. No sci-fi, no omniscient beings, no magic devices. It should be the sort of set-up I could actually have run into in my career.
- The paraphrase has to be a genuine attempt, not a strawman. So: give me a nearby simpler case where the paraphrase plainly *works* — a theory with only a handful of quantities, where you can swap numbers for comparative relations among concrete things and lose nothing predictive — show it working there, and only then show the hard case breaking it.
- The nominalist must not lose by stipulation. Don't just declare the paraphrase impossible. I want the blocking feature to be structural. My own guess is that its something like the theory needing more independent degrees of freedom, or finer resolution, than the concrete world can supply — a bandwidth problem, call it — but whatever it is, name it and show it doing the work.
- The platonist shouldn't win for free either. They still owe a story about how we manage to be reliable about causally inert objects, and I want you to say honestly whether this case makes that problem better or worse.

After the scenario: walk through what each view says and why, isolate which single feature of the case is doing the work, tell me what happens if you remove that feature (does it collapse back into an ordinary indispensability argument?), and then tell me how a smart nominalist would attack the case — I expect something like "that's exactly why I'm a fictionalist and not a paraphraser," or "the extra concrete structure was already implied by the physics" — and how good that escape actually is.

What it's for: I want to know whether parsimony arguments about mathematical objects should really be arguments about *total* ontology, abstract plus concrete together. So a case that just stipulates the verdicts — nominalist stuck, platonist wins — is cheating and won't help me. And please don't bother with references or reading lists; I can't look anything up and I don't care who said what first.
