I have no training in philosophy or law; I got interested in reparations for historical injustice and have been arguing it out on my own. I've ended up with a chain of reasoning that lands somewhere I can't accept, and I'd like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me.

(1) If A wrongfully harms B, A owes B repair — as close to making B whole as the case allows.
(2) That duty doesn't dissolve when A dies. Like any other unpaid debt, it survives into A's estate.
(3) Estates are inherited: heirs take the assets and the liabilities together. So a living person can owe repair for a wrong their ancestor committed.
(4) By the same logic the claim can be inherited too: a living person can be owed repair for a wrong done to their ancestor.
(5) Chattel slavery, and the seizure of land that went with it, are exactly such wrongs — vast, deliberate, never repaired.
(6) So those who now hold the inherited assets owe repair to the descendants, today.
(7) But nothing in (2)–(4) contains an expiry date. Switch the machinery on and every unrepaired wrong in the record is live: the enclosure of the commons, the dispossession of native peoples, every border ever moved by force, back as far as records reach.
(8) Now add what I'll call the Netting Rule, which seems to me to be mere arithmetic: if I owe you and you owe me, we settle the difference, not both sums in full.
(9) Given (7) and (8), everyone alive is at once debtor and creditor on an enormous scale, and the two positions run through the very same assets — the same acre of land is both the fruit of one theft and the compensation owed for another. The ledger can't be untangled, and certainly not well enough to say that any particular person owes any particular sum to any particular person.
(10) A duty whose content nobody can determine, and which therefore cannot be discharged even approximately, isn't a duty.
(11) Therefore no one owes reparations for historical injustice.

I reject (11), but (1) and (5) strike me as close to undeniable and (8) as arithmetic. My suspicion is that the trouble is in (3) — that you can inherit a duty to make someone whole — yet if I drop (3) I can't see how anyone still owes anything for slavery, which is where I began.

Which step would you give up, why does it look all right on the surface, and what does giving it up cost elsewhere in the chain? Please also state how confident you are in your diagnosis and name the single point you are least sure of. Answer in British English.
