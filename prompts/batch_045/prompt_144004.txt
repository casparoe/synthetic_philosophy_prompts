I'm a high-school senior working on an independent project in epistemology — specifically on the cluster of views about whether whether-you-know can shift with the practical situation — and my debate coach sent me a blog post that presents the whole debate as much ado about a word. I can't tell which parts of it to trust. I'm reconstructing the passage in my own words rather than reproducing it exactly, since I only really remember the gist:

"Epistemologists used to think knowledge was just a matter of your evidence. Then along came contextualism. The classic illustration is the bank case: on Friday you know the bank will be open Saturday, because you were there two weeks ago; but once the stakes go up — you'd be ruined if the cheque didn't clear today — the very same evidence no longer gets you knowledge. So contextualism holds that the truth of a claim of the form 'S knows that p' depends on how much is riding on it for the person making the claim: knowledge is cheap when little is at risk and expensive when a lot is. Pragmatic encroachment is the same thesis in different vocabulary — practical factors make a difference to knowledge — the only disagreement being that encroachment theorists locate the stakes in the subject rather than the speaker. The two views agree on what matters, though. Both say that 'knows' means something slightly different in different contexts, so there is no single fact of the matter about whether you know, and two people who disagree about a knowledge claim are not really disagreeing at all. Which is why the debate sounds deeper than it is: it's a verbal dispute, and the only real lesson is that you should gather more evidence when more is at stake — advice nobody needed a philosopher for."

My instincts say the last third of that is where the trouble is, but I don't have the vocabulary to say what, and I'd rather not just trust a blog post. So I'd be grateful for an accuracy audit: which parts a specialist would accept as stated, which are loose simplifications that a popularizer can get away with, and which misrepresent the views in a way a defender would object to. In particular I want to know whether the post is right to treat contextualism and pragmatic encroachment as one view with a labelling difference, whether the "no fact of the matter / not really disagreeing" line is something these views actually commit their holders to or something an opponent has read into them, and whether the closing moral ("it's verbal, and the lesson is just to gather more evidence") follows even from the parts of the summary that are accurate.

There is also a case my coach and I came up with that the post's framing doesn't seem to handle, and I'd like to see whether the corrected versions of the two views say anything different about it. Ana is the senior lifeguard at a municipal lake. Each morning she uses a rapid test strip for bacterial contamination, and on roughly one morning in five the strip comes back with the same faint, ambiguous reading she gets today. If she closes the beach and the water was fine, the cost is trivial — a refunded morning of swim lessons and some complaints from parents. If she leaves it open and the water really is contaminated, small children become seriously ill. So the stakes are lopsided: being wrong in one direction is nearly costless, and being wrong in the other is catastrophic. On the versions of the two views as you'd state them properly, does Ana know that the water is safe? Does it matter that the *safe* action for her is obvious and cheap either way, so that the practical question is settled while the knowledge question apparently isn't? And would the answer change if Ana only learned later that a day camp had booked the lake that morning, so that the catastrophic outcome was never something she was in a position to take into account?
