We've had this reading group going for about a year — me, a postdoc who works on moral responsibility, and a lawyer friend who mostly shows up to argue — and we keep circling the same dispute about whether a hypocrite can blame. You know the shape of it: someone who does X (or has done X and not owned up to it) blames someone else for doing X, and a lot of people want to say the blame is somehow defective in a way that isn't just "the blame is false" or "hypocrisy is itself a vice." One camp says the hypocrite lacks *standing* — the blame doesn't get off the ground, it's infelicitous or void, like a demand made by someone with no authority to make it. Another camp says no, the blame is in perfectly good order, it's just that the hypocrite's blaming is an additional bad thing they're doing (unfair, self-serving, bullying), so we have two separate evaluations and it's confusing to mush them into one status called "standing." And then there are the skeptics who think there is no distinct phenomenon here at all: every apparent standing failure is really just one of the things we already have names for — no violation occurred, or the blaming is itself wrong, or the blamer is in no position to know — and "standing" is a label we invented to make a grab-bag look like a unified category. We've read the stuff on both sides, everyone can recite everyone else's moves, and nobody has moved an inch in twelve months.

My working theory is that the debate is at least half verbal, and I want you to pressure-test it. Here's how I'm inclined to carve it: when people say "standing," they might mean (a) something like a normative power or speech-act felicity condition — the hypocrite can't coherently address a demand to you, because the demand implicitly commits them to a standard they don't accept, or because they've forfeited the right to be in that conversational role; (b) a verdict about the act of blaming itself, i.e. it's wrong of them to blame, which is a substantive moral claim and not a precondition on blaming at all; (c) an epistemic or relational condition — you're not in a position to know, or you're not in the right relationship with this person, which is why the busybody stranger and the accomplice feel different from each other even though neither is exactly a hypocrite; or (d) really a claim about blame's success conditions in some deeper sense, i.e. about what blame *is*. I also tried out two test cases on the group that seemed to break the tie in a way I didn't expect. First, the private-blame test: imagine the hypocrite never expresses anything, just inwardly resents and withdraws goodwill — no address, no demand, no audience. Second, the paid-up hypocrite: someone who did the same thing, was caught, took their punishment, made amends, and has already blamed themselves at length, and now blames someone else for it. My intuition in the second case is that the objection nearly vanishes, and in the first case it doesn't vanish at all — which makes me think the "hypocrite can't coherently make a demand" story can't be the whole thing, and that what people were really tracking all along is unfair advantage or abuse of a power differential rather than anything about blame's felicity conditions.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it verbal in the way I've guessed — several different questions under one word — or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement underneath, say about whether blame is essentially a form of address to its target (the old Strawsonian reactive-attitudes picture) versus essentially an internal attitude that can be assessed for fittingness whether or not it's expressed? Is some hidden empirical question doing the work, i.e. a question about what ordinary people's intuitions actually are across these cases, which could in principle be settled by survey work rather than armchair? Or is it bedrock — people just differing on an intuition none of them can argue for? Support the diagnosis with what each position actually claims rather than with a survey of who holds which view (I've read enough of the literature; I don't need more of it summarized back at me). And then the part I actually care about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Is there a distinction to draw, a case to run, a piece of empirical work, or a reframing of the question that would move it — or should we expect no progress because the question as posed is ill-formed, and if so, what should we ask instead?
