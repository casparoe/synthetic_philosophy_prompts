Been reading way too many LW/ACX dev-econ threads where "corruption" is basically a conversation-stopper: one camp is working from the standard abuse-of-entrusted-power-for-private-gain definition that aid agencies use, the other thinks the whole category is a Western export that pathologises gift-giving and patron-client reciprocity in places where the state was never going to deliver impartially anyway. Draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration — a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table.

For agreed claims I want at least these two nailed down properly: (1) that the label gets applied asymmetrically — American-style campaign contributions, bundled fundraising and the revolving door are functionally close relatives of the village-level clientelist exchanges that get called corruption, and which of them is criminal tracks who writes the law; (2) the second-best problem — imagine a rural clinic where salaries haven't been paid in months, there's no triage rule, the queue is effectively arbitrary, and if you don't slip the nurse an envelope the slot just goes to whoever does, so the exchange may beat the actual alternative available for everyone involved: a definition built on rule-violation and one built on welfare-loss come apart here, and both camps should say which one they're using and why.

In the table, give each residual disagreement twice: once in wording the universalist would accept for their own view, once in wording the relativist would, and mark explicitly which rows are really disputes about the word rather than about anything substantive. Then list the cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — with one concrete case both would accept as bearing on each, and each side's prediction written down in advance. My guesses at the cruxes, use better ones if you've got them: whether the counterfactual baseline for judging an exchange is impartial administration or the alternative actually available; whether entrustment can do any universal work when what an office is entrusted with varies so much across places; and whether the slow institutional-erosion effect (every small payment individually harmless, all of them together entrenching the norm that you pay) is the load-bearing part of the objection or a post-hoc rationalisation. Two constraints: invent your cases and spell out their institutional details — nothing that turns on facts about a real country — and don't let the relativist resolve anything with "it depends on the culture."
