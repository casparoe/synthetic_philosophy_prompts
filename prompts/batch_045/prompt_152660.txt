Our reading group is slowly working its way through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and one member, Ravi, keeps coming back to the same analogy to settle every argument, and I cant tell whether it's actually doing any work or whether it just sounds good.

The analogy as he uses it: a mind is to a formal system as self-awareness is to a Gödel sentence. The story is something like: Gödel showed that a system rich enough to do arithmetic can encode claims about its own sentences, so it can build a sentence that in effect says of itself that it cannot be proved. From outside we can see that sentence is true; the system itself can't prove it. Ravi says a brain is the same shape of thing: it builds a symbol standing for itself, that symbol is part of the very system it's about, and the loop through it just is what consciousness is. He then uses this for two different conclusions and I'm not sure they pull the same way: (a) there's no leftover mystery — consciousness is what you get when a system's model of itself gets tangled up with the system, so a machine running the right kind of self-model would be conscious, mechanism preserved, nothing spooky; and (b) there's a principled ceiling — no such system, human or artificial, can ever have a complete understanding of itself, which is supposed to explain why introspection bottoms out and why you can never fully know your own mind.

Marta pushed back in a way I found more interesting than my own objection. She said the Gödel sentence is the wrong formal object entirely: its self-reference is a trick for manufacturing a claim about provability, and nothing about that has anything to do with being aware of yourself. She says the right analogue is a system that contains a reproduction of itself — a program that prints its own source, or a machine running a simulation of itself where the simulation feeds into the machine's own next state — and that the second kind of thing, not the first, is what Hofstadter is really leaning on. She also says the switch matters: a program printing its own source hits no incompleteness whatsoever, so (b) collapses if you substitute her object for Ravi's, while (a) might survive.

My own worry, which nobody fully answered, was that a real brain and a real computer are finite, and Gödel's theorem in the strict sense is about idealized systems with unbounded arithmetic; a finite machine's whole input-output behavior fits in a lookup table, so it's unclear the incompleteness result even gets a grip on the thing we're supposed to be analogizing.

What I'd like: lay out the correspondence explicitly for Ravi's version — what plays the role of the formal system, the provability predicate, the Gödel numbering, the outside perspective, the unprovable-but-true sentence, on the mind side. Then which of those mappings actually carry weight and which are just decoration. Then the disanalogies that bite, sorted by which conclusion they bite: which ones undermine (a), which undermine (b), and which don't matter for either. Is Marta right that swapping in a self-reproducing or self-simulating system changes which conclusions survive? And if both analogies are bad, is there a third comparison that carries whatever true thing Ravi is reaching for without carrying the rest?
