I keep running into what looks like the same small argument in unrelated corners of philosophy, and I have not been able to find out whether the argument itself has a name, as opposed to its best-known application. I am hoping someone can identify the pattern, or tell me that it has no established name and help me define it properly.

The first place I met it was a debate about whether truth ever outruns what can be established. Someone who wanted to deny that it does offered a concession that sounded modest: nothing in the relevant domain is of a kind that could not in principle be got at, so for anything true in the domain it is possible that someone should come to know it. The reply was: suppose some truth in the domain is not in fact known. Then the compound claim — that it is true and that it is not known — is itself true; but that compound cannot be known, because knowing it would involve knowing that it is not known, which is impossible if knowing something guarantees that it is so. So the concession, plus the existence of any unknown truth in the domain, is inconsistent: the modest principle turns out to entail that there are no unknown truths there at all.

The second place was a paper on administrative data. The author claimed that every fact about a population is in principle recordable, and a commentator derived that every such fact is recorded. The derivation had exactly the same shape, with "recorded in the archive" in place of "known," on the assumptions that a record of a compound fact is a record of both of its parts and that only true things get recorded.

The third place is really my own attempt. I tried the same move on a principle about justified belief rather than knowledge — every truth is such that someone could in principle be justified in believing it — and the derivation stalls, because being justified in believing something does not guarantee that it is true. That made me suspect that two features are load-bearing and that one of them is the guarantee. But I cannot get clear on whether those two are all that is needed, or on what else the argument quietly presupposes.

So, four things I would like.

One: does this pattern have a name of its own, or is it only ever named by its applications? I have the impression that the best-known label for it is tied to the first debate I mentioned, which would make it narrower than the thing I keep meeting. I also have a vague memory of a single word — something like "blind spot" — being used for the peculiar propositions the construction produces, but not for the argument that uses them, and I cannot place it. If there is a canonical name for the schema, does it come with a canonical statement of the minimal conditions on the operator?

Two: how far does it generalize? I cannot settle three variants. (a) Relativize it to knowers: "for every truth there is someone who could know it," or the stronger "for every truth, anybody could know it." (b) Recast it temporally: "every truth is known at some time," or "could be known at some future time." (c) Weaken the target: "every truth is such that it could in principle be settled — someone could know it, or someone could know that it is false." Do all three still collapse, and if they collapse to different conclusions, what does the difference tell us about what is doing the work?

Three: what is this pattern not? I would like it distinguished from (i) an ordinary modal collapse, of the sort where the mere possibility of a necessity yields the necessity outright; (ii) self-referential or diagonal arguments of the liar variety; (iii) limitative results such as the incompleteness of any sufficiently strong formal system; and (iv) the observation that it is odd to say, of some claim, that it is true and that one does not know it. My sense is that it sits nearest to the first and the second but is neither, and I want the difference stated exactly rather than gestured at.

Four: the fixes. I have seen it proposed that the principle should simply not be allowed to apply to propositions that embed the operator inside themselves — that the quantifier should range over a narrower class. Is that kind of restriction regarded as principled or as ad hoc, does it have a name, and is it the same manoeuvre as refusing to allow a definition to quantify over a totality to which the thing defined belongs?

I should say plainly that I am not after a label. I want to know what the phenomenon is, what its minimal ingredients are, where its edges lie, which of the neighbouring things it is and is not, and — if nothing established fits — a name and a definition I could use in print, with the boundary cases specified.
