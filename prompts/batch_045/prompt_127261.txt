QUESTION

(a) What is a transcendental argument, and how does it differ from an ordinary empirical argument for the same conclusion? (8 marks)

(b) A philosopher offers the following as a transcendental argument for the reality of the past, built on the phenomenon of surprise.

(1) We are sometimes surprised: that is, we sometimes undergo an experience that presents itself to us as the frustration of an expectation.
(2) An expectation can be frustrated only if the subject formed a judgement at some earlier time about how things would later be, and retained that judgement up to the moment of surprise.
(3) Forming a judgement at one time and retaining it to another is possible only if the subject who forms it exists at more than one time.
(4) Therefore there is a past: the world did not come into existence a moment ago, complete with the appearance of age.

Assess this argument in the light of Barry Stroud's well-known objection to transcendental arguments. Does the argument's defender have a satisfactory reply? (12 marks)

STUDENT'S ANSWER

A transcendental argument starts from something nobody can seriously deny — usually something about our own mental life, like that we have experiences or make judgements — and argues that some further claim is a necessary condition of the possibility of that starting point. Because the starting point is granted by everyone, the conclusion follows whether or not we have any evidence for it. The point of doing this is dialectical: a skeptic about the external world, or about the past, will not accept an ordinary empirical argument, since any such argument would have to rely on perception, memory or induction, and those are exactly what is in question. A transcendental argument is supposed to avoid that problem, because it leans on nothing empirical; the necessity involved is a matter of what can be thought or experienced at all, not a matter of causes or statistics.

In the surprise argument, the starting point (1) is meant to be undeniable: even a skeptic about the past cannot deny that it at least seems to us as if we are surprised, and seemings are infallible. The conclusion (4) is then supposed to be extracted from that.

Barry Stroud's objection, as I understand it, is that arguments of this shape are circular. The transcendental arguer needs a starting point the skeptic will accept, but the only starting points the skeptic accepts are weak ones — reports of how things seem — and those are not strong enough to entail anything about how things really are. So the skeptic about the past will simply refuse to grant (1), or will reinterpret it as a claim about mere appearances, and the argument never gets off the ground. That, on Stroud's view, is why transcendental arguments cannot refute skepticism: they have to begin somewhere the skeptic does not occupy.

There is a reply available, though. The defender should say that premise (3) is not an empirical premise at all but analytic: it is part of what we mean by retaining a judgement that the subject who judges persists, and a judgement can only be retained across time by a causal process going on in a persisting thinker. Granted that, (4) follows from (1) by conceptual analysis alone, and no verificationist premise — no assumption that only what we can check is true — is needed at any point, so Stroud's worry never arises. The argument therefore shows that the past is real, and not merely that we cannot help believing in it.

One weakness the defender should concede: (4) says more than (2) and (3) strictly give. The most they yield is that the subject of surprise exists at more than one time; that the world itself is older than a moment is a further step, and someone could accept the persistence of the subject while denying that. But this needs only a small repair, since the skeptic's alternative — a world that came into existence a moment ago with all its apparent traces already in place — is not a hypothesis anyone can seriously believe anyway.
