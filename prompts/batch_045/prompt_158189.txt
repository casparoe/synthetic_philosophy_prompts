I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral status: eighteen students, one ninety-minute meeting, and I want them to arrive having read nothing specific for the session. I need a single in-class activity — index cards, a whiteboard, no money, no devices — that makes them feel the pull of a graded view of moral status and then feel how unstable that view becomes in the hand, without my naming the view first or lecturing at any point before the debrief.

The thing I want them to run into, not to be told: between a thermostat and an adult human there is a long series of cases; nearly everyone will order that series the same way; but the moment they are asked for magnitudes — how many times over a dog matters compared with a carp — the numbers come apart, and much of what moves them turns out to be the vocabulary in which the case was described rather than anything about the case. Then, when they are asked where along the series a difference becomes large enough to justify treating the two differently, thresholds reappear inside the supposedly graded scale, and reappear at places that contradict the ratios they have just assigned themselves.

The design I have in mind, which you should improve on or replace: construct two decks describing the same eighteen systems in the same order, one in a strictly mechanistic and functional register, one in an agentive and experiential register, with no card in either deck permitted to use the words sentience, consciousness, pain, or suffering. Give one deck to each half of the room. Each group receives a fixed budget of one thousand units of moral weight to distribute over its eighteen cards, must spend it exactly, may assign zero, and is told that the ratios are meant to be meaningful — a card at one hundred matters ten times as much as a card at ten. They then tape their cards to the board in descending order.

The range should run from a bimetallic thermostat to an adult human, and I want at least the following in both decks: a chess-playing program; a general-purpose language model; a nematode; a housefly; a honeybee; a colony of roughly forty thousand honeybees; a lobster; a carp; a crow; a dog; a chimpanzee; a human fetus at twenty weeks; an adult human in deep dreamless sleep; an adult human under general anaesthesia; a patient in a minimally conscious state; a cerebral organoid; and one system whose internal organization is stipulated to be unknown to everyone in the room but which reliably produces flexible, apparently goal-directed behaviour.

What I mostly want your help with is the machinery that turns those allocations into a discovery. Please build in: a description audit, in which any card whose two numbers differ by a factor of ten or more must be defended in sixty seconds and the room judges whether the defence appealed to the system or to the wording; a ratio-versus-threshold audit, in which each group marks every adjacent pair on its own board as differing enough or not enough to justify different treatment, and then confronts where those verdicts contradict the ratios implied by its own numbers; an aggregation audit, comparing what the group gave the colony with what it gave the single bee; and a certainty audit, in which the group reassigns a number to the stipulated-to-be-opaque system under the fiction that it is certainly not conscious at all, so that the gap between the two numbers exposes how much of the original was really uncertainty wearing the costume of low status.

Give me the rules and materials, a one-page instruction sheet participants can follow with no verbal explanation from me, a minute-by-minute timeline through the reveal of the two decks, a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific claims about gradations of moral status, an honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and a frank statement of which feature of the graded view the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself. Above all, they should leave able to say why robust agreement about order can coexist with utter confusion about magnitude, and why a single number silently merges a judgment about how much a system matters with a judgment about how confident we are.
