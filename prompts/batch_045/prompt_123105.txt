I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of time — four of us, two philosophy grad students, a cosmology PhD student who happens to be my housemate, and me (I did a joint degree in physics and philosophy and remember embarassingly little of the physics). We got stuck on the direction of time last week, and my housemate kept ending the discussion with 'the arrow of time is just entropy,' or, when he was feeling folksy, 'you cant unscramble an egg.' I pushed back and said that the second law only says entropy increases toward the future, which seems to presuppose a direction of time rather than explain it, and he said that was exactly the confusion the slogan was meant to dissolve — which I dont think it was, or at least not obviously. Three places it was doing work, so you can see what I need out of it: he used it to say that the genuinely puzzling thing isn't that entropy goes up but that the early universe was in such a low-entropy state, and I want to know whether that claim is built into the slogan or is a separate thesis that just travels around with it; he used it to shut down my question about whether the future is 'open' in a way the past isn't, since I'd said that we deliberate about the future and not about the past and that this asymmetry looks like more than a fact about gases, and he said no, it's records all the way down; and he used it to suggest that a region where entropy decreased wouldn't merely be wildly improbable but would be somehow impossible or incoherent, which I'm fairly sure is wrong and can't say why.

What I actually want is a menu of the precise claims that sentence could be making, organised along two axes. First: what 'the arrow of time' is being identified with — the entropy gradient itself, the low-entropy boundary condition at one end of time, or the direction in which entropy happens to increase. Second: what is supposed to reduce to, or be explained by, that thing — our having records of the past but not of the future, the causal asymmetry, the asymmetry of counterfactual dependence (we take the future to hang on what we do now and the past not to), the openness of the future in deliberation, the felt passage or 'flow' of time, or the bare distinction between past and future itself. For each combination that yields a real thesis, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and tell me which one my housemate was probably helping himself to in each of the three uses. Two things I want folded into the menu rather than bolted on. One is a test case I invented and can't find discussed anywhere: picture a universe in which entropy is at its minimum at some middle time and increases toward both temporal ends, with observers on each side who from the inside seem to remember the lower-entropy direction as their past — for each reading, say whether that universe contains two arrows of time, one, or none, and whether the two families of observers could in principle disagree about which way time points, and note anywhere the test collapses two readings into one or exposes one as not really about entropy at all. The other is a question to ask of each reading: is the entropy claim supposed to be merely statistical or nomological? I suspect his third use is quietly trading on the second while the slogan only licenses the first.

Write it like an email you're sending me back: continuous prose, paragraph after paragraph, no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold, no tables. I'd rather have eight messy paragraphs I can argue with than a tidy handout. And please don't just tell me which reading is the standard one and explain that — I want the full menu with verdicts, including the readings nobody would actually defend, flagged as such, and then a plain closing statement of which readings, if any, would really let him close all three arguments the way he tried to, and which of them don't touch the argument he was making at all.
