Last night a friend and I spent the better part of three hours arguing about the two inversions — Darwin's and Turing's — and we stopped without either of us giving ground. I would like a poem that does something with the stalemate rather than merely about it.

Her claim was that the two are not the same move. Darwin's, she said, is a claim about history: the designed look of a living thing — the eye, with its lens and its focusing adjustments, all of it for seeing — is explained by a cumulative, algorithmic process with no foresight and no designer in it, so that a property which seemed as though it had to be there at the beginning turns out to be an accumulated late product. Turing's, she said, is a claim about constitution: a performance that seems to demand understanding — playing a strong move, getting the sums right — is analysed into the organised interaction of parts none of which understands anything at all, so that understanding itself becomes a competence assembled out of non-understanding, what the literature calls competence without comprehension. Take away the designer's foresight, she said, and nothing present-tense is lost; take away the understanding and you have subtracted something that was actually going on inside the performance, at the moment it happened. My claim was that this is one inversion in two time-frames: in both cases a property that looked as if it had to sit at the ground floor is manufactured out of the organised doings of things that lack it, and the only difference is whether the manufacture is spread over geological time or assembled in the working of a machine right now.

The argument then turned on a test I proposed and could not defend to her satisfaction. I said an inversion earns its keep only when you can actually write the procedure down and show that executing it yields the competence — otherwise the appeal to an algorithmic process is just a stylish way of declining to explain. She said a rule does not apply itself: something has to count as following it correctly, and pushing the mystery back to the rule-follower is not dissolving it. That is where we left it, and it is why I want a sestina.

What I am asking for: a sestina in the strict form — six stanzas of six lines with the standard teleuton rotation, then a three-line envoi, thirty-nine lines — using these six end-words: eye, blind, purpose, machine, meaning, rule. Three belong to the Darwinian register and three to the computational one, and the rotation should keep mixing them, so that by the middle stanzas purpose has turned up as the last word of a line about machines and machine has ended a line about eyes, and by the end no word can be said to belong to its own side.

The form has to do the arguing, not the wording. First: the rotation itself is the thesis in miniature — a permutation rule that nobody has to understand, followed line by line, depositing an order so intricate that it looks aimed, with every word visiting every line-position exactly once so that none of them is more fundamental than the others. The reader should feel this without being told it. Second: because meaning and rule trade places across the stanzas, the reader should be unable to say which is downstream of which, which is exactly the point I could not get my friend to concede. Third: the envoi is the turn. All six words crowd into three lines there, and that is where her objection must get its due — the rule does not follow itself, and a poet who understood something applied it — without the poem either refuting her or caving in. End with the disagreement intact but sharper than it was at the start.

Two accuracy conditions, since I intend to read this aloud to close the last session of a reading group of about eight people, most of whom have not read any of this literature. Do not let the poem say that the design is unreal: eyes really do see, and the adaptation really is for seeing; only the foresight is dispensable. Do not let it say that the competence is unreal either: the move really is a good move and the sum really is correct; only the comprehension in the parts is dispensable. And no teleological language about the processes themselves — selection does not strive or aim or try, and there must be no moment where the machine wakes up, or the eye decides, or either inversion turns out to have been a trick. The poem should also stand on its own for a listener who has never heard the argument and is not being handed a key: no epigraph, no note explaining the form, no naming of the form inside the poem, and no sneering at either position.
