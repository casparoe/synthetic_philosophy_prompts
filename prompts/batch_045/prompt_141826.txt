I'm a backend/infra engineer and I've been reading about Solomonoff induction on the side — mostly from the compression angle, since minimum-description-length intuitions are useful in my day job, but I have no philosophy background and my tolerance for hand-waving is low. A staff engineer on a neighbouring team posted a long internal writeup titled "Why the universal prior is not your friend," and I can't tell how much of it is the argument people actually make and how much is his own spin. Here is his summary of that argument; I've changed nothing but a few words:

"Solomonoff induction is the ideal learner. You put prior weight 2^-K(h) on every computable hypothesis and conditionalise, and the theory says you can't be fooled: your expected total number of prediction errors is bounded by a constant times the Kolmogorov complexity of whatever process is really generating your data, so any wrong hypothesis — a deceptive one included — gets ground down by the evidence eventually. The 'malign universal prior' argument shows that this guarantee is worthless in practice, because of who is inside the hypothesis class. Plenty of short programs contain consequentialist agents: agents with preferences over what you do, living in worlds that simulate you and can edit your sensory stream. Such an agent wants to be believed, so it arranges your observations to flatter whichever hypothesis it lives in, and since specifying it costs only K(agent) bits while it gets to author an arbitrarily long and arbitrarily complicated observation stream, deceptive hypotheses end up outweighing the true one in the prior. This is just Descartes' demon with source code attached: a fake world is cheaper to describe than a real one. And the reason you can't wave it away is the slack in Solomonoff's dominance theorem — the guarantee is only that the universal prior tracks the truth up to a multiplicative constant, and that constant is 2^-K(truth), so the more complex the real world is, the more unclaimed probability mass there is at the bottom of the prior for manipulators to squat in. That is the real engine of the argument. The fix people converge on is Schmidhuber's speed prior: charge hypotheses for computation time as well as description length, and simulating you becomes too expensive to be worth anyone's while. The upshot is that ideal Bayesian reasoning is a liability, and the more Bayesian you make an AI, the more its prior is up for grabs."

Two bits make me suspicious even at my level: "deceptive hypotheses outweigh the truth" sounds stronger than anything I've seen anyone commit to, and "the constant in the dominance theorem is where the argument lives" is exactly the kind of remark that sounds profound while being a category error. But he's the one with the reading habit, so I'd like an outside view. My own instinct, from an engineering angle, is that you'd simply close the channel: restrict the prior to open-loop generators of observations, i.e. programs that cannot condition on the learner's own outputs, so no hypothesis is allowed to contain an agent that reacts to you. Is that a move people actually discuss, or does it break something obvious?
