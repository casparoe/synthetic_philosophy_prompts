I am a journalist working on a reported feature about how the unilateralist's curse has migrated out of existential-risk circles and into the ordinary vocabulary of institutional review: grant panels, biosafety committees, model-deployment sign-offs, pandemic response. Alongside the straight reporting I want to run a piece of deadpan satire, and I am commissioning it from you.

The piece: the annual internal compliance bulletin of a government body charged with licensing any action whose downside distribution includes a low-probability, civilization-scale loss. Invent the body, its acronym, its chair, its forms, its committees. Format it as a numbered or lettered internal report of roughly 700 to 900 words, with a memo header, and let it move through the usual sections: a message from the chair, statistics on applications received and actions deferred, two worked examples, guidance for staff, and a closing note on the board's own status.

The mechanism I want the humor built on, which I take to be the real one: many agents each face a choice that looks net-positive from their own desk, because each puts a small probability on a catastrophic downside; but the probability that at least one agent acts catastrophically compounds with the number of agents, while the upside does not compound in the same way. So actions that look fine one at a time look bad in aggregate, and the standard remedy is conformity: defer to the group, require broad agreement, and preserve the option to act later once uncertainty resolves.

Please land these three second-order complications, which are the parts I care most about and where I want the writing genuinely in the weeds rather than generic:

1. The abstention externality. A refusal to act usually does not cancel the action; it reallocates it, to a rival lab, a less scrupulous jurisdiction, an unlicensed contractor. So a board that maximizes its own internal conformity can raise total risk. Have the document notice this, and have it respond the way a bureaucracy would, with a new form or a new subcommittee that compounds the paralysis.

2. The composition problem applied to the board itself. The board's own licensing decisions are unilateral acts relative to every other board, agency, and oversight body, so the board must license its own licenses. Let this regress surface somewhere concrete: an org chart, a footnote, a delegated-authority appendix, the memo's footer.

3. The blame asymmetry. The agent who acts and turns out to be wrong owns the catastrophe; the agents who abstained from preventing it own nothing, and are in fact the people who write the after-action report. Career incentives therefore point uniformly toward abstention even in cases where every member privately agrees the action is net-positive. Give this its own named doctrine in the document's vocabulary.

Two further things I would like worked in: the board's staffing expansion should increase its own measured risk, since adding reviewers adds independent chances that someone is catastrophically wrong, and the document should present this as a budget problem rather than a reductio; and the deferred-action backlog should have grown so large that the median deferral now exceeds the interval between the crises the deferrals were meant to guard against.

Constraints: deadpan register throughout, no winking and no editorial asides, so that a reader who does not know the literature can still tell what is being ironized and a reader who does recognizes that the target has been understood fairly. No named real people, living or dead, and no real institutions; do not quote, cite, or allude to any actual publication. Avoid AI-lab inside jokes and generic tech-company satire; the comedy should come from decision theory and from institutional incentives. The ending should be a reductio rather than a punchline: let the machinery arrive at a state where abstention is the only licensed act and abstention itself requires a license, and let the bulletin report that as a performance metric.

Please write it in American English.
