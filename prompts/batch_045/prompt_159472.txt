I help run a small heritage-and-education commitee — plaques, anniversary programming, whether to keep funding a local archive — and one member has got hold of Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life and now brings it up in every meeting. I've finally read it and I want to bring something to the next meeting that's funny but gets the taxonomy right, because if I get it wrong he'll be insufferable for a year. Can you write it for me?

What I want is a deadpan bureaucratic document: an internal guidance circular or "consultation draft" from an invented body — the Department of Historical Hygiene, the Office for Historical Dosage, whatever you like — that has been tasked with rationing citizens' intake of the past so that history serves life rather than the other way round. About 700–800 words, short numbered clauses or subsections, memo headings, completely sincere civil-service prose. The rule is that every regulation your Department lays down has to be an exaggeration of something Nietzsche actually says, not an invented vice: monumental history as the dose that has to keep persuading the living that the great dead are repeatable and imitable, and that quietly lets the dead crowd out the living; antiquarian history as the indiscriminate reverence that ends up honouring anything merely old, down to bus timetables, because it can't tell what deserves preservation; critical history as the licence to put the past on trial, together with the awkwardness that what you condemn is what you descend from, so the trial can't be a clean amputation; plus the bits about the animal that forgets and is happy, the horizon a culture needs to draw around itself, "plastic power" as the capacity that decides how much past a person or a people can absorb without going under, the historical fever of the modern person dragging around more knowledge than can be digested, and the contrast between the unhistorical and the suprahistorical. Don't have the narrator explain any of this, and don't let the document wink — it should be legible what's being sent up without anyone saying so. It can go Swiftian in one place: let the Department follow its own logic to a proposal a reader recoils from, compulsory forgetting or licensed ancestor-renunciation or whatever. Include one worked case (a citizen or institution whose file is assessed and given a prescription) and at least one objection quoted from a spokesperson for an invented professional body — the Association for Disinterested Historical Research, say — and make that objection more than a strawman: give them one complaint the Department genuinely can't answer, since the joke is that they are exactly the target of the essay.

Keep it fair enough that someone who knows the essay finds the satire accurate rather than a cariculture, and keep it readable for my committee, who mostly haven't read it. Afterwards, in your own voice, add a 100–150 word note: where the piece exaggerates, where it leans on a reading of Nietzsche that's contested (e.g. whether the three modes are meant to be held in balance, whether critical history is meant to be corrosive by design, whether "life" is really doing the work of a criterion), and if you think the premise of the satire misreads him, say that in the note rather than quietly fixing it inside the piece. Finish with one last line in exactly this form: \answer{one sentence naming the liberty you took that a Nietzsche specialist would object to most}.
