You are a moral philosopher who has spent fifteen years teaching eighteenth-century moral psychology, and who has taught Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator more times than you can count. You have a well-developed ear for the difference between a student who has grasped a distinction and one who has merely learned to repeat its name. Take your time with this. Before you write anything like a plan, spend a paragraph listing the three or four things that most reliably go wrong when this material is taught to people who have heard of Adam Smith but never read him, and then set aside the first ordering that occurs to you if it turns out to be the obvious one. Only then give me the plan.

The setting: I am a PhD student in economics, and I have been asked to give a single seventy-five-minute guest session in a second-year business-ethics course. The students are quantitatively trained, have never taken a philosophy course, and have absorbed exactly one thing about Adam Smith, namely that self-interest is what makes markets work. There is no assigned reading; whatever they know at the end they will have got from the session. I want them to be able to use the distinction afterwards, not just admire it. No handouts, no projected passages, no suggested further reading: the board and discussion only.

What I need is not an explanation of Smith's view but a plan for explaining it, and in particular:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what depends on what. My own instinct is that the mechanism of sympathy has to be in place before the internal spectator appears, because introduced the other way round the internal spectator is just a conscience in a wig; but I also suspect that some sense of why anyone cares what an imaginary onlooker thinks has to come earlier than that, or the whole apparatus floats free of motivation. Tell me where I am wrong about the dependencies, including the dependencies I have not thought of at all.

2. One carrying example: the single case I put on the board and keep returning to, together with an account of what such a case must contain and what it must lack. I want a case in which we feel with a person's passion and still judge it unfitting, or withhold fellow-feeling from a passion we perfectly understand — I am genuinely unsure which does more work, so choose and say why. The case has to prise apart the mechanism of fellow-feeling from approval, and it has to keep separate the fitness of a passion to its occasion from the question of what its object deserves. Say explicitly why the natural-looking candidates fail: the stranger in obvious distress, the heroic rescuer, the example everyone in the room will already agree about.

3. A map of the misconceptions, with more entries than the three I can already predict, ranked by how much damage each does to what comes later, and with the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused. The three I predict are: that Smith's sympathy means something close to pity or benevolence, so that the theory sounds like a sentimental variant of altruism; that impartial in impartial spectator carries the impartiality of modern moral theory, i.e. equal weight on everyone's interests; and that the internal spectator is simply whichever audience one happens to face, internalized, so that its verdict is a poll. For at least one of your entries, I also want the opposite advice: a misconception that should be allowed to stand for a few minutes because it is serviceable scaffolding, and the moment at which it must then be removed.

4. A closing case that is not Smith's and could not have been his: a public pile-on in which most of the hostile audience is not real — automated accounts, coordinated amplification — and in which the target cannot stop picturing an audience that is largely imagined. I want the ordering in (1) built so that this case is answerable by the end of the session, and I want you to state what has to have been established earlier for students to see why a million hostile spectators have no more authority over the internal one than a single well-informed one, and why an audience of bots has none at all. If the version of the account you have just had me teach would leave that case unanswerable, say so plainly and revise the ordering.

5. The check: one question or short task at the end that tells me whether a student has the distinction or only the vocabulary, and that cannot be passed by writing the phrase impartial spectator on a page.

6. What I should decline to get into at this level, and the sentence I should use when a student asks about it anyway.
