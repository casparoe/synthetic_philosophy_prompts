I host a long-form interview series on language and machine intelligence, and my next guest is a philosopher of language who defends what she calls recognition-first Griceanism: the view that the load-bearing clause in the classic Gricean analysis of speaker meaning — that meaning something involves producing an utterance so that the audience recognizes one's intention to affect them — is a condition on the output and on its interpreters rather than on any robust inner mental state, so that a system tuned by outcome-based feedback from human raters can satisfy the analysis without having beliefs or desires. Draft me eight to ten questions for her, ordered so that each closes off the move she is most likely to make, each paired with a follow-up keyed to that likely answer; keep them phrased for an intelligent general reader, with no biographical warm-ups.

I want at least one question on each of the following:

1. Whether the effect-on-the-audience clause can be read as reliable uptake without collapsing meaning into the mere causation of belief — the distinction is meant to exclude cases like informing someone of a death by arranging for a severed head to be delivered to them. Press her on whether her reading survives that exclusion, and follow up on what she will surely say, that the recognition clause does the excluding.
2. Whether a self-reflexive intention of the kind the analysis requires — one whose fulfillment depends on the audience recognizing that very intention — can be attributed to a system with no model of its audience, and whether the apparent circularity of spelling out an intention in terms of content is a real problem for her version or only for the original.
3. Whether the overtness requirement Grice added later, after critics argued that intending to produce a belief was not by itself enough, is met when the pressure toward interpretability arrives from raters during training rather than from the utterance itself.
4. Whether implicature survives the standard diagnostics, especially cancellability: if a system's output naturally carries a scalar implicature, does the system itself respect cancelling it, and if not, has implicature become a property of hearers alone, so that her position collapses into saying that meaning is whatever competent interpreters recover?

Add one question about the cases where the literal content is not what is being got at — irony, metaphor, fiction — and about whether producing those forms counts as meaning something non-literally or only as simulating the output of someone who does. Add one on whether her account entails that a confidently false machine assertion cannot be a lie but is instead closer to bullshit, given that lying on the standard picture requires asserting what you take to be false. Close with a single question about what finding would lead her to withdraw the attribution of speaker meaning, for instance a system optimized purely for next-token prediction, with nothing in training rewarding interpretability, whose output audiences read exactly as they read that of an audience-tuned one.
