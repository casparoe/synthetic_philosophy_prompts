Two things came up in section that I keep chewing on. My TA called it the "species-less organism problem" but I dont know if that's a real name for it or he made it up. Second: if species are lineage segments with beginnings and ends, what do you say about the founding members of a new lineage — do they belong to the parent species or the new one, and doesn't that make their membership depend on what happens *after* they're born, i.e. on whether their descendants happen to succeed? My roommate, who's a biochem major, says all of this is bullshit because you can just sequence the genome and be done with it, which feels like it's missing the point but I can't articulate why.

So here's the paper I want to write, and I need someone to think through it with me: read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) through the species problem. Is Victor's creature a member of Homo sapiens? I want the question taken apart concept by concept: he's built out of human corpses and dissecting-room material, so he's human "all the way down" in a material or genetic sense, and yet he has no parents and stands in no reproductive relation to anyone; he's built unnaturally large and everyone who sees him reacts with horror, though he turns out to learn language, read Paradise Lost, weep, and argue; and the one human who treats him decently is old De Lacey, who is blind and so can't see the features that horrify everybody else, until the sighted family comes back and drags him off.
