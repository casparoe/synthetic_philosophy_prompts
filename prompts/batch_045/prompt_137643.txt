So I run this monthly ideas thing in the back room of a pub — half rationalist-blog crew, half people who drifted in from the quiz night — and next month it's my turn, and I've gone and picked moral testimony, by which I mean that business where you end up holding some moral belief (that the new attendance policy at your work is grubby, that you shouldn't eat the prawns) purely because someone whose judgement you trust said so, and you couldn't produce a single reason for it if pushed. My flatmate thinks this is obviously just ordinary epistemic deference: you defer to the physicist about neutrinos, you defer to your friend about right and wrong, end of story. I keep feeling like theres something that doesn't carry over, but every time I try to say what it is I end up sounding like I'm claiming you should never listen to anyone about anything. Forty minutes, a whiteboard, no handouts (I'm not handing anything out and I'm not going to pretend I've read the literature, so don't suggest readings), nobody there has done any philosophy, and they'll all walk in thinking the answer is obvious in the deferring-is-fine direction.

What I want isn't an answer to the puzzle, it's a plan for the session. Two bits of scaffolding I've been using, take them or leave them: I seperate 'thin' deference (you come away with the verdict and nothing else) from 'thick' deference (you come away able to generate verdicts for cases nobody told you about), and the part I actually want them to feel is what I call the handover failure — that a moral belief mostly has to be cashed in later, in a somewhat new situation, at the time, with no-one to ring. So: tell me what has to be in place before the question even registers as a question rather than as a gotcha; the one example that should carry the whole forty minutes and what it must have and must lack (my instinct is that it shouldn't be a case where the object-level is itself contested or we'll spend the hour arguing about whether the thing is wrong, and it needs a follow-on question the deferrer visibly can't answer — but you tell me if that's the wrong shape); the misconceptions in the order they'll actually raise them, with the point in the sequence where each one gets headed off — the 'you defer to experts about everything, what's different here' one first obviously, then something like 'so it's really just about whether the source is reliable', then 'so you're saying never take anyone's word for anything', then maybe 'why not just defer to the reasons rather than the verdict' — and what I should flatly refuse to get into at this level, saying out loud that I'm refusing. Then give me one question to put to the room at the end that separates people who've actually got hold of the issue from people who are just repeating 'deference is a bit off' or just feeling told off, and if you think both my bits of scaffolding are junk or are smuggling in the answer, say so and hand me better ones.

Last thing, and it's the bit I care most about: flag the two places where you think this plan is most likely to come apart, and say what would make you resequence it — the consideration that would change your mind about what has to come first, or the bit where you think the room goes somewhere you haven't planned for. I'd much rather have something I can stress-test than something that reads nicely on the page.
