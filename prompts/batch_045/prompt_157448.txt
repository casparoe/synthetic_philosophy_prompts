hey — I'm running a 90 minute session for about 18 first year undergrads in a course called History, Memory and Power. Mixed bunch, most of them have never thought of an archive as anything other than a warehouse where old stuff lives. I need an in-class activity that makes them *feel* three related claims I keep circling around, rather than me standing at the front asserting them. I'll name them here so you know what the machinery has to do, but I don't want the students to hear any of these words until the debrief.

1. The administrative by-product claim: records mostly exist because some institution needed them for its own purposes — tax rolls, court dockets, hospital intake forms, plantation inventories, school registers. So what survives tracks bureaucratic usefulness, not importance. Nobody decided that the interesting stuff should be kept; the stuff that got kept was the stuff somebody was paid to write down.

2. Problem-triggered visibility: groups who were *not* inside the administration show up in the record mainly at the moments they were a problem for it — an arrest, a riot, an outbreak, a complaint, an inquiry. So their surviving traces are skewed toward the moments they were least agentic and most threatening. The archive doesn't show them living, it shows them being handled.

3. The legibility dividend: this compounds. Being recorded once makes you easier to record again — you're in the index, you have a file number, later finding aids and description and digitization budgets follow whatever is already described and already on the shelf. So a second generation of archiving sharpens the first generation's gaps instead of filling them. Gaps aren't noise sprinkled around a representative sample; they're structured, and they correlate with power, which is exactly why absence-of-evidence inferences from an archive go wrong in a predictable direction.

The thing I most want them to hit is that none of this requires a villain. A room of well-meaning people with twenty minutes, a small budget, limited shelf space, and no instructions about whom to care about will reproduce all three. If the activity ends with students feeling guilty, or feeling like they spotted the bias and avoided it, it hasn't worked.

So: design a runnable simulation. My constraints are — one room, 90 minutes, 18 people, no laptops for students, one projector if you need it, and materials limited to index cards, markers, masking tape and a printout or two. There should be rounds: an early round where they produce or collect records under time/budget/space pressure, a later round where a *different* subset of them acts as a second generation of archivists choosing what to describe, catalogue or "digitize" from what survived, and a final round where yet another subset plays researchers who can only use the surviving archive to answer a historical question about the same little world. I want the researchers to predictably over-claim — to treat "no card about X" as "X didn't matter / X didn't happen" — and I want that to be the moment the room notices something's off, not me telling them.

Give me: the setup and the fiction (keep it cheap and generic, invent a small town / institution / 60-year span, whatever makes the mechanics bite), the roles and what each role does and doesn't know, the rules for each round including the constraints that do the work (time limits, budgets, shelf slots, whatever), a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't need real money but gives people something to optimize, and the counting or display mechanic that makes the skew visible on the wall before anyone names it. Then the debrief: which moment in play maps onto which of the three claims, the questions to ask in what order, and how to run it so the students supply the generalisation themselves.
