hey — I run a small philosophy club at my high school, like 15 people, we get a 45 minute lunch block, whiteboard and that's basically it. A few of the kids are exchange students (Germany, Japan, Brazil) so a couple of them have actually been the confused visitor.

I want a session on whether everyday conventions are really moral obligations — the usual examples people use are tipping, waiting in line, jaywalking — and especially whether a visitor has to follow the local version or whether it's fine to just keep doing the thing from home.

I really dont want to stand up and explain it. I want them to walk into it. Some wrinkles I want the activity to make them actually hit:

- tipping where it can't be a reward: imagine a coffee shop with a jar by the door, you drop money in on the way out, and it gets split among all the staff at the end of the month. You can't be thanking your barista for good service, but people still feel like not putting money in is kind of wrong. Who is that feeling even about?
- the gap between what everyone says and what everyone does: everybody jaywalks, and everybody also says jaywalking is bad and unsafe. Which one is a visitor supposed to copy?
- conventions you can't see: someone visits a place where tipping is confusing or low key insulting, tips with totally friendly intentions, and offends the person. Did they do anything wrong, or is it on the locals to tell them?
- the swarm thing: one visitor following the local rule is totally harmless, but ten thousand of them show up and the local convention basically flips, prices go up, locals get annoyed at them. Every individual is being polite and together they wreck it. I want them to feel that one, not just hear it.
- and the you're-not-from-here thing: sometimes you follow the local rule perfectly and people still treat you as a guest and wave you ahead in line or refuse your money, so copying the locals isn't even on offer to you.

One design thought I had: run most of the game on made-up conventions in a made-up place, so nobody's existing opinions about tipping or lines do the work, and only map it back to the real examples at the end. Tell me if that's a bad idea or if it'll just confuse everybody.

What I want from you: something runnable — a game with rules and payoffs, a roleplay, staged forced choices, whatever fits — with rules, materials, a timeline for 45 minutes, and a debrief. No money, no real prep, and the actual distinction shouldn't get named until after they've hit it. Also tell me what's going to predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing half of them just say 'it depends on the culture' and check out), how to handle that, and be straight with me about which part of this the activity can't do for them and I'll just have to say out loud at the end.

Also — the exchange students. I'd like the activity to use the fact that they've been on the recieving end of this, without putting them on the spot to represent their whole country or explain anything.
