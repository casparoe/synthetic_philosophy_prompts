I write novels, and two of my beta readers got into it in the margins of a draft and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. The character, Merrick, has this thing: every time something blows up in his marriage he goes out to the garage and repaints the garden shed. Same brand of paint, same order — door first, then the side facing the house, then the trim — and when he's done he behaves as if the thing that blew up has been handled. He's done it dozens of times. He knows exactly what he's doing, he hates it, and he does it anyway.

Tasha's note said Merrick isn't really acting in those scenes, he's running. She brought up the old digger-wasp story: the wasp drags her paralyzed cricket to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it on the doorstep, goes in alone to have a look, comes back out and hauls it in. But if you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she comes out, drags it back to the exact spot, and goes in to look again. Supposedly you can do that to her over and over. Tasha says the behaviour keeps running with no regard for whether its point has been achieved, and that's Merrick: the routine fires, some little check gets satisfied, nothing updates. She wrote that he reads as a mechanism with a hat on and that readers will bounce off him.

Dev pushed back on three things. One: that wasp anecdote is one naturalist's story from way back, and people who later went and actually watched wasps for hours didn't get the endless loop — they saw wasps that just dragged the cricket straight in, or that gave up, or that did something else entirely. It's a bad idea, he said, to build a theory of agency on a fact that might just be false. Two: even if you grant it, the wasp is rigid about one cue and flexible about everything else — she still flies, hunts, copes with wind, digs a new burrow when the old one caves in. We're all rigid somewhere; I say sorry when somebody bumps into me. Three: Merrick knows. He could stop. That's the opposite of the wasp, not the same as it.

Tasha came back: knowing doesn't rescue anything, it just adds a floor to the building — there are human compulsions where the self-awareness is part of the machinery, and knowing makes it less free, not more. And she said the wasp's other talents are beside the point, because the only question is whether *this* bit responds to the point of doing it. Her way of putting it was that the wasp isn't checking whether the cricket is in the burrow, she's checking whether the cricket is at the threshold: its a proxy for the goal, not the goal. Merrick isn't tracking "am I fixing my marriage," he's tracking "is the shed painted."

Dev said if tracking-the-goal-instead-of-a-proxy is the test, then basically nothing human passes. I brush my teeth because the timer went off, not because I measured my plaque. Habit is proxies all the way down. So either the test is impossible, or it's just a fancy way of saying the guy has a habit, and having a habit isn't the same as not being free. And if Merrick genuinely could stop, then what I've written is weakness of will, which is a different scene from mechanism, and I should write that one instead.

Tasha: maybe what we call weakness of will just is a routine that outvotes the agent, and maybe the two scenes aren't different at all. Then she tried a test of her own — give him a shortcut. If Merrick is really aimed at the marriage, he'd take it the moment it showed up: one honest conversation, no paint. If what he's aimed at is completing the ritual, he'll walk right past the shortcut, or take it and feel nothing. A character who refuses the shortcut is sphexish, she said, and the real tell is perfect predictability: if a reader can call him every single time, he isn't choosing.

Dev: perfect predictability can't be the tell, because a saint who would never take the bribe is perfectly predictable too, and nobody calls her a machine. Predictability shows you've drawn a character, not that you've drawn a mechanism.

They left it there and went back to arguing about my third act. Can you go through the exchange and tell me who has the better of it at each point, where both of them are missing something, and who is more right overall? I don't need the answer about free will in general — I need to know which of these particular arguments actually hold up.
