Last night me and my roommate got into a three-hour argument at the pub — she's doing a PhD in computational neuroscience, I'm just a philosophy minor who took one seminar on consciousness — and what set it off was a pop-philosophy podcast episode she'd made me listen to on the walk over. The host glossed epiphenomenalism roughly like this: consciousness is a by-product of brain activity that doesn't do anything, it's causally inert, like the steam whistle on a locomotive, so the feeling of pain has no effect on your behaviour and not even on your belief that you're in pain, which is produced entirely by neural states; in fact your judgement would be exactly the same if the pain weren't there at all. And that, the host said, is the paradox: if your pain plays no role in bringing about your judgement that you're in pain, then that judgement isn't tracking the pain, so you've got no reason to think you're ever in pain, so the view refutes itself. Which is why nobody defends epiphenomenalism any more, and the live options are physicalist theories where consciousness does real causal work, or illusionism, which just denies there's anything to explain. He threw in at the end that on this view consciousness doesn't matter at all and we'd lose nothing if it went away. My roommate then pulled up some nineteenth-century essay called "Are We Automata?" on her phone and read out this bit, which she reckons is the real article of faith: "Feeling is a mere collateral product of our nervous processes, unable to react upon them any more than a shadow reacts on the steps of the traveller whom it accompanies. Inert, uninfluential, a simple passenger in the voyage of life, it is allowed to remain on board, but not to touch the helm or handle the rigging."

I want to know how fair that podcast gloss actually is, claim by claim, because we disagreed about nearly every sentence of it. What would someone who takes epiphenomenalism seriously as a live position accept, what would they say is a simplification but still their view, and what would they reject outright? My own hunches, for whatever they're worth: the "it refutes itself" move struck me as too fast — she kept saying something like "the belief and the pain can share a common cause, so the reliability isn't a miracle", and that the thesis is normally restricted to phenomenal properties rather than to beliefs and desires generally, but I can't tell if that's a real reply or a dodge, or whether the paradox only bites if you think knowledge or justification needs a causal connection to its object. The bit about "your judgement would be exactly the same if the pain weren't there" also seems to me to sneak in something the view doesn't say, since if the pain is tied to the neural state doing the causing you can't just delete the pain and hold everything else fixed — am I right that the argument is leaning on that? And the host made epiphenomenalism sound like it's obviously a species of dualism, whereas she insisted it's a thesis about causation that can turn up inside physicalist-looking views too, via that worry where if the physical facts already fix everything there's nothing left for the mental to do — is that right, and does it mean "nobody defends it any more" is just false? Also whether "we'd lose nothing" is sliding from has no effects to is unimportant, and maybe using "epiphenomenon" in the everyday sense rather than the technical one. And is the steam whistle even a good analogy? A whistle makes a noise, it does stuff; the shadow-and-passenger image in that old quote seems closer to the mark. Is any of this whole dispute just verbal, about how demanding your notion of causation has to be before something counts as a cause?

Could you go through the host's claims one at a time and seperate what's accurate, what a careful defender would still recognise as their view, and what they'd call a misrepresentation — and then tell me whether his conclusion (nobody holds it, so it's efficacious-consciousness-physicalism or illusionism) actually follows from the bits that are accurate, or whether he got there by overstating the self-refutation objection. Tell me as well whether that "Are We Automata?" passage is a fair statement of the position or already slanted, given that I think it was written by somebody arguing against the view. Don't give me a bibliography, paper titles or citations — I can chase those up later, and I'm on a train with awful signal anyway — just the arguments, in fairly plain language, without smoothing over the places where the disagreement is genuine. If my roommate and I were each half right about something, say which half was whose.
