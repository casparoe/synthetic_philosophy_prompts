Me and a mate had a long, stupid argument last night about our cost-benefit manual and I want it settled properly.

I'm on the appraisal team at a transport regulator. We do maybe thirty appraisals a year, each one gets signed off by a board member who isn't an economist, and I've got about twenty mintues per project to sanity-check the distributional weighting. Three things we actually do: (1) income step weights — any benefit going to a household below the national median income gets multiplied by 1.6, above median by 0.7, and we recompute who counts as below median using post-project incomes; (2) an "identifiability premium" of 1.25 on any fatality-risk reduction that accrues to identifiable named people rather than statistical lives; (3) a 15% haircut on any option whose benefits are spread very unevenly across income groups, applied on top of the weighting.

My mate — philosophy PhD, annoyingly good at this — says Harsanyi's aggregation theorem forces social welfare to be a weighted sum of individual vNM utilities with constant weights, so the only honest reason to count one person's pound for more is that their marginal utility of money is higher, which makes (2) and (3) pure hand-waving and (1) doubly bad because we recompute it after the project. I said no: the theorem is conditional — if the social ranking satisfies the vNM axioms and something like Pareto, then you get the weighted sum — so a regulator who doesn't buy independence for social choices just isn't bound by it, and we can weight by relative position, or by who's actually exposed to the risk, if we want. He said fine, then say out loud what you're giving up. I couldn't, and its been bugging me all day.

The hard bit is the translation — keep whatever Harsanyi's result actually forces (weights attaching to utility rather than money, additivity across persons, the way a social ranking handles lotteries) but in language my board member can follow, and be honest about which of those distinctions survive being turned into a handbook rule and which get lost.
