I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on the metaphysics of persons, and we have just finished the unit on teletransportation and what matters in survival. For the final paper I have to defend one account of what matters, and I would rather start from a specification than from the usual menu of isms. Please write me the desiderata — exactly five — that any adequate account of what matters in survival would have to satisfy.

Treat the plain destructive-scan case as settled background: every view on our reading list handles it, and handles it in a way I already understand, so do not spend one of the five on it. Build the list out of harder cases instead. Please draw on the following cases, which my seminar has not discussed:

- the Relayed Reconstruction: the scan is destructive but carried out one cell at a time, each cell on Earth being replaced as its counterpart is assembled on Mars, so that at every instant there is exactly one physiologically complete brain's worth of tissue, but it is distributed across two planets.
- the Cold Archive: the scan is recorded and never instantiated; the original later dies of old age; three centuries after that death the record is finally run.
- the Aborted Twin: a second copy is transmitted, but the signal is corrupted and the second replica is destroyed milliseconds after assembly without ever having had a conscious experience.
- the Lossy Scan: the reconstruction preserves something like 99.9 per cent of the relevant structure, and the resulting person is recognisably the original but noticeably changed.
- the Reluctant Replica: the reconstruction wakes up, is told what was done, and sincerely disclaims any prudential concern for what happens to the person they will become, regarding the procedure as nothing but the creation of a successor.

Four constraints on the list:

1. State each desideratum as a demand together with the case that makes it a demand, and pitch each one so that a defender of any of the familiar views — identity is what matters; some psychological continuity or connectedness relation is what matters, with or without a normal-cause or non-branching clause; there is no deep further fact — could in principle accept it. The point is to specify adequacy, not to settle the dispute by stipulation.
2. At least one desideratum should police the boundaries of the concept: what an account has to keep apart — survival from what matters, what matters from what it is rational to anticipate or fear, the obtaining of the relation from the value of the outcome.
3. At least one should say what an account owes us if, as the Lossy Scan suggests, what matters admits of degrees: a cardinal scale, a threshold, a rule for aggregating lesser degrees across many recipients, or a principled reason for refusing to give any of these.
4. At least one should say whether how much matters at a given time is allowed to depend on what happens later or elsewhere, which is what the Aborted Twin and the Cold Archive press on.

Tag each item [non-negotiable] or [negotiable: a preference of one camp], and cross-tag any item you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied with another, saying in half a sentence what the conflict is.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with the bracketed tag at the end of the item, and no introduction, no conclusion, no summary table, and no scorecard of the theories against the list. Work from my cases as I have described them rather than from anything you recall about how the literature sets up these problems, and do not cite, quote, or name any author, paper, or book — I need a specification, not a bibliography.
