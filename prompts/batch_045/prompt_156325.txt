I am a professor of philosophy — my own work is in general philosophy of science, and I am not a physicist — and I have been asked to draft the section on epistemic warrant for the scoring rubric of our university's internal high-risk research fund. The panel meets twice a term, sees between twelve and eighteen proposals per cycle, spends roughly twenty minutes on each, and must attach a one-page written rationale to every award; those rationales are read by the provost and by the faculty senate's research committee. There are five of us, only one of whom is a physicist, and that colleague will stop reading altogether if the rubric sounds like philosophers policing physics. So what I need is something the whole panel can actually run, in plain language, within the time we have, and reusable across cycles rather than a set of wise remarks about one application. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list: I have the literature. What I lack is a procedure.

The proposals that defeat us share a shape. They come mainly from fundamental cosmology and quantum gravity — a framework such as the string landscape or eternal inflation, whose defenders concede outright that no decisive test will be available during the grant period, and which is supported instead by arguments of a few recurring kinds: that a large, well-motivated community has searched hard for a rival framework of comparable unifying power and not found one; that the framework displays unexpected internal coherence, drawing together results that were not constructed to fit; that theories of this ambition and difficulty have historically gone on to empirical success, so the track record of the genre is itself some evidence; and, in the cosmological cases, that the observed values of certain parameters are roughly what one should expect to observe if the theory's vast space of possibilities is real and we occupy an untypical, observer-permitting corner of it. Proposals from other fields make structurally parallel claims: simulation-based programmes in macroeconomics or in neuroscience, whose models can be checked only against stylized facts or against other simulations, and whose defenders invoke coherence, unification, and the absence of rivals precisely when no out-of-sample test is available. At present our panel either defers to the physicist member or dismisses these proposals as not science. Both are evasions, and I want something better.

What I want from you is a decision procedure — ordered gates, each with a question, a specification of what counts as a sufficient answer, an instruction about what to do when the panel cannot get an answer (which in twenty minutes will be often), and a stop rule that says when the answer is no. It must handle at least the following difficulties.

First, a mapping from strength of warrant to strength of decision. Our current rubric conflates three things: whether the framework deserves further work; whether it may be presented to students or the public as a live option rather than a speculation; and whether other fields may treat it as a premise. My suspicion is that the non-empirical arguments, even at their strongest, only ever license the first, and that our awards quietly drift into licensing the second and third. I have been calling this drift warrant laundering. Give me a way of catching it, including a check that reads the proposal's own aims, hiring, and public-impact sections against its epistemic section, since the slippage usually happens between them.

Second, the confound I keep running into with the no-rivals argument: the probability that no alternative exists is not the probability that a search of this character, duration, and sociological composition would have found one, and I do not think a panel can estimate the latter even to an order of magnitude. Tell me what the panel should do instead — my own thought is to require the applicants to name one rival they take seriously, however undeveloped, and to score both frameworks on the cited evidence, with the absence of any nameable rival recorded as a fact about the field rather than about the world. Tell me if that is the wrong fix.

Third, discriminating power. The evidence cited is usually described only as something the framework predicts or accommodates. I want the panel to ask how much more expected that evidence is under the framework than under the best rival actually on the table, and to note that when no rival can be articulated the comparison collapses. Say what the procedure does at that point: whether it stops, whether it downgrades the award, or whether it caps the decision at the weakest of the three levels above. I am also inclined to ask, as a lighter check, whether the same form of argument would have recommended a framework we now regard as dead; if so, that is a reason for caution rather than a refutation, and I want you to tell me how much weight such a historical probe can bear given that we will be applying it from memory and under time pressure.

Fourth, the anthropic and typicality step. Some proposals rest largely on the claim that what we observe is unsurprising within a huge space of possibilities once selection effects are accounted for. I want a test for whether the typicality claim is independent of the outcome it is meant to explain — whether the measure over possibilities is motivated for reasons other than that it delivers the answer — and for whether there is any case in which the argument would have come out the other way. If there is not, say plainly that the step is unfalsifiable in a local rather than a global sense and tell me what the panel should then do.

Fifth, a progress or horizon gate. A programme that receives non-empirical support only can still be worth a bet, but it is a bet that needs a stated termination condition: what would count, by the end of the award, as the programme having stalled rather than merely not yet succeeded? For these programmes there is no clean failure signal, and I would rather the rubric admitted that than pretended otherwise. I also want the renewal case handled explicitly, since sunk costs and the panel's own earlier language are working on us.

For each gate, tell me what counts as a sufficient answer, and mark the points at which the remaining question is one of values rather than of warrant — opportunity cost across fields, the training and infrastructure a programme sustains, its effect on the prestige and composition of a department — and must therefore be handed back to the panel as a vote rather than disguised as an epistemic score. Equally, tell me which of my distinctions do not survive being made usable, and should be dropped rather than kept as ornament.

Then run the procedure, in full, on three applications.

The first: a new three-year proposal from a cosmology group working on eternal inflation, arguing that the observed value of the vacuum energy is what an anthropic selection argument predicts, conceding no direct test within the period, naming a rival single-universe approach but describing it as undeveloped, and requesting funds that include a public lecture series presenting the multiverse as the likely large-scale shape of things.

The second: a third renewal from a quantum-gravity group. It cites dualities and unexpected connections to pure mathematics, points to a long search without alternatives, asks for continued funding, and also asks that the panel record in its rationale that this framework is the default setting for quantum-gravity work at this university, to be reflected in a hiring line and in graduate curricula. I expect your procedure to stop somewhere here, and I want to see exactly where and in what words.

The third is the one I expect to dislike. It is an agent-based macroeconomic programme whose models are validated only against stylized facts and against other simulations, with no out-of-sample test available; its lead investigator is a colleague whose work I think is first-rate, and I want to fund it. If your procedure returns a no, say so without softening it. Then tell me honestly whether any amendment to the procedure that would save it is principled or is merely special pleading by someone who already knows the answer he wants.

Close by telling me, in language I can put in the preamble to the rubric and that a provost can read in one sentence, what this procedure cannot do. I have in mind that it will not deliver a credence in any framework, that it will not compare a bet in fundamental physics against a bet in neuroscience, and that it cannot settle whether a failed search tells us about the world or about ourselves — but I would like you to decide, rather than to agree with me.
