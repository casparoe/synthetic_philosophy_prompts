Hey — I teach a final-year metaphysics seminar and I'm stuck on the final. The last block of the course was on dispositions, and I want one essay question that actually separates the students who get why finks and masks matter from the ones who've just memorised that finks and masks are counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis, so we need some reformed analysis instead. My current draft is basically "do finks and masks refute the simple conditional analysis? discuss", and it's useless — everyone writes the same paragraph and everyone gets the same mark.

Two things I want built into the question.

First, give them at least one case they can't have seen before, ideally one where the labels we used in seminar don't obviously apply. I was thinking of something like (a) a mask that counterfactually depends on the disposition it masks — the shielding only kicks in because the thing is fragile, so you can't hold the disposition fixed and then subtract the interferer, which is what the usual reformed analyses want to do — and (b) a case where the disposition is lost partway through the stimulus-or-manifestation window rather than cleanly before it or cleanly after it, so it's unclear whether we even have a fink or a mask. Use whatever versions of those work best; the point is that reciting the standard pair of examples shouldn't get anyone very far.

Second, make them take a stand on whether the two problems pull in the same direction. Does a fix that handles finks (roughly, something about holding the causal basis fixed across the counterfactual) thereby handle masks, or do masks push somewhere quite different — toward extrinsicness, or toward denying that the masked thing really has the disposition, or somewhere else? A strong answer should be able to say what a reformed analysis would have to look like to get both right, or else say why no single reform can, and what that shows about what dispositions are.

Constraints: one question, 90 minutes in the exam room, 20 marks. Give me the question text as I'd put it on the paper, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks for each and one line on how people usually get each one wrong. Also tell me explicitly what separates an A from a B: I want the A answer to do something beyond hitting the five points — notice something about the cases I hadn't flagged, or draw a consequence I hadn't put in the question — so say what that extra move is and how you'd recognise it.

Working background, which the question can just assume: dispositions are real properties had in virtue of a causal basis, reasonably intrinsic ones, and the seminar question has been whether they can be analysed counterfactually, not whether they exist. Nobody needs to defend that. The students have only read my handout, so please don't presuppose any particular published version of the fink and mask cases, and don't put any citations or reading suggestions in the question, the model answer, or the scheme. And no "discuss": I want there to be a best answer, or at most a short list of answers that are clearly better than the alternatives, and I want the scheme to say which ones count as correct and why.
