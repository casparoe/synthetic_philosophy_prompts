I am a novelist. For nine of the past twenty-one years I have also been one of two story leads on Anselm, a text-only persistent world: everything in it is rendered as prose by the engine, with players supplying the descriptive writing. I am now drafting the world's first written moderation code, and I want the central chapter of it to be a procedure, not an essay.

Three features of Anselm matter. First, the Ledger: an append-only record in which every instantiated thing has exactly one entry, and no entry can be duplicated. Copies are possible, but a copy receives a new entry with a new history, and the world renders it visibly as second-hand. Second, unmaking: the holder of an object may unmake it, which deletes the entry, after which every text in the world that named the object renders as a blank space, called a gap. Gaps are permanent, and each one appears in future as a blank line in the unmaker's own character description; the community calls this carrying the blanks. Third, binding: a character may be confined by another faction, and while bound the player may not act as that character, though they may raise a new one that begins with nothing. At character creation every player accepts the Charter, which lists confinement, unmaking and memory-editing as mechanics that may be used on them without further negotiation.

The disputes we actually get are not about rules violations; the acts are almost always legal. They are about whether something real was lost, and therefore whether anything is owed. The moderators are volunteers with no philosophical training, they have fifteen minutes and one page, and their ruling has to be defensible on appeal to me and to the player who complains. So I need ordered questions rather than a theory: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, at which points the procedure stops with a no, and at which points it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values and not of fact. It must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and it must say what it cannot settle.

I do not want it to settle, or to appear to settle, whether virtual objects are real in the abstract; the moderators cannot adjudicate that and the players do not care. But I do want the metaphysics to do work where it bites. In particular I want the procedure to distinguish an object with a Ledger entry from something that existed only in a player's prose, and I want it to contain a test that disposes of the doctrine of description-parity — the argument, which I hear constantly, that any object is fully specified by its description, so that a fresh description of an identical thing is the same thing recovered. My sense is that this is false for anything whose identity depends on a provenance chain rather than on its qualities, and that the way to show it without metaphysics is a copy-substitution test: ask whether the complaint would survive the insertion of a qualitatively indistinguishable duplicate. I am not certain that test is sound, and I would rather have it corrected than preserved.

Please apply the procedure to three cases from last season.

One. A ship with a Ledger entry dating to the world's third year, whose description is a palimpsest of sentences added by four successive holders, was won legally in an in-world wager by a player in a rival faction, who unmade it in the harbour as a political gesture, expecting the archive to restore it. It did not; the gaps are permanent. The four former holders demand restoration or remedy. The destroyer says it was his to unmake, and that they may describe another ship. Note that two of the four have not logged in for six years, and the Charter provides that an object whose holder has been absent more than four years and named no successor has passed to the harbour.

Two. During a war they had no part in, a faction bound a player's only character for nine days. Nothing of theirs was unmade and no text of theirs was altered; the Charter plainly permits binding and the player accepted it. They say they lost nine days of a life they had been living for fourteen years and will not start a new character. I should say clearly that of the three cases this is the one I am most certain involves a real deprivation, and I would rather be told that my procedure is defective than be told that the binding was in order.

Three. A player spent three years describing, in prose only, a city that was never instantiated: no Ledger entry, no mechanic, no gap when a neighbouring region's editor retconned it out of shared canon as part of a plot. Nothing in the engine changed. The player says something they loved was destroyed, and in some sense I believe them. If your procedure reaches the same result here as in case one, I will suspect it is tracking grief rather than loss, and I want to know which of the two it ought to be tracking and why.

Finally: I am not asking for sources or further reading; I have no library here and do not want one. I am also writing a novel about people who do this work, and I want the procedure to be true to what the work actually feels like rather than to a philosopher's cartoon of it. At the end, tell me how confident you are in each of the load-bearing steps of the procedure you have given me, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of — the one you would most want a philosopher, or an experienced moderator, to check.
