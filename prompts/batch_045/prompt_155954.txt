Something I keep running into when people argue about copying policies from one country to another, and I want to know whether there's a word for the thing I'm noticing.

The argument always runs the same way. Someone says: look at the Nordics, they have high taxes, big universal programs, light-touch enforcement, and it works, let's do that here. And the reply is: it works there because people there trust each other and trust the state, and you can't import that, trust is something that takes generations. I get the point. But I've started to think that reply is doing two really different jobs and getting away with it by sounding like one objection.

Some policies look like they *use up* trust. If I already expect that most other people are paying what they owe, not claiming things they aren't entitled to, not faking the paperwork, then a generous low-surveillance system works great for me. If I don't have that expectation, the identical system looks like an invitation to be the sucker, so I take what I can, other people watch me do it, the expectation drops further, and you've burned whatever stock there was. Import that kind of policy into a low-trust place and it doesn't merely fail, it leaves you worse off than before you tried.

But other policies look like they *make* the trust they need. The clearest case I can think of is the same pot of money given to everyone regardless of income versus targeted at people below a line. The targeted version needs a lot of checking, it draws a bright line between the people who get it and the people who pay for it, everyone resents it, and it seems to erode the very goodwill you'd need in order to run it decently. The universal version needs almost no checking, everybody is in it, and — this is the part I have no vocabulary for — after a while people actually do seem to start expecting that the others are in it too. The attitude the policy needed at the start is the attitude the policy produces once it exists. It bootstraps, at the cost of taking a long time to do it.

So what I'm after isn't really a label. I want to know whether that second pattern is a genuine distinct thing or just the first pattern with a time axis stuck on, and if it is distinct, whether there's an established concept for it and how it differs from the nearest ones. The things I've bumped into that feel adjacent: people talk about social capital, about path dependence, about situations where everyone would cooperate if only everyone expected everyone else to cooperate, and I've also seen an argument that means-tested programs are self-undermining because they break up the coalition that would defend them. Those each grab a piece — the social capital stuff is about a stock of trust, the everyone-waits-for-everyone stuff is about expectations being self-reinforcing, the coalition stuff is about the politics — but none of them seems to be about the timing structure, where a policy needs X at the beginning and manufactures X over a couple of decades.

Two cases I can't sort out, if you want something to test a definition against. First: a policy that builds trust among the people receiving it while destroying it among the people paying for it — same phenomenon, or the opposite one wearing the same clothes? Second: a policy that does generate the trust but only slowly enough that the generation which has to vote for it never sees the payoff — does that still count, or is that better described as a different problem entirely, about democratic time horizons rather than about trust?

Please don't just reassure me that this is a familiar thing in the literature, i dont have library access and I'm not writing anything, I just want the concept to be sharp: what exactly it is, what it isn't, where its edges are. And if the honest answer is that the trust-eating versus trust-making distinction isn't real and it's all one mechanism with different parameters, say so and tell me what the parameters are.
