I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether anything computational could be creative in the sense that interested Hofstadter, and I would be grateful for a careful conceptual explanation that would let me work out which of us, if either, was missing the point. I should say at the outset that we were both working from memory of a book neither of us has opened in years, and I may well be misremembering details; part of what I want from you is to be told where my reconstruction has slipped.

The crux was Hofstadter's knob fantasy: the picture of creativity as the exploration of a large bank of dials, each with a range of settings, where having an idea is a matter of wandering around the space of combinations. As I remember it, he sets the picture up in order to qualify or reject it — I associate it with his writing on variations on a theme as the crux of creativity, though I would not swear to the venue — on the grounds that in real creativity no space is given in advance: the dimensions themselves, and above all the sense of which dimensions matter, have to be discovered, and that discovery is where the interest lies. My friend's reply was that this only relocates the picture, and that the relocation is cheap: large learned representational spaces with smooth structure behind them are exactly what contemporary generative systems have, so knob-turning at one level is knob-finding at another, and no deep distinction survives. I said that the hard part is not turning knobs but forging them, and that forging cannot itself be knob-turning without circularity. Neither of us could say precisely where the other had changed the subject.

So here is what I would like explained, at a level that goes past the dialectic I have just sketched. First, I suspect we were equivocating on the word space, and I would like you to prise apart at least three things that might be meant by saying a space is given: that a fixed set of dimensions with fixed ranges is specified in advance; that the dimensions are specified but their interpretation is not, so that one can move around a space without being able to say what one's moves mean; and that the dimensions are themselves products of the search. My own contribution to the argument was what I called the legibility condition — the thought that a bank of knobs functions as a space of ideas at all only if the agent can tell, of a given turn, what changed and whether the change preserved the theme, and that this is a semantic requirement rather than a metrical one, so that a smooth but uninterpreted continuum is not a knob bank in the sense that matters. Is that condition well drawn, and does it do the work I want of it, or does it smuggle in what is at issue?

Second, the regress. If forging a new dial is just turning dials in a larger space of dials, is that a benign bootstrapping or a deflationary collapse? I would like you to say what the difference would consist in, and in particular whether the distinction between a genuinely new dimension of variation and a nonlinear recombination of the old ones can be drawn at all, or whether it dissolves under pressure. For if it dissolves, my friend's it was a bigger space all along looks unfalsifiable, and I cannot tell whether that is a defect in his position or simply the truth of the matter.

Third, a worry that occurred to me on the way home, which neither of us raised: variation seems to require an invariant. To vary a theme one must already have perceived it, and to perceive it is to treat some features as held fixed; yet the choice of what to hold fixed looks both indispensable and itself revisable. Call this the fixed-point requirement. Does it generate a second regress running alongside the first, does it supply the non-arbitrary stopping point that the first regress needs, or is it a separate problem wearing the same clothes?

One further thing, if you can manage it: my friend also turned the objection against Hofstadter's own models, arguing roughly that an architecture with a slipnet of concepts that slip into one another, a stochastic temperature governing how daring the search is, and fluid conceptual slippage throughout is itself a fixed representational space being searched, so that the objection proves too much. I would like to know whether Hofstadter has any resource against that, and whether the point about legibility helps him here.

Please write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with citations, page references or reading suggestions: I want the conceptual anatomy, and I would rather have your own reasoning than a report on what has been published. Two things would help me most in closing. Say how confident you are in the central claim you make, and single out the point in your account that you are least sure of. And please flag the places where you are reconstructing or extending Hofstadter's view rather than reporting it, along with any place where my framing above misdescribes what he actually argues.
