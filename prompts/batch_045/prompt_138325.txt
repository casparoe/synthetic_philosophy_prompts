I have been reading a well-reviewed popular book about the deep past — written by a journalist who has plainly read a good deal of the anthropological literature, and who names the specialists he relies on — and its chapter on the origins of farming has left me unable to tell the parts I should retain from the parts I should discard. The passage that does the real work runs as follows:

    For most of our history we lived as hunters and gatherers, and for most of that time we lived better than the first farmers did. In the 1960s anthropologists timed a group of San foragers in the Kalahari and found that they spent something like twenty hours a week getting food; the rest was sleep, talk, and play. Their wants were few and their environment supplied them, so by their own standards they were affluent — what the literature calls the original affluent society. Agriculture reversed all of this. It did not liberate people from work, it chained them to fields and multiplied their labour; the grain diet made them shorter, sicker and more vulnerable to famine, and a single failed harvest could starve a village that a foraging band would simply have walked away from. Farming was, on this account, the worst mistake in the history of the human race. It also created inequality for the first time. Grain can be stored and counted in a way that a giraffe carcass cannot, and the moment there is a surplus there is something to defend, inherit and tax: property first, then chiefs, then states. Before that, in a band of twenty-five people who shared everything and owned nothing, hierarchy was not merely absent but impossible. Nobody could be richer than anybody else because there was nothing to be richer in. The story of civilisation is therefore the story of a bad bargain: many more people, living worse lives.

My own instinct is that the noble-savage flavouring is overdone but that the skeletal evidence about health and stature must be solid, since I have seen it repeated everywhere. Beyond that I cannot tell where the accuracy ends. Two people I trust have each made a remark that I could not evaluate. An economist friend said that even if every individual farmer was worse off than every forager, the transition could still have raised total welfare, because agriculture supports a far larger population on the same land, and that until you specify whether you are averaging over persons or summing over them, calling the transition a mistake is not even a well-formed claim. Is that a real point or a debating trick, and if it is real, what does it do to the book's upshot? Separately, an acquaintance who works in archaeology said the inequality paragraph is the weakest thing in the passage, because accumulation is doing all the causal work in it and accumulation is not unique to farming — she mentioned peoples who stored salmon rather than grain and ended up with ranking and slavery anyway — which would mean the book has credited agriculture with something that was really done by storage and inheritance. I did not follow the details.

What I would like is an accuracy audit I can act on. Take the passage apart claim by claim, and for each one tell me: what a specialist in hunter-gatherer studies or prehistoric archaeology would accept as stated; what is a defensible simplification of a more careful claim that the specialists actually hold; what is a genuine distortion; and what is simply contested, naming the shape of the disagreement. Where you find a distortion, I would rather have the diagnosis than the correction: say which two claims the careful version of the argument keeps apart, what each of them would commit someone to on its own, and what the book gains by running them together. I am especially unsure about the word affluent, since the passage seems to use it in a way that is neither quite about material abundance nor quite about how contented people felt, and about the sentence calling hierarchy impossible.

Then tell me whether the book's conclusion survives its own corrections: does the claim that civilisation was a bad bargain, and that inequality arrives with farming, depend on the parts you have identified as distortions, or does it stand without them? If it depends on them, say what weaker claim the passage could defensibly have made instead.

Finally, and this matters to me as much as the rest: mark how confident you are in each of your verdicts, distinguish what you know from what you are reconstructing, and single out the one judgment you are least sure of — tell me what kind of evidence would settle it, without pointing me to anything I would have to look up. And end by telling me which single sentence from the passage I should refuse to repeat to anyone.
