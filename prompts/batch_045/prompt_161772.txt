I teach a fourth-year seminar on rational choice — 17 students, one 75-minute slot. They've all seen expected utility and basic Dutch book stuff, but nothing about what happens when you don't have a number. I want one session where they feel the difference between having a sharp credence you're unsure about and genuinely not having a number, and where the not-having costs them something they can see. Not the known-urn / unknown-urn thing that every google result is about — I want imprecision to cost them points, and then to strand them at the moment they have to act.

Constraints: no money changing hands (points, a leaderboard, mild shame). Materials: index cards, a projector, an opaque bag of 100 ping-pong balls whose composition gets fixed by a helper outside the room, so that neither I nor they know any more than "somewhere between 20 and 80 of them are red." No lecture up front, and id rather the words imprecise, ambiguity, and Knightian not be said out loud until the debrief.

What I'd like designed:

1. A reporting round where students write down, not a number, but a pair of bounds, and get scored by something that penalises both being wrong and being wide — so that "0 to 100" is safe but expensive and "0.5" is cheap but risky. I want the width-versus-accuracy tradeoff to be something they can see and argue about, and I want you to be upfront about how much of the result is being driven by your choice of penalty parameter rather than by anything deep.

2. A round that separates three things my students definately conflate: (a) "no idea what the chance is," (b) "the chance is probably around a half but I'm not confident about that," (c) "I have a number, I'm just rounding it." These should come apart in observable behaviour — ideally something where the student who has a second-order spread over chances still ends up transacting at some number, while the student with an open interval refuses to take either side of the bet, and the round makes that refusal visible as a refusal and expensive to fake.

3. A forced-action round: the group as a whole has to commit to one of two options, and which option is better flips depending on which number inside their interval you act on. They should discover for themselves that an interval doesn't tell you which member to use, and then argue about whether it's legitimate to just pick one.

4. I have a half-memory that with intervals, conditioning on new evidence can leave your interval wider than it was no matter which way the evidence comes out — which sounds like a bug rather than a feature. If that's a real phenomenon, can a short fourth round make it happen in front of them? If it isn't, say so and give me some other way to make intervals look strange.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the session onto the distinctions, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guesses: everyone just writes 0.5 because it's the middle, and at least one student treats the whole thing as a puzzle about what I'm thinking), and be honest about the part of the idea the activity can't deliver — the part where I have to stand up and say something normative myself.
