My friend Priya and I spent most of last night after our department's reading group arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle — the case where you are handed a large sum simply for having formed, by a certain hour, the genuine intention to drink at a later hour a vial that will make you miserable for a day, with the money already yours whether or not you drink. I claimed the puzzle reaches further than its science-fictional wrapping: there are thoroughly ordinary situations, with no tycoon and no mind-reader, in which you have a real reason to form an intention and, having formed it, a real reason to act, even though — as you know perfectly well while forming it — there will be no reason whatsoever to act when the moment arrives. She claimed every such example cheats: either a future reason smuggles itself back in (a reputation to protect, a deposit already paid, a predictable change in your own tastes), or the state I am calling an intention is not one at all but a wish, a forecast, a bit of self-deception, or a performance for an audience. We could not settle it by appeal to intuitions, so I want to settle it by construction.

You are a philosopher of action who works on intention, commitment and diachronic rationality; I want you to take this as a design problem, sketch two or three candidate setups, discard the ones that cheat, and write up the one that survives. Construct a single-paragraph scenario, set in ordinary academic or professional life (a hiring committee, a collaborator, a landlord, a family member — no large cash prizes, no infallible judges of mental states, no drugs, implants or devices better than a shrewd human observer), in which the following all hold at once. First, at the early time there is a genuine reason to form the intention — a benefit a real person would care about, and a reason to intend rather than merely to appear to intend: whatever delivers the benefit has to be responsive to the intention itself, not to its outward signs, but it must be detectable by an ordinary person through some ordinary intermediary that a merely pretended intention could not reliably produce, such as what the intention does in the intervening hours to your attention, your planning, or the way you deliberate out loud. Second, at the later time there is no reason at all to perform the action — not a reason that is outweighed or defeated, but a reason that simply is not there — and the agent knows this at the earlier time, calmly and correctly. Third, the agent is at neither time self-deceived, intoxicated, forgetful, or weak-willed in the sense of failing to respond to reasons; they are fully lucid throughout. Fourth, the case must make two accounts of intention come apart, with the verdicts falling out of the accounts rather than being stipulated. Call the first the belief-constraint account: an agent can intend at one time to do something later only if they believe, at the earlier time, that they will do it, and since a lucid agent who knows there will be no later reason cannot believe they will act, they cannot form the intention — so no intention, no benefit, no action. Call the second the two-tier account: reasons for intending are not reasons for acting, so the intention can be formed for the earlier reason alone, and once formed it itself supplies a reason to act at the later time, through the commitment it constitutes and the rational pressure not to abandon it — so the intention is formed, the benefit is collected, and the action is performed.

Begin your reply with the scenario itself: no preface, no note on method, no restatement of what I asked for, and no sentence like "here is a case that works" — the first sentence should already be inside the case. Then, in that order: (a) run the check, stating the intention, the reason available at the early time, why no reason remains at the later time, and what each of the two accounts says at each time and why; (b) identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work, and show what happens to both accounts' verdicts if that feature is removed; (c) give the strongest reply available to a defender of the belief-constraint account — specifically, what they will say is being smuggled in — and say plainly whether you think the reply succeeds or whether the case stands. If you conclude that some subset of my constraints cannot be jointly satisfied, say which one has to give and why, and give the nearest case that does work rather than quietly relaxing a constraint.
