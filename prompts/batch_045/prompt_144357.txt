I need a hand with something for Friday's meeting of my university's Committee on Religious Life, and I'm short on time.

We're the body that grants official "religious organization" status to student groups. Status gets you a line in the student-activities budget (fixed pool, currently split among eleven groups) and the right to book the sanctuary in the old chapel, which is the scarce part — eight Sunday slots, all claimed. Our written criteria, more or less: (i) the group must be organized around shared convictions concerning the divine or ultimate reality; (ii) it must not be primarily political or philosophical; (iii) the Committee does not inquire into the truth or falsity of any group's beliefs.

We just recieved an application from a group calling itself Second Naivety — about twenty-five undergrads, meeting Sunday evenings: twenty minutes of sitting in silence, some readings, discussion, a shared meal. Their statement does three things. First, it explicitly rejects what they call metaphysical realism about the divine: they say they don't think of God as an object that exists or fails to exist alongside tables, and they won't describe their sentences as describing a mind-independent reality. Second, it rejects with equal force the suggestion that their language is merely expressive — they insist their claims are genuinely true or false and that their practice is answerable to something they didn't invent, and they say this is exactly why they are not just an ethics discussion club. Third, on the strength of that, they refuse to tick either box on our form (We affirm the existence of God / We do not affirm the existence of God) and have written in "not answerable as posed." They're also slippery about whether what they do is worship — happy to be called a practice, happy to be kept out of the sanctuary if we decide that space is for worshipping communities only.

As you'll spot, this is roughly the position Putnam works out in his late writings on religion and on Rosenzweig and Wittgenstein, though the students don't cite him and I'd rather the memo not read like I've been waiting years for someone to hand me this case.

Two complications. First: three years ago we turned down the Secular Humanist Alliance on the ground that they were a philosophical society, not a religious one. They have announced they will reapply in March with almost the same wording — truth-apt claims about there being no God, not descriptions of a mind-independent reality, a Sunday meeting answerable to standards they didn't make up. Our chaplain (evangelical) says that if we let Second Naivety in we have to let SHA in, that this just is the death of the category, and that we should therefore deny both. Our student member (a fairly militant atheist) is fine with admitting both and letting the budget sort it out. Our Catholic campus minister thinks the real difference is that Second Naivety has a practice and SHA has lectures and a speaker series. Second complication: the cost isn't fictional — if the category swallows every reflective group on campus, the per-group allocation falls to nothing and we've effectively abolished the funding scheme by reinterpretation.

What I want from you is the memo I'll circulate on Thursday. Give me a bottom line — recognize, deny, or recognize with conditions — stated as a rule general enough that we can apply it to SHA's application when it lands, and take the sanctuary question separately from the funding question rather than bundling them. Put in a concrete time frame (provisional for a semester, revisit in April, whatever you actually recommend). Give the strongest version of the case for whichever side loses, not the version that's easy to knock over — I'd rather be braced for the chaplain than reassured. And don't turn it into a seminar paper: the chaplain stops reading at a page, so keep the body under 800 words, and if you need to explain internal realism or the fact/value business to make the rule work, stick that in a footnote or an appendix where only I will read it.

For what it's worth I lean toward recognizing them, but I haven't managed to write the rule in a way that doesn't also commit us to SHA, and I'm genuinely unsure whether that's a bug in the rule or whether the chaplain is right that it's a reductio.
