I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in analytical chemistry, most of it method validation, which is a boring way of saying I spent my career asking why two labs running the same protocol on the same sample get different numbers. I've gotten into philosophy lately through a reading group of other retired science people (we meet in a pub, no wifi, so nothing that needs looking up, please: no citations, no reading lists, and if you're not sure whether a position has an established name, just call it "unnamed" and describe it by its claim).

We've got badly stuck on the experimental philosophy business — studies reporting that people from different cultural or linguistic groups give different answers to the same vignette. The four we've been chewing on: the fake-barn style case where the driver passes the one real barn among the facades; the case of the man with the temperature device implanted in his skull who has no idea it's there or how it works; the case about who a name refers to when everyone in the community knows only one thing about the person it was borrowed from; and the chairman who starts a new program that harms the environment as a side effect. Half the group thinks this sinks a chunk of philosophy, half thinks it tells us nothing at all, and I think its because we are collapsing a whole checklist of different failure modes into the single sentence "the intuitions differ."

In my old job the checklist would have been: is the instrument calibrated differently in the two labs, is the detection threshold set differently, is there a matrix effect so that it is really measuring a different quantity in the two samples, or is it contamination, sampling, or the operator misreading the scale. I want you to build me roughly that tree for this debate.

What I'd like:

- A decision tree I can print on one page for the group: 6 or 7 branch nodes, 8 or 9 leaves.
- It should start from a claim we all already accept — "some studies report cross-cultural differences in responses to these vignettes" — and end at a named position.
- Every branch node should be a question a person can answer without knowing which position it leads to: a concrete case, or a concrete hypothetical follow-up finding. Please make these do the work: (a) a node where the same comparison is rerun with a comprehension check and a forced-choice format instead of an agree/disagree rating, and the difference shrinks or disappears; (b) a node where the difference survives for one of the four cases but not the others; (c) a node about whether respondents disagree about the case itself or about how much evidence is enough / how bad it would be to wrongly call something knowledge; (d) a node where the difference persists even among people trained in the relevant subject; (e) my matrix-effect node: a node asking whether the words in the two languages are doing the same job for the two groups.
- Leaves that must be there: the variation is in the underlying judgment and so discredits intuitions as evidence; restrict to the intuitions of people whose intuitions are properly reliable or calibrated; the two groups are deploying different concepts, so there is no single thing being theorized about; philosophy doesn't actually rest on intuitions in the first place; it's a measurement artifact and the underlying competence is the same; the variation is real but is variation in the respondent's implicit error-cost settings rather than in the concept (this is my hobby horse — like a decision criterion in signal detection: what varies is how bad people take it to be to wrongly ascribe versus wrongly withhold, not what they think the word means — include it as a leaf even if you think it's wrong, and say which node would kill it); and the variation is itself a datum a good theory of the thing should predict.
- One line per leaf on its most awkward commitment, the way you'd put it to a room of skeptics.
- Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: give me the questions and the answer combinations they allow first, then locate the actual positions in that space.
- After the tree, two short sections: the combinations of answers that no position actually occupies, each with a one-line guess why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), and any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, with the extra question that would separate them if there is one.
- Don't let the tree depend on which differences have actually replicated. I don't want the group arguing about that and I couldn't check it anyway. Make each node conditional — "suppose the difference survives X," "suppose it vanishes under Y" — so the handout works whichever way the evidence goes.
- Keep the language plain. These are ex-lab people. If you use a term of art, gloss it in a clause instead of assuming we know it.
