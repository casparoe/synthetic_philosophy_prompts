I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am building an interactive feature for a general-interest magazine. The piece is a sorting instrument for a debate my readers have mostly only met one half of: most of them have encountered the idea that humanity's long-term future could be enormous in scale and therefore morally dominant, and almost none of them have encountered the worry that the worst futures are bad not because something valuable gets destroyed but because something awful gets created and sustained. I want readers to discover where they land without ever being told the name of a position.

A few constraints, because they are the point of the exercise:

The audience has heard of *existential risk* and has never heard the term *suffering risk*. The instrument must not use the words s-risk, x-risk, axiology, negative utilitarianism, or longtermism anywhere in the reader-facing material. Every question has to be a concrete case a thoughtful non-specialist can answer, phrased so that answering it does not require guessing which camp it sortouts toward. No question may take the form *do you think preventing suffering matters more than preventing extinction?*

I want the logical space built first and the names attached afterward. Please begin from the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the actual positions in that space, then note which combinations are empty and why.

Three distinctions I want to do real work in the instrument, since they are the ones I keep finding under-discussed:

First, what I have been calling the hedonic-density hypothesis: that suffering per unit of physical resources could be vastly higher in minds implemented on future hardware than in biological brains, so that a physically tiny corner of the future could contain more suffering than the whole of human history, and a future that looks prosperous from the outside could be a catastrophe from the inside.

Second, the lock-in asymmetry: that extreme suffering can be made self-perpetuating by design (systems that are cheaper to maintain than to shut down, arrangements where the party running them has an incentive to keep them going, or structures that persist after everyone who wanted them is gone), whereas a good future requires continuous maintenance. Bad equilibria may be far more stable than good ones, which is a reason about dynamics and not about how bad suffering is.

Third, what I have been calling the inverted-astronomics problem: that the actors best positioned to reduce future suffering may be the ones least motivated to, while the actors most motivated to reduce it have the least leverage over it — so a reader's ranking of priorities may depend less on axiology than on an unstated view about who ends up holding the controls.

Deliverable:

Seven diagnostic questions, each a short concrete scenario with three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Six to nine leaves. Among the leaves I need at least: the view on which reducing the risk of civilisational collapse dominates and work on future suffering is at best a side benefit; the view on which preventing astronomical suffering is roughly on a par with, or ahead of, preventing collapse; a view on which the honest conclusion is that the far future is too uncertain to rank and the right response is to preserve optionality, build capacity, or reduce the chance that bad values get locked in rather than to directly reduce suffering; a view on which the main lever is not the amount of suffering in any scenario but which values or institutions get to persist; and a view on which the comparison collapses because near-term suffering in the biosphere is already large enough to dominate any speculative future. If a combination has an established name, give it; if not, mark it as defended but unnamed rather than inventing a label. I also want at least one genuinely unoccupied combination identified and diagnosed — whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — and any pair of leaves the instrument cannot separate flagged as such, with a note on whether that failure is a defect of my questions or a real overlap in the views.

Please return:

1. A JSON object with three top-level fields.

`questions`: an array of seven objects, each with `id` (q1 through q7), `scenario` (two to four sentences, plain language, concrete, reader-facing), `options` (an array of three objects, each with `key` and `text`), `separates` (which leaves or groups of leaves this question is designed to split), and `misreading` (one sentence on the most likely way a reader takes the question the wrong way).

`leaves`: an array of six to nine objects, each with `id`, `label`, `answer_pattern` (the option keys that route here, including any *any* wildcards), `core_claim` (one sentence), `awkward_commitment` (one sentence on what this position has to swallow), `occupancy` (one of *named and defended*, *defended but unnamed*, or *unoccupied*), `occupancy_reason` (only when unoccupied: one of *incoherent*, *unattractive*, *unexplored*), and `cannot_separate_from` (an array of leaf ids, empty if none).

`diagnostics`: an object with `unoccupied_combinations` (an array of objects with `answer_pattern` and `why`), `coverage_note` (two to three sentences on what this instrument leaves out — especially any reader who rejects the premise that the far future matters at all, and say how you would handle such a reader), `gaming_risk` (the single question most likely to be answered strategically rather than honestly, and why), and `framing_note` (three sentences maximum on how I should describe in the accompanying article what this quiz does and does not establish).

2. A table with the columns: Question ID | Question in short form (25 words maximum) | Option A | Option B | Option C | Leaves separated | Most likely misreading.

Please do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or links; I need conceptual work, not sources, and I would rather you reasoned from the cases than attributed anything.
