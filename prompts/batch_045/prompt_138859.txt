I have recently joined my town's climate action committee, and I find myself unable to tell whether the scheme we have just adopted is coherent. The scheme is this: every household receives an annual allowance expressed in tonnes, calculated by a commercial footprint tool that multiplies our metered gas, our electricity, our car mileage and our diet survey by published average emission factors; households above the allowance receive a letter from the committee, households below it receive a certificate, and the town reports its progress toward its 2035 target as a simple sum of the household reductions achieved. What puzzles me is that the same committee voted down a proposal to pursue compensation for residents whose homes were damaged in last year's flooding, on the explicit grounds that no identifiable tonne emitted by any identifiable household caused any identifiable flood, and it declined to join a neighbouring municipality's suit against a regional utility for much the same reason. One member of the committee now says the whole accounting scheme is theatre: if my driving caused no one's flood, then my not driving prevents no one's flood, so the letter I am about to sign is either a false statement or a purely symbolic gesture dressed up as arithmetic.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory the practice presupposes, and to be careful about the difference between what it strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with. In particular: does the practice have to presuppose that individual acts make a causal difference to the harm, or can it be reconstructed on an expected-difference-making view, on a fair-share view on which each person owes a proportionate part of the collective response, or on a complicity or expressive view on which the point is to refuse participation in a wrongful practice rather than to change outcomes? Does summing household reductions presuppose that the harm is a smooth additive function of emissions — so that every tonne does roughly the same damage — and how would the practice have to be reshaped if the harm were instead threshold-shaped or tipping-point-shaped, so that almost all reductions do nothing and only the marginal tonnes around a threshold do any work? And can the practice's two halves be reconciled: is it consistent to insist that contributions are causally inert when assigning liability or compensation, yet to treat the very same contributions as the individual's own, as hers to reduce, and as a legitimate object of praise and blame?

Two further details from our meetings, which I suspect are where the real commitments are hidden, and about which the committee is divided. First, the calculator assigns electricity emissions using the average grid mix, which here is roughly sixty per cent hydro; but our marginal kilowatt-hour almost certainly comes from a gas plant, so the number on my letter is not the difference my reduction makes, and the same problem arises with the petrol I do not buy, which is presumably bought by someone else. Does the practice presuppose an average-attribution or a marginal-attribution view of my emissions, and does it need to answer for leakage and rebound at the population level at all, or can it claim that the aggregate figure is all that matters? Second, the allowance is per household rather than per person, so a household of five and a household of one receive the same number, and there is no credit for emissions one causes others not to emit — campaigning, say — and no share assigned to the emissions of one's children. What view of the unit and the currency of responsibility does that combination presuppose, and what would the scheme have to look like if it were rebuilt on each of the rival views you identify?
