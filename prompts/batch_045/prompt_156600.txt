I am a retired field biologist — thirty-odd years of comparative work on alarm calls and predator recognition in social mammals — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the nearby university, where we have recently been discussing whether it makes sense to attribute beliefs to AI systems. I keep noticing a pattern in the cases that I do not have a word for, and I would like help carving it rather than labelling it. Two instances. First: I tell a conversational system a meandering story about my neighbour's goose — she sleeps in the garage, she is frightened of the vacuum cleaner, she limps on her left foot — and an hour later, in the same conversation, I ask what I should do about cleaning the garage without distressing her. The reply is sensible in a way that depends on where the goose sleeps, what frightens her and which foot she favours, and the natural description of what just happened is that the system believes the goose sleeps in the garage, is afraid of the vacuum and so on. Yet every bit of that content came from my own utterance; the system has no perceptual contact with the garage and no way of acquiring any; if I say later that she has in fact been moved to the shed, the system revises without friction and without anything that looks like a psychological event; and if I actually move the goose in the world while leaving my messages untouched, nothing whatever changes in the system. Second: if I open two separate conversations with the same system and tell one that the centrifuge is in room 12 and the other that it is in room 14, each conversation then produces downstream behaviour coherent with its own story, and there is no interference between them, no balking, no sign that anything has to be reconciled. A technician who had been told both things would hesitate, would ask which to trust, would look for a way to check.

What strikes me is that the ascription is genuinely indispensable — I cannot predict or explain what the system will say without it — and yet it fails nearly every other test I would apply before attributing a belief to an animal. In my old work, a vervet's apparent belief that a leopard is nearby earned its keep by persisting, by surviving into situations other than the one that produced it, by being expensive to get wrong, by being tied to the animal's sensory contact, and by generating surprise and correction when the world contradicted it. Here the ascription has no persistence across episodes, transfers nowhere, is tied to nothing, and never surprises; its content is sourced from the interpreter's own speech and its predictive payoff stops at the boundary of the conversation that supplied it. It is not ordinary error or ignorance, since a mistaken belief still tracks the world and still costs something. It is not pretence or acting, since we do not attribute the belief to the actor playing a part. It is not quite the mere tendency to agree with an interlocutor that people discuss under the heading of sycophancy, because what is in question is the content of a world-directed state rather than a desire for approval. And it does not seem to be captured by saying that the belief is merely as-if or instrumental, because the ascriptions I make to animals and to my colleagues are not instrumental in that thin sense and earn their keep in the further ways I have listed.

So my question is whether there is an established concept for this configuration — an attitude-ascription that is locally indispensable, globally inert, and whose content is supplied by the very interpreter who then finds it useful — or, failing that, what the nearest established notions are and exactly how my case differs from each. I have encountered the phrase the intentional stance, and as I understand it the view is that if the stance pays off then the belief is real, which would make my case simply a real belief; but the payoff here is confined to one episode, and that seems to me to mark a difference in kind of ascription rather than a belief that failed some external test. I have also seen mention of derived or borrowed intentionality, of contextual or relational ways of understanding belief attribution, and of fragmented or compartmentalised belief in human subjects, and none of these looks like a clean fit either. I am not after a name for its own sake: I would like to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie — in particular what a system would have to do in order to count as having the belief rather than merely being well described by it, and whether the costliness of being wrong, which does so much work in my animal examples, is doing legitimate work here or is a red herring. I have to lead the reading group's session next month and would like to say something precise. Please answer in British English.
