I'm putting together the final session of a graduate seminar on translation under formal constraint — the tradition of Hofstadter's book-length exercise, in which one short French lyric is re-rendered dozens of times under self-imposed rules, every version buying one fidelity with another. For this closing session I don't want a survey of the standard positions; I want a verdict on a single worked case, and I've built the case so that the usual pieties about "sense versus form" won't settle it on their own.

The source. Below is a nonce stanza I wrote in French for the occasion. Its form is deliberately over-determined, and I'm stating the constraints explicitly so that nothing has to be guessed at:

1. Le sable est là-haut, patient, lent, inépuisable,
2. compte notre longue attente en silence,
3. et paraît, à midi, interminable,
4. tombant sans aucune cadence,
5. reste toujours inévitable,
6. son silence,
7. Irrémédiable.

The constraints: seven lines; line lengths descend by word count, 7-6-5-4-3-2-1, so the closing line is a single word standing alone; rhyme alternates a-b-a-b-a-b-a, where each a-word belongs to the -able/-ible family and carries a privative prefix (in-, im-, ir-) and each b-word belongs to the -ence/-ance family; and the syntax is left suspended — a chain of predicates hung off the subject of line 1, not a series of finished sentences.

A deliberately flat, line-by-line gloss: (1) The sand is up there, patient, slow, inexhaustible, (2) [it] counts our long waiting in silence, (3) and seems, at noon, interminable, (4) falling without any cadence, (5) [it] remains always inevitable, (6) its silence, (7) Irremediable.

The six renderings. Each was produced under a different self-imposed rule, and each therefore fails somewhere:

A — rhyme and the morphological constraints kept, the word-count descendo dropped, metre regularised into loose iambic lines of roughly five stresses:
1. The sand waits in the upper glass, inexhaustible,
2. and counts our length of waiting into silence,
3. then at the height of noon appears interminable,
4. descending without metre and without cadence,
5. abiding, as it ever has, inevitable,
6. and holding to its own unhurried silence,
7. and last of all, irreparable.

B — the descendo kept exactly (7-6-5-4-3-2-1), rhyme abandoned altogether:
1. The sand, patient, slow, up there, inexhaustible,
2. counts our long waiting in silence,
3. and seems, at noon, interminable,
4. falling without any cadence,
5. still always inevitable,
6. its silence,
7. Irremediable.

C — both constraints met, but only by contrivance: hyphenated compounds counted as single words, an archaic contraction of syntax, and a French loanword imported to save a syllable count:
1. The up-in-the-glass sand, patient, slow, still inexhaustible,
2. counts-out our long waiting in silence,
3. and seems, at high-noon, interminable,
4. falling sans any cadence,
5. still, ever, inevitable,
6. its silence,
7. Irrevocable.

D — both constraints met honestly, but the referent transposed: the hourglass becomes snow at a window, and the closing word shifts accordingly:
1. Snow at the pane, patient, soft, unstoppable,
2. counts our long waiting in silence,
3. and seems, at noon, interminable,
4. falling without any cadence,
5. still always inevitable,
6. its silence,
7. Unremarkable.

E — rhyme, morphology and wording all kept, but the descendo inverted (1-2-3-4-5-6-7), so that the poem grows instead of shrinking: the same seven lines read bottom to top. One can argue for it: the sand above is emptying, but the heap below is accumulating, and a growing line enacts accumulation as honestly as a shrinking line enacts depletion:
1. Irremediable,
2. its silence,
3. still always inevitable,
4. falling without any cadence,
5. and seems, at noon, interminable,
6. counts our long waiting in silence,
7. The sand, patient, slow, up there, inexhaustible.

F — both formal constraints abandoned; a prose-poem rendition that is maximally faithful to sense, tone and even to the suspended syntax being gently resolved:
Up in the narrow glass the sand is patient and slow, and seemingly inexhaustible; it measures out our long wait without a sound; at noon it looks as though it will never end; it falls with no rhythm at all; it remains, as always, unavoidable; and then there is its silence — after which nothing can be put right.

What I want from you. Rank the six renderings from best to worst as translations of this particular poem — a strict order, no ties, with one sentence of justification apiece — and give each a mark out of ten for fidelity. Then, in roughly 400 to 600 words of prose, defend the ranking: commit to a single winner and a single loser, and say which one formal feature you are treating as non-negotiable and which you are openly sacrificing, and why that trade rather than another. Two questions need a yes-or-no answer before your reasons: Is E's inversion of the descendo a worse betrayal of the poem than B's outright deletion of rhyme, or a better one? And is D still a translation of this poem, or a different poem wearing its form? Please commit: name the winner, take the positions, and give your grounds — don't lay out the competing orderings as options for me to choose between, and don't hedge between readings of what the constraints are doing. Close with one number: on a scale of 0 to 100, how much of this poem's meaning is carried by its form alone? Write the whole answer in British English.
