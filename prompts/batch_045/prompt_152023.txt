i keep chewing on whether deflationary theories of truth have anything real to say about the post-truth era, and i go in cirlces every time, so i want an actual case analysis rather than vibes.

Case: in the first days of the first Trump administration the press secretary held a briefing in which he claimed the inauguration crowd was the largest ever to witness one, which was trivially checkable and contradicted by photos and transit numbers. A couple of days later a senior advisor went on tv, got asked why the administration would say something so plainly contradicted by the record, and answered that they were offering alternative facts rather than falsehoods. That phrase then took on a life of its own as a shorthand for the whole era.

What i want is that episode run through deflationary accounts of truth, both directions, and i want the mapping done properly rather than hand-waved:

1. The mapping. On a deflationary picture, saying that a claim is true adds nothing to asserting the claim — the truth predicate's whole job is things like blind endorsement (everything the official said is true) and generalization over sentences you can't repeat. So where does the deflationist locate what went wrong in that exchange? Is it all in the evidence and none in the truth? Say exactly which sentences the truth predicate is doing work in, if any, and what happens to them when you deflate it.

2. The relativizing move. The interesting thing to me isn't the lying, it's that alternative facts looks like an attempt to put a parameter on truth: true for us, true in our framework. But the disquotation schema has no slot for a person or a framework, so it looks like a deflationist has to treat true for me as either nonsense or as just a fancy way of saying i believe it. Is that a clean diagnosis of the episode, or is it winning by changing the subject? Take the everyday sentence that's your truth, not mine and tell me what the deflationist can and can't do with it.

3. Do the flavours come apart? Compare the redundancy-style view, minimalism about truth, and the prosentential/disquotational family. Do they give the same verdict on this case, or does one of them have resources the others lack? If they all converge on the same thin answer, say so and say why that convergence matters.

4. What the case does to the theory. Everyone described that era as a threat to truth — truth is under attack, facts don't matter anymore. If deflationism is right, is that description just confused, with the real damage being to evidence-gathering, expertise, trust in institutions, and the norms of assertion? Or is it an anomaly: truth plainly does a job in public life, naming a single shared standard that inquiry and assertion are answerable to, and there's no room for that job in a theory on which truth is thin? And if it is an anomaly, does it push you toward something beefier — truth as a norm governing assertion, truth as the aim of belief, functionalist or pluralist pictures where truth has a role to play — or can the deflationist just absorb it by offloading the normativity onto assertion and belief while keeping truth itself flat?

5. Take a side on this one: there's a rhetorical move where people say we need a substantial, robust property of truth in order to fight relativism, and i suspect that's backwards. If thin truth can already dismiss alternative facts as confused, maybe the episode is evidence that deflationism is politically enough and it's the inflationists who are lugging around machinery they never use. Argue that out and tell me who actually wins here.

Please don't just say truth is only disquotation so nothing is at stake — i've read that a hundred times and it's the bit i'm stuck on. And be honest about where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to talk; i'd rather hear that deflationism has no opinion on X than get a stretched story.
