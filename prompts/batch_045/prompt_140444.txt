A friend whose judgement on philosophy I usually trust sent me this summary of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, ahead of a reading-group session I've agreed to lead. I'd like an accuracy audit before I build anything on it:

Strawson's Basic Argument is a version of the old argument from determinism. If what I choose is the inevitable upshot of my character and circumstances, and I did not author my character, then nothing I do is ever up to me. The argument's real move is the regress: to be responsible for what I do, I must be responsible for how I am; to be responsible for how I am, I must have chosen to be that way; and to choose that I would already have to have some character or nature that prompted the choice. So I would have to be causa sui, self-created, which is impossible. Strawson's conclusion is therefore hard incompatibilism. Since no one is ever morally responsible, resentment, gratitude and blame are never strictly appropriate — morally speaking there is no difference between a murderer and a landslide, though we cannot help reacting as if there were. The only real reply is to reject the second step: a selfish adult can, by a single effort of will, ratify or endorse the character he was handed, and that act of self-ratification makes the character genuinely his even though he never chose it — so he can be responsible without having created himself. Everything else in the literature is a variation on this, and in the end the argument is just the standard consequence argument dressed up.

Go through it claim by claim: what Strawson would accept as stated, what he would accept only with a qualification (say what it is), and what he would reject outright. I care most about three things. First, whether the determinism framing is fair, given that the argument is meant to be neutral between determinism and its denial, and how indeterminacy is supposed to fail to help. Second, whether the conclusion about blame is overstated in a way that matters — he is a sceptic about a certain kind of responsibility, not obviously about all of it, and I want to know where the line he actually draws sits. Third, whether the self-ratification reply is one his regress has the resources to absorb, working it through rather than citing a passage: what would he say about the ratifying act itself, and does the manoeuvre help if the ratifier's will is as much a given as the character was? Finally: if the summary's last sentence is wrong, is there a version of the argument that a compatibilist should still take seriously, and where exactly does the live disagreement then sit?
