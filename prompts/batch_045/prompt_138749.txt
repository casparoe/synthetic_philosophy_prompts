Last night a friend and I spent three hours circling the same loop. Every trait I offered as evidence for the adaptationist programme — a close match between form and an independently specified optimum, a heritable fitness effect, a phylogenetic correlation with the right ecological variable — she was able to redescribe as the residue of architectural constraint followed by co-option. And every constraint story she offered I was able to point out had long since been taken over and refined by selection. We never established whether we were disagreeing about the history of any organism or only about which question is worth asking.

So I would like a thought experiment built to specifications.

**What it is for, and therefore what counts as cheating.** It is for the second section of a paper I am drafting on whether the adaptationist programme is best defended as a research strategy or as an empirical claim about the causes of form. That fixes the failure condition: you may not stipulate the verdicts. The disagreement has to fall out of what each position is actually committed to.

**Specifications.**

1. One organism and one trait, or a small complex of two or three developmentally coupled traits, in realistic biology — a vertebrate, an arthropod, or a plant that a field biologist could plausibly work on. No aliens, no artificial selection, no access to deep time, no fitness oracle. It must not be one of the canonical textbook examples: no panda's thumb, no human chin, no male nipples, no actual Venetian architecture. One paragraph for the scenario.

2. The trait must be a strong case by the engineering criterion: there is an independently motivated optimality or mechanical model — one not fitted to the trait after the fact — that the trait's realized form matches closely, together with a demonstrable present-day fitness effect.

3. A byproduct story must be genuinely live rather than merely conceivable: there is a measurable, present-day mechanistic coupling — developmental, allometric, or structural — between this trait and some other feature that did change, such that the trait's presence would be unsurprising even if it had never been selected for.

4. The dialectical direction must be reversed. The standard complaint is that the adaptationist can absorb any finding by inventing a selective scenario. In your case it should be the constraint story, not the adaptationist one, that turns out able to absorb any outcome without being tested by it — and it should do so for a reason internal to that style of explanation, not because you handed the constraint theorist an unusually vague or weak hypothesis to defend.

5. **Verdicts required.** State what each of four positions must say about the trait, and why: the optimality or engineering adaptationist; the Gould–Lewontin structuralist-constraint critic; the co-optionist, who separates a trait's origin from its current utility and holds that selection routinely retrofits whatever structure is available; and the heuristic adaptationist, for whom adaptationist hypotheses are testable nulls to be discarded when they fail. At least one of the four must disagree with the others about whether the trait's presence calls for a historical explanation at all.

6. **Evidence constraint.** The evidence available to the researcher must be restricted to what present-day workers could actually possess — comparative and phylogenetic data, experimental manipulation, developmental and biomechanical evidence — and you must say which piece of evidence each verdict rests on. You may not stipulate that selection was observed acting in the past.

Then, after the scenario and the four verdicts: identify the single feature of the case doing the work; say what happens if you remove it, and in particular whether the case collapses back into the standoff my friend and I were stuck in, where each side can redescribe the other's evidence in its own vocabulary; give the strongest reply available to whichever position comes out worst — I assume the constraint theorist, who will want to say something about whether structural explanations are explanations of historical events at all; and finally, honestly, tell me whether the case supports my claim that there remains a factual disagreement about the retrofitting of traits, or my friend's claim that the dispute is verbal.

One last thing: I have been using the labels *passenger* and *scaffold* for the two variants of the byproduct story, depending on whether the trait merely comes along with something else that was selected or goes on to constrain which variants later selection can act on. Keep them if they are useful, replace them if they are not, but keep the distinction visible, since part of what I want to know is whether the two variants fare differently in your case.
