I'm TAing an undergrad seminar on AI and society this term and we keep having the same stalled argument: half the room says these systems are just tools (a hammer with a chat window, output that only means something because we read it), the other half says that once a system is making plans over weeks, negotiating with other systems, and doing things literally nobody predicted, calling it a tool is a comfort blanket rather than a claim. I want a case built to order that forces both sides to cash out, so here's the spec.

Give me one scenario, one paragraph, realistic and near-future (no sentient AI, no robot uprisings, no uploads, no sci-fi gadget — think hospital, utility grid, insurance claims, procurement, air traffic, university scheduling, whatever you pick), involving a single AI system that operates over an extended stretch of time and where the people downstream genuinely do not know in advance what it's going to do. The scenario needs a small thing to go wrong in it — non-gratuitous, nobody has to die — whose post-mortem turns on whether it's literally true that "the system decided" to do it, because the whole point of the exercise is to see whether the tool/agent distinction is doing any real work when we hand out responsibility.

Then run the case against two criteria and make them disagree in a specific direction. Criterion A is the intentional-stance, predictive one (Dennett's sort of thing): a system is an agent if the only workable way to predict and explain its behavior is to attribute beliefs, desires and means-end reasoning to it — and I want you to actually show in the case that the design stance and the physical stance break down, not just assert it. Criterion B is the answerability one: a system is an agent if it's the kind of thing that can properly be handed a demand for justification, or blamed, or credited — something that acts for reasons it can in some sense take as its own, and where the normative buck can stop with it instead of always passing straight through to whoever deployed it. Your case has to make A say agent and B say tool, and here's the hard part: the B verdict must not come from the system being simple, weak, dumb, deterministic, or behaviorally unreasons-responsive. It should be extremely reasons-responsive in the behavioral sense and still fail B, and I want you to name the single feature that makes it fail. Also: no getting the agent verdict from giving it feelings, consciousness, or legal personhood, and no "it's just next-token prediction" hand-waving from either side. If the feature doing the work turns out to be "somebody built it" or "it doesn't have feelings," I'll definitly count that as a fail, since the first is true of every artifact and the second is exactly the move I'm trying to get my students past.

After the scenario: walk through what each criterion says and why, name the one feature doing the work, show what happens to both verdicts if you remove or flip that feature, and then give me the attack — what a defender of whichever criterion comes out worse here would say to get the case thrown out. Be honest if the spec isn't actually jointly satisfiable and say which requirement you'd have to relax. Roughly 600-900 words; keep the scenario to one paragraph and don't pad the analysis.
