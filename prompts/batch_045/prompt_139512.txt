THE QUESTION (20 points, short answer):

Read the following fictional case and answer (a), (b) and (c).

Dr. Rhea Halvorsen's group at the Institute for Comparative Ethology announced last year that they can predict, from 40 milliseconds of premotor cortex activity, whether a rhesus macaque is about to give a deceptive food call — the kind that draws a rival away from a cached fig. In the press release, Halvorsen said the result dissolves the last skyhook in animal communication: intentional vocabulary like deception, she claimed, is a skyhook, and her decoding result is the crane that replaces it. She has also proposed a general criterion she calls the bottom-out test — an explanation counts as a crane only if every mechanism it cites operates at a level below the phenomenon being explained (neurons below behavior, chemistry below neurons, and so on down).

A magazine commentator replied that Halvorsen is a greedy reductionist. The decoder, he says, is a real finding, but she is using it to skip the intermediate rungs: the animal's social learning history, the developmental story about how the call gets calibrated, and the population-level selection pressure that keeps deception stable in the first place. He adds that Halvorsen's own supplementary data show the same 40 ms pattern firing before honest, kin-directed calls — which, on her own terms, means the pattern doesn't distinguish deception from anything else. Deception, he concludes, is a real pattern at its own level, not a placeholder waiting to be eliminated.

Dennett's gloss, for reference: good reductionists explain apparent design without skyhooks; greedy reductionists try to explain it without cranes.

(a) 8 pts. Is the commentator right that Halvorsen's move — treating deception as a skyhook and the neural decoder as its replacement crane — is greedy reductionism? Say precisely why or why not.
(b) 7 pts. The commentator leans on a population-level selectional explanation. Is that a crane or a skyhook on Dennett's terms? What does the difference actually consist in?
(c) 5 pts. Assess Halvorsen's bottom-out test as a rendering of Dennett's distinction.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER (transcribed as written):

The crane/skyhook distinction is about whether an explanation does its own lifting or gets a free ride. A skyhook is an unexplained explainer — God, a vital force, intrinsically intentional mental stuff that is simply posited to have content — something that hoists design into place without itself being grounded in anything dumber. A crane is a mechanism that really does the work and can itself be broken down into parts that do less impressive things. Greedy reductionism is the doctrine that all the higher-level explanations will eventually be eliminated once the fundamental science is in, and Dennett's complaint about it is that greedy reductionists try to skip the rungs: they explain away the level above instead of explaining it.

Halvorsen is doing exactly that, so on (a) the commentator is right. Her decoder is a genuine crane for the neural production of the call — it's a real, grounded mechanism and it deserves credit for that — but 40 ms of premotor activity is not a substitute for an account of deception. Finding the mechanism that implements a pattern doesn't show the pattern was a skyhook; it shows it was implemented. Using the neural result to declare the intentional vocabulary dissolved is skipping straight past the intermediate rungs and treating a description at one level as if it competed with a mechanism at another. That is textbook greedy reductionism.

That said, the commentator should be careful, because his own position is not obviously stable. If deception is a real pattern at its own level and is not eliminable in the way greedy reductionists expect, then he seems committed to higher-level causes that push the neurons around from above, and that is a skyhook of his own — a spooky top-down cause that does lifting without being grounded.

On (b), the population-level selection story is the weakest part of his reply. Explaining one animal's behavior by citing a process operating over whole populations means citing a mechanism larger than the thing to be explained, which is the opposite of what a crane is. Cranes are built out of smaller parts than the phenomena they lift. That story does no work that a lower-level account wouldn't do better.

On (c), the bottom-out test follows straightforwardly from Dennett's picture. Cranes lift from below: they are the grounded, decomposable machinery that replaces a skyhook. Anything that cites a mechanism at or above the level of the phenomenon being explained is, on Dennett's own terms, doing no real lifting — so the test captures the distinction correctly and simply makes explicit what his metaphor implies.
