I staff a standing committee at a private American university that adjudicates petitions about wrongs the institution inherited rather than committed. Typical petitions ask that a building named for a nineteenth-century donor be renamed, that a collection of ancestral remains be repatriated, that the university apologize for and make material amends for a historical expropriation of land, that a scholarship be endowed for the descendants of a group the university excluded, or that it do several of these at once. We meet monthly and handle fifteen to twenty petitions a year; each one produces written findings with reasons that go to the board of trustees, which has the final say. The committee has five members — a historian, a university lawyer, a faculty member, a student, and an alumni trustee — none of them a philosopher. Findings have to be drafted within about three weeks, be readable by someone with no training in the relevant literature, be consistent with what we have decided before, and survive being published. There is essentially no legal mandate behind any of this: in almost every case we are deciding what we owe, not what we are compelled to do.

What I need is a decision procedure, not a position paper. Concretely: an ordered set of questions a member can run through on a petition, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the points at which the procedure stops with a no and issues a short finding, and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the open question is one of values rather than fact.

Several things make this harder than it looks, and I want the procedure to handle them rather than hand-wave them.

First, petitions and press coverage run together four questions that I think have different answers and different conditions: whether the university should acknowledge that a wrong occurred and that it stands in some relation to it; whether it owes anyone anything, and what kind of thing (return of a specific object, repair, money, a program); whether it may or must apologize, and who is in a position to apologize for what; and whether it is entitled to describe itself as guilty. Please keep these separate, with separate conditions, and say which of them can be inherited across generations and which cannot.

Second, continuity. The institution routinely gets to be the same entity that owns a two-hundred-year-old endowment and a different entity when asked about the wrong that funded it. I want a continuity test that forbids that asymmetry, with sub-questions that a non-specialist can actually answer from an archivist's memo: legal and charter continuity; asset continuity; whether the wrong was done by someone acting in the institution's name or with its resources; whether it was ratified at the time or later, or condemned at the time and merely tolerated; and whether the institution still holds a benefit it would not hold but for the wrong. I also want the procedure to treat present-tense honoring — a name on a building, a portrait, a named chair — as a present act rather than a mere inheritance, and to say what follows from that.

Third, the wronged party and its successors. Some of our cases have an identifiable successor (a federally recognized tribe, a family whose land was taken); some have a dispersed successor set; some have rival claimants. Say what the procedure does when the successor set is contested, and what kinds of remedy remain available, or stop being available, when nobody can be identified to receive one. Relatedly: some wrongs are over and done with, and some are still going on — an object still held against a continuing objection, an exclusion still reproduced in present admissions or hiring patterns. Cases of the second kind need no theory of inherited guilt at all, and I want that recognized early in the procedure rather than buried.

Fourth, evidence and burden of proof. The university controls the archive, and in many cases the record is simply silent about the thing that matters. State where the burden lies, what the default is when the record is silent, and whether silence is a no or a maybe-and-then-what.

Fifth, adequacy of a remedy. I want a test for the failure mode where the institution buys public credit with a gesture that costs it nothing — a plaque, a land acknowledgment, a renaming that a donor's family had stopped caring about — and I want a test for the opposite failure mode, where the procedure ratchets toward whatever the most demanding petition asks for. Tell me how to check whether a proposed remedy is one the successor group would accept, and who is actually bearing its cost.

Sixth, precedent and cost. "If we do this we will get forty more" is a real consideration for a committee with a finite budget and a board that reads the newspapers, and it is also an easy way to do nothing. Say how floodgates may and may not enter the procedure, and include a consistency check against decisions the university has already made.

I know the fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt that Jaspers drew in his postwar writing on German guilt, and giving me those four labels back is not a procedure. But if one of them is doing real work at a particular step — for instance, if moral guilt is the one that cannot be inherited while something like political liability can — say which step it does the work in and what work it does there.

Please then run the finished procedure on three petitions in front of us:

1. A building named for an alumnus merchant whose fortune came from a firm that financed plantation mortgages in the 1840s and 1850s. His gift endowed a chair that is still funded from the original principal. The petition asks that the name come off and that the endowment be redirected to scholarships for descendants of the enslaved people on those plantations. The donor's family currently gives to the university separately and has told us privately that a separate large bequest will be withdrawn if the name comes down.

2. A teaching collection of ancestral remains and sacred objects taken in the 1920s by a professor working with university funds and university transport, whose field notes record the objections of the community at the time. A federally recognized tribe has requested repatriation of everything. Some of the individuals in the collection cannot be confidently affiliated with any present-day group.

3. A national organization demanding that the university, as an American institution, formally accept guilt for slavery and contribute to a national reparations fund. There is no specific institutional act and no traceable asset; the benefit, if there is one, is the diffuse benefit of operating inside a slave economy and its aftermath.

I expect at least one of these to come out somewhere my committee will not like — I would rather be told that than be told what we want to hear — and I expect the procedure to say plainly, somewhere, "this one is a values decision and the procedure stops here." Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will systematically misdescribe, and where a committee using it should distrust its own output.
