My friend and I spent most of last evening going round in circles about indirect normativity, and I came away with an argument that seems to demolish the position I hold while also seeming, somehow, to be up to something. I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature, and I would be grateful if you answered in British English.

The position I hold is what I will call the split view. The property that makes an act right — the criterion — is maximizing impartial expected value. But the procedure an agent like me should actually use to decide is a set of acquired non-consequentialist dispositions: keep your promises without first checking whether keeping them is best, favour your own children, do not aggregate across people in real time. The split is itself justified consequentially. For agents like us, real-time consequentialist deliberation makes things worse, because we lack information, we rationalise, and the habit of calculating corrodes precisely the relationships and commitments that do most of the moral work.

Here is my friend's argument as I reconstructed it this morning.

(1) On the split view, the agent's dispositions are the procedure she is to use, and whatever authority they have is borrowed entirely from the consequentialist criterion.

(2) Any such disposition must at some point be either exercised or set aside, and the agent must decide which. Call a disposition sealed if it is to be exercised no matter what the agent foresees, and porous if she may set it aside when she judges that setting it aside would be better.

(3) A sealed disposition is one she exercises even when she can see, without doing any calculation at all, that following it will produce a disaster. But on the split view rightness is a matter of consequences; a disposition that holds firm exactly where the consequences are worst is not tracking the criterion that is supposed to justify it. So her dispositions cannot be sealed. This is the familiar charge of rule-worship, and I granted it.

(4) So they must be porous. But then, in any given case, whether to exercise or override the disposition has to be settled by some judgment that tracks the criterion. And the only judgment that tracks the criterion is an estimate of expected value: will deferring do better here than overriding?

(5) That estimate is precisely the first-order consequentialist calculation whose cost was the original reason for abandoning first-order consequentialist calculation. So the split view destroys its own rationale: it forbids the very reasoning that its own account of the dispositions requires.

(6) Hence the split view is unstable. Either the agent's dispositions are sealed, and the view has quietly abandoned its criterion and become a fixed-code view in everything but name; or they are porous, and the agent is a calculating consequentialist after all. There is no stable middle.

(7) And the argument generalises. Any view that separates what makes acts right from how an agent should decide needs a handover rule — a rule saying when to hand deliberation over to the acquired procedure and when to take it back — and every handover rule faces the dilemma in (3) to (5). That includes the ideal-deliberation view I am also attracted to, on which what I have reason to do is what I would want myself to do if I deliberated from my own motivational set with full information and full imaginative vividness. That view fixes reasons by a counterfactual procedure, and I still have to decide, here and now, whether to defer to its deliverances; so I still need a handover rule.

(8) But the phenomenon of self-defeating deliberation is real — some procedures genuinely are worse to run than to have — and indirect views are not obviously silly. So one of (1) to (7) has to go.

I tried the obvious reply, which is to give the dispositions built-in defeater clauses — keep your promises unless keeping them would be cruel, and so on. My friend said this only relocates the problem: either the defeater clauses are themselves justified by their expected value, in which case we are back at (4) and (5), or they are arbitrary, in which case we are back at rule-worship. I could not answer that, and I do not think it is right, but I cannot say why.

Please tell me which step you would reject, and why that step looked sound when it was introduced. Then tell me what rejecting it costs me: specifically, whether I can keep both the consequentialist criterion and the claim that non-consequentialist deliberation is often the procedure one should use, or whether one of the two has to be softened, and into what. If you think the trouble lies in the sealed/porous dichotomy, say exactly which distinction is being run together, and what a genuine third option would have to look like in order to be more than a relabelling of the dilemma.
