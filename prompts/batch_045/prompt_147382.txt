A friend who works in longtermist philanthropy keeps pressing an argument I can't shake. If one cosmological theory says the universe holds a billion observers and an equally well-supported one says a billion billion, then my very existence is supposed to count as overwhelming evidence for the second; and that is supposed to underwrite the claim that the human future is vast, and so worth directing money towards. I don't believe the inference. But when I try to say why not, my reactions to neighbouring cases don't line up, and I'd like to know what principle I'm actually appealing to. I'm deciding whether to move a large share of my giving in that direction, so this isn't idle. I've seen the labels self-sampling and self-indication, and I don't care about the labels.

My verdicts, on my gut:

1. A species either dies out after 100 members or continues to a billion. I find myself at birth rank 50. I do think this raises the odds of the early end — moderately, not dramatically. That one I won't give up.

2. Two cosmological theories, equally well supported by telescopes, predicting a billion observers and a billion billion. I don't think my existence moves me at all, and nothing follows for philanthropy.

3. A fair coin is flipped: heads, one observer is created in a room; tails, a thousand, in identical rooms with identical experiences. I wake up in a room. I feel a real tilt towards tails — modest, but I can't make it go away.

4. As in 3, except that on tails the extra 999 are created in a separate, causally disconnected space-time that never interacts with mine. The tilt disappears.

5. Two hypotheses, each with exactly a thousand observers just like me; one of them also contains a million thin observers — genuinely conscious, but with far simpler and more fragmentary experiences, none ever in my evidential position. My gut says they count for something, but less than the full ones, and I can't say how much.

1 and 2 sit comfortably together. 3 and 4 pull apart a distinction that 2 says shouldn't matter, and 5 suggests that counting comes in degrees rather than yes-or-no.

My own stab: weight each possible observer by the fraction of their total evidence that matches mine, count only those inside my causal order, and let the weighted total be the hypothesis's likelihood. Test it against the five verdicts, repair it where it breaks, and tell me plainly which verdicts it can't keep. Then tell me what the repaired version says about a case I haven't listed, and which I think is decisive: both hypotheses posit infinitely many observers overall, but in different proportions of observers with my evidence — where the two familiar moves both seem to stall. And finally: does any of it rescue my friend's argument, or does 2 survive?
