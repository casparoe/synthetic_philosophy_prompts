I'm a PhD student in human-computer interaction doing fieldwork on how organisations actually use written rule systems — hospital escalation protocols, a housing co-op's rulebook, content-moderation guidelines, and (this one is from a side project) a distributed-systems spec. Case one. A hospital protocol has two rules that both fire: escalate immediately if a patient's sats drop below X, and never escalate without a registrar's sign-off. At the incident review, the nurse who escalated without sign-off was described as having done the right thing, and also as having a case to answer — and both of those were said in the same breath, by the same people, without anyone thinking the second remark cancelled the first or demoted either rule to a guideline.

Case two. The co-op passed one rule in 2019 (every member votes on any decision affecting their tenancy) and another in 2022 (no member votes on a matter where they have a financial interest), both entrenched, both still in the book. When they collided over the bike-shed conversion, the chair's line was: both rules are in force, we are not repealing either, we are not saying either was a mistake, we note the collision in the minutes, and here is the procedure we follow when it happens.

Case three. Two invariants in a spec that provably can't both hold during a partition. The engineers won't weaken either one or delete either one. They built a component whose entire job is to notice when both can't hold and keep the consequences inside one module so the rest of the system can still be reasoned about normally.

So the features I think matter: (i) both requirements stay fully in force, for the same agent, at the same time; (ii) the conflict isn't resolved by ranking, by repair, by calling one of them merely provisional, or by adding an exception clause; (iii) it doesn't leak — nobody concludes that nothing is really required, or that everything is permitted, or that the rulebook is worthless; (iv) there's a repeatable procedure, and part of that procedure is a kind of accounting afterwards, which is not the same as a punishment and not the same as saying the conflict was fine.
