I'm the TA for an upper-level metaethics seminar, and I have to grade and defend a mark on the following take-home answer. Please grade it.

**The question (one of four; nominally 30 minutes of a 2-hour exam).**

(a) Set out the two horns of Street's Darwinian Dilemma as they bear on the robust realist, and say what each horn is supposed to show. (b) A robust realist proposes the following way out; call it the Supervenient Coverage Reply. "Moral facts supervene with metaphysical necessity on descriptive facts. Suppose evolution built us so as to track reliably the descriptive facts about cooperation, reciprocity and kinship. Then, given supervenience, the moral beliefs those mechanisms produce are guaranteed to be true: the realist can concede the debunker's premise — that selection was entirely insensitive to moral truth — and still claim that our moral beliefs are by and large correct. The correlation between moral belief and moral fact is then a metaphysical necessity, not a coincidence crying out for explanation." Assess this reply. (c) State whether your objection in (b) also tells against the standard third-factor reply, i.e. the reply that cites a single factor which both made certain moral judgments adaptive and made the corresponding moral facts obtain.

**The student's answer.**

Both horns concern what the realist can say about why our moral beliefs match the moral facts, supposing they do. On the tracking horn, the realist claims that evolutionary pressure led our ancestors to hold the moral beliefs they did *because* those beliefs were true — the moral facts themselves did explanatory work. Street regards this as a non-starter, since the best evolutionary explanations of moral judgment cite the fitness benefits of cooperation and reciprocity and never mention moral facts. On the sceptical horn, the realist concedes that selection was insensitive to the moral facts; the debunker then argues that we have no reason to think our moral beliefs are true, since we would have held roughly the same beliefs whether or not they were true. (Street herself is not an error theorist: she takes some evaluative judgments to be true, relative to a standpoint.)

The Supervenient Coverage Reply tries to slip between the horns. Its appeal is that it seems to get reliability for free: if the descriptive facts in the supervenience base are tracked truly, and the moral facts ride necessarily on those facts, then moral error becomes impossible. But I think it fails. Supervenience is a relation among facts, not a competence of a mechanism. Selection does not aim at descriptive truth either — false but useful representations can be fitness-enhancing — and even a mechanism that represented the base perfectly would still have to bring the right moral concepts to bear on it, which is a separate achievement. So supervenience guarantees nothing about beliefs, and the realist still owes us an account of why our moral beliefs line up with the moral facts.

Two further points. First, the coincidence objection — the complaint that the realist is left with an unexplained harmony between our moral beliefs and the moral facts, sometimes called the pre-established harmony problem — is best understood as the tracking horn in different words: both ask the realist to show that the moral facts made a difference to what we came to believe. Since that demand is precisely what the Supervenient Coverage Reply abandons, the reply has nothing left to answer, and the realist is thrown back onto the sceptical horn. The debunking argument is therefore vindicated, and robust realism collapses into the error theory. Second, as to (c): my objection does not generalize. The third-factor reply relies on supervenience not at all; it says that one and the same factor — the social value of cooperation, say — both made certain moral judgments adaptive and made the corresponding moral facts obtain. That is a genuine explanation of the correlation, so my complaint about mechanism reliability does not touch it.
