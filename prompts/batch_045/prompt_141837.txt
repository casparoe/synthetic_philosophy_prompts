so — i'm writing a novel and I've painted myself into a corner: one of my characters has to have written a poem, and I can't write poems.

The book is set mostly in the clinical audit office of a made-up NHS trust (present day, but the printers and the fax machine are from about 2003). Bernie, late fifties, does the audit, keeps everything in a ring binder, and has found the following in her printout. Two drugs for the same thing. The new one was expensive and rationed, so it went mostly to the patients who were already bad on arrival; the old one went mostly to the ones who walked in. Of the 150 who got the new drug: 45 of the 50 mild cases got better, 30 of the 100 severe cases got better — 75 in 150. Of the 150 who got the old drug: 80 of the 100 mild cases got better, 10 of the 50 severe cases got better — 90 in 150. So the new drug wins in both bands and loses overall. (I have checked this adds up; it does. Don't quietly fix my numbers.)

What I need is the poem she writes about it in the back of the binder, and it has to survive a statistician reading it: no wincing at the maths, and no suggestion that the table contradicts itself.

Form: a pantoum, six quatrains, each rhyming ABAB, with the usual machinery — the second and fourth lines of each quatrain come back as the first and third of the next, and the closing quatrain takes back the two lines it hasn't used yet, the first stanza's third line and then the first stanza's first line, so the poem's last line is, word for word, the line it opened on (punctuation can shift). Which is really the whole point: I want a line that in the first stanza sounds like a verdict on the drug and in the last stanza sounds like a fact about who was handed it. The repetitions should be doing the work — the same lines turning up in different company and meaning something else, which is more or less exactly the thing she's staring at, tho she wouldn't put it like that.

What it has to get right:

- Nothing here is contradictory. It's weighted averages, and the reason it flips is that the two drugs didn't get comparable patients — two thirds of the new drug's patients were severe, only a third of the old drug's. Bernie's actual gripe is that nobody randomised anything.
- The turn: the two readings aren't rivals, they answer different questions. "Is the new drug better?" wants the split by severity, because severity is what pushed patients into one arm or the other and it's also what kills them — like with like. "What happened to the people who got it?" wants the top line. Both numbers are true, and they only look like a fight if you can't hear the difference.
- A second, smaller turn, later on: slicing is not automatically the honest move. Bernie's boss wants her to break it down by whether the fever had gone by morning — something the drug itself causes — and that sort of slice can lie in the opposite direction. So "just break it down" isn't a reflex you can trust.
- She never calls it a paradox and she doesn't know it has a name. No jargon, no "lies, damned lies", no correlation-isn't-causation bumper sticker, and don't let it turn into a table with line breaks. It's a tired woman being funny about denominators.

Someone with a pencil should be able to rebuild my little table from the poem, so all four cells and both totals need to be in there — but in her voice, not in brackets.

British English throughout please (favour, learnt, -ise where you'd use it, trust/ward/consultant sort of vocabulary). And no notes or explanation tacked on after it — I'll get my partner to read it and he'll tell me if its wrong.
