I'm an undergrad in a philosophy-and-lit course and I'm giveing a ten-minute after-dinner speech at the end of our course's Freedom and Happiness symposium, and I'm completely stuck. The occasion is a retirement dinner for Professor Alina Vance, who taught our Dostoevsky and political philosophy module, spent her career arguing that the Grand Inquisitor chapter is basically the most devastating attack on the idea that people can be made happy by being given freedom, and who definately had zero patience for any sentimental talk about the dignity of suffering (she thought that was just a way of making peace with other people's pain). The audience is mixed: half are literature undergrads who read the chapter for the first time last week and think Ivan is cool and mysterious, and half are analytic philosophy postgrads who think freedom just means not being coerced or having effective options, and who will switch off if I start doing literary impressionism. Professor Vance's festschrift is called Happiness Without Burden, and the speech has to toast her without turning into a hagiography or, worse, a sneaky critique of her. Here's the twist I want to build it around: imagine a version of the Inquisitor's society where the engineered happiness is frictionless — no-one is ever tortured or forced to confess, a gentle predictive system just quietly removes every situation in which someone might make a serious moral mistake, so they end up contented, harmless, and incapable of choosing wrongly. I want the speech to argue that this is an even darker version of the Inquisitor's bargain than the one in the novel, because it doesn't just trade freedom for bread and peace, it deletes the loss that makes freedom tragic, and so turns the contented masses into something like pets rather than forgiven sinners. The Grand Inquisitor's distinction between freedom and happiness has to do the real structural work: it should decide what I can praise the professor for, what I refuse to say about suffering, and how I end on a note about why we owe more to people who can choose badly than to people who are safely happy. Can you write the speech? Please write the speech itself in British English, around 700 words, warm but a bit provocative, and no footnotes or citations — just the words I'd actually say.
