Hey — I'm one of five maintainers on a pretty widely used open-source agent framework (think tens of thousands of downstream projects, some of them wired into hospital record systems and payment flows). Any one of us can merge to main and cut a release, no second approver required, and that's not going to change — the whole point of the team is that we move fast. We merge maybe fifteen things a week. Most are obviously trivial. But every couple of weeks something lands that isn't: it has a real upside, a tail risk that would hit every downstream user at once, and no way to recall it once it's out.

There's this idea I keep half-remembering from a paper somewhere — the unilateralist's curse, or something like it — about situations where lots of people can each act alone, the benefit only pays out once no matter how many act, but the downside gets shared by everybody. The upshot as I understand it is that the more people who could each pull the trigger, the more each individual one of them should hold off, because the chance that *someone* acts on thin evidence goes up with the number of people, while the upside doesn't. We want to actually run that.

So: write us a decision procedure we can run in twenty minutes, on a laptop, by someone who has never read any decision theory, on any PR that isn't obviously trivial. I need:

- the questions in the order we ask them, and what counts as a good enough answer to each (we can't get real numbers on most of this — give us coarse bands and say what to do when even a band is a guess);
- where the procedure just stops with a no and kicks the thing up to the release lead or to our slow two-week review path;
- where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because the question is about values, not facts — my teammates will bin anything that pretends to be a flowchart all the way down, so be explicit about which steps are judgement calls wearing a lab coat;
- and the distinctions from the actual idea that have to survive the simplification, versus the ones we can drop for the sake of it being usable. Don't water it down into generic "be careful when the stakes are high".

A few things I think it has to handle, which is where I keep tying myself in knots: upside that's redundant (if any of five of us ships it, shipping it twice adds nothing) versus upside that accumulates; the counterfactual where someone else on the team — or someone in the wider ecosystem, call it the shadow quorum, since plenty of other forks could independently ship the same change — would have done it in the next month anyway, so my acting isn't what produces the benefit; the fact that the downside is shared and non-excludable while the credit accrues to me; and the point I find spookiest, that the people who end up acting are selected for being the ones whose evidence was thinnest, so my own confidence isn't really the variable — presumably the question is closer to "how many people with my evidence would ship this". There's also the mirror image: sometimes *not* merging is the unilateral move with the scary tail, and deferring to the group is itself a way of acting.

Then run it on three real-ish cases:

1. An outside contributor's PR adding an escape hatch to the command sandbox. Big user win, fast, and a maybe-one-in-a-hundred chance of a sandbox escape that would compromise every downstream deployment simultaneously.
2. A PR that quietly downgrades the agent's capabilities whenever it thinks it's being used for something on a blocklist. Could prevent a lot of harm, could also be a rug-pull that destroys trust in the project, and we genuinely can't tell which yet.
3. My own patch. It closes a prompt-injection hole that's being exploited in the wild right now, I've spent two weeks on it, I'm confident it's good, and it rewrites the tool-call parser — so if it's subtly wrong it breaks every downstream app in a way that looks like the model failing. Run the procedure honestly on this one and if it comes out as "don't merge today", please just say so plainly; I'd definately rather hear it from you than read it in a postmortem.

Finally, tell us what the procedure can't do — especially anything about us five all being similar enough that our errors are correlated, which I suspect quietly breaks the whole thing.

Oh and write it in British English, our docs are all en-GB and I get moaned at otherwise. No need to tell me who came up with the idea or where to read more about it.
