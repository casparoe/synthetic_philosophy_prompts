I convene a fortnightly problem session for a small group of quantitatively trained master's students — mostly finance and statistics people with no philosophy background — and I am assembling one sheet on betting criteria and growth-rate reasoning. The pedagogical target is specific: I want to break the habit of reciting the slogan that expected value is a sucker's criterion, and to get across the point I have become convinced of but cannot get onto the page cleanly, namely that "maximise the growth rate" and "maximise what will typically happen" are two different rules of thumb that march together only asymptotically, and that over the sort of horizon a person actually faces the second can be the most timid of the lot. So I need a case that forces arithmetic rather than recollection.

What I want you to build is a single repeated gamble — one decision-maker, one bankroll, the same terms each round, stake any fraction of current wealth, winnings reinvested — under these constraints. It must be realistic: something a person could genuinely do, such as backing long-priced runners on a betting exchange, buying deep out-of-the-money options, or writing small angel cheques; no casino with a reset button, no magical bookmaker, no perfect foresight. The number of rounds must be fixed and modest — say twenty or fewer, because that is what "a person actually faces" means here. There must be no borrowing, no outside income, no partial cash-out, no pooling or sharing with other bettors, and no ensemble or population of parallel selves anywhere in the story: the bettor is one person compounding one pot. The gamble must be genuinely favourable in expected-value terms, so that the expected-wealth maximiser's answer is the boring one, stake everything, and the interest lies elsewhere. And the three verdicts I need separated must fall out of the three criteria alone, with no additional assumptions about the bettor's feelings about risk: (i) maximising expected terminal wealth says stake everything; (ii) the Kelly criterion, understood as maximising expected log wealth per round, says stake a small but strictly positive fraction; (iii) maximising the median of terminal wealth — the outcome of the typical bettor — says stake nothing at all, declining a bet that is favourable in expectation and favourable in log terms too. Show me the arithmetic for each of the three, including the binomial probabilities that pin down the median, and keep the scenario itself to a single short paragraph.

My own attempt goes the wrong way round, which is why I am asking: with twelve rounds at 20/1 and a true winning chance of 6 per cent, the stake that maximises the median comes out at roughly 3.75 per cent of bankroll while the Kelly stake is roughly 1.3 per cent, so the median rule looks bolder than Kelly, and in any case a 26 per cent edge is not something a real market hands out. Retune it or rebuild it so that the ordering comes out as specified. Then give me the diagnostics: which feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the three verdicts if that feature is removed, in particular if the horizon is allowed to grow without bound or if the payoff is made symmetric instead of long-shot-like; roughly at what horizon the median stake first becomes positive, since that is the honest measure of how fragile the effect is, and whether the convergence back onto Kelly is smooth or lumpy; and how a defender of each criterion would attack the case — the Kelly advocate objecting that the median is not the criterion the growth-rate argument actually recommends, and the median advocate replying that a single finite life gets one realisation and that the growth rate is an asymptotic property the bettor may never live to see. Finally, in one sentence at the end, tell me which criterion you would hand to the person in the story and why. If the constraints cannot all be met at once, say plainly which one has to go and what that concedes. Please answer in British English, and do not include references or suggestions for further reading — I want only the case, the numbers, and the analysis.
