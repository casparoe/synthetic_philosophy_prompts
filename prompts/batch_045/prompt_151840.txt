I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've got a scene I can't get right until I understand the argument my character is making. Short version: two estranged sisters, Ada and Bo, sit in separate rooms and each gets the same one-shot offer — "share" or "grab." Both share, each gets 5. Both grab, each gets 1. One grabs, the other shares, grabber gets 10, sharer gets 0. No communication, no reputation, twenty years of silence between them. Ada shares, and I want her reasoning to be the actual argument rather than vibes. So I wrote the thing below to get my own head straight. Could you read it and tell me where it's wrong, where it's boring-and-already-known, and where it's actually onto something?

---

**Notes toward "Two Sisters and a Lockbox" (the argument Ada makes in ch. 11)**

**1. Superrationality as a restriction on the outcome space, not a causal or evidential claim.**
Standard game-theoretic reasoning holds the other player's action fixed, varies only mine, and notes that grabbing dominates. What Ada does instead is hold the *profile* fixed: she deletes the off-diagonal cells — (share, grab) and (grab, share) — from the table *before* maximizing. Not because she thinks they're improbable, but because she takes them to be unavailable. If the solution is a determinate function of the situation, and Ada's situation and Bo's situation are relevantly one situation, then asymmetric outcomes aren't candidates at all. On this reading superrationality isn't "my act causes yours" and it isn't "my act is evidence about yours." It's: narrow the candidate set to the diagonal, *then* run ordinary maximization. Hofstadter's version, as I remember it from the June 1983 Scientific American column (reprinted in *Metamagical Themas*), is usually glossed with that recursive line about superrational thinkers including in their calculations the fact that they're in a group of superrational thinkers, and I think the "restriction" gloss is the cleanest way to cash that out.

**2. The restriction reading and the evidential reading are different, and I want them to come apart in my scene — but I'm honestly not sure they do.**
If Ada were doing evidential decision theory, her credence that Bo shares would ride on how confident she is that Bo reasons this way, and it would degrade continuously as that confidence drops. Bo has spent twenty years as a competitive powerlifter and a day trader. So: EDT Ada grabs, restriction-Ada shares. Except — and this is the part I keep going in circles on — if I actually write out the expected utilities with a credence q that Bo is superrational, and a baseline rate r at which a non-superrational Bo shares, both readings seem to deliver a threshold in q, and the thresholds only separate when r > 0. If the divergence needs a nonzero r to show up, the distinction in §1 might be doing much less work than I want it to, and Ada's big speech is about a distinction with no cash value. Is that right? Is there a cleaner case where the two readings genuinely diverge?

**3. The restriction reading needs a correspondence relation it doesn't have.**
"Everyone does the same thing" is only well-defined given a canonical map from my option set to yours. Symmetric case: obvious. But scale all of Bo's payoffs by 1.2, or give Bo a third option ("share, but keep the receipts"), and "the same thing" is suddenly up for grabs — pair Bo's "grab" with Ada's "share" and you're left with a different diagonal and a different verdict. As far as I can tell there's no agreed extension of superrationality to asymmetric games, and it looks to me like that's not an oversight but a symptom: the rule has an unstated input.

**4. My fix, which I'm calling role-neutral superrationality.**
Don't ask "what should everyone do." Ask: what distribution over *roles* would I want the population to instantiate, given that I don't know which role I occupy? Choose a mixed strategy for the population, and evaluate by expected utility with the expectation taken over roles, not just over the other player's move. In the symmetric two-person case this hands back the ordinary superrational answer. It also handles the "exactly one of you must act" setup: everyone acts and nobody wins, nobody acts and nobody wins, so the answer has to be randomized — send the telegram with probability 1/N. That's the move Hofstadter makes in his telegram scenario (the "Platonia" thing — the name may be wrong, I read a battered used copy years ago and definately don't trust my memory on it).

**5. The bit I like most and trust least: independent randomization doesn't fix it.**
If all N superrational players independently randomize at p = 1/N, the probability that exactly one acts is (1 − 1/N)^(N−1), which for large N is about 1/e. Not 1. Thirty-seven percent. For a million players you'd burn two-thirds of the value purely for want of a shared coin. So role-neutral superrationality only delivers the good outcome if the population can *correlate* its randomization — a public signal, a shared lottery, a rendezvous. Absent correlation it's a partial fix with a hard ceiling, and the ceiling doesn't go away by thinking harder. This is dramaturgically useful to me, because in act two Ada and Bo are in the exactly-one setup and genuinely cannot communicate, so the ceiling *is* the plot.
