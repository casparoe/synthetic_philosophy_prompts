I'm stuck on the lying-vs-misleading thing and I'd like you to actually explain it rather than define it. Where I am: I don't care about the definitional move (lying is asserting what you believe to be false, so misleading isn't lying, so there's your difference). I want to know what the mechanism of the extra wrongness is supposed to be, assuming it's real.

Set the case up so the usual variables are controlled. You're the only person who knows whether X happened. To A you say the flat false sentence: X didn't happen. To B you say something strictly true that you've engineered, knowing everything you know about B, to leave them certain that X didn't happen. Same false belief produced, same intention, same downstream consequences, B never gets corrected. If there's still a moral difference, what is it made of? If there isn't, what's the best explanation of why the intuition that there is one is so stubborn?

Two things I want you to push on rather then glide over. One: is deniability load-bearing? Suppose B's version was chosen partly because, if challenged, you can honestly say "I never said that." Is any extra blame there about the act itself, or is it just blame for cowardice stapled on? Two: my own guess is that the real variable is who authors the falsehood — whether you put a falsehood into the shared record or arrange for the hearer to generate it themselves — which predicts the difference nearly vanishes in contexts that assign the audience no epistemic work (a small child, a chatbot, someone panicking) and is at its thickest in contexts like cross-examination or peer review where the audience is supposed to do its own. Tell me where that story breaks.

And please don't settle any of it by saying it depends on the relationship or the consequences — I want the shape of the explanation, not the verdict.
