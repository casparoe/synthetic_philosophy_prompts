I'm putting together the final for my upper-level seminar on blame and the reactive attitudes (mixed MA students and advanced undergrads) and I need one essay question with a model answer and a marking scheme. Last year I made the mistake of setting something like "is hypocritical blame objectionable, and if so why?", and every paper came back as a restatement of the two stock families of answer — the one that locates the problem in what the blamer's blame implicitly commits them to (that they'd accept the norm applying to people like themselves), and the one that locates it in the blamer being blameworthy for blaming given their own record. Fine as far as it goes, but the top and the middle of the class were indistinguishable, so this year I want a question whose crux is somewhere the readings don't really go.

The thing I've been kicking around with a colleague over coffee is what she calls "unqualified" blamers: cases where the blamer hasn't done anything wrong, hasn't done the thing they're blaming the target for, and isn't being inconsistent in any obvious sense — but where the "you of all people" or "you're in no position" reply still seems to have some grip. E.g. someone who has never been remotely tempted by the kind of thing they're blaming you for and genuinely can't imagine it; someone who quietly benefits from the arrangement that made your wrongdoing the path of least resistance; a manager who enforces the rule strictly downward but not upward; someone who did exactly the thing they're now blaming you for but was fully justified in doing it. My question is whether the standard machinery has anything to say about these, or whether it quietly just doesn't apply. If it does apply, what's the unifying condition? If it doesn't, are we looking at two different phenomena that both get called "standing" — and does the second one deserve the name at all, or is it really about unfairness, or a bad epistemic position, or about what the blamer is implicitly claiming about their comparative moral status?

So: write me one 90-minute exam question (out of 20 marks) that forces students to take a position on that. It should also require them to settle, or at least address, two sub-questions: (a) what the failure actually is — is blame without standing infelicitous in the way an assertion made without knowledge is (does the "blaming is like asserting" analogy hold up here), or is it a further wrong the target may properly resent, or something else entirely, and how would we tell which it is by looking at what the target's appropriate response is; and (b) whether you can lack standing to blame yourself, since if standing tracks your own record then unrepentant self-blame looks like it should be defective too, which smells like a reductio. Deliverables: the question as I'd actually put it on the page (with whatever brief case descriptions it needs), a model answer of roughly 700–800 words, and a marking scheme with five or six must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one- or two-line note on how a middling student typically botches each one. The model answer shouldn't be a fence-sitting survey — pick a side and defend it, but flag the one objection you'd concede is hardest. Dont bother with author names or citations, I'll fill those in myself, and I dont want the scheme to depend on students remembering who said what.
