I fell down a rabbit hole reading about "information cascades" — the thing where it's rational to just copy the person in front of you, because their choice carries information you don't have, so after a few people the crowd locks in and everybody's own private signal gets thrown away. I've been trying to use it on the great toilet paper panic of spring 2020 and it definately half fits and half doesn't, so I want someone to do the mapping properly.

Facts as I remember them (say if I've got one wrong): within a week or two everyone was stripping the shelves; manufacturers and grocery people kept insisting there was no supply problem — that the stuff is bulky and low-margin and the whole system runs on a few days of slack, so any spike in buying empties the shelf even though nobody was actually using more paper; and the shelves stayed patchy for months.

What I want:

- The mapping, done honestly: who the agent is, what counts as a private signal, what the public signal is (the bare shelf itself? a photo of somebody's overloaded cart?), and what the hidden state of the world was that everyone was guessing at.
- My main problem with it: the state people were trying to guess — will there be paper when I need it — was partly *created* by people buying. I've started calling that a "self-licking cascade" and I want to know whether that's just a cascade with the state written down wrongly, or a genuinely different animal (self-fulfilling prophecy, coordination problem with two equilibria), and whether the model has any resources for saying which one we landed in.
- The fragility bit: I read that cascades are fragile and a little public information is supposed to break them. There were weeks of official statements that supply was fine, and nothing broke. Is that an anomaly for the theory, or is "somebody saying so" just not the right kind of public signal next to "I saw the empty shelf with my own eyes" — and can the model actually make that distinction, or does it treat all public information the same?
- One more: in the model each person decides once, in a fixed order, and never again. Real people shop every week for months. Shouldn't a repeat-purchase version of the story have collapsed the cascade in a week or two, once shelves restocked? It didn't. Does the theory say anything about how these things end, or is that just outside what it does?

Where I think it gets interesting: if the shelves really were empty, stocking up was rational, so there's no irrational herd to point at — the cascade manufactured the very shortage it was a sensible response to. Tell me whether you buy that framing and where it overreaches.

And please: say how confident you are in each part of what you claim, and name the single point you're least sure of.
