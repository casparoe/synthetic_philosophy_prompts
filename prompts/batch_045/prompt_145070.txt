I'm a novelist, three books out, and twice a year I mentor six writers for a low residency program — I get a 2,000 word chunk from each of them every fortnight, notes back within the week, and I share the load with a co-tutor who is a poet and has read zero theory and won't. So I have maybe twenty minutes per chunk, I can't read the whole manuscript, and whatever I come up with has to be pasteable into feedback notes without making me sound like a crank. Also the writers have started quoting Tolstoy at each other in workshop, which is my fault, so now I need something better than vibes.

I fell down a rabbit hole with Tolstoy's What Is Art (finished 1897, published in English first because of the censors) and here is what I think I took away, tell me if I have it wrong: art is when someone calls up a feeling they once had and passes it to other people through external signs, so the mark of art is infection rather than beauty; how strongly it infects depends mostly on how sincere the maker was, i.e. whether they were actually feeling the thing; there are four usual ways of faking it — borrowing, imitation, effectfulness, diversion — with borrowing being recycled props off a fairly specific list (moonlight, doves, lions, the sea, long hair), imitation being the descriptive-realism version where a scene turns into a list of faces and weather; then the weird part, which is that very bad feelings still count as art if they infect, but good art is the stuff that carries feelings of brotherhood, or plain everyday feelings accessible to everybody, and on that basis he throws out Shakespeare and Beethoven and most of his own novels. That last bit is the part I keep arguing about.

What I want is a procedure, not an essay. A set of gates I run in order on a chunk, where each gate has one question, a description of what counts as a yes, and a rule for what happens on a no (stop as not-art, stop as bad-art, pass through, or hand it to me because it's a values call), plus a rough minute count per gate so I can see whether this fits in twenty minutes. Constraints worth respecting:

- I can't test sincerity by asking the writer did you feel it, they always say yes, so I need proxies. Two I invented and want you to vet rather than bless: the ninth-read test (read it aloud a ninth time in a foul mood; if it still lands it was infection, if it's flat it was effectfulness), and the naming test (three readers who don't know my intention each name the feeling in one sentence, unprompted; two naming the same feeling counts as infection, and if they name a subject instead of a feeling — it's about grief — that's report, not transmission). Tell me honestly which of those is actually in Tolstoy and which is me dressing up a mood as a test.
- The accessibility gate worries me. My writers work in dialect, inside particular immigrant households, in a queer nightlife register, and Tolstoy's complaint about obscurity and in-group allusion reads to me like a class snobbery test if I state it straight. Give me a version of that gate that keeps whatever he was actually after without penalising work rooted in a specific world, or tell me to drop the gate and say why.
- Sincerity needs a time index and I don't know which one he'd pick. Which moment has to be the felt one: the draft, the revision, reading it out loud to the group, or reading it in the finished book? There is a real difference for us, because half of what gets submitted is rewriting.
- No theory vocabulary in the output. My co-tutor has to be able to run it.

Then run it on three things, all prose fiction, all from real manuscripts including mine:

A. My own showpiece opening, four pages, forty drafts, the most praised thing in my last book. It is allusive as hell, and now that I look at it, it uses at least three items off Tolstoy's borrowed-props list. I built it like carpentry and felt approximately nothing while writing it.
B. Two hundred words a writer dashed off in twenty minutes during a terrible week, technically sloppy, she wanted to cut it, hasn't touched it since. It makes me cry every time and I can't tell you why.
C. A humiliation scene — a minor character, a kid at a school — written so that the reader enjoys the cruelty. The workshop laughs out loud, it is by a distance the most effective piece of writing in that manuscript, and infecting people with contempt is exactly the case where Tolstoy's basic definition and his moral one come apart.

I'd rather have at least one verdict I don't like, and I suspect it's A. Also: outside the three, there's a case I don't think any procedure settles, which is a passage assembled almost word for word out of the writer's dead mother's letters — it infects everybody, and nobody knows whose feeling it is or when it was felt. Put that wherever it belongs and say what the procedure should do with it.

Finally, tell me where this whole thing breaks: which gate does most of the work, which gate misfires hardest on chapters that only exist to make the next chapter land and on ironic or mixed-feeling writing, and whether Tolstoy's theory can be turned into a grading tool at all or whether I should just be using it on my own work.

Give me the gates and the three worked cases in plain sentences first, then one JSON object I can keep in my notes app, with this schema. Top level keys: gates, verdicts, heaviest_gate, worst_failure_mode, my_call. gates is an array, one object per gate, with keys: n (integer, the order), question (one sentence, no jargon), pass_looks_like (what evidence would count as a yes, concretely), if_no (one of the exact strings stop_not_art, stop_bad_art, pass, hand_to_human), minutes (number), kind (one of fact, values, mixed). verdicts is an array, one object per case, with keys: case (A, B or C), stopped_at (the gate number, or null if it reaches an end verdict), verdict (one of not_art, bad_art, good_art, unsettled), confidence (number between 0 and 1), reason (one sentence), flips_if (the one thing that would change the verdict). heaviest_gate is a string naming the gate number that decides most of the outcomes; worst_failure_mode is one sentence; my_call is one sentence on the thing the procedure cannot decide for me.
