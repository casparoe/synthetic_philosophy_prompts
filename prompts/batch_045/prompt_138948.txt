I teach moral and social philosophy, and for some time now I have been circling around an asymmetry in ordinary normative experience that I cannot place. I would be grateful for your help in placing it — but, to be explicit about the shape of the help I want, not with a label. I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. If there is an established concept for it, tell me which one and in what respects the thing I am describing departs from it and from its nearest neighbours. If there is not, say so plainly and give me a term, a definition, and the boundary cases that would test the definition. Please do not assemble a reading list or point me to literatures; I can find those myself. What I cannot do is search for a distinction whose name I do not have.

Three cases, stylized but each drawn from something I have watched happen.

First. A visitor from a country where tipping plays no part in the practice of eating out dines in an American restaurant and leaves nothing. The reaction of his companions, and of the server, is not mere disappointment. It is resentment, and it is voiced in the idiom of having been slighted. Crucially, it survives the information that he did not know. The observation that he should have found out is treated as a sufficient reply; the observation that he did not know is not treated as an answer at all.

Second. A visitor from the United States dines in a country where tipping is not part of the practice, leaves money on the table, and is pursued down the street by the staff so that it can be returned. Here too the visitor is ignorant of the local convention and gets it wrong. But what I have observed in response is embarrassment, at worst a slightly pained amusement. It is not resentment, and it is not the reaction to a slight. Nobody says he should have found out, or if they do the sentence has an entirely different force.

Third. In a city where crossing against the signal is simply what everyone does, a visitor waits at the curb. The crowd divides around him; someone is irritated. Here it is the over-compliance that annoys. So the contrast I first wanted to draw — under-compliance resented, over-compliance excused — cannot be the whole of the phenomenon, and I want to know what the more general pattern is of which all three cases are instances.

My own tentative diagnosis is that the reaction tracks which reading of the behaviour is available to observers. Under-compliance is exactly what a free rider would do; the behaviour is therefore ambiguous between ignorance and defection, and observers resolve the ambiguity toward defection. Over-compliance cannot be read as self-serving, so it must be ignorance — unless it imposes a cost on others, as in the third case, where it gets reclassified as a nuisance rather than as a slight. If that is right, the asymmetry is not really about conventions at all but about the conditions under which non-conformity is legible as taking advantage.

Three things I have considered and set aside, in case they shortcut the answer. It is not simply that conventions solve recurrent coordination problems and departures impose costs: both visitors fail to coordinate, and the second departure arguably imposes a cost as well. It is not simply that we are harsher when an outcome is bad, with the badness fixed in advance: whether anything bad happens when no tip is left is precisely what is at issue. And it is not the legal maxim about ignorance of the law, nor the philosophical literature on culpable ignorance traceable to the agent's own earlier conduct: the visitor's ignorance is often traceable to no fault of his, and the judgment that he should have known stands anyway.

There is also a boundary I cannot locate, and I suspect it is where the real work is. Whether the excuse is granted seems to depend on how visibly foreign the visitor is. The obvious tourist is frequently let off; the long-term resident, or the returning visitor, is not. Is that because perceptible foreignness makes the ignorance reading available, or because long residence makes the ignorance itself culpable? Those strike me as different mechanisms doing very different kinds of normative work, and I want to know which one is really operating.

Finally, and this is what I actually care about: if the asymmetry is real and has the structure I have guessed at, does it tell us anything about whether everyday conventions are moral obligations, and about whether visitors must follow those of the places they are in? It seems to me possible that the resentment in the first case is free-rider detection wearing the vocabulary of obligation, in which case what binds us in these matters is a fairness norm rather than a duty to the local practice — and, if so, the question whether visitors must follow local conventions has no uniform answer at all. But I cannot tell whether the phenomenon points that way or instead toward treating these as genuine obligations that simply fail to generate a matching reaction in one direction.

An answer at whatever length the carving requires, and no shorter.
