I run a monthly discussion night at a hackerspace — about fifteen people, mostly programmers plus one or two social-science grad students, none of whom have read anything on the topic — and I have ninety minutes next week on the gender-equality paradox: the pattern that more gender-egalitarian and richer countries tend to show *more* gendered sorting across fields of study and occupation, not less.

Don't explain the paradox to me; give me the plan for explaining it. What I mainly want is the dependency structure: which distinctions have to be in place before the finding even says anything, and in what order. My instinct is that the load-bearing one is that the two variables sit at different levels — a between-country measure of how egalitarian a society is, and a within-country measure of how segregated its choices are — so nothing about individual causation follows directly, and that this has to be nailed down before anyone is allowed to be surprised. Tell me if that's the wrong first move.

Two other things. First, the carrying example: I want one concrete contrast to build the whole session around. It needs to be a case where the "choice" is genuinely voluntary and the downside of a mismatched choice is genuinely cushioned, so that "freedom" and "preference" can't quietly mean two different things, and it shouldn't be one where a straight pipeline story about gatekeeping does all the work. Tell me what to use and what it has to have and lack.

Second, I want to put my own pet hypothesis on the board and have the group stress-test it, and I want to know where in the sequence it belongs. Roughly: if a welfare state compresses the penalty for choosing "wrong," then removing economic risk doesn't flatten choice, it lets people sort more finely on whatever small mean differences in intrinsic fit exist — so a relaxation of constraint *predicts* more sorting, and the paradox is what a preference-based model looks like from the inside, not evidence against culture. Is that a real mechanism, a confused way of stating something better stated otherwise, or a motte-and-bailey I'd be smuggling in? Where should it come so it functions as a hypothesis rather than as a conclusion the room adopts?

Give me the misconceptions in the order this audience will actually generate them — I expect "egalitarian means gender-neutral, so the finding is self-contradictory," then "so it's innate, case closed," then a swing to "the index is just badly constructed" — and the point at which each should be headed off. Finish with one question I can ask at the end that distinguishes someone who has the structure from someone who has just been persuaded of a side, and with what I should explicitly refuse to settle in ninety minutes.
