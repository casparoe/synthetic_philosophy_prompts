I'm a journalist with a column on science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece (~1,200 words) about whether philosophy makes progress. I don't want another opinion-survey piece; I want to try out one specific, slightly contrived diagnostic, and I'd like you to write the essay that develops it and lands on a verdict.

Call the diagnostic the obsolescence share of a discipline: the fraction of a new researcher's training devoted to material the field itself currently treats as superseded or merely historical, as opposed to material it treats as live. In molecular biology that share is close to zero — you learn the current machinery, and older work enters only as history. In philosophy it looks high: graduate students still read Plato, Hume, and Kant as interlocutors rather than ancestors, and positions the field once wrote off keep returning in new dress (sense-data talk in the philosophy of perception, for example). If a field's obsolescence share stays high generation after generation, there is a prima facie case that it isn't accumulating.

The essay should do three things. First, sharpen the diagnostic until it could actually be applied — what counts as "live," who counts as the field, over what window — so that it isn't just a rhetorical way of asking whether philosophers agree more than they used to. Second, run it honestly on philosophy, with at least one comparison case for scale. Third, confront the strongest objection I can anticipate: that the test is rigged, because for philosophy's subject matter — conceptual and normative questions — old texts remain evidence rather than being superseded, so a field can improve enormously in articulation, argumentative hygiene, and the machinery it exports to other disciplines (decision theory, Bayesian formal epistemology, the modern apparatus of modal logic) while still legitimately reading its classics as live. If that objection wins, say what the failure of the test teaches us.

Distinguish at least three candidate kinds of progress — eliminating positions (the fate of the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge after the Gettier-style counterexamples is the model here), building reusable machinery that other fields adopt, and merely redescribing old disputes in new vocabulary — and say which of them the diagnostic can even see. Don't sit on the fence: conclude by saying whether philosophy progresses, in what sense, and how much.

Write for an educated general reader: concrete, every piece of jargon glossed, no citations or reading lists (the piece runs without footnotes). As the very last line after the essay, state your verdict in machine-readable form: \answer{...} containing a short phrase such as "Yes — locally, and in exported tools, not in grand doctrines" or "No — redescriptive churn only."
