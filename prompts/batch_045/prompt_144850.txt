Me and two friends have been going at Nozick's utility monster in a group chat for about a month now — not for a class, just the three of us being insufferable — and I finally got annoyed enough to write up my actual position. I'd love comments on it before I try to expand it into something longer. Please don't hand me a reading list or tell me which papers to look up; I know that literature exists, I want to know whether the argument below works as it stands, on its own feet.

Here's the essay (short, more notes than finished prose):

*The monster is a claim about the aggregator, not about utilitarianism.* The standard use of the monster is as a reductio of total utilitarianism: if some being converts resources into welfare at a vastly higher rate than anyone else, then for any number of ordinary people and any amount of their suffering, there's a conversion rate at which summing requires us to feed the monster and let everyone else go without, and that's supposed to be absurd. The stock escapes each cost something. Rights or side-constraints leave aggregative morality altogether. Average utilitarianism inherits the usual population weirdnesses. Prioritarianism looks monster-proof at first, but only because we picture the monster as already well off; a monster that *starts* badly off still accrues weighted gains at r times everyone else's rate, and if the weight function only discounts by a bounded factor, a large enough r wins again. Leximin has the mirror-image worry: spend everything on whoever is worst off, however little good it does.

What I want to say is that all of this is one phenomenon showing up in different places. Call an aggregator *pin-prone at the top* if, for any fixed configuration of everyone else's welfare, there is some welfare level for a single individual that settles the ranking no matter what the rest of the distribution looks like. Sum-ranking is pin-prone at the top (the monster) and, if suffering is unbounded below, pin-prone at the bottom too. The monster isn't really a discovery about utilitarianism; it's a stress test that tells you which tail your aggregator is hostage to. Run the test on other rules and you get a little menu: the product / geometric mean is pinned at zero, so we must sacrifice arbitrarily much to lift one single being off zero — structurally the same vice as the monster, one coordinate dictating the whole verdict, except that we find it appealing because it photographs as compassion; call it the reverse monster. Max and leximin are pinned at the bottom full stop. The only way I can see to escape pinning entirely is to bound each individual's marginal contribution, i.e. build in satiation — but then the theory stops tracking welfare above the ceiling, and its verdicts hinge on a satiation parameter nobody can measure, which I'll call ceiling blindness.

And there's a second escape that fails differently: make the aggregate sub-linear in the *number* of individuals, so cloning the monster doesn't multiply its claim. But whether a huge gain is one being having many excellent experiences or many beings each having one is a question about how to individuate subjects, and you can slide continuously between those descriptions, so any rule that responds to them differently has an arbitrary cliff somewhere along the slide. Monster-proofing by sub-linearity makes the theory hostage to a counting convention.

Where I'm least sure is the last step. If I don't know which creature in the population I'll turn out to be and I give each of them equal chance, then maximizing my own expected welfare *is* maximizing the sum — so ex ante impartial benevolence seems to endorse feeding the monster, provided the monster is one of the things I might have been and its gains are big enough. That suggests the repulsion everyone feels isn't coming from impartial benevolence at all. Maybe it comes from an unstated assumption that I'm overwhelmingly likely to be one of the ordinary creatures, so the monster branch is just improbable ex ante; or from something like desert or reciprocity — the thought that the monster's efficiency is moral luck it has no right to cash in; or from the suspicion that we're being asked to compare welfare magnitudes across minds too different for the comparison to have a determinate answer, in which case the monster scenario may not contain the numbers its own argument needs. I can't tell which of these is carrying the weight, and I suspect different people are running different ones.

Concretely, I'd like: (1) which single claim above you think is most likely false, and the strongest version of the objection to it; (2) whether the 'pin-prone' framing is doing any real work or is just a relabeling of things everyone already knew; (3) whether my menu of tails is anywhere near exhaustive — is there an aggregator that genuinely escapes pin-proneness, ceiling blindness, and the individuation cliff all at once, and if you think rank-weighted or prioritarian rules with a bounded weight function escape, show me why I'm wrong; (4) your own answer to the puzzle in the last paragraph about where the repulsiveness comes from, if ex ante reasoning gives you the sum; (5) which paragraph a reader who already knows this debate would skim, and what's missing that would make such a reader sit up. Also flag any place where you think I've slipped into a merely verbal dispute rather than a real one.
