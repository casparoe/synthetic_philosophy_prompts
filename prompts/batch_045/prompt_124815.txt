Analyze the split-brain studies of the 1960s and 1970s as a case study for global workspace theory, and then tell me where the theory runs out. I do not want a literature review, and I do not want citations, references, or suggestions for further reading: I want the mapping done carefully, in both directions, and I want the boundaries of the theory stated honestly rather than papered over.

The case, as I will stipulate it. Sperry and Gazzaniga studied patients whose corpus callosum had been surgically cut as a treatment for severe epilepsy. Material presented briefly to one visual half-field is processed first by the hemisphere on the opposite side; objects shown on the left could be selected by the left hand but not named, while objects shown on the right could be named but not selected by the left hand; and when the left hand acted on information the speaking hemisphere did not have, the patient would offer a confident explanation of that action which was simply invented. Whether the disconnection was ever complete is itself contested, and I want you to use that rather than set it aside: treat the amount of residual cross-talk as a variable in the analysis, and say what the theory predicts as you vary it.

What I want first is a disciplined translation. Under global workspace theory, what in this case plays the role of the workspace, what would count as ignition or broadcast, and which consumer systems count as evidence that a content has been made globally available or as evidence that it has not? Is the case best described as one workspace with a broken output line, or as two workspaces in one skull, and what would settle that? Then say what the theory predicts about the status of the right hemisphere: conscious, not conscious, or a question the theory is not in the business of asking.

Three complications I want handled rather than smoothed over.

First, the problem of report. The theory ties consciousness to broad availability to consumer systems, not specifically to verbal report; yet in this case nearly all our evidence about the right hemisphere arrives through one consumer system, the language-production system, which by hypothesis is exactly the one it has lost access to. Distinguish availability for report from availability to consumer systems generally, and tell me whether the case is evidence of phenomenal consciousness without access in either sense, or only of access without report. Please do not simply conclude that the theory is a theory of access and therefore silent on phenomenal consciousness: I know that distinction. Tell me whether this case in particular can carry any weight on the phenomenal side, or whether it is structured so that it cannot.

Second, the unity postulate. Standard presentations assume a single global workspace. Is that a definitional commitment, or an empirical claim about mammalian brains? If it is empirical, what would count as evidence that there are two workspaces here, and do these data deliver it? I have a candidate principle of my own and want it tested rather than accepted: a workspace is the largest set of consumer systems among which content is mutually available, and cutting the callosum can in principle split that set. Does that principle give the right answer here? Does it make the counting of subjects a matter of degree in a way the theory cannot tolerate? Or does it smuggle in the conclusion by stipulating which systems count as consumers?

Third, confabulation. The speaking hemisphere produces confident, false explanations of behavior initiated on the other side. If the workspace is where broadcast content reaches self-monitoring and action selection, does this show that broadcast is constructive rather than transparent, that what arrives is a narrative partly manufactured downstream of the actual causes of behavior? Does that embarrass the identification of broadcast with consciousness, or is it exactly what a workspace architecture should predict?

Then the reverse direction. Is the episode a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built to address? In particular: does it tell us anything about how to count conscious subjects, about whether presence in a workspace is sufficient for consciousness or merely necessary, and about whether the access-versus-phenomenal distinction does any real explanatory work here rather than merely relabeling the puzzle? Be explicit about where the theory is silent instead of making it speak, and about which of your claims follow from the theory and which from a reading of the case that the theory itself does not constrain.

Finally, if you think the global workspace is the wrong apparatus for this case, and that a recurrent-processing, higher-order, or some other account maps it better, say so, and give enough of the alternative mapping on the same three complications for me to compare the two side by side.
