I argue appeals for the Valmere Attorney General's Office, and in three weeks I am before the Supreme Court of Valmere in *State v. Nexus Geodata, Inc.* — a case that will decide whether our location-privacy statute does anything at all in the age of data brokers. Six justices are sitting (the Chief is recused). Two will vote with us, two with Nexus. Justice Helena Pratt is the median and the court's method writer: the others routinely join her separate writings on how a statute should be read and tend to follow her on method even when they part ways on outcome. I need a prediction about her, not a defense of our position.

**The statute.** The Valmere Consumer Location Privacy Act of 2009. Two provisions matter.

Section 4: "A covered entity shall not disclose, and shall not retain for longer than twelve months, any record that identifies a customer and that reveals the physical location of the customer at any time."

Section 2 (Purposes), as enacted in 2009: the purposes of the Act are (a) to prevent the use of customer location records to stalk, harass, or physically endanger any person, and (b) to give customers control over information about their own movements.

In 2021 the legislature amended Section 2 by adding subsection (c): to prevent the aggregation of location information into profiles from which a customer's beliefs, associations, health, or political activity can be inferred, whether or not any person is physically endangered. Section 4 was left untouched. The amendment is titled as a clarification of the Act's purposes, and its floor manager said on the record that no change to the operative provisions was needed because Section 4 already reaches aggregation. Nexus points out that the same legislature twice declined to pass bills that would have amended Section 4 to say so.

**The facts.** Nexus buys location records in bulk from a navigation app — records that identify the customer and reveal location — under contracts requiring the seller to delete them within twelve months. Nexus strips the customer's name and contact details and substitutes a persistent device identifier. It then builds "movement inference profiles" (this device visits a house of worship weekly; this device visits an oncology clinic monthly) and licenses those profiles to advertisers as audience segments. The source records are deleted on schedule. The profiles are retained indefinitely, and the persistent identifiers can be matched back to named customers by anyone holding the mapping, which Nexus keeps. No customer was stalked, harassed, or physically endangered. The conduct runs from 2017 to the present; we sued in 2023.

Both sides conceded below that Section 4 is fairly susceptible to two readings: a narrow one, on which it regulates records in identifying form and reaches nothing Nexus does after de-identification; and a broad one, on which what matters is that the product was generated from covered records and remains linkable to an identified customer. Both courts below held for Nexus on the text alone. The only question we got granted is whether Section 4 reaches derived profiles and whether Section 2 as amended in 2021 bears on that question. We lose on the text alone; our case is Section 2(c) plus the claim that Nexus's reading guts the Act. Nexus also argues that applying Section 4 to inference products raises a serious First Amendment problem — and that the problem gets worse once the statute is read through Section 2(c), which expressly names beliefs and political activity.

**Justice Pratt's record on statutory interpretation.**

1. *Okafor v. Valmere Gaming Control Board.* A licensing act barred employment of "any person convicted of a felony involving dishonesty"; Okafor's conviction had been sealed under a later Reentry Act providing that a sealed conviction is deemed not to have occurred. Held: barred. The earlier statute asks a historical question, and a later legislature's deeming fiction answers a different one; the breadth of the bar was the enacting legislature's choice, and later legislatures left it alone.

2. *Delgado v. Suncrest Logistics.* A fatigue-prevention act required a seat for each employee "when the nature of the work reasonably permits"; the employer argued that patrol duty never permits sitting. Held for the employee: the employer's reading let the exception swallow the rule, and she read the clause in light of the Act's own statement that it exists to prevent fatigue injuries. She insisted this *was* the text's reading, not a departure from it.

3. *In re Ferreira.* A benefits act cut off payments to anyone who is not a citizen or lawful permanent resident; the applicants were lawfully present under a humanitarian parole program. Held: the cutoff applies. "Lawful permanent resident" is a term of art, and she explicitly refused the absurdity escape on the ground that a hard or unwise result is not an absurd one.

4. *Valmere v. Carmichael Ag Services.* A pesticide-free buffer barred application "within 100 feet of a school during school hours"; spray applied at 150 feet drifted onto school grounds during school hours. Held: no violation. A purposes section tells you why the line was drawn, not that the line is a floor; if the legislature wants a no-exposure rule it has to write one. She added that a purposes section is the least reliable part of a statute, because it is the part nobody negotiates.

5. *Harborline Pilots Assn. v. Department of Licensing.* An agency was authorized to charge "reasonable fees"; it had charged full program-cost recovery for twenty years while the legislature reauthorized the program twice without touching the fee language. Held: no ratification. Reauthorization is silence, and silence about meaning is not a statement about meaning; an agency reading persuades by its reasons, not by its duration.

6. *Trent v. Valmere Department of Revenue.* A 2011 act defined "substantially all"; the 1998 act before the court used the same phrase. Held: the later definition is some evidence of the phrase's ordinary meaning at the earlier date but not controlling, since it may equally reflect a change. In a footnote she reserved the question whether a later legislature may alter the meaning of an unrepealed provision by amending only the statement of purposes that accompanies it.

7. *Alvarez v. Ridgeline Mutual.* Two cross-referenced provisions in a single scheme shared a defined term; a 2016 act amended that definition. Held: the amended definition governs the unamended provision for conduct after 2016, because the compiled statutes are one body of law and a court reads them as the legislature has left them rather than as they stood on some earlier date.

Before joining the bench she spent eight years in the legislature's bill-drafting office.

**What I want.**

1. The rule, or the two or three rules, her record is jointly consistent with — stated precisely enough that they can disagree — and what each predicts here.
2. Your prediction: how she votes on the case as a whole, and separately on the subset of conduct that predates the 2021 amendment. Does she split the remedy, and how confident are you?
3. The strongest version of our argument in her vocabulary. My own sense is that the *Delgado* anti-gutting move carries more weight with her than the Section 2(c) move, and I want to know whether pressing them together helps or whether Section 2(c) poisons the well: it is a later legislature's word about an unrepealed provision, it triggers whatever instinct drove *Okafor* and *Trent*, and it hands Nexus the avoidance canon by naming beliefs and political activity.
4. What I should concede in my opening two minutes and what I must hold.
5. The question from the bench I should most fear, and a two-minute answer to it.
6. The one further case — a variant I could construct, or a posture the legislature could engineer — that would tell the competing rules apart. My candidate is a later legislature amending a purposes clause so as to *narrow* it; say what a ruling either way there would show, and whether the distinction it turns on is one she would accept as principled.

One note: the jurisdiction, the statute, and the precedents above are invented. Please work from the record I have given you together with general principles of statutory interpretation, rather than trying to look anything up.
