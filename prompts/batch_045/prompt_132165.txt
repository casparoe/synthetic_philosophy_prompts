I'm a fourth-year PhD student, and next month I'm giving a works-in-progress talk on the third chapter of my dissertation. What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. The chapter defends a position I've been calling diamond presentism. Standard presentism says that only present things exist; read against special relativity, the present has to be a simultaneity slice, and the relativity of simultaneity then seems to leave the presentist with no slice to pick. My move is to give up simultaneity: I say the present is not a global hypersurface but the Alexandrov interval of a vertex event — the lens-shaped region given by the intersection of that event's future and past light cones — so that the present is a finite, frame-invariant, four-dimensional neighborhood rather than a three-dimensional slice of the universe. I get there in three steps: an argument from change, that a durationless instantaneous present cannot host a process at all, since causation and rates of change need extended temporal intervals, so any presentism worth having is committed to a thick present; an argument from invariant size, that once the present has thickness, the only thickness available in a relativistic setting that does not amount to a choice of frame is the light-cone one, and that this is what buys immunity to the standard simultaneity objection; and an argument from the phenomenology of temporal experience, that what we are acquainted with is durations rather than instants, so the thick present is the only candidate we have any introspective grip on. I try to blunt the obvious cost — that on this view a distant event simultaneous with me in some frame is not present — by claiming that ordinary tensed discourse can largely be paraphrased in terms of causal order plus the speaker's own diamond.

The audience is my department's works-in-progress series, around thirty people. Two of them are philosophers of physics who are eternalists by default and think the debate was settled by special relativity: they will want to know what, if anything, my view predicts that plain eternalism does not, and whether it is a distinct position at all rather than eternalism with an extra primitive bolted on. One is a general relativist visiting from the physics department, sympathetic to foundational discussion but insistent on definitions stated precisely enough to check; I expect him to press on whether any of this survives the move from special to general relativity, especially in spacetimes that admit no global time function. One is a metaphysician who works on grounding and fundamentality; I expect her to say that how many things exist is not the real question, that my dispute with the eternalist is really about which quantifier or which notion of existence is metaphysically significant, and that the diamond geometry is an idle wheel once the dispute is reframed that way. Two or three graduate students are sympathetic to fragmentalist or branching-time views of tense, and will press whether diamond presentism is just fragmentalism with an odd geometry, and whether it can say anything about the objectivity of temporal passage that they cannot say more cheaply. The one senior person in the department who is genuinely friendly to dynamic views is on sabbatical, so I should not count on help from the floor. The format is forty-five minutes plus twenty of questions, which is maybe ninety seconds per answer.

What I'd like: the six to eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by likelihood times damage rather than by my own sense of what matters philosophically. For each, give me the objection phrased the way that particular person would put it — I want the general relativist's version and the grounding theorist's version as recognizably different sentences, not one generic objection with the serial numbers filed off; a note on how much of it really lands; what I should concede outright versus what I must hold if the chapter is to survive; and a reply I could actually deliver in ninety seconds, with the weakest joint in that reply marked so I know where I am bluffing. Also tell me the one or two things I should concede in the first five minutes to buy goodwill from this particular audience, since I know I'll be read as a presentist trying to smuggle something past them. Please end with the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the honest answer to it. My own guess is that the dangerous material is what fixes the vertex of the diamond — whose here-now it is, what the view says about a universe with no observers in it, and how thick the present is even supposed to be — but I'd rather you told me which formulation of that cluster is hardest to answer than assume I've already located the worst version myself.
