hey — hoping you can help me draft something. I sit on the outcomes committee for our county's reentry workforce fund: we distribute money to four nonprofit job-training providers, and since last fiscal year about a fifth of each provider's payment has been tied to a single number, "unsubsidized job placement within 90 days of program exit, verified by pay stub." The providers responded exactly as you'd expect, and faster than any of us guessed. Two of them now screen applicants for recent work history and quietly route anyone with a gap longer than a year into a "readiness" track that doesn't count as enrollment, so those folks never enter the denominator. A third has gotten very good at placing people in two- and three-day temp assignments that technically produce a pay stub. Over two years our headline placement rate went from 31% to 58%, but when a state agency ran the same cohort through unemployment-insurance wage records, median quarterly earnings hadn't budged, which is the part that made me feel stupid for not seeing it coming. We meet again in a week and a half and I have to circulate a recommendation beforehand.

I keep going back and forth because a friend who works in ML evaluation got me half-convinced that not all of this is the same phenomenon. His rough taxonomy: sometimes you optimize a proxy that was only ever correlated with what you care about, so the correlation frays as you push on it; sometimes the problem is that you're pushing into the extreme tail where the measure behaves differently than it does in the ordinary range; sometimes the optimizee figures out the causal path from its behavior to the measurement and short-circuits it; and sometimes it's straight-up strategic gaming where the agent knows the target and doesn't share your goal at all. That feels right to me but I'm not sure it tells us what to do. The options on the table are: (a) keep the placement metric but weight it against a risk-adjusted baseline so providers aren't punished for taking hard cases, (b) drop the formula entirely and pay a flat rate, then audit a random sample of case files against a rubric that costs real staff time to satisfy, (c) something one board member calls a "decoy metric" — publish a simple, clearly gameable number for providers to chase while the actual funding decision uses a weighted composite we don't disclose, on the theory that gaming effort gets absorbed by the harmless number, and (d) keep a formula but deliberately leave parts of it vague and hand-adjusted so it can't be reverse-engineered.

Could you draft the memo I'd send to the full board? Short — say 600 to 700 words — and written for people who are sharp about budgets but have never heard the term Goodhart's law, so no jargon without a one-line gloss. Lead with the bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate my question back at me. You need to actually pick one of the four and defend it, and the losing option with the strongest case against your pick should get a fair hearing rather than being waved off. Also, at the end, tell me how confident the memo should make the board in its central claim and flag the single point you're least sure about — I'd rather hand them something honest than something that sounds airtight.
