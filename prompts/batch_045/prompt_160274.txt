Our privacy reading group has been going on three years and it keeps hitting the same wall, so I want a diagnosis of the wall, not a verdict and definitely not a survey of the positions.

The wall: the reductionist side says that talk of a violation of privacy is always shorthand for some other interest being set back — property, bodily integrity, autonomy, freedom from being judged or manipulated — and that 'privacy' is just a handy label for a shifting cluster of those, so there is no distinct thing to theorise about. The non-reductionist side says there is a distinctive interest in not being observed, accessed, or known, and that it can be set back even when nothing else is. The case they always end up on is the idle observer: someone watches you through a hidden camera, never acts on it, never tells anyone, the footage is destroyed unwatched and you never find out. Reductionists say nothing bad happened that isn't already covered by the other interests; non-reductionists say you were wronged and the wrong just is the observation. Everyone in the room has heard the other side's version and nobody has moved in three years.

My hypothesis is that it isn't one disagreement but three stacked on top of each other, and that people switch between them without noticing: whether the concept can be analysed in other terms; whether a privacy violation involves a harm over and above the harms of the things privacy protects; and whether the interest, if there is one, is ever weighty enough on its own to ground a claim or a right. But I cant tell whether that's the real structure or whether I'm inventing distinctions to hide the fact that the two sides just have bedrock intuitions neither can argue for.

Two cases I keep pushing because they seem to cut the options differently than the usual ones do. First, the mirror case: two colleagues end up knowing exactly the same true proposition about you — that you're job hunting, say — one because you told her, one because he pieced it together from things you did in public. Same proposition, same number of people who know it. Almost everyone in the group says only the second one feels like a privacy loss, and I want to know which accounts that actually tells against and why. Second, the sealed vault: your diary is photographed today and locked in a vault with a provably unbreakable seal, to be read in 2200 when everyone who knows you is dead. When does the loss happen, and where does the wrong land? The two sides of our stalemate seem to give different answers here, and I can't work out whether that's because they disagree about substance or because they're using 'privacy' to mean different things.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, a hidden empirical or conceptual question doing the work, or just bedrock? Tell me what each side would actually say about my two cases, and whether they discriminate between the views or are neutral. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Please don't settle this by appealing to legal doctrine or what any jurisdiction happens to hold, and no reading list; I've read the literature, I want the shape of the argument.
