Please act as a professional philosopher who works on causation and explanation and who also knows how working historians actually write. Take the question seriously and don't shortcut it: start by brainstorming six or eight candidate principles — including ones you end up rejecting, with a sentence on why — before narrowing to the best one, and then give me (1) the principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a new case, (2) a clause-by-clause note on which of my verdicts forces that clause, (3) your principle's verdicts on the cases I haven't listed, and (4) an honest list of any of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, together with what I would have to give up to keep them.

Some background on where I'm coming from: I'm a retired civil engineer with no philosophy training, I read a lot of popular history, and two friends and I argue about it in a pub reading group; I've realised I have firm, slightly inconsistent-feeling instincts about which causes a history ought to name, and I can't turn them into a rule. Here are my verdicts. Two people independently set fires at opposite ends of a warehouse, each fire alone sufficient to destroy it, warehouse destroyed: both of them caused the destruction, and an account that names only one isn't false but is misleading — I won't give that up. But in the version where two kids throw rocks at a bottle and Suzy's rock arrives first while Billy's would have shattered it a moment later, I say only Suzy's throw is a cause, even though the bottle was doomed either way just as in the fire case; these two are the pair I can't reconcile. With the Titanic I want to say the iceberg caused the sinking while the shortage of lifeboats did not cause the sinking but did cause the deaths, and I want those treated as the same kind of claim about different outcomes rather than as a real cause versus a mere background condition. In a stylised Chernobyl I happily cite the reactor design that misbehaved at low power and the operators who disabled safety systems to run a test, I cite the culture of secrecy uneasily, and I deny that a hypothetical earlier near-miss at a sister plant, covered up so that nobody in the control room knew, is a cause of the explosion — my friend insists it is, and I'll concede at most that it explains why they didn't act differently. In a stylised 2008 I cite subprime lending, the bundled securities, the ratings agencies and the leverage, and I flatly refuse to cite greed: greed is present in every boom and has no contrast. For the First World War I cite the Sarajevo assassination and the alliance commitments, I take the driver's wrong turn (if the story is right) to be a cause but not one worth putting in a book, and — this is the bit that bothers me — I want to say the alliances mattered more, in some sense, than the wrong turn, even though the wrong turn probably did more to raise the probability that a war happened at all; so whatever "mattered more" means for me, it isn't just probability-raising. Methodologically I also hold that there is no such thing as the real cause or the root cause, that singular "the cause of X" talk is always a mistake, and yet I do rank the causes I cite.

Here is the rule I've been trying out — please test it against these cases and repair it rather than just replacing it. Call a factor a live-swap cause of an outcome when there was some alternative to it that was live in the situation (something that plausibly could have happened, or that someone on the scene was actually considering) and, had that alternative obtained, the outcome would probably not have happened; and add a second clause, that it doesn't count if the outcome would have been produced anyway by a second route already moving independently of it. Clause one is why I reject greed, and clause two is why I reject Billy's rock, but as far as I can tell clause two also kills both arsonists, which I refuse to accept; and I can't see how either clause separates "is a cause" from "is worth citing in a history", which I'm now convinced are two different questions. There's also the case I flip-flop on weekly: imagine a submarine officer during a nuclear crisis who declines to launch when he could have — on Mondays I say his refusal caused there to be no war, and on Fridays I say nothing happened, there is nothing to explain, and causation of an absence is a category mistake, which then sits badly with my lifeboats verdict. So: fix my rule if it can be fixed, tell me which clause is hopeless if it can't, say what principle best captures the rest of the pattern, and give me its verdicts on the cases I haven't raised — whether "no war was fought on its soil" is a cause of a country's twentieth-century prosperity, whether a dam that held is a cause of the town's survival in a history of that town, and whether my grandfather's decision to emigrate in the 1920s is a cause of my being alive and, separately, whether it is worth citing anywhere.
