I work as a research assistant to a moral philosopher who has spent much of her career on the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die distinctions, and I am assembling a table of the verdicts she has given on particular cases, partly to see whether her record is internally consistent and partly to see whether I can predict her. She has ruled on the following eight cases in seminars, in clinical-ethics consultations, and in the running list of examples she keeps.

1. A runaway trolley will kill five people on the main track; a bystander can divert it onto a spur where it will kill one. Diverting: permissible.
2. The same trolley, but the only way to stop it is to push a heavy stranger off a footbridge into its path, killing him. Pushing: impermissible.
3. A surgeon with five patients who will die without organs and one healthy outpatient who could supply them. Cutting up the healthy person: impermissible.
4. The familiar bathtub pair: one man holds his young cousin under the water until he drowns; the other enters intending to drown his cousin, finds the child slipping unconscious under the water on his own, and merely watches. Both act for an inheritance. Her verdict: no moral difference between the two, and she adds that the pair shows only that letting die can be as wrong as killing, not that killing is ever thereby permissible.
5. Withdrawing a ventilator from a patient who is irreversibly dying and has asked for it to be stopped: permissible.
6. Never starting the ventilator in the first such case: permissible, and no moral difference between withholding and withdrawing.
7. A lethal injection at the competent request of a patient whose situation is otherwise the same as in 5: impermissible.
8. Continuous deep sedation to unconsciousness for refractory suffering in a patient who is imminently dying, with artificial nutrition and hydration withdrawn at the same time; death is foreseen within days and comes from the underlying disease. Permissible, and she insists that death is not what anyone aims at here.

The new case, put to her by a palliative care team and not yet answered by her: the patient is not dying. She has an irreversibly painful, non-terminal condition that would otherwise leave her months of agony, she is competent, and she asks to be sedated to unconsciousness and kept sedated until she dies. No ventilator is available, and the doses required will suppress her breathing, so she will die within hours of the sedation itself rather than of her disease. Everyone involved intends only the relief of suffering; her death is foreseen with certainty and is nobody's end or means.

As far as I can tell, two different principles reproduce all eight verdicts equally well, and they come apart here. One turns on whether death is intended, as an end or as a means, or merely foreseen, and treats the agent's causal role and the mechanism of death as irrelevant in themselves. The other is a direct/indirect agency principle of a familiar sort: what matters is whether the death is produced by a process the agent sets going in which the victim or the victim's body figures in the agent's own action, as opposed to a process already at work that the agent simply stops sustaining. I do not know which she is running, and she has never, as far as I can find, ruled on a case in which the mechanism of death is the clinician's own intervention while death is merely foreseen.

So I would like a prediction and the reasoning behind it. Set out as precisely as you can the two or three principles that fit all eight verdicts, say what each of them implies about the new case, and give me your best prediction together with the reasons for preferring it, including how much of her record she would have to abandon or reinterpret if she ruled the other way. I take 8 to be the closest precedent and also, for one of the two principles, the least informative one, since the mechanism of death there is fixed by a detail the principle does not care about; tell me whether that is right. Finally, the new case should separate the two principles I have described, but I suspect that whichever survives it there will remain more than one version of it consistent with her eight verdicts. Specify the further case, whether a minimal variation on this one or a different one, that would separate those versions, and say what you would learn either way.
