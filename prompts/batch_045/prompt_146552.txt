Next Friday I am giving a departmental colloquium paper — forty minutes of presentation, twenty of questions — defending a reconstruction of evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics that I think escapes the standstill the literature has settled into, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than merely ready for the paper.

The audience is small and I know roughly who will be in it. There is a non-naturalist moral realist who has published on companions-in-guilt replies and who thinks any debunking argument with real force against morality generalizes to mathematics and to epistemic norms and therefore destroys itself by overgeneration. There is a moral naturalist who is broadly sympathetic to debunking arguments but who insists they must be run as an explicit Bayesian comparison of likelihoods — the probability of our moral beliefs given realism versus given the debunking hypothesis — or else they are not arguments at all. There is an experimental philosopher whose own work is on cross-cultural variation in moral judgment and who is professionally suspicious of any premise about what "our moral beliefs" are when the evidence base is largely Western undergraduates. There is a philosopher of biology who will not allow me to say that moral judgment was selected for anything unless I can specify the trait, the level at which selection operated, and the evidence that would distinguish an adaptation from a byproduct of social cognition selected for other purposes. And there is a graduate student writing on debunking arguments in the philosophy of religion, who has read the general worry that if this style of argument works anywhere it works everywhere, and who will want to know what confines mine to morality.

Here is the position I am defending, so that the objections have something concrete to hit. First, the reconstruction: I argue that debunking arguments should not be formulated as claims about whether mind-independent moral facts causally figure in the explanation of why we hold the moral beliefs we do. That formulation invites the third-factor reply and generates no progress. Instead I propose what I call the flexible-response formulation. On this reconstruction the claim is about the functional profile of the faculty: moral judgment is best explained as a device for producing motivationally efficacious, publicly legible, and locally coordinated verdicts, selected for flexible responsiveness to shifting strategic and ecological conditions rather than for detecting a fixed subject matter. The debunking force comes not from the absence of a causal link but from what I call counterfactual fragility: had the ancestral selective regime differed in specifiable ways — different pathogen load, different kinship and residence patterns, different scale of intergroup conflict — the outputs of the faculty would differ in specifiable directions, and the realist has no independent grip on which of those counterfactual outputs is the correct one. That leaves the realist with what I call the selector's trilemma: deny the counterfactuals, accept them while treating our actual verdicts as a benign coincidence, or retreat to a realism about only those moral beliefs that survive counterfactual perturbation — a residual realism much thinner than the view advertised. I also argue that third-factor replies owe a surcharge they have not paid: they must show not merely that the independent moral facts and our beliefs actually align, but that the alignment is modally robust across the counterfactual perturbations the flexible-response model predicts; and I claim they cannot pay it, because the third factors they cite are identified retrospectively by looking at what the faculty already delivers, which makes the alignment claim circular. To block the companions-in-guilt reply I appeal to an audit asymmetry: mathematical and epistemic norms have external checks on their outputs — proof, and downstream predictive and inferential success — whereas the only check available on a moral verdict is further moral judgment, so the parallel case fails where it matters.

Second, the empirical wager, which is the part I am least sure about and most expect to be attacked. I claim that a debunking argument earns its keep only if it predicts something, and I stake my version on a divergence ranking: a predicted partial ordering of moral domains by expected cross-cultural stability, with verdicts about harm and reciprocity comparatively stable and verdicts about purity, authority, and loyalty comparatively labile, and with the residual variation tracking specific ecological predictors rather than being noise. I present this falsifiability as the main advantage of my reconstruction over the standard etiological arguments, which I criticise as unfalsifiable just-so stories.
