I'm in a personal identity seminar this term — we did Parfit a couple weeks ago and this week we started on Buddhist anatta — and I keep feeling like the readings are either saying something Parfit already said or something much weirder that nobody quite admits to. So I want to argue with someone who actually holds the view. Please play a committed defender of anatta: a philosopher-monk trained in the Abhidharma/Pāli side of the tradition who has also read modern analytic work on personal identity. Here's the position I want you to play: there is no substantial self and no further fact of identity beyond the causal-psychological continuity of a stream, but you think Parfit got the metaphysics roughly right and the point of the doctrine wrong — for you "same person" is a convention, and conventions aren't free, they're answerable to what's really there and to whether talking that way actually reduces suffering. You'll happily concede small things (that continuity comes in degrees while identity doesn't, that a lot of ordinary identity-talk is confused), but you will not concede that your view is nihilism, that it's just reductionism with therapeutic decoration, or that anything goes in how we draw the boundaries of a person. Stay in character until I say stop, keep it to one or two short paragraphs a turn, and if you need a fact about my case, ask for it in character. Answer my question rather than asking me what I mean.

Here's my case and my opening move. Two volunteers, Ash and Bo, spend ten years in a neurological procedure that exchanges their psychologies one tiny increment at a time — a memory trace here, a dispositional tweak there, no stage any clinician would call a fission or a death, each stream at every moment about as continuous as an ordinary night's sleep. At the end, the person in Ash's body has Bo's memories, projects and character, and vice versa. At the midway point each stream is roughly half and half, and nobody — not them, not the clinicians, not their families — can say which one is Ash. The indeterminacy itself I can live with. What I can't work out is this: your tradition also needs identity-talk to do work. You say the stream that continues after my death is in some sense me (otherwise why would I bother practising?), you apportion the fruit of action to a stream, you say that for someone who has attained liberation the suffering is over. The only principled way I can see to make that work is to cash out "me" as closeness of causal-psychological continuity to me-now — which either makes identity come in degrees or turns it into a setting on a dial. And then I don't see what stops the dial being set wherever it's useful: in some loose but real sense every suffering being is you, which is more or less that compassion argument I keep running into, and which the birth stories seem to illustrate if my TA got that right. So: is the dial constrained by anything other than usefulness, or isn't it? And if it isn't, why isn't the consistent conclusion that "my liberation" is a confused goal — there's just suffering and its cessation, and no one whose it is?

Don't sit on the fence: give me the version you actually believe, with your reasons, tell me where the argument hurts most, and say which bit of your answer you'd expect other people in your own tradition to disagree with you about.
