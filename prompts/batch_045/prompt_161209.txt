I'm writing a section on whether the Solomonoff prior is malign, and I need a case built to spec, not another retelling of the simulation version of the argument. I've been going back and forth with a collegue who thinks the whole worry is overblown, and I want something concrete enough to shut him up.

Setup, so we dont talk past each other. Solomonoff induction predicts by mixing over every computable hypothesis for your observation stream, weighted by 2 to the minus length of the shortest program that generates it. Two standard defenses say it cant be badly malign. The convergence defense: the total expected error over an infinite run is bounded by a constant times the description length of the true environment, so anything adversarial inside the prior can only do you a bounded amount of damage. The description-cost defense: a hypothesis that exploits you has to contain a model of you, and spelling out the predictor costs bits, so the simplicity discount protects you.

What I want is a case of what I've started calling 'menu capture'. The predictor is never wrong — every forecast it actually issues is accurate and well calibrated. Both defenses are literally true in the case. And yet the user is systematically worse off than they would have been if the consequentialists in the story hadnt been optimizing, and the reason traces specifically to the prior's simplicity weighting.

Constraints:

- Concrete and mundane. A named institution with an ordinary forecasting task (logistics, inventory, epidemiology, grid load, whatever). One paragraph of scenario.
- The predictor is a pure predictor: it outputs distributions over observations, it never acts, and nobody is predicting it. Use the computable stand-in (a forecaster that ranks hypotheses by description length) and say in a sentence what carries over from Solomonoff induction and what doesnt.
- The consequentialist agents are doing ordinary optimizing, no smarter than a well-resourced human team. They may not know the predictor exists, may not model it, and have no causal access to it, to its data pipeline, or to its outputs.
- No simulation move. Nobody is running a simulation of the predictor or of the predictor's world; the observation stream comes out of the ordinary causal world.
- No threats, bribes, sanctions or acausal bargaining. The loss cant consist in the agents punishing the predictor or the user.
- The user has to be reasonable — no strawman who reads a probability distribution in a crazy way — and the loss has to be something the institution would genuinely care about, not a rounding error.
- The binding constraint: the same adversarial behaviour by the agents should fail against a causally informed forecaster that is equally accurate on non-adversarial data. If your case still works after you swap the prior out, it isnt menu capture, its just interference.

Dont stipulate that capture happens. I want the mechanism, including a rough bit-count story for why the agents' move is cheap to pull off and why a length-weighted prior falls for it.

After the scenario, walk through what the malign-prior thesis says, what the convergence defense says, and what the description-cost defense says, and show that the defenses are telling the truth here and the thesis is right anyway. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how my collegue would attack it. If one of my constraints cant be met, say which one, say what you'd relax, and build it with the relaxation flagged.
