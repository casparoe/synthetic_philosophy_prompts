my friend and I spent like two hours last night fighting about Hofstadter's idea that pieces of a person's self keep running in other people's brains after they die. He thinks it's metaphor; I think its literally true, and I'm giving a colloquium on it next week, so I need to be ready.

The version I'm defending: a self is a pattern, not a substrate, so X's self can be partly realized in Y's brain when three conditions hold — (1) the version in Y is rich enough to generate X-ish answers to new counterfactual questions, not just memorized facts about X, (2) it got there by actual interaction with X rather than from reading a biography or from being coincidentally similar to X, and (3) Y sometimes lets it drive behavior instead of just keeping it on file. I call the result a carried self. If that's right, death is a gradual decay rather than a switch, how much of X is still around is a real quantity with a measurement procedure (how well a bereaved spouse predicts X's responses to novel questions versus a stranger who read X's letters), and personal identity should be replaced by degree of co-realization. I also want the normative bit: we host carried selves, and vandalizing one is a way of harming the person it came from. His best shot was that on my view there are already bits of me in a dozen other brains right now, so it isn't clear who's giving the talk — I don't have a good answer to that one yet.

About half are analytic philosophers of mind who work on personal identity and memory and are basically Parfitian — psychological continuity, no further fact, identity isn't what matters. Two are memory neuroscientists, friendly but they think in terms of reconstructive memory and generative models of other agents. One is a clinical psychologist who treats bereaved patients.

Short reply to each, plus where the reply is soft.
