I inherited from my grandmother a small oil painting, a coastal scene, which hung in her hallway for as long as I can remember and which the family always believed to be by a minor Victorian marine painter. A few months ago I had it appraised for insurance purposes. The appraiser says it is a forgery, painted in the 1970s, almost certainly by a woman who turned out several hundred such pictures, and worth a few hundred dollars as a decoration. She adds that it is unclear whether pictures like this one were made in order to deceive or were sold openly as decorative and acquired their labels further down the chain.

What I want advice about is not the money. It is the argument in my own family, because the four of us seem to be talking past each other and I cannot say what the disagreement is about.

My wife says nothing about the painting has changed: the same surface, the same colours, the same object we have all looked at for forty years. To feel differently about it now, she says, would be to fetishise a piece of paperwork. My brother says the value was always relational and is untouched — the picture has a real history, bought at a church sale in 1968, hung in that hall, carried out of the flood — and that the only thing which has changed is a story about who held the brush. My daughter says it is more interesting now than it was before: an authentic object made by a real person who fooled the experts, an achievement in its own right. She points out that a collector has already offered more for it as a genuine forgery than it was ever insured for as an original. And my brother adds, as if it settled the matter, that nobody in the family cares that the etching he inherited is one of fifty pulled from the same plate, and that the museum holding two works by the same marine painter now catalogues one of them as "studio of."

Two further things. The forger, alive and ninety-one, has written to say she would sign the back of the canvas in her own name. And I have to answer the museum's request to borrow the picture for an exhibition about her, to be hung, under her name, in a room alongside the paintings she imitated.

I also find that I cannot see the picture as I did. The brushwork now looks furtive to me, and I do not know whether I should be ashamed of that or whether it is simply what knowing amounts to. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that no examination could ever have distinguished this picture from a genuine one: would that change your answer, and if not, why not?

A survey of what various writers have said will not help me. I need a position and the reasons for it. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which of these reactions, if any, is closest to right, and whether the offered signature and the museum's request should change what I do — and then give your reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my situation, and no closing summary.
