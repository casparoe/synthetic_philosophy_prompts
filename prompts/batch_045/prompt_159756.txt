I teach eleventh-grade English at a school that has spent the last eighteen months in a slow, exhausting argument about a new bias-incident reporting system, and I have found myself unable to think clearly about it. One side of the faculty says the system teaches students to compete over who has been hurt most and to settle scores by recruiting an audience; the other says that the old advice — shrug it off, don't make a scene — was only ever a way of telling certain children to absorb whatever they were given. Someone on the committee sent around the three-culture scheme from the sociology of morality, and it has been rattling around in my head ever since: honor cultures, in which standing depends on how one is regarded and an insult has to be answered, by force if need be, and disputes are settled face to face; dignity cultures, in which worth is held to be equal and independent of what anyone thinks, slights are to be ignored or routed into formal law, and an impartial authority applies rules; and victimhood cultures, in which moral standing attaches to injury, grievances are made public, and the way to win a conflict is to display one's wound to a sympathetic third party rather than to strike back or to sue. I am teaching Miller's The Crucible this spring, and I would like to use the scheme — carefully, and without turning the play into a costume drama about current affairs.

What I want is a reading that takes both sides seriously. My own sense is that Salem is not a clean instance of any one of the three, and that this is precisely what makes the play useful: Parris's anxiety about his position and Proctor's fixation on his name look like honor logic; the girls' fits and the court's willingness to credit them look like victimhood logic, with injury converted into authority; and the court presents itself in dignity terms — rules, evidence, impartial procedure — while actually being governed by honor, because Danforth cannot afford to be seen to have condemned the wrong people. So is the play dramatizing one culture mutating into another, or three logics living on top of one another and exploiting one another? I also suspect the framework blurs a distinction the play forces apart: appealing to a third party to adjudicate by rules, and appealing to an audience to award sympathy, are very different things, and Danforth's court seems to want the prestige of the first while running on the mechanics of the second. Where does the scheme genuinely illuminate a character's choice, and where does it only relabel something?

And I need to know where to put it down. Salem is a theocracy in which the decisive witness is God rather than any public; the court's authority is magisterial rather than a matter of audience approval; and Miller wrote the play as an allegory of the anti-Communist hearings of his own period, where the instrument of pressure was naming names — a mechanism I cannot place in any of the three cultures. Tell me where the honest critic has to say the framework distorts more than it reveals. Then, concretely: if you were designing a single seventy-five-minute discussion for seventeen-year-olds, which two or three scenes would you build it around, and what would you want students to notice in each — Proctor's final refusal to sign his confession and to name others, Hale's reversal and his urging of false confessions, Rebecca Nurse's refusal to lie, Corey being pressed rather than entering a plea? Finally, the question my department actually argues about: does the play have a view on whether any of these cultures is morally superior? Miller's ending appears to side with a man dying for his name, which is an honor verdict, while the framework treats honor as the most violent and least defensible of the three — so does the play end up complicating the ranking rather than answering it, and what does it know about grievance, accusation and reputation that the sociology does not?
