So me and my friend got into a huge argument last night about whether species are real things or just labels we invent — he did his masters in ecology and is very sure of himself, and I mostly flailed. He'd just finished one of those popular books about how humans name and sort the natural world, and he gave me his version of the whole species problem, roughly like this:

"Darwin basically settled it: he showed that species are just strongly marked varieties, so where you draw the line between one species and the next is a matter of taste. Mayr tried to patch that up by defining a species as a group of populations that actually interbreed with each other and can't produce fertile offspring with other groups, and that's still the working definition everyone uses for animals — but it obviously falls apart for bacteria and asexual organisms, which swap genes sideways all the time, so for microbes we just use a percentage cutoff on how similar their genomes are. Since none of these works everywhere, the modern consensus is pluralism: there are lots of species concepts, each biologist picks whichever one suits the question they're asking, and no one concept is more correct than any other. Which really means species aren't real — they're like constellations, groupings we find useful, not joints in nature. Ring species prove the point: you get a chain of populations where A interbreeds with B and B interbreeds with C, but A can't interbreed with C, so there just isn't a line to draw."

I tried to push back and don't think I did it well. My counter-suggestion, which I made up on the spot, was: a species is the largest group of organisms among which gene flow is still frequent enough to hold the group together genetically — go smaller and you're just picking out a local population, go bigger and the thing starts splitting in two — and the "frequent enough" part is a threshold we set by convention, but the group under the threshold is real. He said that's just Mayr's view with a dial bolted onto it and that I'd smuggled in exactly what I was supposed to be arguing for.

I don't know who's closer to right. Can you go through his summary and tell me which parts a philosopher of biology or a working systematist would just accept, which are half-right simplifications of something real, and which are actually wrong? I'm most suspicious of three things but please don't limit yourself to them: (a) whether Darwin really thought species weren't real, as opposed to thinking the rank or the label was conventional; (b) whether "pluralism" really means no concept is any more correct than another, or whether most pluralists still think the groups themselves are real; (c) whether the cutoff-for-microbes thing is meant as a reductio of the whole enterprise or is just a practical fix that nobody thinks is deep. Also: does the constellation analogy do any real work, or is it just a flourish?

Where I'm at personally: I'm inclined to think species are real in some sense and that his summary hasn't actually given me a reason to give that up, but I can't say why, and I'd of done better if I could. I genuinely don't know whether my "largest group held together by gene flow" idea survives his objection or collapses into something already on the table.
