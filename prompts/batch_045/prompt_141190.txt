I've been going back and forth on karma as a theory of desert and I want to know whether my gut on cases actually adds up to anything. I don't want a survey of what various traditions teach (I've read a bit, and the general flavour I got was that the intention behind the act is what does the work) — I want to know whether my own verdicts fit together, and what principle, if any, they commit me to.

My cases:

1. A guy runs a quiet fraud for fifteen years, wipes out the savings of a couple hundred retirees, never gets caught, and dies at 92 with his family around him and no regrets. I want to say something is left outstanding. Not that anyone ought to have punished him — just that he got something he had no claim to and the books aren't closed.

2. Same guy, but at 60 guilt eats him alive, he confesses, repays what he can, and lives the rest of his life broke and wretched. I feel like most of the debt is gone here. Maybe all of it.

3. A man with a tumour that wrecks his impulse control assaults someone. The tumour comes out and he is horrified by what he did. Little or no debt — it wasn't really his act.

4. Someone does something expensive and genuinely helpful for a friend, partly out of care and partly because they knew how good it would look. I want to give partial credit: not zero, not full.

5. Someone does something generous while sincerely believing it's wrong, and hates themselves for doing it. I have no idea whether this generates credit, debt, or neither, and I notice my answer here would tell me something about whether karma tracks the will or the agent's own take on what they're doing.

6. Someone does something cruel and small-minded which, through a chain nobody could have predicted, makes the victim's life much better. I don't think the good outcome cancels the debt at all. I guess that's consistent with 4 — both say the will matters and the results don't — but worth writing down.

7. Someone raised from birth to believe a particular kind of cruelty is a duty, who does it at nineteen with full deliberation and only much later comes to see it was wrong. I go back and forth. Compare 3: there the will was bypassed; here the will is intact but the moral information was wrecked. I can't tell whether that difference should matter.

8. Two verdicts I can't hold together but hold anyway. The fraudster's children inherit his money and live extremely well on it, and I don't think they inherit any debt — the idea makes me recoil. But a university still sitting on an endowment built on stolen land, many turnovers of staff and students later, is a different matter: I do want to say something is owed, and owed by that institution. Both sets of current people are innocent and both benefit, so "they didn't do it" and "they profit from it" can't be what separates them.

9. A wrongdoer who has completely forgotten his wrong — dense amnesia, and he has since become a gentle, decent person. I still want to say the debt is there. But that sits badly with 2, where a psychological change did seem to discharge things.

10. Someone who lives carefully and generously and dies of a painful cancer at 41. I don't want to hear about past lives. I'd rather give up any other verdict on this list than say that was owed.

The principle I keep trying out: maybe the currency isn't suffering at all. Maybe a bad act creates unpaid-for advantage — the agent got to have his way at someone else's expense — and what's owed is that the advantage gets closed, either by his own recognition or, failing that, by some experiential setback sized to how much he got away with. That seems to handle 1 (never closed), 2 (closed by recognition), and 6 (the good outcome doesn't give back what he took). But I can't see what it says about 3 and 7, and I suspect it makes 10 come out as "nothing owed" too fast, in a way that feels like cheating rather than like an answer.

Also nagging at me: if karma is supposed to be built into how things work rather than handed out by anyone, then can it ever fail to arrive? And if it can't fail to arrive, is it still desert, or just causation with a moral-sounding label? I think that worry might sink the whole project, and I'd like to know whether it does.

So: is there one principle that gets 1 through 10, or nearly? State it tightly enough that I could apply it myself, say which case motivates each clause, give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed (there must be one it handles badly), and be straight about which of my verdicts it can't save. If my unpaid-advantage idea is the wrong currency, say what the right one is and why.
