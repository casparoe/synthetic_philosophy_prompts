I direct collections at a mid-sized city museum in northern Europe. We hold thirty-one cast brass pieces from the Benin court — wall plaques and one commemorative head — that reached us through two mid-century auction purchases and a single private bequest. Nothing in our records documents a looting event; nothing documents a clean title either. Next month I present a proposal to one joint panel, in a single session with a long question period, and I need to be ready for the questions rather than for the talk.

The proposal, in brief: separate custody from authority. Physical custody stays with us, on a rolling and revocable basis. What we transfer outright and permanently to a heritage body designated by the Edo court is interpretive and dispositional authority — final say over label text and the language of display, over conservation treatment and handling protocol, over outgoing loans, and over whether an object is displayed at all. Every twenty-five years the arrangement comes up for review, and at any review the designated body may convert that authority into full transfer of title without reopening the underlying question. My argument is that most of what is objectionable about the present arrangement is not who holds the objects but who gets to say what they are and what may be done to them, and that the usual binary — return them or keep them — conceals this.

The panel, who have all read a five-page summary of the proposal: two representatives appointed by the Benin court (a senior court historian and a younger Edo artist-archivist); one official from the Nigerian federal antiquities service; our own head of conservation, who has cared for these objects for two decades; the city's cultural-property lawyer, who drafted the museum's governing statute; three trustees — a retired judge who chairs the board, a hotelier, and a former ambassador — and the city's finance director.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each attributed to the person most likely to raise it and phrased in that person's vocabulary — the conservator's in terms of material risk and cumulative handling, the lawyer's in terms of inalienability and acting beyond our statutory powers, the judge's in terms of precedent and consistency, the finance director's in terms of cost and indemnity. For each objection give a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds, and one line on where that reply is weakest. Then, separately and briefly: what I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk rather than during questions; which two objections have enough force that I should let them modify the proposal instead of answering them; the single reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the meeting at all; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not summarize the proposal back to me, and spend no words on the general case for restitution or on the history of how the collection came to us — this panel is well past that. Keep the entire prep sheet under 1,000 words.
