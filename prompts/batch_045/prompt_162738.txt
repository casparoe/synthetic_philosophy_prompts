I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, and I'm trying to write accurately about the philosophy that is buried in an institution I have been reporting on for two years: the monetized benefit-cost analysis that federal agencies must produce before issuing a major regulation. I can describe the machinery but I can't get clear about what it commits the people running it to, and I'd rather understand that than repeat the standard complaints about it.

Here is the practice as I understand it. An agency proposing a major rule must tally benefits and costs in a single unit, dollars. The largest benefits are usually mortality-risk reductions, and they are valued by summing the tiny probability reductions the rule buys across the exposed population and multiplying by a value per statistical life on the order of ten million dollars — a figure derived partly from the wage premiums workers appear to accept for riskier jobs, and partly from survey questions about what people would pay for small reductions in risk. Benefits that fall on people who never chose anything — future generations, children, people who will never learn that the risk existed — are counted in the same coin and then discounted: a benefit arriving in 2100 counts for less, at a rate that in the more technical guidance is decomposed by a Ramsey-style formula into a pure rate of time preference plus a term reflecting the growth of consumption, weighted by a parameter for the curvature of the welfare function in consumption. But not everything gets monetized. Mortality risk, travel-time savings and some health effects do; pain and suffering, humiliation, the existence of a species, the desecration of a site that matters to someone, generally do not — those go into a qualitative appendix that, in my experience of how decisions actually get made, rarely moves anything. And some expressed preferences get thrown out rather than recorded: I have watched stated-preference work be discarded as invalid when respondents say they would pay roughly as much to protect two thousand birds as two hundred thousand, on the grounds that a preference which is insensitive to scope is not a preference at all.

What I can't reconstruct is the implicit theory. The apparatus looks like it was built by people who think that a person is better off to the extent that her preferences are satisfied and that preferences are revealed in choice — and then it operates in ways that sit badly with that. It monetizes hypothetical survey answers while also discarding some of them as irrational. It counts a dollar of benefit the same whether it lands on a rich person or a poor one, which seems to concede nothing to the diminishing marginal utility of money that welfare economics nominally asserts. It prices a one-in-a-million risk reduction at exactly one-millionth of a statistical life, which assumes that welfare is linear in probability, even though the very surveys it leans on are full of people who are not linear in probability. And because risk reduction is bought with money, the amount of safety a rule can justify is a function of how much money the beneficiaries have — a wealthy population gets more safety, apparently because it cares more, in the only sense of caring the method can register.

So, four things:

First, reconstruct the theory of utility — or of welfare, or of what it is for someone to be better off — that this package of practices presupposes, and do it as charitably as you can.

Second, tell me which features the theory strictly requires and which are merely contingently attached: habits, legal constraints, tractability, political cover. I want to know which parts of the practice would survive if the underlying view of utility were a different one. In particular, does the practice really need the revealed-preference reading it seems to gesture at, or would it run just as well on a hedonic reading, or on an objective-goods or capabilities reading, provided some money-metric were available?

Third, where is the practice internally inconsistent? Do the discounting rule, the linearity-in-risk assumption, the exclusion of scope-insensitive answers, and the refusal to weight by income in fact presuppose different theories? One detail I keep coming back to: the discounting guidance already uses a curvature parameter for the welfare function in consumption, which only makes sense if utility is cardinal and comparable across generations — so the practice may already be committed to interpersonal comparability in the time direction while insisting, in the cross-section, that a dollar is a dollar. I have also seen it floated in draft guidance that agencies report benefits in explicit welfare units, dividing each dollar of benefit by the recipient's income raised to that same curvature parameter, which would simply extend the comparability across persons at a given time. Is that proposed move continuous with what the practice already presupposes, or does it cross a line the current practice stays behind?

Fourth, what would actually have to change if the presupposed view were replaced? Take two rivals and work out the consequences concretely: (i) utility is a measurable mental state, so benefits should be measured in units of experienced well-being rather than in dollars of willingness-to-pay; (ii) welfare consists in objective goods or capabilities, so what people want, or say they want, is evidence about their welfare only when it tracks those goods. For each, tell me the most surprising downstream consequence for real regulation — does either view license monetizing what the practice now refuses to monetize, or forbid monetizing what it now does?

And one more, which matters most for how I frame the piece: is there a defensible reading on which the practice presupposes no theory of utility at all, because benefit-cost analysis is better understood as a decision procedure for aggregating considerations that are incommensurable, or as a political technology for constraining agencies, rather than as an attempt to measure welfare? If that reading is available, what exactly does the dollarization buy that a multi-column ledger with no common unit would not?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important claim about what the practice presupposes — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating my questions back to me. Six hundred to a thousand words.
