I'm writing an essay on Indian epistemology and cinema, and I want to read Kurosawa's *Rashomon* (1950) through Jain anekāntavāda rather than through the relativist or perspectivist framing the film usually attracts. I'd be grateful for a reading that takes both the film and the doctrine seriously, and that is willing to say where the pairing fails.

Here is the doctrine as I understand it — please correct me where I have it wrong. Anekāntavāda is not the claim that there is no fact of the matter; it is the claim that any judgment about a real thing holds only under the qualifier syāt, standardly rendered as in some respect: relative to the standpoint (naya) from which it is made, and relative to the substance, place, time and mode one is speaking of. The characteristic error is ekānta, taking one partial standpoint for the whole truth. Syādvāda supplies the logical instrument: the sevenfold scheme in which a predicate can be conditionally affirmed, conditionally denied, both successively, or declared inexpressible (avaktavya), on the grounds that no single assertion can capture opposed aspects of the same thing at once.

The film looks almost designed for this. Four accounts of one death in a grove — the bandit Tajōmaru's boastful confession, the wife's, the dead samurai's delivered through a medium, and the woodcutter's belated telling at the Rashōmon gate — each apparently catching something the others miss, and each flattering its teller. The film then goes further: the woodcutter's own account is unmasked as self-serving too, since he has pocketed the jewelled dagger. So no standpoint in the film is trustworthy in the way perspectivalism seems to need.

The questions I want the essay to answer:

1. Does the film dramatise anekāntavāda, or dramatise motivated falsehood that merely mimics it? What would have to be true of an account for it to count as a genuine naya rather than a fabrication? If the doctrine needs something like truthfulness — or at least non-deceptiveness — in the reporter, then the film is pressuring an assumption the philosophical literature may simply make. Is that the right way to put the point?

2. Is the application coherent in the first place? Anekāntavāda was forged for disputes about the nature of a thing — permanent or changing, one or many — where the opposed aspects are jointly assertable. A contested killing is a case where, presumably, one man died by a particular hand at a particular moment. Is my essay's premise a category mistake? If so I would rather concede it in the introduction than quietly slide past it.

3. Is there anything in the film that does the work of the fourth mode, the inexpressible? Something the film shows but cannot state? Or is avaktavya simply idle in this context, and if so, what does that tell us about the doctrine's scope?

4. What about the ending — the woodcutter taking the abandoned baby at the gate and the priest's recovered faith in people? If the film answers perspectival anxiety with an act of practical compassion rather than with an epistemic resolution, does anekāntavāda have resources for that move, given its traditional association with non-absolutism and ahiṃsā? Or is the film leaving the doctrine behind to do something else — and is that a criticism of the doctrine or just a different interest?

5. Which scenes should the essay be built around, and where does the reading break down? I am especially interested in what the film shows that the philosophical expositions cannot: whether a standpoint can be less a view of the thing than a project of self-presentation, and what that does to a doctrine built out of standpoints.

I don't need citations or suggestions for further reading — I'm after the interpretation itself, and I'd like it argued rather than surveyed.
