In our Friday journal club I'm the one presenting on predictive processing, and I keep getting stuck on the analogy my supervisor uses for literally everything: the brain is a scientist. He unpacks it in a more specific way than the pop-science versions I'd seen before — hypothesis is the generative model, data is sensory input, updating is prediction-error minimisation, and action is just the experiment, since acting samples the world so the prediction can be confirmed or shot down. But he folds in two extra things I haven't seen anywhere else: he says the same system also decides how much any given error counts, which he jokes is the funding decision (which errors get the money, i.e. precision weighting), and that it also builds and maintains its own instruments, because it grew the sensory epithelia it's reading off. The analogy is doing real argumentative work in the reading group: it's supposed to license the strong claim that perception is controlled hallucination in exactly the sense hallucination is, so that there's no principled difference between my seeing the mug on my desk and what someone in psychosis reports — only a difference in how well the hypothesis happens to track whatever is out there — and a softer one, that a delusion is just bad science done by a lab that won't give up a hypothesis, so the fix has to be at the level of the priors.

I'd rather audit it than just nod along, so: give me the mapping explicitly, a table is fine, with a row for each bit of the source — hypothesis, experiment, data, background theory, instrumentation, funding, peer review, replication, the possibility of being wrong, the intention to find out — and for each either what plays that role in the brain or a blank where there's nothing there. Then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they actually bear on the two conclusions above or are just noise. The ones I suspect are load-bearing: a scientist's data is independent of her hypothesis and she can't move the world to fit it (except that she does choose which experiments to run, which is its own kind of sampling bias), whereas the brain's next input depends on what it just did, and it can reduce error either by changing the model or by changing the world; and then there's the dark room thing, which on this analogy looks like a scientist who'd rather unplug the apparatus than revise the theory. Also: which features of an actual scientist does the analogy quietly import into the brain — intentions, a community, the ability to state what it predicts, a fear of being wrong, a career — and does either conclusion survive once those are taken back out?

Two more things I can't sort out. First, when he slides from predictive processing into the free energy principle proper, the scientist seems to evaporate: he says the same story covers a soap bubble or a thermostat, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's depth or whether the analogy has quietly stopped doing any work. Second, I was toying with a replacement in the pub afterwards — the brain as a lab that generates its own electricity, writes its own grant proposals, publishes nothing except its choice of tomorrow's experiment, and gets fired if it ever notices it's wrong — and I can't tell if that's an improvement or just the same picture with more moving parts. If there's a better analogy for the same target, say what it buys and what it costs. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I just want the argument laid out.
