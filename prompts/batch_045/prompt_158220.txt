**Incommensurability as a failure of calibration, not a failure of translation**

1. The thing that bugs me is how the whole debate seems to assume translation is the model. The standard picture: after a revolution the key words change meaning, so the old and new theories can't be measured against a common measure. Fans say yes, and that's why the choice isn't purely rule-governed; critics say then you can't even say the two theories *disagree* about the same world, so there's no rational comparison and no progress, and Kuhn is a relativist. Both sides seem to accept that either the old theory can be rendered into the new language or it can't.

2. My own case. In the mid-fifties and sixties people had been towing magnetometers over mid-ocean ridges for years and getting these striped wiggles in the record, symmetric about the ridge crest. Ships had sailed over that pattern repeatedly and it was in the files. Then the sea-floor spreading story plus the Vine–Matthews(-Morley) reading of the stripes turns the wiggle into a record of geomagnetic reversals, and the distance of a stripe from the ridge axis into an *age*. My claim (a): before that, the stripe was signal, not data. You need a calibrated procedure that converts a deflection on a chart into a measurement of something, and the spreading hypothesis is what supplied the calibration. Evidence isn't just sitting there waiting to be noticed. My claim (b): after the change the same numbers meant something different — a magnetic high became a time marker — and the holdouts, people who'd built careers on the mantle being too rigid for drifting continents, weren't failing to understand an argument, they were reading the same trace under a different calibration and getting nothing out of it.

3. The positive proposal. In metrology two instruments are commensurable if there's a transfer standard that both can measure against. When the standard gets redefined you don't say the old numbers are untranslatable; you say they need recalibrating, and recalibration is (i) partial — it works for some quantities, not all, (ii) asymmetric, and (iii) retrospective, you can only do it once you know both procedures. Map that onto paradigms: rival paradigms share a large *transfer lexicon* — instruments, field procedures, low-level phenomenal talk, all the stuff that lets you keep working in the same lab — and differ in a smallish set of what I'll call *scale-setting* terms, the ones that do the theoretical work and that fix what counts as a measurement of what. Local incommensurability = no transfer standard for those terms. Nothing global, nothing about whole conceptual schemes, no claims about untranslatable languages.

4. The part I like most. Recalibration runs one way. The incoming taxonomy has slots in which it can reconstruct the old theory's successes — you can explain why the old approach worked where it worked, which is also why those successes get explained away rather than carried over — but the outgoing taxonomy has no slot for the new stuff at all. That asymmetry, not symmetric mutual untranslatability, is what produces the experience that the other side isn't merely wrong but not-even-wrong. And note this is not the same as saying the new theory is true; it's about comparative *reconstructive power*, whichever way it happens to point.

5. The bit I think might be new, and where I'm least confident. Call terms *sample-anchored* when their reference is pinned by pointing at a local stuff — you point, the theory can be wrong about what you pointed at, and reference survives theory change; the water/H2O kind of story works fine for these. Call them *role-defined* when the referent is whatever plays a certain part in the theory: mass, force, gene, plate. There's no sample to anchor those to, and if the role changes the thing changes. So the direct-reference rescue only covers the cases revolutions don't actually turn on. Is that distinction any good? Is there an obvious counterexample — a role-defined term that came through a revolution with its reference intact, or a sample-anchored one that didn't?

6. My own suspicion, which I can't settle: I think at least some of what I say in section 2 is not about incommensurability at all but about observation being theory-laden, or maybe about underdetermination, and I'm not sure I could tell you which is which if pressed.
