Hey — I'm prepping the last session of my undergraduate ethics seminar, the one on the paradox of deontology: constraints are supposed to express the special significance of individuals, and yet they can require you to stand by while more of exactly what the constraint forbids happens, to the very people the constraint exists to protect. The standard case — one killing to stop five — they've all seen before they walk in, and I've decided the number-swapping is doing too much work. Half the class just quietly flips into consequentialist mode, the other half hides behind doing vs. allowing, and we never get at the structure underneath.

So: build me a version of the paradox with the numbers taken out. Specs:

- One paragraph, realistic. A clinic, a family, a department, a small firm, a hospital ward. No trolleys, no villains with buttons, no sci-fi.
- Exactly one person whose interests are on the table: the person the constraint is there to protect. No comparing counts of violations, nobody on another track.
- Pick some agent-centered constraint — not killing; use promise-keeping, confidentiality, consent, a role-based duty, whatever you like — that a reflective deontologist would say must be respected here, and where respecting it leaves that same person clearly worse off than violating it would. Worse off by their own lights, at the time of action, with everything relevant known to them.
- Close the usual exits, and say in a line how each is closed: (a) the protected person can't simply release you from the constraint, and you don't get there by making them incompetent — if they're not competent to release you they're not competent to fix what's "worse off by their own lights" either; (b) no uncertainty anywhere — no mights, no probabilities, no appeal to what a rule like this does in general; (c) nothing hangs on aggregation or on anyone else's welfare; (d) the constraint isn't void for duress, coercion, or vagueness; (e) the earlier-self's authority over the later self (if you go that route) can't rest on the later self being impaired or less informed, since that's just the ordinary advance-directive case and it dissolves the puzzle.
- And don't let it be two deontological constraints colliding — no "keep the promise vs. don't lie." That's a different and easier problem. It has to be the constraint versus the good of the person it's for.

What I want after the scenario:

1. Say what each of these says, and why: a constraint-theorist who forbids violating the constraint even to prevent further violations of that very constraint; someone who thinks deontological constraints are only moderately stringent and give out past a threshold; and a contractualist who grounds constraints in what could be justified to each person individually. No names needed — I know who's who, I want the moves, not the reading list.
2. Which single feature of the case is doing the work? Show me that it's generating these verdicts rather than the case just stipulating them.
3. Now break it: which feature, if I removed it, collapses the whole thing back into the one-vs-five version — or into nothing at all?
4. Give me the best thing a deontologist would say to wriggle out, and be honest about whether that's a solution or just a place to stand.

If the specs turn out not to be jointly satisfiable, say so and tell me which one has to go. I'd much rather have that then a case that cheats.
