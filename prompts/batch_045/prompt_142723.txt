Hey — I'm a PhD student (formal epistemology / phil science adjacent, though my advisor would say I'm really just a stats person with philosophy envy) and I definately keep running into the same wrinkle in my own modeling work and in the literature, and I can't find a word for it. Here's the pattern.

Take the standard setup: some data is already on the books, everybody knows it, and then a new theory comes along and explains it, and people treat that as a big confirmation — the classic example being the anomaly in Mercury's orbit that had been known for decades and then fell out of a new theory of gravity. Now compare a case I keep seeing in my own field. A theorist is building a model of, say, voting behavior. There's some regularity that everyone in the area has known about since the 90s. She doesn't fit her model to it — no parameters are tuned to match it — but she does scribble in her notebook that it would be neat if this also handled X, and when she has to choose between two formal variants of the model, she picks the one that looks more promising for eventually dealing with X. Years later the model does explain X, and she presents it as a successful prediction. Half her readers say it's legit because the data never entered as an explicit constraint; the other half say it's cooked because X shaped the whole shape of the framework.

So here's what I'm after. I know about the basic Bayesian headache: if you already know the evidence, its probability is 1, so plain conditionalizing on it can't raise your confidence in a new theory at all. And I know the usual workarounds people wheel out — go back to a counterfactual prior from before you learned the data, or else update on the newly learned logical fact that the theory actually entails the data, which you didn't know before. Fine. But none of those seem to sort out the cases I actually care about, because the question isn't just whether the data was known, or even whether it was explicitly used. My cases sit right on the seam between used in construction and never considered at all: the data hovered in the background, it informed informal hunches, it decided wich formalism got explored, it made the theorist suspect relevance without ever testing it. Sometimes the theorist knew the data but had no idea it mattered until after the theory was finished; sometimes she suspected all along and picked variants accordingly; sometimes the community knew the data but she personally didn't; sometimes she deliberately avoided looking at it until the theory was done, which feels like the cleanest case but might be self-deception.

Is there an established concept or literature for this middle zone — for the difference between data that explicitly shaped a theory and data that merely hovered in the background, and for how that difference bears on whether old data can confirm? I know people talk about whether the data was used in building the theory, and I know there's the more general distinction between predicting something fresh and merely accommodating something you already had, but my cases sit right on that line and I don't think a bare used/not-used or prediction/accommodation distinction captures them: the influence is real but implicit, and it comes in degrees. What are the nearest concepts here, how does each differ from what I'm describing, and which fits best, if any? If nothing established fits, I'd like you to coin a term and define it, with boundary cases — e.g., what do we say when the data was known to the community but not to the theorist, or when she deliberately avoided it but the whole research program was aimed at it anyway? I'm not after a label to slap on it; I want the carving. What exactly is the property that makes some old-data confirmations count and others not, and where exactly is the line for these in-between cases?
