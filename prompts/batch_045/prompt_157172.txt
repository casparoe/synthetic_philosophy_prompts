I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas — and I'm working on a piece about big irreversible life decisions (having a first kid, moving countries, retraining in your forties). A well-reviewed popular book on decision-making that a source sent me has a section on L. A. Paul's 2014 book *Transformative Experience*, and I want to lean on it, but I don't trust my own eye for where its gone wrong. Here's the passage, more or less as written:

Some experiences are so big that they change you: the philosopher L. A. Paul calls these "epistemically transformative" — experiences that change your preferences, your character, your sense of who you are. Because you don't know what you don't know, you can't even attach probabilities to the outcomes, and the whole expected-utility apparatus collapses: it needs you to list the outcomes and say how much you value each one, and in a transformative case you can do neither, so any number you feed it is invented. Paul's example is becoming a vampire. You can read everything ever written about vampires and interview as many actual vampires as you like, but until you've been turned you have no idea whether you'd enjoy it, and no amount of second-hand information helps, because other people's experiences aren't your experiences. Having a first child works the same way. Her conclusion is that such choices aren't, strictly speaking, rationally decidable at all — there's no such thing as making them well or badly — and the honest response is to see them as a leap of faith, chosen on the basis of who you want to become. The lesson generalizes: cost-benefit analysis, in economics or in policy, is worthless whenever the stakes are high enough to change someone.

A few things I'm inclined to believe and a few I'm suspicious of. The "you don't know what you don't know" line is the sentence I'd like to build the piece around, and the vampire bit matches what I've heard second-hand. But the claim that there's no such thing as choosing well or badly can't be right, can it? My editor is going to want a "so what should people actually do" paragraph, and "nothing, it's a leap of faith" is a paragraph I'd rather not file.

So: can you go through that passage and tell me, bit by bit, what a philosopher sympathetic to Paul would happily endorse, what's a fair popularization or simplification, and what they'd call a misreading? I especially want to know whether it handles the vampire example right — including the bit about learning from other people's reports — and whether the "cost-benefit analysis is worthless at high stakes" conclusion survives once the mistakes are fixed.

One more thing, wich is my own extrapolation rather than anything in the book, so tell me if I'd be out on a limb: the case I actually want to open the piece with is a parent deciding whether to get a cochlear implant for a deaf infant. The child will grow into a different person, with different experiences and values, depending on what the parents choose, and obviously can't be consulted. Does Paul's framework have anything to say when the person making the choice isn't the person who'll be transformed, or does the "choose who you want to become" move just not transfer? I'd rather cut the example than print a claim I invented.
