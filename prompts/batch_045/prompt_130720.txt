I run a fortnightly discussion group at work — eight to ten backend and ML engineers, none of us with any philosophy training, and nobody willing to read a paper beforehand. So I need material that stands entirely on its own. What I'd like from you is a dialogue.

Here is the thing I keep circling, in my own terms. Take a codebase with a test suite that has been passing for years. Someone then writes a module that, as a consequence of how it works, predicts that those particular tests would pass — including the odd, arbitrary-looking ones nobody had explained. Intuitively that is a point in favour of the module: it gets the weird cases right for the right reason. But the standard Bayesian picture says confirmation works by conditionalising: you raise your confidence in a hypothesis by the factor you get from evidence whose probability wasn't already one. If the passing tests were already known, their probability is one, conditionalising does nothing, and the fact that the module predicts them looks like it can't count at all. The evidence was already priced in. That is the puzzle I want dramatised — a theory's tidy fit with data everyone already had seems like evidence for it, yet the machinery says it can't be.

I want two characters who are equally good at this, both engineers, neither a pushover, each of whom gets to make the other concede something real. One of them defends the Bayesian machinery, roughly as follows: the fix is counterfactual bookkeeping — ask what your confidence in the hypothesis would have been had you not yet known the data, and treat the gap between that and your actual confidence as the confirmation the data supplies. The other thinks that move is where the trouble starts: there is no settled fact about what you would have believed without the data, so the answer is a free parameter you can tune to get whatever verdict you like, and we would do better to say that confirmation is a matter of the hypothesis genuinely explaining the data rather than of when anybody happened to learn it.
