The proposition I have in mind is this: reality contains a plurality of concrete, maximal, spatiotemporally and causally isolated totalities, each of them as concrete and as real as ours, with necessity and possibility analysed as unrestricted quantification over them. That is, concretist modal realism of the kind associated with David Lewis is true, and not merely convenient. By true I mean true in whatever sense is at issue in metaphysics: not the best available regimentation of our modal discourse, not indispensable to the semantic machinery we happen to have, not true-if-you-like-this-way-of-talking.

Let me also say what would count against it, so the number has something to answer to. I would count the view as effectively refuted if any of the following held: the quantifier over worlds in our best semantic theories can be eliminated in favour of primitive modal operators without explanatory loss; some non-concrete surrogate — set-theoretic, linguistic, or combinatorial — performs every job for which worlds are invoked; or the account of modal knowledge available to the concretist is either circular or collapses into scepticism.

5. That we can have modal knowledge on a plenitudinous picture.

And one more, which is the term I actually want your view on, because I do not think it sits in the standard dialectic:

6. Call it the trilemma of plenitude. The concretist typically secures the richness of the plurality from a principle of recombination: any arrangement of duplicates of the fundamental entities is itself a world. But then: (i) if recombination ranges only over the fundamental entities that actually occur, the resulting plurality cannot contain alien natural properties or alien kinds of individual, and their possibility looks like exactly the sort of thing the plurality was supposed to settle rather than inherit from the actual inventory; (ii) if recombination is loosened so that alien entities may be recombined, then the space of possibilities is no longer generated by anything, and plenitude becomes a brute posit; (iii) if plenitude is simply brute, the theory's traditional boast — that it costs almost nothing in ideology and buys an enormous amount — begins to read as a cost, since a posit that generates everything explains nothing.
