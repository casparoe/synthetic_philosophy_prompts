I am a graduate student in a seminar on causation this term, and I am drafting a paper around what I have been taking to be the central fault line in the reading. Before I build an argument on it, I would like to check that I have the contrast right, and I would be grateful for a reply in American English.

As I understand the dialectic: Lewis's counterfactual account analyses causation as counterfactual dependence between events, and it is generally agreed to come apart on cases of preemption and symmetric overdetermination. The interventionist account is presented as the successor, and I have been reading its advantage as follows. Instead of asking what would have happened in a merely possible situation, it asks what actually happens when someone manipulates the cause, and so it swaps speculative modality for something empirically checkable.

Two things seem to follow, and they are what I want to press. First, if interventionism really turns on actual manipulation, it can have nothing to say about causes no one can manipulate: the role of a nearby supernova in a mass extinction, or of the money supply in a national economy. It is strange to call a theory of causation general when it is silent there. Second, if the only problem is preemption, I do not see why manipulation is needed at all. My own proposal, which I have been calling a route-locked counterfactual, is to evaluate whether, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred, while holding fixed the value of every variable that does not lie on a directed path from C to E. That appears to dispatch preemption for free and to work in domains nobody can intervene in.

I tried it against a case from an engineering project I worked on before graduate school. A cooling loop has a primary pump A and a standby pump B that a pressure sensor starts only if A fails. When A runs, coolant flows; had A failed, the sensor would have started B and coolant would have flowed anyway. My rule gives the wrong verdict here, because the standby chain from A through the sensor and B to the flow is itself a directed path from A to the effect, so B does not get held fixed, and A's running comes out as no cause of the flow. I do not see how to repair this without holding B fixed by hand, which feels like cheating.

So my questions: is my reading of what interventionism buys us correct? Can route-locking be repaired, and if it can, does it make interventionism redundant rather than merely equivalent? And if both readings are wrong, where does the actual disagreement between the two accounts lie?
