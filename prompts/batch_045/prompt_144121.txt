I write about science and ideas, and I'm finishing a feature on what real-time synthetic video has done to the everyday concept of knowledge. My stalking-horse case: a reporter spends two hours on a video call and comes to know that she is talking to a particular human being; she also knows that if she is talking to that human being, then the call is not a real-time synthetic avatar driven by someone else. Does she thereby know that the call isn't synthetic? The standard dispute is about whether knowledge is closed under known logical entailment, and I'd like a number rather than a tour of the literature.

Take the principle in its usual form: if a subject knows that p, knows that p entails q, and comes to believe q by competently deducing it from p, then she knows q. Give me your credence that this principle is true, and decompose it. I want, at minimum: your credence that some modal condition in the neighbourhood of Nozick's tracking requirement — sensitivity, or the safety-style conditions that came after it — is at least a necessary condition on knowledge; conditional on that, your credence that such a condition transmits through competent deduction; your credence that the intuitive pull of closure in cases like the video call is better explained away (by shifts in what alternatives are relevant, by the attributor's context doing the work, or by something pragmatic) than by closure itself; and your credence that the anti-sceptical payoff people want from the case really needs closure at all, rather than being available some other way.

One further thing I'd like you to price, since I want to use a distinction in the piece and I don't trust my own handle on it. Call a case *homomethodic* when the entailed belief and the belief in the entailment are certified by the same cognitive method as the premise belief — perception certifying both, or testimony, or a priori reflection — and *heteromethodic* when the entailment is certified by a different route than the premise. It has seemed to me that closure is most attractive in the homomethodic cases and least attractive where the sceptic needs it, which is usually heteromethodic. So split your final credence three ways: that closure holds in full generality, that it holds at best homomethodically, and that it holds in no form an anti-sceptic could lean on. Those three should sum to one.

Then tell me which term in the decomposition you are least confident in, which single sub-question your headline number is most sensitive to, and what argument or finding — not what reading — would move it the most. Finally, say whether you think the synthetic-avatar case is structurally different from the brain-in-a-vat case in any way that matters here, or whether it is the old argument in new costume. Please reason from the case and from first principles; I have no use for citations and won't check them.
