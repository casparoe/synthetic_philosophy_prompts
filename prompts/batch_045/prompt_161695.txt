I write to you in a private capacity, but the question has stopped being academic for me. My daughter's school has rolled out an AI tutor for mathematics, and the parent advisory group I sit on has been asked to endorse extending it to unsupervised homework sessions next term. The vendor's white paper makes an argument I keep meeting in different forms, and I would like it audited rather than merely disagreed with.

The argument runs like this. Reasoning, properly understood, is generate-and-test; that is all it has ever been. Darwinian creatures acquire design without a designer through blind variation and selective retention, at the cost of the individuals who carry the losing variants. Skinnerian creatures acquire it within a lifetime, by trying things and being shaped by what actually happens, so that some of their behavioural variants die instead of them. Popperian creatures go one better: they can rehearse candidate actions against an inner model and let the candidates die in their stead, which is why they are the first creatures that can be said to think. Gregorian creatures, the fourth kind, do not get smarter; they inherit smarter, by absorbing the tools their culture has already built — notation, arithmetic, argument — so that the space of candidates is pruned before any candidate is born. So: a system that at inference time produces many candidate solutions, scores them, and emits the winning one is a Popperian creature. And Popperian creatures are exactly the ones you can leave alone, because they anticipate failure internally rather than paying for it in the world. Hence the tutor can be trusted to catch its own mistakes, and the demand for a human in the loop is obsolete — or worse, condescending to a thing that reasons.

I would like the mapping set out explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine — naming what plays the role of the variation, of the selection, of the environment, of the unit of retention, and of whatever dies when a candidate is rejected. My own gloss, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that the four kinds are not a ladder of intelligence but a ladder of where the cost of a mistake falls: offspring; then the creature's own wasted and sometimes painful trials; then a rehearsal that costs almost nothing; then a tool that spares the trial entirely. If that is right, then the load-bearing question is what dies when the model discards a candidate, and my suspicion is that the answer is nothing — not because the discarded candidates are worthless, but because nothing in the setup ever stood to pay for them.

Then I want the disanalogies sorted by how much of the conclusion each one removes. Among the ones I can see, and I would like any I have missed:

1. Popperian-ness may be a property of a creature with a stake in the outcome rather than a property of a mechanism. A rehearsal protects you only if some later you is the thing being protected.
2. The candidates are drawn from a distribution learned from a corpus, so the variation is neither blind in the Darwinian sense nor conjectural in the Popperian sense; it is pre-weighted by what the lineage has already seen. I have taken to calling this amplified sampling, and I would like a better name if there is one, plus a verdict on whether it matters that no candidate is ever genuinely new relative to the training distribution.
3. The scoring is a learned statistical proxy rather than a run in the world. A Popperian creature tests hypotheses against a model of the world that it can be surprised by; a reranker is trained to agree with human preferences about text, which looks closer to a Skinnerian history than to a test.
4. Nothing is retained across the episode — no heredity, no accumulated niche, no individual that outlives its mistakes. The session is a lifetime that ends when the window closes.
5. Gregorian status belongs to a creature embedded in a lineage of tools. On the face of it the tutor is a tool rather than a tool-user, and the child in front of the screen is the only Popperian or Gregorian agent in the room; if so, removing the human does not remove a redundant safety net, it removes the only agent there ever was.

I also want the timing question settled, because I suspect it is where the argument quietly moves house. If the generate-and-test that matters happens in training, then the selection is real — weights change, and there is genuine heredity across generations — but what is Darwinian or Skinnerian is the lineage, not the session, and a lineage that has been well shaped is not thereby a thing that can catch its own errors at eight o'clock on a Tuesday. If it happens at inference, then there is no retention at all and the analogy has no heredity to work with. Does the conclusion need one of these readings specifically, and does the white paper say which it is using?

Finally: is there a better analogy for what these systems do, and is there a version of the argument that supports keeping the human in the loop rather than removing it — the tutor as a Gregorian tool the child takes up, with the generate-and-test that matters happening in the child rather than in the model? And at the end I would like an honest verdict on whether the white paper's conclusion was being carried by the analogy the whole time, or whether some part of it can stand on its own.
