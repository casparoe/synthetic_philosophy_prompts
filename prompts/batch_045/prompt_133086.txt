I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book that has a weird decision-theory knot right in the middle of it, and I could use help working out what's actually worth my time to understand. The setup, roughly: my protagonist — 44, a river pilot, not an academic — gets a letter from a small foundation with a very good predictive model of her, trained mostly on things she wrote online between nineteen and twenty-five. The letter says, more or less: we ran your model, we asked it whether you'd send us 5,000 euros if we told you the coin came up tails, it said yes, the coin did in fact come up tails, so here we are; had it come up heads we'd have quietly sent you 100,000 and never mentioned the coin. That's the standard counterfactual mugging as I understand it, except for two things I've invented and can't find discussed anywhere: (a) the thing they simulated is a much younger version of her, from before a stretch of life that changed what she cares about pretty radically — she doesn't want money the way that girl wanted money, and she's not sure she wants that girl's money either; and (b) in the heads branch the payout would have gone not to her but to her daughter, which is about the only thing she still unambiguously cares about. (There's also that blackmail-letter case in the same family — the XOR one, where whether you even receive the letter depends on some exclusive-or between what you'd do and whether you're sick — but I may be garbling it; I read about it in a blog post years ago and I can't look anything up from where I'm writing.)

What I want is not the solution to the puzzle and definately not a reading list or a pile of citations. I've got maybe four months of reading time before I have to draft these chapters, no maths beyond basic probability, and no university library, so I need to know where to spend the little I've got. So: which of the open questions clustered around this kind of case would you prioritise for someone in my position, and in what order? Things I have in mind: how fine-grained the "decision algorithm" the predictor is supposed to be tracking has to be, and what happens when there isn't one stable such thing across a life; whether the case really turns on what's rational in the moment or on what sort of person it's good to be; how much the standard verdicts depend on the predictor being near-perfect, and what happens as you dial reliability down; whether anything changes when the beneficiary in the branch that didn't happen is someone other than you; and whatever else you think is load-bearing that I haven't thought of at all.

For each one, tell me how much else hangs on it — if I get it wrong, does the chapter become nonsense, or is it a detail I can fudge — how much of it someone like me can actually get their head around in the time available, and whether you think it's an overrated question that philosophers like becuase it's clean rather than becuase it matters. Defend the ordering rather than just listing things. And at the end, say how confident you are in the ranking overall, and single out the one claim in it you're least sure of — I'd much rather know where you're guessing than be handed a confident story I then go and build a hundred pages on.
