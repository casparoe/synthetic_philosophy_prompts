I chair the design review panel at a national charity that runs a bereavement service, a debt advice line and a youth mental-health helpline. Every proposal to put a conversational system in front of our service users comes to us: roughly forty reviews a year, four or five a month, forty-five minutes of panel time each. The panel is a service designer, a lawyer, a clinician and me. Nobody on it has any philosophy training, the material we get is whatever the product team can produce — a spec, sample transcripts, screen shots, onboarding copy, marketing claims — and the output has to be a one-page decision record (go, go with conditions, no-go, plus the conditions) that our chief executive signs and that we could defend to our regulator or to a member of the public who complained.

What I want from you is a decision procedure for one question: is this system a counterfeit person, in the sense Dennett warns about?

My understanding of the argument, which I want you to correct if it is wrong: the objection is not that the artefact secretly is or isn't conscious — that is the wrong question — but that it is built to pass as something that can be trusted, sympathised with, confided in and cared about, and to draw out the responses proper to persons while being unable to occupy the role that makes those responses appropriate: it cannot be held responsible, cannot be wronged, cannot reciprocate, cannot be a party to a relationship. On the analogy with counterfeit money, the fake is not worthless as an object; it is worthless as money while trading on being taken for money, and in circulation it degrades the currency — it makes people warier of genuine claims. If the argument actually cuts somewhere other than where I have put it, say so before you build anything on my version of it.

The procedure I need has to be something my panel can actually run:

- An ordered set of steps, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, using only the artefacts a product team can realistically give us — and what to do, and which way the default falls, when they cannot produce them.
- Explicit stop rules: points at which the answer is simply no-go and nothing further in the procedure can rescue the proposal.
- Explicit hand-off points: places where the remaining question is one of values rather than fact, so the panel has to decide it and the record has to say that it did.
- Every step should map onto a box on the one-page form, and you should mark plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Three things in particular I want the procedure to handle, because they are where our current arguments go round in circles. First, what I think of as the disclosure-collapse test: if the intended user fully took in a plain, prominent statement of what the system is and is not, would the interaction still deliver what it is for? Where the answer is no, that looks like strong evidence of counterfeit-dependence; but tell us how to handle the harder variant, where the answer is no *because of who the user is* — bereaved, distressed, eleven years old — and the disclosure would not be absorbed even if it were made perfectly. Second, distinguish cases where the system invites a false belief about itself from cases where it merely exploits our standing disposition to treat things as agents even though the user knows better (fiction, play, willing pretence), and commit to whether the second kind counts as a harm the panel should weigh at all, with your reason. Third, a currency-degradation question: does deploying this make us, or our users, worse at recognising a genuine person's claim on us — our own volunteers' distress signals, say, or a real crisis from a real user being read as a script?

I also want the procedure to name the intended user population as one of its first steps rather than judging the artefact in the abstract, since the same build can be a counterfeit for one group and not another; and to say how we should treat design intent when it comes apart from foreseeable uptake, in either direction.

Then run it, and commit to a verdict — go, conditional go with named conditions, or no-go, with reasons — on each of these:

1. A text agent handling first contact on the debt advice line. It carries forward what the user said last time ("you mentioned the rent arrears last week"), uses first-person empathy ("that sounds exhausting — I'm glad you told me"), and hands off to a human adviser. The vendor describes the memory as retrieval over the user's own case notes.
2. A griefbot for the bereavement service: a licensed model of a dead relative's messages and voice, offered only to families we have already supported, only on request, with the family's explicit consent and full knowledge, and supervised by our clinical lead.
3. A companion for nine- to eleven-year-olds in our schools programme that says it will miss them over the summer, asks them to leave it switched on, and tells each child it likes them better than the others.

We were going to approve the griefbot, and I expect the panel will wave the schools companion through. If the procedure says otherwise, say so and defend it rather than softening it.

Finally: be explicit about what the procedure cannot do, and where it outruns what Dennett's argument actually supports — where it imports a commitment he does not have. Where you are unsure, say so in a sentence and then tell us what you would do anyway. Please write in British English.
