I'm writing a short position paper for a graduate reading group in philosophy of cognitive science, on whether cognition is computation and, more specifically, on what it takes for a physical system to genuinely implement a computation rather than merely be describable in computational terms. Before I commit to print I'd like an audit of my commitments as a set. Here they are.

1. Computationalism about cognition: every token cognitive state of a system is, or is constituted by, a token computational state of that system, and the explanations cognitive science aims at are computational explanations.

2. Implementation is constrained, not free. I concede the triviality style of argument — that for any ordinary open system and many an abstract formalism there is some mapping under which the system satisfies the formalism — but I think this shows only that unconstrained mapping is too cheap. My own proposal: perturbation-robust implementation. A system implements a computation C only if there is a mapping from its physical states onto C's states that survives counterfactual nudging: move the system into near-by states its physics permits, and the transitions it then undergoes still track C's state-transition function over a non-trivial neighbourhood. A mapping that holds only along the actual trajectory is gerrymandered and yields no implementation.

3. Pluralism. Even with that constraint in place I think a single system implements many inequivalent computations, at different grains and over different state variables, and I take this to be harmless. The computation that constitutes a given mental state, on my view, is the one our best cognitive-scientific explanation actually cites.

4. Case: the prosthesis. Consider an implanted silicon device fitted to reproduce a hippocampal subregion's input-to-output transformation from recorded spike trains, standing in for damaged tissue, with performance on the memory task restored. (Treat it as hypothetical, though it is close to designs of a kind that has been tried in animals.) I want to say yes: the animal with the device is in a genuine memory state realized by the device.

5. Case: the giant lookup table. No. A system that reproduces a person's conversational input-output function by table lookup over a vast but finite history does not think or understand — and I don't only mean that it is psychologically or computationally implausible. I want to say that it implements no computation that constitutes thought.

6. Simulating is not duplicating. A simulation that merely reproduces a brain's input-output pattern in some other medium is not thereby a mind; a system that duplicates its causal-computational organization at the right grain would be. I take this to be compatible with 1, not a retreat from it.

7. Representationalism about computation. The computations cognitive science posits are computations over representational states; a purely formal, syntactic account does not capture the kind of computation that does explanatory work here.

8. Two methodological commitments that sit oddly together: cognition is substrate-independent, so whatever realizes the computation can in principle be made of anything; and cognitive neuroscience should nevertheless model at the level of dendritic and synaptic mechanisms, because that detail does real explanatory work.

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on the topic. I don't know where the trouble is — it may be in a pair I haven't noticed — and I have no settled view about which commitment I would abandon. I would rather you told me which of these are in good order than that you found something wrong with each. In particular, I want you to discriminate between pairs that genuinely pull against each other and pairs that only look as though they do.

Please structure the response as follows:

- Ranked tensions — about 400 words. The genuine tensions, ordered most to least severe. For each: name the two commitments, state in a few sentences the mechanism of the conflict (not just that they conflict), and say what I would have to give up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the package.

- Apparent tensions that dissolve — about 150 words. The pairs that look inconsistent but are not, with the distinction that dissolves each.

- Does the robustness constraint bear the load? — about 150 words. Whether perturbation-robust implementation can do the work I assign it in 2 given 3, and whether it can separate 4 from 5 without further assumptions I haven't stated.

- Cheapest revision — about 120 words. The single revision that would clean up the most, and its price.

- Verdict — about 80 words. Whether the package is defensible as it stands, and what I should be most worried about.
