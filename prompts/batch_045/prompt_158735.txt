I'm applying for a nine-month postdoctoral fellowship in political philosophy, and I need a two-page proposal (about 1,000 words) on a question that has become oddly practical for me over the last year.

Two things happened. In the spring I sat on a criminal jury that deadlocked; in the autumn I joined the board of a small health foundation that is rewriting its bylaws, where the live question is which decisions have to go to the full board and which may be settled by the executive committee or the director. Rereading the Condorcet jury theorem in that light, what struck me is how completely the standard statement takes the agenda for granted: voters have some competence above one-half, votes are independent, and the question arrives from nowhere. But bodies like juries, boards, and legislatures do not vote on a random sample of questions. Items reach a vote partly *because* they are contested, because no subcommittee could settle them, because someone with partial information chose not to route them elsewhere. If the difficulty of a question is correlated with whether it reaches a vote — and if the people doing the routing have their own, differently-informed view of how hard a question is — then the population of voted questions is selected on something close to the dependent variable.

My half-formed idea: call it the selective submission problem. Once you endogenize the agenda, the theorem may license a weaker conclusion than its defenders want, and there are two conclusions that get run together and should be separated. One is that majority rule is reliable *for the questions it actually decides*. The other is that a group improves its overall decision quality by *routing more questions to majority rule*. It looks to me as though the first can survive selective submission while the second does not — a body can raise its observed hit rate precisely by keeping hard questions away from the vote, in which case the epistemic case for majority rule and the epistemic case for majoritarianism come apart.
