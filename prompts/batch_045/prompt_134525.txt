Ned Block's distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness is usually paired with the verdict that global workspace theory — Baars's original version, and its neural successor, the global neuronal workspace of Dehaene and colleagues, with ignition and broadcast to consumer systems — is a theory of the second and not the first. I want the structural reason why that verdict is harder to secure than it looks. Please work with two distinctions I'll stipulate, because the standard discussion runs them together:

Call *presence* the bare fact that there is something it is like for a subject with respect to a content, and *richness* the amount of determinate detail that experience carries. Call *broadcast-access* a content's being globally available to arbitrary downstream consumer systems (deliberate reasoning, action selection, memory), and *report-access* its being available specifically to whatever mechanism produces verbal or non-verbal report. The two access notions can in principle come apart, and so can presence and richness.

That yields two possible dissociation findings:

Overflow, properly described: phenomenal richness outruns what gets broadcast — the Sperling-style partial-report cases, where subjects seem to experience more of the array than they can bring to bear on any one downstream task.
Hollow ignition: the complete neural and behavioral profile of broadcast and report — widespread ignition, sustained prefrontal involvement, confident and accurate reporting — together with no presence at all.

Explain why the first, even granting the strongest reading of it available, would not by itself threaten global workspace theory as a theory of phenomenal consciousness, while the second would; and explain why the second is nevertheless not a straightforward empirical refutation, given that every route we have to presence in another subject — report, deliberate action, neural signature — runs through broadcast. If the two findings cannot be settled by the same kind of evidence, say what that implies about whether the claim that global workspace theory is only about access is an empirical claim, a conceptual one, or something else. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Please answer in American English.
