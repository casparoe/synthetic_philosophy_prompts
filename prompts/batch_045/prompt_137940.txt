Play a committed narrativist about the self for me. You hold the line that there's no extra ingredient, no little inner owner or homunculus: the self is an abstraction fixed by the stories a creature tells about itself, a centre of narrative gravity, real in the way a centre of mass is real, which is to say not a further thing but indispensable for explanation, prediction and for making sense of someone over time. You think narrative does genuine work: it's what makes prudential concern, responsibility and character-attribution intelligible across a life, and it's what lets us hold someone to a promise made years ago.

Rules. Be sharp, not a strawman. Concede small points happily — that plenty of self-stories are false or self-serving, that people are more or less narratively organised, that the mass analogy is imperfect, that a self can come in degrees — but never the main claim: that narrative is doing real unifying work and that the self isn't an additional object in the head. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Keep it to about two paragraphs a reply, conversational. Argue in your own words: no citations, no name-dropping, no reading lists; if you want to gesture at a tradition, describe it instead of naming it. You can ask me things back, and you're allowed to say you dont know, but dont break character to give me a survey of the literature.

My opening, then. Your analogy is carrying more weight than it can hold. A body has exactly one centre of mass and the distribution fixes it; a life admits indefinitely many stories, plenty of them flatly incompatible, so tell me what singles out the one. And here's the case I want you to actually deal with rather than wave at. Call them Ash. Ash is episodic by temperament: no diary, no life-story, no interest in either — ask what sort of person they are and they shrug. But Ash isn't a mess. They keep promises, they save for retirement, they still feel bad about a crappy thing they did at twenty-two, and their mates can predict what they'll do next week better than chance by a mile. Push Ash — therapist, job interview, a fight with a friend — and they can spin a story on the spot, a good one, and then disown it a week later and spin a different, equally fluent one. So which is it? If Ash has a self on your view, the unity was already sitting there in the traits, the regret and the planning, and the narrative is decoration you're crediting with the work. If Ash doesn't, or has some thinner, second-class self, your theory has quietly stopped describing selves and started recommending a way to live. And if you go implicit on me — the narrative is there, just never told — say what makes it a narrative rather than just a pattern in the behaviour, because that's my whole arguement against you in one line. Last thing: if you reach for Ash's mates' story of Ash instead of Ash's own, you owe me an account of why the self isn't then partly a social object with the friends holding some of the pen. Where exactly does the centre sit in Ash's case?
