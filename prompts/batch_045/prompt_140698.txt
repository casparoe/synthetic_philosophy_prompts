I work in a bike shop and got dragged into this by arguing with my friend Dave (he does CBT-flavoured therapy) about fear of flying: he says the person white-knuckling their way over the Atlantic doesn't really believe they're in danger — they'd tell you flying is statistically safe — so the fear must be something over and above any belief, and I kept saying, okay, but then what even is it? Since then I've read around enough on my own to see there are two big camps, and I want more than a summary of the fight. Camp one says an emotion is, or at the very least constitutively involves, an evaluative judgment: to be afraid of the loose dog is to judge, or take it, that the dog is dangerous, and if you gave up that judgment you'd stop being afraid. Camp two says emotions are perceptions of value — about things, assessable as fitting or unfitting, but not beliefs: they don't get revised just because you recieve better evidence, they stick around after you've concluded they're silly, and they aren't the kind of thing you can arrive at by weighing reasons. Both camps also seem to quietly agree that the really old "it's nothing but a bodily feeling" story can't be right, and yet the gut-drop and the tight chest have to live somewhere in each account, and I'd like the statement to say where.

The part I want you to actually build in, so this isn't a generic answer: I've invented two cases myself that I think are load-bearing, and I want each camp's response written into the document. First, the orange room — suppose someone ends up, through some VR-flooding accident or a bit of neural rewiring, with an immediate fear-response to the colour orange (heart rate up, avoidance urge, the works) while sincerely and stably believing that orange things are harmless, never acting on it, and being slightly embarrassed about it. Is that an emotion, or just an affect or urge with no real content? Camp one looks committed to saying it isn't really an emotion, and I want to know whether that's a cost they'd feel or a result they'd welcome. Second, a made-up empirical finding: imagine a drug that shuts down interoceptive feedback, so you no longer feel your own arousal at all, without touching belief formation or reasoning — and it turns out that people on it report having no emotions whatsoever, even though they still form all the same evaluative judgments and still avoid the same things. I want each camp to say in advance what they'd predict, and how much it would hurt them if it came out the other way. I suspect camp two wants to say "yes, that kills the emotion" and camp one wants to say "no, they're still afraid, they just can't tell that they are" — and I want that prediction pinned down in writing before the result comes in.

Also, my own hunch, which you should feel free to shoot down: I think a decent chunk of this might just be a fight about words. If camp one means by 'judgment' nothing more than a state with evaluative content that can be accurate or inaccurate, then camp two are already judgment theorists and there's no dispute; if they mean something that behaves like a belief under evidence and reasoning, then the sticky emotions — the ones that survive your saying to yourself, out loud, "this is fine" — look like straightforward refutations. So in the joint statement, tell me which parts of their disagreement are merely verbal and why, and what's genuinely left once that's cleared away. Please format it as a short numbered section of claims both camps could sign (including claims about the flying case itself, about whether babies and animals have emotions, and about whether an emotion can exist with no felt bodily component at all), followed by a table of the remaining disagreements with each side's version phrased the way that side would accept for itself, and then a cruxes section where, for each crux, you name one case, argument, or finding both sides agree bears on it and write down what each predicts. And if you end up concluding that basically nothing would move either camp, say that plainly and say whether that's a defect in theories of this kind or just what you'd expect from this kind of question.
