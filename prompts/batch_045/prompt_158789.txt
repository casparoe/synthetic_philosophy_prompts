I am a teaching assistant for an introductory logic course, and our department reading group recently spent a session on Lewis Carroll's short dialogue in which the Tortoise grants Achilles the two premises about the triangle and the conclusion that things equal to the same are equal to each other, grants as well the hypothetical connecting them, then demands that each new hypothetical connecting the growing list to the conclusion be written down as a further premise, and so on without end. I came away convinced that the usual lesson drawn from it is too optimistic. As I read it, Carroll is pressing the old trilemma: any justification of deductive inference is either circular, or pushes the justification back forever, or stops somewhere in a brute, unjustified acceptance of a rule. Carroll seems to show that the second option is forced on us, since every putative stopping point can be re-converted into a premise and then questioned again. My own proposed fix, which I floated in the group and which nobody really engaged with, is what I call the Omnibus Premise: instead of adding conditionals one at a time, state once and for all a single universally quantified premise to the effect that for any propositions P and Q, if I accept P and accept that if P then Q, then Q is true and I should accept it too. My thought was that this collapses the infinite ladder into one rung, because the general premise covers every step the Tortoise might ask for. But when I tried to write it out formally I noticed that to get from the Omnibus Premise plus my acceptance of P and of the conditional down to Q, I still seem to need an application of universal instantiation together with modus ponens, so the rule appears to be doing work that no premise, however general, can do — which makes me think Carroll's regress is genuinely unstoppable and that our acceptance of basic rules of inference is, in the end, an unjustified habit rather than something we have reason for.

This matters to me outside the seminar room because of an argument I had with a friend about a staffing decision at the hospital where we both work. She accepted the hospital's written policy, accepted that the case in front of us fell under it, and then simply declined to draw the conclusion, saying that I could not make her infer anything and that deduction has no foundation anyway. I did not have a good reply, and her stance now looks to me uncomfortably like the Tortoise's, which Carroll seems to present as silly but never actually refutes. So I would like your help with three things, in order of how much they are bothering me. First: is my Omnibus Premise the right way to stop the regress, and if it is not, is the reason that no proposition can ever perform the function of a rule, or is the reason something else that I am missing about what Carroll was actually doing? Second: if no premise can do that work, does it follow that our acceptance of deduction rests on nothing and is therefore arbitrary, or is that inference from cannot be established by argument to is not justified at all itself a mistake, and if so, what would a non-argumentative justification of a rule even look like? Third: what exactly is the Tortoise failing to do, and is my friend doing the same thing — is refusing to infer from premises one accepts a form of irrationality, a form of skepticism, or neither, and does the fact that no one can compel acceptance by piling up premises give her any purchase at all? Please tell me plainly where I have the shape of the problem wrong rather than answering my questions as I have posed them; I would rather leave with a corrected picture than with an answer to a confused question. I do not need readings or references, just the conceptual geography and, where philosophers genuinely disagree about it, a clean statement of what the disagreement is.
