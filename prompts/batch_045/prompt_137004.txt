The question: **(a)** State Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation as it emerges from his discussion of radical translation in his 1960 book *Word and Object*, and explain why, for Quine, the thesis is not simply the observation that a field linguist has too little data to go on. In your answer, distinguish the indeterminacy of translation from what Quine calls the inscrutability of reference, and say how the two are related. **(b)** Someone in the seminar proposes the following way out of the puzzle. Suppose that after years of work the linguist has mastered enough of the natives' grammar to start asking questions, and discovers that the natives have a second expression, *gavagai-tuk*, which they assent to when only a rabbit's hind foot can be seen protruding from a burrow and dissent from when a whole rabbit is in plain view — and which they use in constructions the linguist provisiionally glosses as the-gavagai-tuk-is-part-of-a-gavagai (assented to) and the-gavagai-tuk-is-a-gavagai (dissent). Does this settle that *gavagai* means rabbit rather than undetached rabbit part? Why or why not?

The student's answer: Quine's thesis is that the evidence available to a radical translator — the totality of the natives' dispositions to assent to and dissent from sentences under stimulation — does not determine a unique translation manual. The linguist hears *gavagai* in the presence of rabbits, and the obvious translation is rabbit; but undetached rabbit part and momentary rabbit stage fit the same evidence, since wherever there is a rabbit there is an undetached rabbit part and a rabbit stage. The competing hypotheses differ only in what the speaker privately has in mind, and because the linguist has access to behaviour and not to the native's mind, there is no way to tell which of them is the one the native means. That is why the indeterminacy survives any amount of behavioural evidence: it is not a de facto shortage of data but an in-principle gap between the evidence and the fact to be established, and it is really a form of scepticism about other minds applied to meaning. It is, moreover, closely related to — perhaps just a special case of — the Duhem–Quine thesis that theories are underdetermined by evidence.

Note also that the indeterminacy arises only once we leave the observational periphery. Observation sentences are the exception, because their translation is fixed by stimulus meaning: the linguist can simply check which stimulations prompt assent and dissent, and speakers who are prompted by the same stimulations thereby mean the same thing. So *gavagai*, as a one-word observation sentence, is determinately translatable as rabbit. It is only when the linguist moves on to vocabulary that outruns what is directly observed — part-talk, sortal terms, expressions like rabbithood — that the candidates multiply and nothing decides between them. The inscrutability of reference, then, is a problem about theoretical terms, whereas observation terms are safely anchored to stimulus meanings.

As for part (b), the proposed evidence settles nothing, because no observation could ever favour one manual over another. What Quine calls analytical hypotheses go beyond all possible behavioural evidence, so the evidence never discriminates between candidate manuals: whatever the natives do or say, a manual that renders *gavagai* as undetached rabbit part can always be defended by adjusting the translation of the rest of the language. In the end Quine's claim is not that translation is impossible, but that it is multiply possible, and that there is no fact of the matter as to which of the admissible manuals is the right one.
