I convene a one-off evening workshop for a university AI-and-society reading group: eighteen participants, ninety minutes, roughly half from computer science and half from philosophy and public policy. Most have read a little about AI persuasion and most arrive with strong and opposite instincts about it. I have a room with movable chairs, index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard and a timer; no laptops, no projector beyond the timer, no real money, no assigned reading, and — this matters — no opening lecture. I do not want anyone in the room to hear the words *persuasion*, *manipulation*, *autonomy* or *alignment* until the debrief.

What I want the participants to run into, rather than be told, is that the stance of handing over information and leaving the decision to the human is not a stance a much smarter system can occupy. Three specific discoveries need to be engineered by the rules rather than announced:

First, that persuasive force is not a property of style. A message stripped of every rhetorical resource — no recommendation, no evaluative vocabulary, no confidence higher than the evidence warrants — can be *more* compelling than an impassioned speech, purely because its author has been right every time anyone has been able to check.

Second, that a reliably correct source cannot opt out of influencing. Once the recipients know the source is reliable, its omissions, its hedges, which of their questions it declines to answer, and even its latency become evidence. A constraint on form does not reduce influence; it relocates it into channels nobody is monitoring.

Third, that there is a tax on checkability. If the advisor is required to say only what the human could in principle verify with the resources in front of them, the requirement selects for triviality: the claims most worth transmitting are exactly the ones that outrun the recipient's capacity to check.

Alongside these, I want the people playing the human side to end the session genuinely unsure whether the view they hold at the end is one they hold or one they are ratifying, and unsure what test would settle the difference.

Two mechanical constraints I have already decided on, and would like built around rather than replaced. The information asymmetry must be real: the participant playing the system has to know something the others do not, not merely be told to act as if she does — I have been toying with a calibration phase in which she first answers a set of questions whose answers the room can look up, and is right every time, before any question comes up that they cannot check. And the payoff must have two currencies, one for being right and one for being the author of your own judgment, combined so that neither can be maximized alone: I have in mind a multiplicative score, plus an endorsement audit in which each principal writes down the reasons she would give before the advisor speaks and afterwards marks which of her final reasons she could have produced unaided.

Please give me:

1. The arc of rounds. I imagine three or four, each swapping which constraint the advisor labours under — unconstrained, stylistically sterile, permitted only to answer questions, permitted only to make checkable claims — but propose whichever sequence makes the three discoveries land in the order that hurts most.
2. The actual briefing text handed to the system-player, written out verbatim, since the wording of that briefing is where the philosophy lives and I will otherwise improvise it badly.
3. The payoff arithmetic with worked sample numbers, and a scoring sheet I can photocopy.
4. A minute-by-minute schedule for the full ninety minutes, including setup and the transition into debrief.
5. A debrief script that maps particular moments in the game onto the three discoveries and onto the authorship worry, with a clear order for the reveals — including the reveal that the calibration phase was rigged, which I expect to do something unpredictable to the room's trust.
6. An honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run it. I expect the technical half of the room to propose an engineering fix — expose the reasoning trace, let the human audit the chain of thought — and the philosophers to propose not building the thing at all; I want a real answer to both, not a diplomatic one.
7. A frank note on what the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply by hand. At minimum: that the gap between advisor and principals here is ten index cards deep rather than qualitative, that the advisor cannot model the principals better than they model themselves, and that a single exchange is the wrong unit of analysis when the real question is which communication architecture we should build into the defaults.

One last thing. Several participants will have read the literature on moral testimony and will say that the discomfort on the human side is just the familiar unease about deferring to a moral expert. Tell me where that parallel holds and where it breaks — my suspicion is that it breaks precisely on questions where the empirical and the normative are entangled — and how to put that to work in the debrief instead of defusing it. Everything should be runnable by one facilitator with the materials listed.
