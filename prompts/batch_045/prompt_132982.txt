I work on the monitoring team for a regional cold-chain logistics network — roughly eighteen hundred refrigerated cabinets and cold rooms, each reporting temperature, compressor duty cycle, door-open counts and defrost behaviour at five-minute resolution. We maintain a shared taxonomy of "anomaly mechanisms", and it is not a decorative artefact: the same taxonomy has to serve three jobs at once, namely deciding which alerts get paged out at 3 a.m., deciding which spare parts and which trade a dispatcher sends to a site, and feeding a forecasting model that estimates how much of the fleet will need attention next quarter. Two senior colleagues have been deadlocked since a post-incident review last March, and I am convinced they are largely talking past each other, so I want to force the issue properly rather than keep refereeing. The first, the mechanism realist, holds that two anomalies are the same mechanism if and only if they are produced by the same generating structure — same component, same failure physics, same causal pathway from initiating condition to observable effect — and that this is a fact about the world we are trying to discover, so that a taxonomy which lumps distinct generating structures together is simply mistaken no matter how convenient it is. The second, the interventionist, holds that a mechanism just is an equivalence class carved out by intervention: two anomalies are the same mechanism when the same repair or the same change to operating procedure prevents or clears both, and when they share a predictive profile, so that a distinction which no available or foreseeable intervention can act on is a distinction the taxonomy has no business making.

Three cases in our own corpus seem to do all the work, and I would like the joint statement to be answerable to them rather than to abstractions. The first is what I have started calling a silent twin: a perished door gasket and a failing evaporator fan both present as a slow upward creep in cabinet temperature with a rising duty cycle, are indistinguishable in the telemetry we currently collect, and differ completely in what fixes them. The second is a chameleon: a single underlying cause, a marginal refrigerant undercharge, manifests as short-cycling in a dry ambient and as an iced evaporator coil in a humid one, so that the interventionist's signature clustering splits one mechanism in two. The third is a shared-fix degeneracy: a great many unrelated anomalies all clear after a controller reset, which the interventionist criterion seems to collapse into a single mechanism, and which I take to be the strongest prima facie objection the realist has. I have also floated two proposals of my own that neither side has properly answered, and I would like them adjudicated: first, a carry-over test, on which a mechanism-kind earns its place in the taxonomy only if a fix validated on one instance transfers to a matched instance without fresh diagnosis — which would let the taxonomy be checked against something other than intuition; and second, the observation that any mechanism can be subdivided indefinitely (gasket failure by corrosion versus gasket failure by impact debris, and so on down), so that "same mechanism" may be a three-place relation, same relative to a granularity and to an intervention repertoire, rather than a two-place one. A related question I care about: whether mechanism sameness is required to be transitive, and whether a discovered intransitivity in the anomaly corpus — a chain of pairwise-similar cases that refuses to close into clusters — should count as a discovery about the fleet or as a reductio of one of the two accounts.

What I want from you is the document the two of them should have written after that review: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the mechanism realist and the interventionist about what counts as the same mechanism in anomaly detection. Please begin with a numbered section of claims both could sign — about what a mechanism taxonomy is for, about the three cases above, about what the telemetry can and cannot settle — and follow it with a table of the residual disagreements, in which each row states the point at issue in neutral terms and then gives each side's own preferred formulation of its position, worded so that that side would accept it as fair. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of them. For each crux, specify the test, argument or case both would accept as bearing on it — including, where you can, whether the carry-over test or a transitivity audit of the corpus would in fact discriminate between them — and record in advance what each side predicts the result will be, and what each would concede if it came out the other way. I also want you to say plainly which parts of their dispute are verbal — disagreements about the word "mechanism" or about what it would be convenient to mean — and which are substantive, and to say what the substantive ones are really about. Please write in British English, keep the discussion tied to the cold-chain setting and to the three cases rather than drifting into general philosophy of causation, and don't give me citations, references or suggested reading: I want the reasoning from the case itself, and I'll be showing this to both of them, so formulations that only one of them could sign are exactly what I'm trying to avoid.
