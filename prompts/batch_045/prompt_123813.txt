I run a small type foundry — me, two other designers, and a junior who's talented but has never read a word of theory — and we keep having the same fight in design reviews: is that glyph still an 'a'?

It comes up maybe two or three times a week, and it's not academic for us: it decides whether a commissioned family gets approved, whether a variable font ships with a broken heavy end, whether we can legally call something a text face. We can't just say "does it look like an a," because we've all worked out that's circular — a single-storey Futura 'a', a double-storey Garamond 'a' and a blackletter 'a' share almost nothing as shapes and are all obviously a's, and meanwhile the exact same shape can be a Latin 'a' or a Cyrillic 'а' depending on which system it's doing a job in. (I keep thinking about that "Letter Spirit" chapter in Hofstadter's Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies, the one about the essence of a letterform as something separable from its style, and honestly half the reason I'm writing is that I can't tell whether that's a thing we could actually use or just a nice metaphor.)

What I want is a procedure, not an essay. Something the junior can run in about ten minutes with printouts and no theory background, in this order: the questions to ask, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to me because it's a question of taste or client negotiation rather than fact.

Things it has to handle properly:

- Three different ways a glyph can fail, kept apart: nobody can read it as an 'a' at all; it reads as an 'a' but collides with another letter in the same style (o, d, q, g — my hunch is that a letter's identity is partly relational, defined against the rest of the alphabet, so a glyph can be perfectly fine in isolation and still not be this alphabet's 'a'); and it reads fine but doesn't belong to the family, i.e. it looks like it was drawn by somebody else in a different style.
- A deformation test: can we get from some canonical 'a' to this glyph by a continuous series of small steps where every intermediate still reads as an 'a'? Tell me which exemplar to start from, what breaks the path, and what to do when there's no path in one style but obviously is in another.
- The homoglyph case, where shape and role come apart — one drawn form serving as two letters in two systems. What does the procedure say, and is there any case where it has to answer "both" or "neither"?
- A fallback for when we can't afford real reader testing with actual users, which is most of the time, since we're four people and the budget is a joke.
- Be upfront about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I'd rather know than be comforted.

Then run it on three of ours: (1) the heavy end of a variable text face where the counter of the 'a' has basically closed up at small sizes but looks deliberate and fine on a poster; (2) a client's bilingual brand asking for one shared glyph to be both Latin 'a' and Cyrillic 'а'; (3) our founder's "signature a," detached bowl with a swirl off the terminal, which is on all our marketing and which I have a bad feeling about. Give me the answer the procedure actually gives on that last one even if I won't like it.

Finish with a one-page version I can paste into the studio handbook, and be clear about what the procedure can't tell us — whether the letter is any good, whether the family as a whole hangs together, and what to do when we're drawing for a script with no established reading comunity to appeal to.
