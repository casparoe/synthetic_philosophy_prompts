Hey — I'm on the program committee for a small academic society (seven of us, we meet four times a year, and we somehow always end up arguing about procedure). We mostly decide two kinds of things: which submissions to accept given a rough capacity limit, and a big pile of low-stakes housekeeping motions where the status quo wins if nothing passes. Right now we use plain plurality with the chair breaking ties, everyone hates it, so we've agreed to pick a new rule — and this is the part I keep going back and forth on — we've also agreed to re-open the choice of rule every two years, using whatever rule is currently in force to decide whether to change it. I've been reading about Arrow's theorem and Gibbard–Satterthwaite and I think I get the headline: no ranked-ballot rule gives you unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship all at once, and any non-dictatorial rule with three or more possible outcomes is manipulable somewhere. I also get the standard move that cardinal methods like approval and score aren't really in the theorem's crosshairs because they ask voters for more than a ranking — though a friend of mine who does mechanism design says that just relocates the problem, since you then need interpersonal comparisons of intensity, and Harsanyi-style aggregation only works if you can actually put people's utilities on a common scale, which we definitely can't for a room of seven tired professors.

What I actually want from you is a ranked tier list. Please sort these — plurality, instant-runoff, Borda, a Condorcet/Schulze-type method, approval voting, score voting (0–5), random dictatorship (one randomly drawn member's ballot decides each motion), and a sociocracy-ish consent rule where a motion passes unless someone formally objects — into tiers by how good they'd be for a committee like ours, and give each a 0–100 score plus a sentence on why it lands there. Judge them on the things that actually bite for us: how often they'd pick something most of us really prefer, how badly they degrade once people start voting strategically instead of sincerely, how easy they are to explain and hand-count at 6pm, how exposed they are to whoever sets the agenda or the order of motions, and how they handle the fact that most of our votes are near-unanimous ratifications of a status quo rather than real contests.

Then the bit I can't get straight, and which matters more to me than the list itself: the meta-level. Our ranking of the rules is itself a collective choice among more than two options, made by voters whose preferences over the rules are vague, unstable and partly strategic — so by Arrow's own logic, shouldn't we expect that ranking to be exactly as impossible to do well as any other? Does the impossibility eat itself, or is choosing a rule relevantly different from choosing an outcome, e.g. because we choose it rarely, behind a partial veil of ignorance about which proposals we'll face later, or because it's the kind of choice where we'd happily accept randomness in a way we wouldn't for an actual decision? And then concretely: if we re-vote on the rule every two years under the rule itself, which of your top-tier rules has a stable fixed point, i.e. would it ever vote to replace itself with something lower on your list? Is a rule's tendency to preserve itself a point in its favour or an argument against it?
