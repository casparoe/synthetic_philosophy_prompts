I teach a first-year seminar on moral education, and this term I am pairing a short selection from Plutarch's Parallel Lives — where he remarks at the head of his Life of Alexander that he is writing lives rather than histories, and expects the reader to be moved toward emulation or away from it — with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I would like a reading of the novel as a case study in moral learning from a life.

Stevens is an unusually pure instance of the Plutarchan pupil: he has taken his father, and then Lord Darlington, as his standard of what a great butler and so a good man is, and he measures himself against a person rather than against any principle he could state — while his own narrative is a life he is writing, as much encomium as confession. Two questions. First, is the novel a tragedy of a badly chosen exemplar, loyalty and bad luck of a kind that any method of moral education would be vulnerable to, or does it dramatize something stronger, which I have been calling the bootstrapping problem: that emulation cannot audit itself, because the judgment required to assess an exemplar is exactly what submission to an exemplar is supposed to produce? Tell me whether the text really bears out the strong version. Second, does the closing movement — his half-concession on the pier that his service may have been wasted, and his resolve to make something of the evening that is left — amount to reorientation toward a better exemplar in Plutarch's sense, or is it a change for which the Plutarchan frame has no vocabulary?

I will need scenes to point the class to: I assume the tiger in the dining room, the dismissal of the two housemaids, his father dying upstairs while he goes on serving, and his practising of banter for his new employer do the work. Say where the novel resists the reading as well as where it illustrates it, and say what, if anything, the novel knows about learning from lives that Plutarch's own practice does not.
