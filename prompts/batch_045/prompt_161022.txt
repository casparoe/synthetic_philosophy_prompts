I've more or less talked myself into a deflationary view of the dollar auction, and I want you to try to break it.

Setup, in case your version of the game differs: prize is a dollar, open outcry, minimum increment c, and at the end the highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar while the second-highest bidder pays theirs and gets nothing.

The view: the famous escalation needs no special psychological explanation, and the game isn't really a sunk-cost demonstration at all — because here the "sunk" bid isn't sunk. If I've bid b and I'm currently second, dropping costs me b for real, so my past bid legitimately enters my forward-looking calculus. Concretely: if he's at b + c and I can top him at b + 2c, then escalating beats dropping iff 1 − (b + 2c) > −b, i.e. iff 2c < 1. So escalation at any b whatsoever, driven entirely by the increment being small relative to the prize, with no appeal to irrationality and no role for how much I've already lost. (All of this is one-step: it assumes he folds after my raise. I know that's doing work.) Call it the structural account.

Two predictions I take myself to be committed to, which is where I want the attack:

1. Scaling. Add a withdrawal option: a bidder who withdraws pays back f times their standing bid and is out. f = 1 is the plain dollar auction, f = 0 is an ordinary auction where losers pay nothing. Then escalating beats withdrawing iff 1 − (b + 2c) > −fb, so the escalation ceiling should be roughly (1 − 2c)/(1 − f): unbounded at f = 1, about a dollar at f = 0. It should track the prize-to-increment ratio only — doubling the prize and every dollar figure together should change nothing.
2. Path-independence. Two subjects in the identical current position (say they're at $2.40, opponent at $2.60, c = 20 cents) should behave the same whether they crawled there through a dozen small raises, got dropped into that position by the experimenter, or had every prior bid annuled and their stake refunded immediately before the decision.

What I want: what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically — (a) which of the two predictions is load-bearing, i.e. which one failing would definately sink the account rather than force a patch; (b) what a defender should concede in advance as a real defeater, as opposed to what they're entitled to dismiss as a confound or as not bearing on the claim, because I can already see the escape hatch where any observed behaviour gets rationalized by some belief about whether the opponent will fold — has the account been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a problem for a claim of this kind; (c) whether any of this actually separates the structural story from a psychologial one about commitment, arousal, or having invested too much to quit now, or whether the two are notational variants in this particular game.

For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is evidence that escalation tracks the history of the bidding rather than the current payoff geometry. And I care because of the real-world analogues — takeover bidding wars, Concorde-style "we've spent too much to stop" in project funding. If the honest answer is that the structural story is fine for the lab game and useless for those, say so and explain why.
