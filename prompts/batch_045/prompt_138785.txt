The topic. By indirect normativity I mean the family of views on which what an agent has most reason to do is not settled directly by her actual reasons, but by some idealized surrogate: what a fully informed, fully coherent, unbiased counterpart of her would deliberate her way to, or would advise her to do. My half-formed idea is that these views are under-specified in a way the literature has not taken seriously, because the surrogates are generated by applying several distinct idealizing operations at once, and there is no reason to assume those operations commute. If they do not, then what the idealized agent says depends on the order in which you idealized, and the theory has no determinate verdict until an order is specified. I want this turned into a proposal.

A second half-formed idea I want you to use: distinguish two roles the idealized verdict can play. It can be modeled as advice issued to the actual agent by someone who knows more than she does, in which case opaque deference seems permissible — she may rationally do what she is told without being able to see why. Or it can be modeled as her own deliberation carried through to completion, in which case transparency seems required, and a verdict she cannot in principle follow the reasoning to is not available to her. Call this the advisory/continuation ambiguity. My sense is that the two readings pull in opposite directions on how much information the surrogate may have, and that this interacts with the ordering problem rather than sitting alongside it.

What I need the proposal to contain:

3. The method, sequenced by term or quarter across two years rather than month by month, with three or so work packages and a deliverable for each. 4. The possible conclusions, named and distinguished. My guesses are: a collapse verdict, on which indirect normativity is revealed as a family of theories with no determinate output and so fails as a reductive account; a principled-ordering verdict, on which some sequencing of the operations is itself normatively privileged, which would mean the idealization procedure carries normative weight the view was not supposed to need and the theory is less indirect than advertised; and a robust-core verdict, on which only the order-invariant verdicts bind, preserving a central domain of application at the cost of leaving many ordinary cases indeterminate. For each, say what would decide for or against it, and give your own assessment of which is most likely.
