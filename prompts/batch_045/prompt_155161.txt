I'm the copy editor for a small interdisciplinary journal — I volunteered, nobody else wanted the job — and I've just been handed a special issue on autonomous systems where the word "man" keeps coming up. We have a house style guide with a non-sexist-language section and a stack of minutes from the committee that maintains it, and I'd rather apply our own precedents than my politics. The trouble is that once I laid the minutes out side by side, I realised they fit more than one rule equally well, and the case actually in front of me seems to sit right where those rules come apart. So: predict what this committee would say, or rather tell me what it should say if it wants to be consistent with itself.

The precedents, as recorded in the minutes: "chairman" to "chair", replace; "manpower" to "workforce", replace, with a note saying explicitly not "personpower"; "man-hours" to "person-hours", replace, and here a literal "person-" compound is fine; "middleman" to "intermediary", replace; generic "he" for an indefinite researcher to singular "they", replace; "mankind" to "humankind", replace; "guys" when addressing a mixed audience to "everyone", replace (so the rule clearly isn't about the letters m-a-n occurring); "manhole", keep, minute says fixed engineering and legal term, no male is pictured; "human", "humane", "manic", "manifest", keep, minute says "man" isn't a morpheme there; "woman", keep, with "woperson" and "womyn" rejected outright; "man cave", keep, minute says male-specific on purpose and none of our business; "man the phones" to "staff the phones", replace.

The new case: the survey manuscript keeps saying "man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack", and I have to rule on it. Three things make it unlike the earlier ones. First, the acronym is what people actually cite and search, and MITM survives unchanged whether the expanded form is "man-", "machine-", or "adversary-in-the-middle", so the usual stability argument for leaving technical terms alone costs almost nothing here — unless you think the expanded form in running prose is what does the damage. Second, there are ready-made neutral alternatives that are arguably more accurate, since the thing in the middle is a hostile machine and not a guy in a hoodie, and the authors tell me some people in their field already write "machine-in-the-middle" or "adversary-in-the-middle". Third — and this is what I got stuck on when I was arguing with my housemate, who does feminist linguistics and said something about markedness that I only half followed — there is no such thing as a "woman-in-the-middle", which on the markedness story suggests "man" is doing generic-human work here, but that is equally true of "manhole", which we left alone.

What I want from you: the two or three candidate rules that fit that whole track record — I can think of a "would a reader picture a man" imagery rule, a "don't touch established terms of art" rule, and something like the old Hofstadter-style parody procedure where you mechanically swap in "person" and ask whether the absurdity you get is the revealing kind or just silliness — what each of them predicts about "man-in-the-middle", and your best guess with the reason for preferring it.
