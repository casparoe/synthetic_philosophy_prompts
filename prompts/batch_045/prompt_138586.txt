hey — longtime lurker, been reading this corner of the internet since forever (LW, the old blog comment sections, the whole "is h² bullshit" arc that comes around every eighteen months or so). I want you to do something more useful then another round of that argument.

Set up an adversarial collaboration between two people, and write the document they'd produce at the end of it.

Side A, call them the Quantitative Geneticist (QG): holds that narrow-sense heritability is a legitimate, well-defined population parameter (additive genetic variance over phenotypic variance in a specified population in a specified range of environments), that the twin/adoption/family/GWAS literature gives broadly trustworthy estimates for things like height, IQ, educational attainment, BMI, personality, and that h² carries real information: how much resemblance to expect among relatives, what any environmental theory has to explain, and how hard the trait will be to move with the environmental levers we actually have. QG thinks "heritable doesn't mean unchangeable" is true but grossly overused and mostly functions as a conversation-stopper.

Side B, call them the Developmental Critic (DC): holds that h² is a bookkeeping ratio that is systematically misread as being about individuals ("60% of your IQ is genetic"), about immutability, and about the causes of differences between groups, and that once you notice how environment-relative and gene-distribution-relative the number is, "highly heritable" tells someone deciding whether to intervene almost nothing. DC thinks the "it's just a well-defined population parameter" defence keeps the prestige of the number while disowning every use anyone actually makes of it.

Give me the claims both can sign, the exact disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own view, and the cruxes with predictions written down in advance. Please make the agreed claims non-vacuous — if a claim amounts to "correlation isn't causation" or "definitions matter," cut it. And where a dispute is really about words, say so explicitly, and separately from the real ones.

Three cases I definately want built in, because these are where I can't tell whether the two sides are disagreeing or just talking past each other:

1. The Uniform Boarding School. A state raises every child from birth in identical state boarding facilities — same diet, same schedule, same curriculum, same everything — with infants randomly assigned to facilities and staff randomly assigned to infants. Everyone stipulates that this is logistically absurd and ethically horrifying; it's a thought experiment. My understanding is that in the resulting population h² of something like height or IQ goes *up*, because you've stripped out environmental variance, while the mean moves wherever the now-uniform environment pushes it and pre-existing between-group gaps from the original population mostly disappear. Both sides should be able to sign that description. What I want is whether that result is a reductio of h² as a quantity anyone should care about, or exactly what the quantity was supposed to mean all along, plus what each side says the number is *for*.

2. Trait K. Fictional, but modelled on real deficiency conditions. K is a nasty developmental condition, prevalence ~12% in population P, caused by a loss-of-function allele at a single locus; nearly everyone homozygous for the variant gets it. There's also a micronutrient: supplement the diet with it and K essentially never develops. Nobody in P supplements. Measured h² of K in P is about 0.9. Then a public-health program puts the micronutrient in the salt; prevalence falls to ~0.3%, and the remaining cases are people who don't use the salt. In both voices, the document has to say: what did that 0.9 mean? Is "K is highly heritable" a misleading sentence to put in front of a policymaker? In front of a parent of a kid with K? And does QG have a way of saying "yes, 0.9, and here's what it bought us" that isn't just retroactive humility?

3. Whose environment is it? The genetic-nurture / passive gene–environment correlation problem: parents transmit alleles *and* environments, so some of what ordinary designs count as genetic is really the effect of what the parents did, and different designs carve the variance up differently. I want both sides to say what each design actually estimates and how much the gap matters; if they can agree on a decomposition, write the decomposition down in the agreed-claims section.

Also somewhere in there: within-group heritability versus between-group differences. Both sides officially agree that within-group h² doesn't license a genetic explanation of a between-group gap. I want the strongest version of that point that both can sign in the agreed claims, and I want each side to state what, if anything, *would* license such an inference.

Format, please, in this order:

1. "What we both accept" — 8–12 numbered claims, one to three sentences each, no hedging and no weasel words.
2. "Where we still disagree" — a table, 4–6 rows, with columns for the crux, QG's formulation (in QG's own preferred vocabulary), DC's formulation (same), and one line on why these aren't just the same claim in different words — or a note if they are.
3. "Cruxes and predictions" — 3–4 cruxes, each with the concrete hypothetical study, dataset, or natural experiment both accept as bearing on it; what QG predicts it shows; what DC predicts; and what each would concede if the other's prediction came out right. About 120–180 words per crux.
4. "Verbal disputes" — about 150–250 words on which parts of the public argument are pure terminology, and which words each side would have to give up.

Casual register is fine, but keep the numbers and the distinctions exact. Don't give me citations or further reading — I want the argument, not a bibliography.
