I'm in an undergraduate seminar on ethics and public policy, and we have just spent two weeks on social discounting. I want to get clearer on what our actual institutional practice commits us to. I don't need citations or a reading list, and I'd rather you worked from the practice as I describe it than from any canonical statement of it.

Here is the pattern I can't turn into a single theory.

(a) When a government cost-benefit analysis compares a regulation that saves lives soon with one that saves lives a century from now, it discounts the later lives at a positive annual rate, so a hundred statistical lives saved in the distant future come out worth a small fraction of a hundred saved next year.

(b) The same government, licensing a repository for long-lived radioactive waste, evaluates the risk over a horizon of many thousands of years and in some frameworks declines to discount the harm at all, or applies something close to a zero rate.

(c) The same government borrows at long maturity and funds basic research and disease-surveillance infrastructure, justifying the spending by benefits that arrive decades out, which implies a far lower implicit rate than the one in (a).

(d) Courts in the same jurisdiction reduce an award for lost future earnings to present value, but do not discount the pain, the loss of a faculty, or the wrongfulness of the injury itself: those are made good in full no matter how far into the future they fall.

(e) And in the personal case we treat it as a failing, not a preference, that I care less about my own toothache next year than about an identical one today.

So: what view of the moral significance of time does this set of practices presuppose? More specifically, does (a) strictly presuppose pure time preference, or could the whole of the observed discounting be reconstructed as two non-temporal factors that happen to shrink with distance: a hazard factor, the chance that the benefit never arrives because the beneficiaries, the institution or the state does not survive, and a satiety factor, the thought that future people will be richer, so a unit of resource buys less welfare? If that reconstruction is available, is the practice underdetermined, licensed equally by a date-sensitive and a date-neutral theory? And where do the zero-rate and declining-rate schedules in (b) fit: do they show the practice was never committed to pure time preference, or do they substitute a different view, on which uncertainty about the rate, or extinction risk, is the only legitimate discounter?

One proposal my seminar kicked around, which we called the Two-Key Rule, is that a public evaluation may apply a hazard factor and a satiety factor and no other temporal factor, and must state the two separately. I have three questions about it. Would the practice as it actually stands look any different under the Two-Key Rule, and if so in which of the cases above? Is the current practice really Two-Key in disguise, which would mean it presupposes nothing about pure time preference at all? And what would the defender of an explicit pure rate say is the strongest reason not to accept the reconstruction?

Finally, one of us suggested that the discounting in (a) is not about dates at all but about identity: if who exists in a century depends on what we do now, there may be no particular person whose life is being weighed less, so the worry about pure time preference never gets a grip. Does that dissolve the puzzle or just relocate it, and does it make (a) easier or harder to reconcile with (b) through (e)? Please also tell me where in this I have described the practice wrongly, or where an inconsistency I am reading into it is only apparent.
