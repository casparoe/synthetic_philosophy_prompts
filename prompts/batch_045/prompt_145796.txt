I'm working on a magazine piece on organ donation, and the more transplant people I talk to the more the definition of death starts to look like something the system designed rather than found. I need help working out what the practice actually commits anyone to. No citations or reading lists please, I'll do that legwork myself — I want the shape of the argument.

Here's the package as I understand it.

1. Brain death. Someone on a ventilator after a catastrophic brain injury: warm, pink, heart beating, wounds healing, blood pressure maintained, in rare cases a pregnancy carried for months. The whole brain including the brainstem is destroyed. They are declared dead, and the organs are removed while the heart is still going.

2. Circulatory death. The family withdraws support in the ICU, the heart stops, the team waits a few minutes — somewhere between two and five depending on the hospital — declares death, and rushes the body to theatre. Nobody at that point believes the brain is destroyed. At minute one the brain is arguably still salvageable if you got the heart going again; at minute five it isn't.

3. The dead donor rule: the retrieval is not allowed to be what kills the patient.

And then the detail I can't get out of my head. A coordinator described to me a variant of (2) in which, the instant death is declared, they put the body on a pump and restart circulation through the belly and chest to keep the organs in good condition — but they deliberately tie off or clamp the arteries to the head so that no blood reaches the brain. I have no idea how common this is, and it doesn't matter much for my purposes: take it as that hospital's written protocol whether or not it is real.

So: what theory of death does this package presuppose? Is it one theory or two? Loss of the brain, loss of circulation, irreversible loss of the organism as an integrated whole, or irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness — and where do those come apart across the three cases above? Specifically:

- Is the two-to-five-minute wait a test for irreversibility, or a convention that constitues it? If it's a test, what makes five minutes the right number, and what are we supposed to say about the body at minute two, which on that picture isn't dead yet — which makes the wait look like the thing that causes death rather than the thing that confirms it, and starts to make the dead donor rule wobble.

- What does the head-clamping give away? It seems to only make sense if restoring blood flow to the brain would bring the person back, which is a commitment the circulatory route doesn't otherwise appear to have. Or can the clamp be justified without that?

- Which parts of this are claims about what death really is, and which are safeguards chosen to keep public confidence? I keep being told the dead donor rule functions as a kind of noble lie, and I want to know whether dropping it would change anything at the bedside or only change what we say out loud.

- Where is the practice underdetermined — how many different accounts of death would license exactly this protocol? If the honest answer is "most of them," I don't have the story I thought I had, and I'd rather know now.

Last thing: when a hospital tells a family that their warm, seemingly sleeping relative is definitively dead and there is nothing to discuss, and the family says no and goes to court to keep the ventilator running — what is the hospital committed to in that moment, and is it the same thing it is committed to when it waits five minutes by the bedside with a stopwatch?
