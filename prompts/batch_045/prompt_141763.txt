Me and my flatmate ended up having a proper three-hour argument last night. It started with some guy in the pub saying the welfare state should be a trampoline, not a hammock, and then got much more specific once my flatmate produced his own version of the point: he thinks a benefit claim is basically an insurance claim. You pay your National Insurance while you're in work, the covered risk (redundancy, illness, having to stop work to care for someone) either lands on you or it doesn't, and if it does you draw on the pool. He got three conclusions out of it: conditionality is fair because every insurer requires you to mitigate your loss and won't pay out while you sit there watching the damage grow; benefit fraud matters more than soft-hearted people want to admit, because claims fraud pushes up everyone's premiums; and the deserving/undeserving distinction is really just the distinction between losses that were the covered event and losses you brought on yourself, the way an insurer won't pay if you set fire to your own kitchen.

I half believe him and half think the analogy is doing all the work and would collapse if you actually pressed it, so I want it audited properly. Give me the correspondence table first: premium, covered peril, the claim, the excess (he maps the unpaid waiting days at the start of a claim onto a policy excess), duty to mitigate, moral hazard, claims fraud, non-disclosure, and anything else in the source domain that has an obvious target — and say plainly what each becomes on the welfare side, including the ones where the welfare side has nothing to recieve it. Then break it. The seams I keep catching on: National Insurance isn't priced to your risk or to your likely payout, and it isn't really a pot you've paid into, so the premium half of the mapping is thinner than it sounds; a big chunk of benefit spending is means-tested and goes to people who've barely contributed, so the pool isn't a pool of contributors; insurance is a voluntary contract you can be refused cover under or not have renewed, and the state can do neither; insurers have no interest in your character or your future conduct beyond the risk itself, whereas conditionality, work-related activity requirements and the claimant commitment are all about shaping behaviour; and the state is also partly a cause of the risks it insures, through the labour market it makes. Which of those matter for the three conclusions he drew, and which are just noise?

Then the bit I actually care about. Test the mapping on three people rather than in the abstract: a 58-year-old made redundant after thirty years who turns down a night-shift warehouse job because of his back; a 22-year-old who turns down a call-centre job because he's keeping himself free for DJing; and a single mum who misses a Jobcentre appointment because her kid was ill. Which of the three conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and which one was only ever being carried by the analogy? And he had one repair I can't get out of my head — that a sanction should never be allowed to touch the housing element of a claim, because the kids aren't the claimant — and I can't tell whether that's a tidy fix to the insurance picture or whether it quietly concedes the picture is wrong. If the whole thing is a dud, tell me what the better analogy is. I floated something like a fire brigade funded out of general taxation, where nobody asks whether your house deserved to burn down, but he said that just begs the question about conditionality. Oh, and write the whole thing in British English please — I'm taking a social policy module this term, we're on conditionality, and my spelling keeps getting flagged by my supervisor.
