I teach a first-year gen-ed course on science and society, and I want to use Arrival in the week on Kuhn. I'm bored of the usual Sapir-Whorf reading — everyone walks out saying language determines thought and nothing else happens — so I want you to read it instead as a story about incommensurability and about whether translation between paradigms is possible at all.

Here's the bit I keep chewing on. I've been playing with splitting incommensurability into three seperate claims: (1) the other side's terms can't be translated into ours without residue, (2) there's no common measure for weighing the two sides' evidence and standards, (3) the two sides literally inhabit different worlds of objects. My hunch is the film is a case where one or two of these fail and the third is kind of true, but I can't get clear on which is which, and I suspect working that out tells us something about whether Kuhn is entitled to any of the three in the first place.

Also: Louise's conversion doesn't look much like a Kuhnian one. No crisis, no professional community, no resistance from a discipline — and importantly no epistemic cost, since she never has to give up an earlier way of seeing anything. It's closer to learning an instrument than to a Gestalt switch. Is that just the film cheating, or does it show something Kuhn's picture leaves out?

I'd like a reading I can hand to students: honest about where the film fudges things (the time-perception premise arguably dissolves the agency that makes conversion interesting in the first place), clear about where the Kuhnian frame illuminates a scene and where the film resists it, and finishing by saying whether the film adds anything to the argument that the papers don't.
