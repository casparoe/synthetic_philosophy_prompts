After forty years as an experimental spectroscopist in a plasma physics laboratory, I have taken up philosophy of science in retirement, and one topic continues to resist my attempts to get clear about it: the problem of old evidence. The presentations I encounter are too schematic to let me test the competing remedies against the untidy way in which a datum actually gets woven into a theory, so I should like the argument conducted over a case of the kind I spent my career on.

The case is invented, but please treat its details as fixed data. Since 1978 the standard tables have listed a narrow far-ultraviolet emission feature in the solar corona, catalogued as the Naylor–Okada feature at 118.42 ± 0.05 nm; several proposed identifications were advanced and each failed, and by the 1990s most of us regarded the line as real but unaccounted for, with a suspicion that it might be an unresolved blend of two weaker transitions. In 1997 an orbiting spectrometer re-measured it at 118.394 ± 0.004 nm and showed it to be a single line rather than a blend; the new value is about six of its own standard errors away from the old one, so strictly speaking the old value was displaced rather than refined.

In 2011 the Vaska–Lindgren cascade model of dielectronic recombination in highly ionised iron predicted a single narrow line in that region. Two cascade rate coefficients were tuned so that the line came out at the 1978 wavelength and with the observed intensity, so the datum was used in the construction of the model. The published derivation of the wavelength contained an algebraic slip in the assignment of one recombination-cascade energy; this was corrected in a 2013 erratum, after which the model yields 118.395 nm. The corrected model thus agrees with the 1997 measurement to within about a milli-angstrom-equivalent, and it agrees with the very number it was tuned to only to within 0.026 nm — better, that is, with a measurement that was never used in fitting it than with the one that was. A rival, the Adeyemi–Brant model, also accommodates the feature, by postulating a blend of two weak transitions together with an auxiliary assumption that the coronal iron abundance exceeds the photospheric value by a factor of 2.3; it can be adjusted to either measured wavelength, but it does not account for the narrowness of the line.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of science, both fully competent, neither a foil for the other, in which they work through whether and how the Naylor–Okada feature confirmed the Vaska–Lindgren model. Let one of them, a Bayesian confirmation theorist, hold that confirmation just is probability-raising, and that the old-evidence case must be handled by locating some proposition the agent did not already believe — paradigmatically the proposition that the model implies the datum — and conditionalising on that. Let the other, an explanationist, hold that what makes a datum evidence for a theory is that the theory would explain it, that explanatory relations are not reducible to relations among conditional probabilities, and that the difficulty with old evidence is therefore an artefact of the Bayesian formalism rather than a fact about scientific reasoning. Set the exchange wherever you like — a seminar room after a talk, a correspondence, a walk between sessions — and give each speaker a name, a discipline, and a temperament, but let the argument carry the scene.

I would ask you to observe the following.

First, the two of them should agree completely about the physics and disagree only about the epistemology; neither may win a point by re-describing the case.

Second, each must state her or his account precisely enough to be applied, and must then engage the other's strongest objection rather than a convenient nearby one. In particular, the Bayesian must explain how uncertainty about whether a model implies a datum is rationally possible for a theorist who has the derivation in front of her, must say what the probabilities are attached to, and must give an explicit toy numerical assignment with the arithmetic shown rather than asserting that the probability rises; she must also face the worry that on her account the confirmation a theorist gets seems to depend on how slowly she does her algebra, since the theorist who sees the entailment immediately has nothing left to learn. The explanationist must say how one is to judge that the cascade model explains the line better than the rival does, without covertly reinstating likelihoods or a Bayesian apparatus he has just rejected, and must answer the charge that explanatory power is evidence only because, and when, it tracks something probabilistic.

Third, they must reckon separately with each of the awkward features of the case: that the datum had been on the books for over thirty years before the model existed; that the model's parameters were tuned to the 1978 value; that the 1997 re-measurement displaced that value and settled the blend question, and that the corrected model tracks the unused measurement better than the used one; that the derivation was faulty for two years, so that even the modelers were unsure what their model entailed; and that a rival accommodates the same feature at the cost of an auxiliary assumption. The exchange should take a clear position on whether it matters that the datum was used in constructing the theory, and on whether the 1997 re-measurement restores confirmatory value that the 1978 entry lacked, given that it is nominally a measurement of the same quantity.

Fourth, each speaker must make at least one concession she or he has not prepared in advance and revise something said earlier in consequence. The dialogue should not end in agreement, and it should not end with one side routed; it should end with the residual disagreement stated sharply enough that I can see what I would have to settle in order to settle it.

Finally, nothing is to be settled by appeal to the literature: no citations, no recommendations for further reading, no asides about who has defended which view. I want the reasoning laid out from the case itself, because that is the only part I am competent to check. Aim for somewhere between twelve hundred and sixteen hundred words, in a polished register that still sounds like people talking — no stage directions, no theatrical interjections, no dialect humour, and no summarising coda after the final speech.
