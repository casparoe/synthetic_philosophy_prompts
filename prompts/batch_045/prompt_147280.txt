I am in the third year of a DPhil in philosophy and I am writing a thesis chapter on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I have come to think that the debate around it is stuck in a way that neither side seems willing to name. The set-up is familiar enough: a demon offers you a large sum if you admire him, or threatens you unless you believe something; the consideration he supplies is a reason to want to have the attitude but not, intuitively, a reason for it; and a fitting-attitude or buck-passing account of value has to exclude such considerations somehow or it collapses into triviality. The standard way of drawing the exclusion is the distinction between object-given and state-given reasons, and my sense is that everyone now agrees that the distinction tracks the intuition while nobody has explained why it should, or what work it does beyond restating it.

My hypothesis is that the stalemate is three disputes run together under one name, and that only one of them is doing any real work: an extensional dispute about which considerations get excluded, which may be purely terminological; a substantive dispute about whether there is one normative relation of being a reason for that runs across belief, desire and the emotions, or whether reasons for belief are evidential in a way that reasons for admiration are not; and a structural dispute about whether the exclusion can be stated without helping itself to the notion of value it is meant to explain, on pain of circularity. I also want one non-standard case used as a probe throughout, because I think it discriminates between accounts in a way the usual cases do not: suppose the demon offers you a large sum if you come to admire him *in virtue of* a feature he genuinely has, say his generosity, so that the attitude you would end up with is one held for the right reasons. Call this the laundering case. The bounty looks straightforwardly state-given and so wrong-kind by the standard test, and yet what it gives you a reason to acquire is an attitude that is admirably based.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey: tell me whether my three-way diagnosis is right, which strand is load-bearing, and what would actually move things. Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, in British English, with no introduction and no conclusion, as follows.

1. Your verdict on what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive, or different questions under a single heading, and which of my three strands is load-bearing.
2. The strongest thing an opponent of that verdict would say, stated in its most charitable form.
3. Your handling of the laundering case, and what it shows about whether the object-given/state-given contrast explains the intuition or merely relabels it.
4. One concrete thing that would count as progress: a distinction, a formal constraint, a reclassification of cases, or a change of question.
5. Whether, on your own diagnosis, progress should be expected at all, and what that implies for how I should frame the chapter.
