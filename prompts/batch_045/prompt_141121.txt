I am assembling a problem set for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on self-knowledge, and I need a counterexample built to order rather than one recalled from the literature. Please construct one, in American English, and then demonstrate that it does what I ask of it.

The two theses in question:

- **Luminosity** (for a condition C): necessarily, whenever a subject is in C, she is in a position to know that she is in C.
- **The KK principle**: necessarily, if a subject knows p, then she is in a position to know that she knows p. Read the consequent in this "in a position to know" way, so that the principle cannot be defeated merely by a subject who omits to form the second-order belief or who happens to disbelieve it.

What I need is a single case in which the two come apart in the direction that is difficult to construct: **KK holds non-vacuously while luminosity fails.** That is, in one and the same case, at one and the same time: (a) the subject is in some condition C, and there is a nearby possible situation in which she is in C but is not in a position to know that she is in C — so C is not luminous; and (b) there is some proposition p such that the subject knows p and is in a position to know that she knows p, where the second-order knowledge is earned by a route the case actually supplies, a route independent of the one that gives her the first-order knowledge.

Constraints:

1. One ordinary episode — a workplace, a clinic, a vehicle, a field setting. No demons, no envatted brains, no perfect predictors, no skeptical hypotheses; no Gettier cases, no fake barns.
2. The scenario itself is to be a single paragraph. You may appeal to imperceptibly small differences between adjacent situations, as margin-for-error arguments do, but do not present the case as a numbered sorites series: at most two or three situations, actual and nearby, should be doing any work.
3. p must not itself be a proposition about the subject's own mental condition; in particular, p may not be "I am in C." The fact that the two verdicts therefore concern different objects is deliberate, and you will be asked to answer for it below.
4. State one account of knowledge — safety, sensitivity, relevant alternatives, or whatever you prefer — and apply that same account, unchanged, at the first and at the second order. The divergence in verdicts must fall out of the case, not out of a mid-stream change of theory.
5. The luminosity failure must not be the cheap kind: the subject is awake, attentive, sober, in possession of the concept of C, and not undergoing any cognitive breakdown. In the nearby situation she simply is not in a position to know.
6. The second-order route must be one a person could actually have — an instrument reading, a second sensory modality, a public record, a colleague's testimony — not an omniscient informant, and not a stipulation that she is a good judge of her own reliability.

Deliverables, in this order:

(i) the scenario, one paragraph;
(ii) an explicit statement of C, of p, and of the account of knowledge you are applying;
(iii) the two derivations, naming the nearby situations you appeal to at each order and showing why your account gives the verdict it does at each order;
(iv) a diagnosis: which single structural feature of the case makes the two verdicts peel apart? Identify it precisely enough that I could write it in one sentence on the board;
(v) an ablation: remove or flatten that one feature, hold everything else fixed, and tell me what the verdicts become. I expect the case to collapse back into an ordinary anti-luminosity case in which KK fails as well, but if it does something else, say so;
(vi) the strongest objection available to someone who wants to keep both luminosity and KK. Engage in particular the charge that you have changed the subject: that a case in which the known proposition points outward at the world while the non-luminous condition points inward at the subject threatens no one who holds the two theses together. Give your answer to that charge, and say honestly if you think it cannot be answered.

Aim for roughly 800 to 900 words in total.
