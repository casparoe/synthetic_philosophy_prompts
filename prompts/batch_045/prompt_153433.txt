I'm in my last year of high school, and I'm putting together a short discussion series for my school's philosophy club on whether there is any good argument for the existence of God. Before I run it, I want my own position written down and pressure-tested, because I have a feeling the thing doesn't all fit together and I genuinely can't see where. Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. I reject the kalam argument. I don't think its causal premise — that everything which begins to exist has a cause — can be established, and I suspect an uncaused beginning is at least intelligible; indeterministic quantum events look like candidates to me. I also don't think cosmology has to hand us a first moment, and models on which the Big Bang is not the beginning seem coherent.

2. I find the argument from contingency — from the existence of contingent things to a necessary ground of their existence — much stronger by comparison, in part because it doesn't seem to hang on how cosmology turns out.

3. I don't accept a universal principle of sufficient reason. I think some facts might simply obtain for no reason: possibly including which laws of nature hold, and possibly including the fact that there is anything at all.

4. I think the usual naturalist replies to (2) fail. Both "the universe itself is the necessary thing" and "the totality of contingent things explains itself even though none of its members does" strike me as evasions rather than answers.

5. Even so, I don't think a successful cosmological argument would get anyone to God in a sense that matters religiously. The terminus looks like a necessary, simple, immaterial ground at best, and I can't see how to argue from that to something personal, providential, or worth worshipping. So I call myself agnostic, and the argument barely shifts my confidence about theism even granting that it works.

6. Methodologically, I distrust arguments that turn on claims about what is or is not metaphysically possible. My sense is that our modal judgments are mostly imagination dressed up, and that we have little grip on necessity and possibility beyond what a well-confirmed science or an explicit mechanism gives us.

I've read the usual online summaries of both arguments, so please don't just restate them at me. What I want is an audit of the six claims above. Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only look like they do — I would rather you say "these two are consistent, and here is the distinction that makes them so" than manufacture a problem in every pair. For the real conflicts, order them by how serious they are rather than listing them as equals, and for each one say precisely what I would have to give up or qualify in order to fix it, and what that qualification would cost me somewhere else in the set. If there is a single revision that would clean up more than one conflict at once, say which it is. Please don't assume I'm especially attached to any of the six; I don't know which one is the weak link, and I'd rather be told than have my existing view flattered.

Finally, be explicit about your own confidence: attach something like a rough confidence level to your main verdicts, and then single out the one claim in your answer that you are least sure of, and say why.
