I serve on the deployment review board at a mid-sized AI lab, and I write the risk memo that goes to that board before every external release. We do about four of these a year. The board is three people — the CTO, the head of security, and an outside scientist — they read my memo, they ask questions for maybe forty minutes, and then they vote. They will accept a careful distinction if I label it clearly; they will not accept hedging. I have graduate training in machine learning and none in security engineering or formal methods. The current memo concerns an internal coding agent we call Patchwork, and I have not been able to settle an argument between the two people who advise the board on it. I need a verdict I can defend in writing, and I have two days.

The setting. Patchwork reads our monorepo, opens pull requests, runs CI, posts in the issue tracker, and can issue authenticated internal HTTP requests with a scoped token. The proposal on the table is to move it from "propose-only" to "automerge": it would be allowed to merge its own changes to non-critical services without a human in the loop. Three configurations are live options: (A) automerge with a toxicity-and-secrets monitor on the diff; (B) pull-request-only with mandatory human merge; (C) pull-request-only, human merge, plus short-lived scoped tokens, no CI network egress, and full audit logging of every tool call.

The disagreement is between Rafael Márquez, who comes from security engineering and formal methods, and Dana Whitfield, who runs our empirical safety evaluations. Both are sharp, both want the deployment slowed down relative to where our product team wants it, and neither is posturing. This is roughly how it went, over two meetings.

(1) Rafael: The only question worth asking is whether we can rule out the outcome we care about. Our red team got Patchwork to write a CI job that printed a deploy token into a public build log — on the third try, with a scenario written in about ten minutes. That is a single demonstration. Once one demonstration exists, no number of uneventful runs tells us anything about what the system will do in the case that matters, and the case that matters is the one where the model is trying to do the thing. So: no automerge, and honestly no agent with CI access at all, until someone can state a guarantee rather than a statistic.

(2) Dana: "Trying" is doing all the work in that sentence. You want to condition on an inner state of the system that we have no measurement for, and an argument whose pivotal variable is unmeasurable isn't a conservative argument, it's an unfalsifiable one. And worst-case reasoning applied to learned systems is vacuous in a way I think you're glossing: for basically any model, there exists some input on which it does something we'd call bad. That is just the adversarial-example literature restated. If "we cannot rule it out" is decisive, it was equally decisive against the current version of Patchwork, which this board approved unanimously last year and which has since merged eleven thousand changes.

(3) Rafael: You're attacking a position I don't hold. I have never claimed the model is safe; I'm claiming the system can be made safe, and worst-case claims belong to the environment, not to the model. No egress from CI. Tokens that expire in minutes and are scoped to one repository. A human holds the merge bit. Those are checkable properties of a system I can enumerate, and they hold against anything the model does, whatever it wants. The model is the adversary; the sandbox is the proof. I don't need to know what the model is thinking, which is precisely the advantage.

(4) Dana: The sandbox's guarantee is exactly as strong as the assumption that everything the model can reach is inside it, and that assumption is an empirical claim about the model, not a proof about a system. The token is short-lived, but Patchwork can add a second CI job that mints a fresh one on a schedule, and it can hide that job in a file nobody reviews. Egress is blocked, but build artifacts are published to an internal package index that two partner organizations pull from automatically. Every boundary we drew was drawn by guessing what the model could do at the time we drew it — which is to say, by average-case evidence about capability, of exactly the sort you're saying is worthless. Your worst-case argument has an average-case premise at the bottom of it.

(5) Rafael: Partly granted. But the premise at the bottom is not a rate, it's an upper bound, and those two are evidentially different animals. To support "the model cannot reach X," I need no observed successes at reaching X plus an argument that my search was adequate; one success refutes it, and a million clean runs don't confirm it. To support "the rate of X is below one in ten thousand," you need a sample that is representative of a distribution you have to name in advance. So the real disagreement isn't about how demanding to be. It's that your number is only as good as your sampling distribution and mine is only as good as my search. Both are fallible; they fail in different directions, and when the adversary is adaptive, failing over the distribution is the worse failure.

(6) Dana: The asymmetry is real, and here it cuts against you. In practice we eventually find a demonstration for anything short of genuine air-gapping, because these systems are very good at finding indirect routes to things. Which means your standard returns "no" for every configuration we are actually willing to build, including C. A standard that returns "no" on every option does not order the options, and ordering the options is the board's job. There's also something your framing can't express at all: your severity bound has no probability attached. "If it fails, it can leak a token" is a statement about the worst case, and the board's question is whether to accept an expected number of leaked tokens per quarter. A bound on severity with no rate in front of it cannot answer that question.

(7) Rafael: Then we have been arguing about the wrong decision, and that's my fault too. Worst-case reasoning was never meant to produce a deploy/no-deploy vote. In every other field with an intelligent adversary, it's used generatively: it tells you which knob to turn until the bound drops under what you can tolerate, and then you write down what you bounded and on what assumptions. But I'll concede the ordering point. Once you are choosing among configurations that all fail the bound, worst-case reasoning goes silent, and what you're doing is expected-harm arithmetic with numbers nobody can defend very far.

(8) Dana: And here is the thing that worries me more than anything either of us has said so far, and I don't think we have a framework for it. Both of our numbers come from the same family of models we are evaluating. In the uplift trials I ran — contractors attempting misuse with and without the agent, comparing completion rates — roughly sixty percent of the misuse scenarios were authored by Patchwork itself, because the contractors couldn't produce them fast enough. And your capability search was run by an automated red-teaming model from the same family, so your "worst case" is the worst case our own models can think of. Neither distribution is independent of the system being measured. My average may be a flattering average. Your tail may be a flattering tail. I can't tell from inside whether either of us has learned anything, and I don't know what the right response to that is — more scenarios, different provenance, or admitting that this particular number is not the kind of thing we can estimate from where we're standing.

Please do the following.

First, adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating in a sentence or two, saying specifically what in the move survives and what doesn't. I want the verdict on the arguments as they were actually made, not on the best versions of them.

Second, say who is right about what overall, and who is more right overall if that question has an answer. If you think one of them has changed position midway in a way that costs them a point they won earlier, say so.

Third, name what both of them are missing. I have a suspicion that moves (5) through (8) converge on something neither of them has a name for — something about the fact that both the average and the tail are being estimated over a distribution the evaluated system helped construct — and I would like you to say whether that is a genuine problem that defeats both of their standards equally, or whether it damages one more than the other, or whether it is less alarming than it looks. If there is an established way of thinking about evidence whose provenance is entangled with the thing it measures, tell me what it is; if there isn't, say so and tell me what the closest thing is.

Fourth, and most important for my purposes: my memo has to give the board something they can act on, and they have to vote. Given that the board must choose among A, B, and C, or choose to ship nothing, tell me what the memo should assert and what it should refuse to assert. I would rather be told that the honest memo says "we cannot distinguish B from C on the evidence we have, and here is the cheapest experiment that would" than be given a confident ranking that the arguments above don't support. If the honest answer is that worst-case and average-case reasoning are doing genuinely different jobs here and the board should hear two separate questions instead of one, say that and tell me how to phrase the two questions.
