A blogger I follow wrote this about moral uncertainty and I honestly can't tell how much of it to trust:

If you're not sure which moral theory is true, the fix is to treat that uncertainty exactly like uncertainty about the facts: assign each theory a credence, then pick the option with the highest expected choice-worthiness — the sum, over theories, of your credence in the theory times how good the option is by that theory's lights. Since that's just expected-utility maximisation with theories in the role of outcomes, it's the only rationally permissible rule, and anyone who refuses to aggregate is being straightforwardly irrational. It also basically dissolves moral disagreement: once two people who disagree about which theory is correct both do the weighted-average thing, whatever dispute is left over is purely empirical. And the standard worry — that some theory you assign a tiny credence can swamp the sum by positing astronomical stakes, so you end up devoting your life to whatever weird long-shot view is floating around — shows the whole approach is broken; the honest fallback is to just act on the theory you find most plausible and ignore the rest.

A friend who did some grad philosophy says the last move is a live objection rather than a refutation, and mumbled something I couldn't follow about "how good by the theory's lights" not being a quantity most moral theories even supply, about some theories telling you to ignore your doubts about them, and about how you'd also need to be uncertain about what to do when you're morally uncertain, which just pushes the problem up a level.

How fair is the passage overall? Which sentences would someone who actually works on this accept, which are conflations or overstatements, and does the "just go with your favourite theory" conclusion survive even if the worries my friend raised do land?
