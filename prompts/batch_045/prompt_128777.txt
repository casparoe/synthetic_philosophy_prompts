I am writing a paper for a graduate seminar on the history of analytic philosophy, and one section is deliberately counterfactual-historical: I want to work out what Frank Ramsey — who died in 1930, at twenty-six — would most plausibly have made of the way the theory of conditionals developed after him. I am not after a biography or a survey of who said what (please argue from commitments, not from references, and do not give me a bibliography). I want a reasoned prediction, and I want you to mark clearly where the text runs out and you are extrapolating.

The only sustained thing he wrote about conditionals is a footnote to his 1929 paper 'General Propositions and Causality'. In the form in which it is usually printed, it reads:

'If two people are arguing "If p will q?" and are both in doubt as to p, they are adding p hypothetically to their stock of knowledge and arguing on that basis about q; so that in a sense "If p, q" and "If p, ¬q" are contradictories. We can say they are fixing their degrees of belief in q given p. If p turns out false, these degrees of belief are rendered void. If either party believes ¬p for certain, the question ceases to mean anything to him except as a question about what follows from certain laws or hypotheses.'

Everything that came later sits awkwardly with parts of this. I would like you to work through four things.

First, the reception problem. The later tradition took this footnote as the inspiration for a unified treatment: a Ramsey test implemented by selecting the closest or most plausible p-world, meant to cover counterfactuals as much as indicatives. But the case the footnote describes is precisely the case where p is unsettled, and the third sentence says that when p turns out false the degrees of belief in question are 'rendered void'. Counterfactuals are exactly the case where the antecedent is settled false. So the canonical later use of Ramsey looks like an application of his test to the one case his own text seems to set aside. Would he have accepted that extension, resisted it, or accepted it only after reconstruing what the test delivers?

Second, the relocation to laws. When ¬p is certain, Ramsey sends us to 'what follows from certain laws or hypotheses'. Now take a case where the relevant law is irreducibly chancy. A sample of a radioactive isotope sits in front of a counter; within a one-minute interval each atom has a chance of roughly one half of decaying, and the counter is highly reliable — it clicks with high chance if a decay occurs, and with very low chance if none does. No atom decays, and the counter stays silent; by one minute past you know all of this. Assess 'If an atom had decayed in that interval, the counter would have clicked'. Here nothing about the outcome follows from the laws plus the antecedent; at most the antecedent plus the laws makes the consequent highly likely, and which chance is supposed to do the work — the actual chances, or the chances in the imagined situation — is itself contested. Given that Ramsey elsewhere treats laws and other variable hypotheticals less as truth-evaluable propositions than as rules or habits governing inference and action, and given his subjectivist treatment of probability as degree of belief, would he have accepted that this case is a genuine counterfactual at all, or would he have said the question lapses in a way our practice does not?

Third, the outcome he could not have foreseen. Later work tied the acceptability of an indicative conditional to the corresponding conditional probability, and then proved that this identification cannot hold in full generality for a non-trivial probability function, on pain of collapse. Presented with that collapse, would Ramsey more likely have concluded that conditionals are not the kind of thing that has a probability — which would rather suit the 'rendered void' remark and his attitude to variable hypotheticals — or that the probabilistic reading needs weakening while the Ramsey test is retained? Say which, and say what in his other commitments forces the answer.

Fourth, a proposal I have been toying with, which I would like tested against him rather than endorsed. One might keep a single Ramsey test but allow it to be instantiated with different revision policies: for indicatives, full revision of the belief state by the antecedent, including revision of beliefs about the past; for counterfactuals, revision that holds the past and the chances fixed and lets the laws run forward. On this picture there is one test and two policies, so the indicative/counterfactual split is a difference in what is held fixed rather than a difference in semantics — which is also, roughly, how the restrictor-style treatments that grew up later handle the contrast. Note that this sits badly with one clause of the footnote: if the two readings are contradictories in the open case, that is conditional excluded middle, and it is exactly the principle that the closest-world treatments divide over. Would Ramsey have welcomed this as faithful to his remark, or thought it abandoned the point of tying conditionals to the practice of two people in doubt?

Please answer in about 800 words, in three clearly separated parts: (1) what you take to be genuinely implied by the footnote and Ramsey's surrounding commitments; (2) your prediction, with the specific considerations that would push him toward monism or toward a split treatment; (3) an explicit statement of where the prediction is underdetermined and what further belief of his would settle it. Distinguish throughout between reading the 1929 text and extrapolating from the rest of his work.
