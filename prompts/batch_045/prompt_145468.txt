Prep me for the Q&A at a workshop next month where I'm presenting a dissertation chapter on performance legitimacy. The room is mostly China-focused comparative politics people who do survey work on political trust, plus one Singapore specialist, and maybe two normative theorists who came for the other paper and are skeptical that legitimacy is something you can argue about rather than just measure as belief. My thesis: performance legitimacy isn't a rival to consent theory, its a degenerate case of it. The regime is both the player and the scorekeeper and it grades itself on a scoreboard it also writes, so the interesting question isn't whether prosperity and order can confer legitimacy, its whether the governed have any way to contest the scoreboard. I propose a contestability condition: a performance claim confers legitimacy only if the people subject to it can institutionally challenge which indicators count and what they show. My second argument is the insurance one: performance legitimacy is a risk-transfer, the governed trade voice for lower variance in outcomes, but the ruler also controls the risk he's insuring against — like buying fire insurance from someone who can start fires and then bills you for the trucks — so the arrangement has a built-in moral hazard.
