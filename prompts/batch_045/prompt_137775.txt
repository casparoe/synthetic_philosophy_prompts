hey, I need a second opinion on something I keep going in circles about.

I'm doing a masters project on the surviving records of a mid-century municipal housing authority — specifically the files of its Objections and Appeals office, roughly 1948 to 1966. Here's the shape of what's left:

- a bound "Register of Objections": about 1,400 rows, one per complaint, columns for tenant name, block, grounds, ruling, date — every column filled in except "action taken by Housing Manager", which is blank for all 1,400;
- the typed monthly board minutes, which mention maybe 40 of those cases in total;
- a run of internal memos, but only for 1958–66;
- nothing at all for 1954–57, and none of the objection letters themselves, even though the register assigns each case a "document no.", which sort of implies the letters existed.

In my reading group everyone reaches for the same picture: the archive as a crime scene. The administration is the perpetrator, the surviving paperwork is forensic trace — the stuff nobody thought to clean up: carbon copies, blank form columns, running numbers with holes in them — and the historian is the investigator working back to what got covered up. They use it to license two moves in particular. One: the blank column and the missing letters aren't just loss, they're evidence of a standing practice of dealing with tenants' objections off the record. Two: the 1954–57 gap is evidence that something in those years was deliberately got rid of. I find it genuinely useful and I also think it's carrying more weight than it can.

So, three things.

First, the audit itself. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the perpetrator, the body, the motive, the forensic trace, the cold case, the investigator. Then where it breaks. My own list so far: a crime scene is a single bounded event where there's a fact of the matter that an investigator could in principle check against some independent witness, whereas here the "event" is forty years of filing and there's no witness outside the archive; a perpetrator conceals, but nearly everything an archive does it does openly and on purpose, and when records disappear it's usually some clerk following a retention schedule; and — the bit that worries me most — the frame seems to smuggle in its own conclusion, because calling something a crime scene already presupposes a crime and a culprit, and it points at a person when whatever happened here was probably structural. Tell me which of these disanalogies actually bite for the two moves my group wants, and which are just decorative.

Second, I've been trying to separate out kinds of silence, and I want to know whether the crime-scene lens can even see all of them:

(a) erasure — something was in the record and got removed, leaving seams: a hole in the document numbering, a destruction certificate, a cross-reference to a file that isn't in the box;
(b) non-creation — nothing was ever written down, because of who was in the room, what counted as official business, who could write, what language, what paper cost; this leaves no seam at all, and arguably isn't a silence you can point at from inside the archive;
(c) attrition — it existed, nobody destroyed it, it just didn't make it: an office move, a flood, a budget, nobody's job to keep it.

For each kind, say whether the crime-scene picture is a good lens, a useless one, or an actively misleading one. And then the concrete question: in material like mine, what would it actually take to tell (a) from (c)? And is (b) reachable by any method that starts from the archive at all, or only from somewhere outside it?

Third: my supervisor's rival line is that an archive is more like a dam than a crime scene — what's in the reservoir is a function of the watershed and of which sluice gates were open, and the water that never reached the reservoir left nothing inside it. That one feels defnately more honest about (b) and (c), but I suspect it makes "no one suppressed anything" too easy to say. Is that fair? And is there a better analogy than either, or is the honest answer that you need different pictures for different silences?
