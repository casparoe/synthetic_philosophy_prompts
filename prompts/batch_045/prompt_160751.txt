im trying to do my own bit of reflective equilibrium for an essay on the boundary problem — who actually belongs to the demos — and ive written down my gut verdicts on who should get a vote, and i want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

- someone who's lived and paid tax here ten years but never took citizenship: yes, definitely
- a citizen who's lived abroad eight years, pays no tax here and doesnt follow the news: no, even though the law still binds him if he moves back
- a commuter who lives over the border, works here and pays income tax here: yes, i think
- a tourist on a two week holiday: no, obviously
- a stateless person who's been stuck here three years and can't be deported: yes, but i'm shakier about this one
- a citizen born abroad who's never set foot here and doesn't speak the language: no — and this is the one that worries me, because it feels like it can't both survive alongside the last one
- a sixteen year old: yes. A nine year old: no
- someone in a persistent vegetative state: no, but I can't say why without wrecking the rest of my list
- a company headquartered here: no, obviously
- people downstream in another country who'd lose their homes to a dam we build: they should be consulted, but not given votes

the principle i keep trying to make work is: you get a say just in case you're subject to the coercive power of the state. but that seems to hand a vote to the tourist, the nine year old and the woman in a coma, and it doesn't explain why i'm less sure about the stateless person than about the ten year resident. is there one principle — or two or three i have to weigh against each other — that gets most of these, stated precisely enough that i can test it, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? please also tell me what it says about cases i haven't listed: prisoners serving life sentences, dual citizens who vote in two countries, and people who own land here but live abroad. no reading lists, i just want the principle and where it bites.
