I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past year or so I've been reading on my own about whether it can be a bad thing to bring someone into existence, and I now run a small fortnightly reading group — eight people, mostly engineers and a nurse, none of us with any academic background in the subject. We keep going in circles, and I've come to think the reason is that we use the same words for genuinely different views. So I want to make something we can actually walk through together: a decision tree that sorts a person by what they say about cases, not by which label they'd claim.

The view we keep getting stuck on is the package of four claims: that a person's suffering is bad for them if they exist; that the happiness they miss out on is bad for them if they exist; that the suffering a merely possible person would have had is *good* if they never exist, and not merely not-bad; and that the happiness a merely possible person never gets to have is not bad if they never exist, merely not-good. I'd rather you didn't start the tree from "do you accept this package?" — that just asks people to recognise a label.

What I want:

**The instrument.** Eight to ten branch nodes, six to nine leaves, every node phrased as a plain-language question with two or three answer options that someone can answer without knowing what the options are called. Entry point: a person deciding whether to have a child at all, with no complicating circumstances. End point: a named (or, if nobody holds it, honestly labelled "unnamed") position, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Add a short glossary at the end for the technical names, but only give a name from the published literature where you're actually confident it's the right one — otherwise describe the view in a phrase and say so. I'd rather have an accurate description than a mislabelled one.

**Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names.** Take your questions first, lay out which combinations of answers they permit, then locate the positions that actually get defended inside that space, and tell me about the cells nobody occupies: is each one empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has bothered? Where your tree can't separate two positions — where they agree on every case we could construct — say so explicitly rather than inventing a question to split them.

**Three distinctions that matter for my purposes.** First, I want the tree to separate views on which the asymmetry is a claim about *value* (about how good states of affairs are) from views on which it's really a claim about *reasons* or about the logic of choice, even when the two give the same advice. Second, I want it to separate views on which the asymmetry only governs existence-versus-nonexistence from views on which it also governs *which* person comes to exist. Third, every leaf has to state what it implies about three things: whether creating one additional happy person (extra, not replacing anyone) is good, bad, or neutral; whether a government policy aimed at raising the birth rate is thereby doing something good, bad, or neutral; and whether, of an existing person with a good life, it can be said that it would have been better had they never existed. If two leaves differ on the tree but converge on all three, I want that flagged — that's the part my group most needs to see.

**Three cases I want used as nodes.** (1) *The switch:* you must bring exactly one child into existence; one candidate would have an excellent life, the other an excellent life that is somewhat less excellent and contains a small amount of suffering the first would not, both clearly worth living — is there any moral reason to bring about the first rather than the second? (2) *The delay:* conceiving now would produce one particular child with a good life; waiting eleven months would produce a numerically different child whose life would be better in every respect, with no other effects anywhere — is there any moral reason to wait? (3) *The margin:* the child you could have would have a life of extraordinary happiness containing exactly one brief, minor pain, nothing that would make the life not worth living — does the fact that this pain would be avoided if the child never existed count at all against having them?

Alongside each node, tell me in a clause which pair of positions it is doing the work of separating.

**How confident you are.** For each leaf, say how confident you are that people actually defend something close to it and that your node set really isolates it, and mark the leaves you think you're reconstructing rather than reporting. Then single out the one step in the tree you are least sure about: the node you suspect does no real discriminating work, or where my phrasing of a case quietly assumes something the positions disagree about.
