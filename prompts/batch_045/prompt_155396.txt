I'm puting together a one-page handout for the decision theory reading group I run — second-year philosophy undergrads, they've seen expected utility theory but nothing at all about infinite gambles. I want a decision tree that starts from "you are offered a gamble whose expected value does not exist" and sorts people into positions. Here's the gamble, written out so nobody needs to know any literature: a fair coin is flipped until it first comes up heads, n = number of flips, you receive $2^n/n if n is odd and you pay $2^n/n if n is even; each outcome has probability 1/2^n, so the expected value is the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2 in its natural order but can be rearranged to add up to anything you like, and the sum of absolute values diverges.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names, and please no bibliography or reading list — the leaves should be identified by what they say about cases, not by who holds them. Leaves I want somewhere in the tree (use their usual names only if you're confident such names exist, otherwise just coin a label): (a) the natural-order sum is the gamble's value; (b) rank by truncated or partial-sum dominance, with the strong version (the truncated difference is eventually always positive) kept apart from the weak one (eventually never negative, sometimes positive); (c) rank pairwise by the expectation of the difference between two gambles, which can be defined even when neither gamble has a value of its own; (d) change the currency — saturating or bounded utility, or something in that family — so that expectation exists again; (e) no value and no ranking, they are simply incomparable, and note that incompleteness is not money-pumpable the way intransitivity is; (f) it was never a well-formed decision problem, because infinite time, infinite bankroll and infinite precision are not things anyone can actually offer you, so the paradox is an artefact of an idealisation.

The branch questions are the real work: each one should be a concrete case or claim a student could answer without knowing where it leads, and none should just ask which school they belong to. Two I definitely want in there because they are the ones people dodge: does your answer change if you may play the gamble as often as you like and keep the running total — and say plainly that long-run averages don't settle down here, since there is no law of large numbers to lean on when the mean isn't finite; and does what you say about the uncapped gamble change if, before the flip, you may pay a fixed fee to have the payout computed on a capped version with n frozen at some large cutoff, i.e. does your verdict depend on whether that capped option is also on the menu. Somewhere I also want the same gamble with a dollar subtracted from every payoff: must your ranking move by exactly a dollar, or can the two be undefined-relative even though one is literally the other minus a dollar.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one per branch point, each one or two sentences: the question as I should print it inside the box, which way each answer sends the reader, and which leaf they land on where the route ends there. No intro, no conclusion, and skip the diagram, I'll draw it myself. If some combination of answers is unoccupied, flag it and say why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored — inside the item where it comes up, and flag any two leaves the five questions can't actually seperate.
