My flatmate Sam is doing a PhD in ML and has been going on at me about interpretability for weeks. He thinks the whole field rests on one mistake, and last night he wrote out the argument. Every step looks fine to me and the conclusion is nuts, so I want to know where the thing actually breaks.

(1) A state S of a system represents X only if it is S's job to track X. Correlation isn't enough: my doorbell's ring correlates pretty reliably with the post van driving past, but the ring doesn't represent the van. Nothing in the doorbell is there in order to indicate anything.

(2) The only thing that gives an internal state of a trained net a job at all is the training process: roughly, gradient descent on a loss, plus whatever the architecture and the regularizers do. So whatever a hidden state is for, it is for being a configuration that lowers loss on the training distribution.

(3) Lowering loss is a property of the whole input-output function, not of any decomposition of the activations. Take any invertible linear map M of a layer's activation space and push the next layer's weights through M-inverse: the function is literally unchanged. Sam calls this basis indifference and says it even works for nonlinear invertible maps if you also transform what the downstream layers do.

(4) So which directions we single out as features is our choice, not the network's. For any state S and any candidate content X you like — Frenchness, golden retrievers, the feeling of being watched — there is a decomposition in which S tracks X and another in which it doesn't, with no difference at all in the function computed.

(5) If whether S tracks X is settled by a choice we make rather than by anything in the network, then S doesn't represent X. We are drawing convenient coordinates on the thing, the way you can draw longitude lines on the sea.

(6) Generalize: no internal state of any net represents anything. Sparse-autoencoder features, circuits, linear probes, all of it — maps, not territory. Interpretability is an elaborate way of describing a curve in a basis we invented.

(7) But output-layer activations are internal states too. So a language model's outputs aren't about anything either. A model can't misdescribe the world; it emits strings. Calling one of them a hallucination is us being sentimental about text.

(8) Sam bites the bullet on (7), which drives me up the wall. He also adds, as a bonus, that (1) and (2) applied to a brain are awkward as well: evolution selects for inclusive fitness, not for tracking, so on this account it isn't obvious that anything in my head is about the post van either.

I keep going between two replies and I dont think either works. One is the swap test people in my supervisors group kick around: line up a direction in net A with a direction in net B trained on the same data with a different seed, splice B's version into A, and if behaviour barely moves then that direction has some model-independent content, which (4) says shouldn't happen. The other is about interventions: (3) is invariance under redescription, but actually clamping or amplifying a direction does change behaviour, and not in a way that survives arbitrary reparameterization. Sam's answer to both is the same — they show a decomposition can be stable and useful, not that it is representational — and I have no comeback to that.
