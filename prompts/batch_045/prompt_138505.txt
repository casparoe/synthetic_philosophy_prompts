I serve on the five-person Alder Ferry Memorial Commission, a body our town created two years ago with a single charge: to decide what becomes of the old customs shed on the quay. In the autumn of 1943 roughly two hundred people, most of them families who had lived here for generations, were locked in that shed for three days before being put aboard a boat; almost none of them came back. Since the war the building has been a salt store, then a garage, and for the last thirty years a municipal depot, and it is now failing: the roof trusses are unsound and our engineer gives it perhaps a decade before it is unsafe to enter. Two proposals are formally before us. The first, backed by a conditional state heritage grant that lapses at the end of June, would stabilise the structure and open it as a small memorial room with the interior left exactly as it is; the grant binds the town to keep the building standing and publicly accessible for at least twenty-five years. The second, backed by the regional housing trust, would demolish it within eighteen months and build twenty-two affordable units on the site, with a marked memorial garden at the quay's edge. Yesterday a third option arrived unasked, in a letter from a digital heritage group at the university: they will laser-scan the shed to millimetre accuracy, build a room-scale reconstruction visitors walk through in headsets, and, this is the part I cannot stop thinking about, re-scan it every year for as long as it stands, so that the virtual shed will crack, sag and darken in step with the real one and will carry its accumulated decay forward after the real one is gone.

What makes this hard is not the money but who wants what. The descendants' association is split almost evenly: one half says the standing room is the only thing that has ever communicated what happened, the other half says the building is saturated and that leaving it up insults the people who suffered in it. The single surviving survivor, now ninety and living abroad, has written to say she wants it levelled, the ground paved over, and nothing at all named after her. The national memorial organisation counters that a duty to remember is owed to the dead and cannot be waived by one of them, and that a scan is a surrogate, not a memory. Meanwhile most of our current residents arrived after 1990 and have no connection to the families, and a significant number are descendants of the people who moved into the vacated houses, which I suspect is why the housing option is quietly popular at public comment. Years ago I read a book on the ethics of memory that argued something like this: memory is owed only within a moral community, and what a town is really doing when it builds a memorial is deciding what kind of community it intends to be. My planner friend, who has watched the shed rot for two decades, says forgetting is simply what places do and that it is often merciful. I find myself leaning toward demolition with the scan and the garden, and I am uneasy about how convenient that is, since it also delivers twenty-two housing units and costs the town almost nothing.

Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate to my fellow commissioners before the vote in nine days. It should come down for one concrete course of action with the dates and conditions you would attach to it, not a menu of options; it should give the strongest case for the option you reject in terms its own supporters would recognise, rather than the version easiest to knock down; and it should say plainly what you take the commission to be deciding, and what it is not entitled to decide, if you think we are being asked to settle something on behalf of people who cannot answer for themselves. Treat the facts above as complete and settled: do not ask me for more information, do not recommend further study, further consultation, or outside expertise, and do not cite or refer to any book, author, or document, since I need something I can use tonight. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, the way I would write a long email to four colleagues the evening before a meeting, and keep it to something I can read aloud in under seven minutes. Finally, I want to know how much weight your recommendation carries: so name the single further fact or argument that would most quickly move you to a different one, say where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail, and tell me honestly whether you suspect you have dressed up a budgetary or political conclusion in moral language.
