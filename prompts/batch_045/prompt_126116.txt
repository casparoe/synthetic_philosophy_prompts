Hi — I need someone to referee an argument, because I honestly can't tell who's got it right.

Background: about six months ago I ended up on the steering committee of a community land trust in my city. Long story, they needed a tenant rep for our two buildings and I was basically the only person who showed up to two meetings in a row. We now have an actual small budget to allocate each quarter and we get to set the rules for how the shared spaces (courtyard, laundry room, the big front room) get used. It's small stuff but people fight about it like it's zoning.

Two members of the committee — I'll call them Ren and Olu — have been going at each other for weeks about *how we should go about deciding things*, and they both keep invoking Dewey. Which is a bit rich since I don't think either of them has read him since one grad seminar. I've read a bit more in the last few weeks trying to figure out who's distorting him, and I still can't call it.

Ren's position: for Dewey, democracy just is the social form of experimental inquiry. So we should run this committee like a lab. Before adopting anything, write down what we expect to happen, in terms we could in principle check later; set a date to look; then actually look and revise or scrap. He's drafted a one-page template: if we do X, we expect Y by date Z, and here's how we'd notice we were wrong.

Olu: that's technocracy wearing a Dewey costume. On his reading, the experimental method in Dewey isn't a procedure a committee runs *on* a population. It's something the people affected run together, because the consequences of a policy are only available as lived by the people living them. So if the committee designs the test, picks the metric, and then reads the results off the residents, we've turned our neighbors into measuring instruments and reinstalled exactly the experts-versus-public split Dewey was arguing against in The Public and Its Problems — the whole point being that the people bearing the consequences are the ones who have to be in on the inquiry, since they're the ones who can identify what the consequences even are.

Ren's reply: okay, but Dewey also says inquiry needs a community of inquirers with free communication and shared standards of evidence. "Everyone's experience counts" with no way of comparing experiences just means whoever talks most and loudest wins. And he says Olu's version collapses into proceduralism: if there's no check on whether a decision actually made anything better, then democracy is just a machine for blessing whatever the meeting happened to feel like, and Dewey thought the alternative to the experimental method was dogmatism or straight-up force.

Olu's reply: the check isn't a metric, it's that the people bearing the consequences can keep reopening the question — that unexpected consequences are what make a public form and organize itself in the first place. And he made a point I thought was sharper: Dewey's ends are ends-in-view, revised in the course of acting, so pre-committing to a metric freezes the exact thing inquiry is supposed to put in question. We'd end up optimizing for whatever happened to be easy to count last spring.

Ren: then how do you distinguish warranted from self-serving? Without some check specified in advance, "we deliberated and it feels better now" is unfalsifiable, and any committee can rationalize anything.

Olu: warranted assertibility is about whether the problematic situation actually gets resolved, not about hitting a number. And some of what we decide isn't a trial at all. Setting a rule about who can use the courtyard isn't an experiment, it's settling a question so the situation becomes stable enough that people can get on with their lives; the point isn't to learn what would have happened otherwise.

One more thing that's now on the table, since whatever we decide will apply to it: someone proposed what she called an open grievance log — any resident can file a claim that a decision is failing, the committee has to respond in writing within 30 days, and once five unresolved claims pile up the policy automatically goes back to a full members meeting for a re-vote. Ren is thrilled, because it's a trigger for re-inquiry that doesn't depend on us remembering to check. Olu hates it, because he says it converts political judgment into a ticket queue and rewards whoever is most willing to file.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who has Dewey right where, and who's overreaching? I don't so much need an overall winner as: which of these claims Dewey would actually endorse, which are defensible extensions of him, and which are imports from somewhere else entirely that are being passed off as Deweyan. And if there's something both of them are missing — especially about what "experimental" can even mean when the thing being decided is a rule about how people live together rather than a causal question — I'd really like to hear that too.
