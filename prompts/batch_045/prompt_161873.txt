I am applying for a one-semester dissertation fellowship and need the proposal itself: about 900 words, written as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in the manner of a letter to a supervisor, and in British English throughout.

The passage I want to work on is the butterfly dream: Zhuang Zhou dreams that he is a butterfly, and on waking he cannot tell whether he is Zhou who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming that it is Zhou.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I should like sharpened rather than flattered. Nearly every reading I have encountered treats the passage as raising one of two doubts, and I suspect both are the wrong doubt. The first is the global sceptical one: the passage as an anticipation of radical doubt about whether there is an external world at all, with the dream playing the part later played by the deceiving demon or the envatted brain. The second is the equanimity reading, on which the point is that the boundaries between things are not fixed, so that little hangs on which thing one is and death is not to be feared. Neither, so far as I can see, explains why the passage leads nowhere in particular: there is no method of doubt, no search for a criterion of reality, no recommended practice of suspension, and no argument either. I want to separate three things that are usually run together — call them content doubt (is the world as my experience presents it?), frame doubt (of the two sequences of experience, which encloses the other, that is, which is the real one?), and indexical doubt (whichever sequence I am in, am I the thing within it that I seem to be?). My suspicion is that only indexical doubt is genuinely in play, that it is evidentially inert in a way the other two are not — no quantity of further experience could settle it, because the doubt concerns not what is there but who is having it — and that this is what the closing remark about the transformation of things registers rather than resolves. I should also like the proposal to say what follows practically, if anything: whether the stance the passage invites is suspension of judgement, a kind of perspective-relative commitment, or plain indifference, and how one would tell those three apart.

What I want in the proposal, in this order: a statement of the question sharp enough to have a determinate answer within a semester, and not merely a topic; an account of why those two standard readings, and any third you would place beside them, leave that question open; the method, stage by stage and dated across the term, including the cases or test-scenarios I should construct in order to find out whether indexical doubt really is evidentially inert and whether it really is distinct from frame doubt; why this way in is better, for the time I have, than the obvious alternatives — a survey of the secondary literature, a comparative treatment of ancient and early modern scepticism, or a reading organised around the vocabulary of transformation — and what each of those would cost me; the two or three conclusions the project could realistically reach, and which considerations would decide between them; honestly, what would count as the project failing, whether by the threefold distinction collapsing, by the practical upshot proving indistinguishable from equanimity, or by nothing in the passage hanging on which doubt is in play; and a candid note on scope, marking the point at which this stops being a dissertation and becomes a book.

Please include no references: I shall supply those myself, and I would rather see whether the argument stands without them.
