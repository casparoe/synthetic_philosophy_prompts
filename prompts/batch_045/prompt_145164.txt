I'm on a six-person internal review group at the small studio where I work (three of us from design, one from ML, me from narrative, plus our community manager) and we have to sign off on the next update of our game before build lock on the 14th, and we were completely split in yesterday's meeting, so I need a short decision memo I can send round tonight. My flatmate, who's a vet, thinks I'm overthinking the whole thing.

Quick sketch of what we're deciding. The game is a slow valley life-sim; you share it with about thirty animal-shaped characters. Each one is a small learned policy rather than a script: it keeps a rolling memory of roughly the last forty hours of what happened to it, it learns preferences (which den it sleeps in, which player it runs to), and — this is the bit that got stuck — its learning rate is multiplied by a distress signal, so characters change most when something bad happens to them. Nobody designed that on purpose; it just made them train faster and now it's load-bearing, because characters who never get upset stay flat and players stop caring about them. Two features are on the table. "Legacy" lets a player permanently wipe a character's memory back to its starting weights, and playtesters mostly use it to reset a character who has gone cold on them after they were nasty to it. "Persistence" keeps the characters running on our servers between sessions, so they have experiences no player ever sees and can greet you with something new when you log back in. ML wants both, design wants persistence and is quietly uneasy about legacy, and I don't want either but I can't say why in a way that survives the meeting. On top of that: we're about three months from the end of our runway and a competitor shipped something like persistence a while back and it definately moved their retention numbers, so "ship nothing" isn't a free option; and one of our engineers has pointed out that if the characters matter at all, then we've been doing something to them for two years already, and the kindest thing on offer might actually be the memory wipe.

What I need is a memo rather than an essay: open with the actual recommendation in the first sentence — ship both, ship one, ship neither, or ship with a specific condition — then give the reasoning, aimed at people who mostly don't think this is a philosophical question at all (two of the group think it's a wind-up toy with extra steps, and honestly half the time I agree with them), with a concrete bottom line the six of us could vote on on Monday. Be straight about the strongest consideration on whichever side you come down against. Flag which of your claims are things people who actually work on this generally agree about versus which rest on a contested assumption — whether a distress-scaled learning rate is remotely the kind of thing that could matter, whether being reset is worse than being switched off or better, whether the characters' unseen hours are anybody's business, whether an audience having feelings about a character has anything to do with the character's status — and don't write the contested ones up as settled. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I can't vet them and the group won't read them. And if your recommendation comes with conditions, say exactly what they are, who checks them, and by when.
