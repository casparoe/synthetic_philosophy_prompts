I'm a second-year undergraduate reading archaeology and philosophy, and this term I'm taking an interdisciplinary option called 'Science, Pseudoscience and the Past', which is mostly about how one tells a genuinely revisionist hypothesis about the past from a pseudo-historical one. The convener has a habit I've come to rely on: on the course page he keeps a running list of verdicts — 'pseudohistory' or 'not pseudohistory' — on individual claims, usually with a sentence of reasoning, and he has been adding to it for about a decade. His verdicts so far include: (i) Atlantis understood as a real, technologically advanced civilisation destroyed in a cataclysm — pseudohistory; (ii) the claim that the pyramids and the Nazca lines required extraterrestrial assistance — pseudohistory; (iii) Fomenko's 'New Chronology', which compresses recorded history by more than a millennium and treats supposedly separate chronicles as duplicates of one another — pseudohistory, and he has stressed that this is so *notwithstanding* Fomenko's mathematical eminence and the quantitative apparatus he brings to bear; (iv) the 'mud flood' / 'Tartaria' idea that an advanced worldwide civilisation was buried and then deliberately written out of the record — pseudohistory, with the specific note that explaining away missing evidence by suppression is the diagnostic move; (v) a geologist's argument that the weathering on the Sphinx enclosure is rainfall erosion and that the monument is therefore far older than the conventional date permits — *not* pseudohistory, but a live and legitimate scholarly disagreement, even though very few Egyptologists accept the conclusion; (vi) the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, i.e. a proposed cometary impact at the end of the last ice age held responsible for the megafauna extinctions — legitimate science, although most specialists now regard it as unsupported; (vii) a proposal to lower the conventional Iron Age dates for the Levant by several decades on radiocarbon grounds — legitimate, indeed entirely ordinary, scholarship; and (viii) a civilisation attested only in other peoples' inscriptions, whose existence most scholars doubted until its capital was excavated — he refuses to classify this as pseudohistory even for the period when there was no direct evidence for it, and he likewise declines to classify as pseudohistory an uncredentialed amateur's identification of a site that later excavation confirmed.

The case I can't work out is a live one he hasn't ruled on, and I'd like to know what he will say and whether he *should* say it given the above. A group calling itself the Interfluve Survey Project — a geoarchaeologist at a Brazilian federal university and a remote-sensing specialist at a British one, so both thoroughly inside the profession — argues that the high ground between the rivers in a large region of the southern Amazon carries a dense network of ditched enclosures, causeways and managed orchards, built by populations far larger and far more sedentary than the standard picture of small, mobile, riverine groups allows. Three features of how they argue seem to me to matter. First, before any data were collected they pre-registered a prediction: lidar surveys of forty pre-specified 25 km² tiles on the interfluves would reveal at least twenty ditched enclosures more than 100 metres across. Second, they explain the present absence of evidence not by suppression but by survey bias — virtually all previous survey in the region has followed the rivers, because rivers are how one gets about — and they say this is itself checkable from the record of where survey effort has actually gone. Third, they have complained publicly that their grant applications were rejected by panels attached to an orthodoxy of 'pristine forest' and small mobile populations. Now the awkward part: the surveys have been flown, and they found four enclosures, only one of them over 100 metres, plus a long causeway. The team's response is that two of the pre-specified tiles were never flown owing to permit problems, that much of the region has been logged since the baseline imagery was captured so their automated detection under-performed, and that they have now pre-registered a *second* prediction — thirty enclosures in a new set of tiles — using a detection pipeline validated on already-known sites, with a commitment to publish whatever comes out. Their critics say this is textbook immunisation of a favoured hypothesis; the team replies that a failed prediction, a specific and independently checkable account of why it failed, and a fresh risky prediction is precisely what accountable science looks like.

So here is what I'm after, and I should say that I don't want a verdict on whether the enclosures are really there — I have no way of judging that and neither, I think, does he. I want the classification question. Please set out the two or three or four rules that fit his whole track record equally well — one keyed to testability and the response to failed predictions, one to the content of the claim (lost civilisations, catastrophes, diffusion), one to institutional position and credentials, one to the conduct of the argument and the rhetoric of exclusion, or whatever better set you can construct — and say what each predicts both about the project as it stood before the surveys and about the team's response afterwards, since I suspect the rules come apart between those two stages. Then give me your best guess at his verdict, with the reasons for preferring it, and identify the further case or further piece of evidence that would actually discriminate between the surviving rules: what would have to happen, or what would have to be added to the claim, for the rules that currently agree to diverge? If it turns out that his precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, please say so plainly and tell me what he would be committing himself to by choosing either way. British English, please, and feel free to be as technical as you like about the structure of the reasoning.
