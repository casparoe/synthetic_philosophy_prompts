I am putting together a graduate seminar module on the moral status of non-human animals, and I want one session given over to a tangle that the standard readings slide past. I'd like you to map its logical geography for me.

The tangle is this. Start from three commitments that a great many animal ethicists hold at once, and that look to me to be jointly unstable.

(1) Anti-speciesism, in its comparative-interest form: species membership is not in itself a reason to discount a being's comparable interests. A given amount of suffering matters the same amount whosever it is.

(2) The replaceability argument: for a being with no conception of itself as persisting through time — no future-directed desires, no projects it is invested in, nothing it could be said to be deprived of in the sense of missing — painless death is not a harm to it, provided another similarly situated being is created to take its place. The logic-of-the-larder defence of humane farming rides on exactly this: the animals in question exist at all only because someone intends to eat them, so no one is made worse off, and one being is better off than it would otherwise have been.

(3) A desire-based or time-relative account of the harm of death: what makes death bad for a being is the frustration of its desires, or the loss of future goods to which it is psychologically connected, so that how bad a death is varies with how much the being has invested in its own future.

Here is the instability. (3) is doing the work in (2), but (3) is graded and comparative in a way that (1) is not. If the badness of a death tracks how much a creature has at stake in its future, then killing a being with thin psychological unity over time is less bad than killing one with thick unity — and on any credible comparative psychology, the animals we farm have much thinner unity than adult humans do. So (3) appears to license a steep hierarchy in the wrongness of killing that looks structurally like the very asymmetry (1) forbids, while licensing it without ever mentioning species. I have been calling this the camouflaged asymmetry problem.

And the pressure runs the other way too: if you flatten (3) — insist that death is equally bad for any creature with a future of positive experience, because what matters is foregone experience rather than frustrated desire — then (2) becomes very hard to block, and it gets its foothold precisely where the account was flattened.

What I want from you, as one connected piece of reasoning rather than a survey:

First, must the anti-speciesist choose between a desire-based or time-relative account of the harm of death, which blocks replaceability at the cost of reinstating a steep hierarchy in killing, and an experiential account, which honours equality but lets replaceability back in? Or is there a stable way to reject replaceability while keeping (1) — say by prising apart how bad a death is for the one who dies from how wrong it is to kill, or by arguing that creating a new being does not compensate even where the victim loses very little, or by some route I have not listed?

Second, is the camouflaged asymmetry really camouflage? Is there a principled moral difference between a hierarchy in the wrongness of killing that tracks psychological features adult humans happen to have more of, and one that tracks species membership? Please engage with the obvious reassuring move — that a hierarchy pitched at psychological features is in principle satisfiable by a differently-psychologized creature, so it is not a status hierarchy at all — and tell me why that move should or should not reassure someone who accepts (1).

Third, is any of this specific to animals? If the same structure shows up for killing human infants, or for a hypothetical species with a radically different temporal psychology, then the right conclusion for the seminar is that the animal case is a symptom of the problem rather than its site. I want your view on whether that is the correct reading.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. I would much rather have an argument I can push back on in front of the seminar than a balanced overview of the literature.
