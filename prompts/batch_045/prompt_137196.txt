I joined the animal care and use committee at a mid-sized research university this past spring, mostly because a senior colleague asked and I could not think of a good reason to refuse. Then, in July, my sister's son was born with a severe cortical malformation. The neurologists put his cortical volume at something like a third of what would be typical for his age, and they told the family that they cannot yet say, and may never be able to say, how much he will understand, remember, or feel. So the question I keep circling in committee meetings has stopped being hypothetical for me, and I would like help understanding what my own practice actually commits me to.

Here is the practice, as I have come to understand it.

Under the federal rules that govern animal research in the United States, the term animal reaches dogs, cats, rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, and nonhuman primates, and institutions must meet minimum standards of housing, veterinary oversight, and enrichment for them. The same rules deliberately exclude birds, rats of the genus Rattus, and mice of the genus Mus bred for research, and they exclude farm animals such as cattle, pigs, and chickens when those animals are used for food or fiber. In practice this means that nearly all of the animal work at my institution, which is overwhelmingly mouse work, falls outside the federal standards, while the small number of dogs and primates on campus falls inside them. The European framework our collaborators in Munich work under is broader: it covers all live nonhuman vertebrates and also live cephalopods. Within my own committee, investigators must justify the species they propose and are expected, other things being equal, to use the least sentient species that will answer the question. Chimpanzees are, as far as I can tell, off the table entirely; macaques are not.

What I am after is a reconstruction of the theory this whole architecture presupposes, and an assessment of whether it is coherent.

First, the reconstruction itself. As I read it, the practice seems to presuppose several separable things: that the moral seriousness of what we do to an animal is a matter of degree rather than all or nothing; that it tracks some psychological capacity of the individual animal; that the sophistication of its nervous system is an acceptable proxy for that capacity; and that the proxy may be administered at the level of a taxonomic group rather than measured in individuals. Which of these does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely fit comfortably with? Where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license exactly the rules we have?

Second, the proxy itself. Brain size comes apart into several different quantities. Whole-brain mass puts elephants and several whales above us. Brain mass relative to body mass puts us at or near the top. Numbers of neurons are a third story again: on the estimates I have seen, counting only the cortex and counting the whole brain give different answers about where elephants sit relative to us, because such a large share of an elephant's neurons is in its cerebellum. My suspicion is that the practice invokes whichever of these quantities happens to yield the answer it wanted in the case at hand. Is that a genuine incoherence, or do the measures diverge only in cases the practice was never designed to reach?

Third, the direction of the scaling. The instruction to prefer the least sentient species assumes that more neural machinery means more capacity to suffer. But one could argue the other way: a larger and more layered brain adds anticipation, memory, and self-representation, which are sources of suffering, but it also adds top-down modulation, distraction, and the ability to situate a pain within a larger narrative, which might reduce it. Does the practice depend on a monotonic relation it has never argued for?

Fourth, the marginal cases. If the rationale is really about the capacities of individuals, then administering it by species looks arbitrary, and it also appears to generate conclusions about human infants and about people with brains like my nephew's that no one on the committee accepts. What are the honest responses here? Abandon the capacity rationale; accept the conclusion; or hold a two-level view on which human beings have full status for reasons that have nothing to do with capacity, while animals have graded status for reasons that do? Is that two-level view defensible, or is it special pleading? And does the practice as it stands strictly presuppose any answer to this, or is it silent?

Fifth, an apparent inconsistency closer to home. Our protocol forms classify severity by procedure: an investigator must search for alternatives when a procedure may cause more than momentary pain or distress, and not when it does not. So we grade the seriousness of the harm by what is done, but we grade which animals count at all by species. Are these two scales in tension, or do they answer different questions?

Sixth, uncertainty. For the species the federal rules exclude, no one has to show anything about what those animals can feel; the silence seems to work in the researcher's favor. Yet the same European framework extends protection to fetal mammals from the last third of their development, which looks like uncertainty resolved in the other direction. Does the practice contain a rule about who bears the burden of ignorance, and is it consistent?

Finally, a colleague has floated a concrete reform: replace the species list with a graded schedule keyed to estimated forebrain neuron counts, applied to any animal regardless of species, with a precautionary margin where the evidence about a species is thin. Does that reform follow from the reasoning already implicit in what we do, or would adopting it reveal that the practice was never really about brains at all? What would have to change institutionally if we took it seriously, and what is the strongest reason to refuse it?

I am not asking for a verdict on animal research as such, and I am not asking for anything about my nephew's care. I want the implicit theory made explicit, and I want to know which parts of it are load-bearing and which are decoration. Please write in American English, and please keep the discussion concrete rather than programmatic.
