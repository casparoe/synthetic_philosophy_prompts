I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and this spring I am running an adversarial collaboration as the final session: two colleagues who have been arguing past each other for years — one a selected-effects theorist in the Millikan–Neander tradition, one a causal-role theorist in the Cummins–Boorse tradition — have agreed to produce a single joint document rather than another exchange of papers. A colleague in the biology department recently told me that in her field nobody means anything historical by function attributions, which is exactly the sort of remark that makes this exercise worth attempting. I have to give the two participants a template in advance, so I would like you to draft the document itself: the text both of them could put their names to. Do not cite or name particular articles and books; I need something they can read as a statement of their own positions, not a survey.

Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims — the things both can already sign without hedging: for instance that not every effect of a trait is its function, that something must distinguish the heart's pumping blood from its producing audible thumps, that function attributions do explanatory work in molecular biology, in physiology and in evolutionary biology somewhat independently of one another, and that any adequate account needs a usable notion of malfunction or dysfunction. Find formulations that are neither vacuous nor quietly smuggled in from one side. Then give a table of the residual disagreement: for each crux, the etiologist's position phrased as she would phrase it, the causal-role theorist's position phrased as he would phrase it, and the case or evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about what that case shows recorded in advance. The cruxes I want covered include whether a trait with the right causal profile but no selection history has a function at all; whether relativising functions to a chosen explanatory project leaves the causal-role theorist able to say which capacities are the right ones; whether the selected-effects account can accommodate malfunction without circularity; and whether the relevant history is the history of genes, organisms, cell lineages or something else.

For the bearing cases, use both the standard ones — a spontaneously assembled duplicate of an organism with no ancestry, a trait that conferred an advantage when it first appeared but had not yet been selected, an old trait later co-opted for a new effect, an unselected by-product — and three I have designed, which I want treated as seriously as the classics: first, a synthetic cell built in the laboratory with reversed molecular handedness relative to all terrestrial life, which metabolises and divides indefinitely but whose entire lineage has no selection history and, by stipulation, no predators, hosts or ecological interactions of any kind; second, a somatic mutation that increases the rate at which a cell lineage within a tissue replaces its neighbours while degrading the tissue's barrier function, so that the trait looks like a function relative to the clone and a dysfunction relative to the organism; third, a gene whose product produced effect F for a hundred million years and was selected because of it, but which a recent regulatory change has rewired so that it now produces effect G and is retained because of G. Close with a short section addressed to me rather than to them: which parts of the dispute you judge substantive and which merely verbal, whether the two accounts are best read as competing analyses of one notion or as analyses of two notions that biology deploys for different purposes, and what, if anything, both parties could agree in advance would change their minds. Wherever you attribute a commitment to one side, mark clearly whether it is a commitment that side must accept or only one it tends to accept.
