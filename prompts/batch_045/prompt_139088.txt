I'm a journalist — I write mostly about science and ideas, which means I spend a lot of my week doing a very boring thing: taking some source's account, pulling out the bits that can be checked against documents or data or a third party, and checking them. I recently fell into reading about how historians do the same thing for material that's two and a half thousand years old (Herodotus happily passing along what he was told, Thucydides being fussy about provenance, that whole tradition of source criticism), and it made me notice a pattern in my own work that I don't have a word for.

Here's the pattern. A source's account mixes claims of two very different kinds. Some are auditable: dates, distances, serial numbers, who was in the room, what the weather did, which lab the sample went to. Others are structurally unauditable: what someone was thinking, what got said in a meeting of three people who all have an interest in the story, what the other side was planning. I check the auditable ones and they come back clean — often impressively clean, dozens of them — and I can feel my confidence in the unauditable ones go up, and my readers' confidence go up much more than mine does. Nothing about a clean set of dates bears on whether this person's account of the private meeting is true, and yet the credibility obviously travels. And there's a quiet reverse version: a source who is demonstrably wrong about one checkable detail gets treated as unreliable about everything, even when the error is the kind that a typo or a garbled secondhand report explains perfectly well.

There's a second version that I think might be the same animal. Some sources flag their own weak spots — I couldn't confirm this part, this bit is hearsay — and the flag reads as honesty and raises my estimate of the rest of what they say. But that feels buyable: a source could cheaply sprinkle in a couple of flagged or slightly self-damaging details and come out ahead overall. It reminds me of the criterion-of-embarrassment move my classicist friend described, where a detail that's inconvenient for the author is treated as likely genuine, except that there the flag is supposed to authenticate the flagged claim and here it seems to authenticate everything else.

People I've asked have gestured at the halo effect and at something called truth-default theory, but both feel too blunt: the halo effect doesn't have this crossing-of-claim-types structure, and truth-default theory seems to say we believe first and don't need the audited details at all, whereas in my case the audited details are definately doing real work. So: does this have a name, or a literature? Are the two versions one thing or two? I'm after the carving more than the label — what exactly the phenomenon is, where the legitimate inference from gets-the-small-stuff-right ends and the leak begins, what the boundary cases are (random errors versus systematic ones, vivid checkable detail versus dull checkable detail, a source who is right about the small stuff because they're careful versus because they simply had access), and whether there's anything a working journalist ought to do differently once they know about it. If there's no established term, coin one and define it sharply enough that I could tell an instance from a non-instance.
