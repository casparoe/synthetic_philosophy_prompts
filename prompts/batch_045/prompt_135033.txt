I work on the evaluation side of a small lab, and in our weekly alignment reading group we keep circling back to a move one of my colleagues makes whenever the conversation turns to how we would supervise systems that are better than we are at the task. He says that debate — the setup from Irving and colleagues' 2018 paper, in which two models take turns making short statements about a question or a proposed action and a human judge picks whichever one gave the more truthful and useful information — is really just cross-examination, and that we already possess a several-hundred-year-old institution for extracting reliable verdicts from two motivated advocates in front of a decider who could never have produced the answer himself: the adversarial trial. He treats that as the empirical case for debate and uses it to license a strong conclusion, namely that a debate-derived signal can serve as load-bearing oversight for superhuman systems, so that we do not need to solve interpretability or capability-limited evaluation before scaling. What makes me suspicious is that the paper's own argumentative anchor is a quite different analogy — the complexity-theoretic one, on which optimal debate with a polynomial-time judge reaches questions far outside what direct judging can reach — and those two pictures seem to me to rest on very different assumptions about the decider and to do very different argumentative work. I would like the audit before I say that out loud in the group.

So please lay the correspondence out explicitly, in a table if you like: what plays the role of the advocate, the client, the witness, the rules of evidence, discovery, the perjury sanction, the appeal to a higher court, the fact that a trial judge's ruling is backed by coercive enforcement, and whatever it is in a real trial that stops both advocates from quietly converging on the same confident falsehood. Then the places the mapping breaks. I have three candidates of my own and would like you to tell me which of them actually bear on the conclusion and which are cosmetic. First: in a trial the decider is resource-limited — competent in principle, short on time — and the process works largely by forcing the disagreement down to a small number of locally checkable factual questions, who signed what and who was where. A human overseeing a system that is superhuman at the task is competence-limited instead; the gap is not that I lack the hours to check the derivation but that I might not recognize a correct derivation if handed one. Cross-examination looks to me as though it presupposes the first kind of gap, and I do not know what remains of it under the second. Second, call it the buried-fact problem: a debater can assert something about a detail buried deep inside a long artifact — that one hunk of a very large diff introduces a race condition, that a particular unit deep in a network saturates on a particular input — and refuting the claim costs the opponent far more than asserting it, because the judge cannot afford to go and look. Trials have discovery, subpoenas, and shared court-appointed experts for precisely this; debate, so far as I can see, has only the statement limit. Third, the recursion worry: a debater's reward is a function of the judge's verdict, so a debater is strictly better off optimizing the judge than being correct whenever the two come apart, and in the deployments I actually care about the judge is not a fresh human each time but a model that was itself trained on debate verdicts produced in an earlier round, which means the neutral arbiter the analogy posits is partly an artifact of the process it is supposed to be checking.

What I want at the end is a verdict on the conclusion rather than on the picture. Which of these disanalogies, if any, does real damage to the claim that debate gives us an oversight signal we can lean on for systems that outclass their overseers, and which are noise that a mature version of the protocol would simply engineer around — for instance with a different turn structure, or with judges assisted by tools that let them check buried facts cheaply? Does the analogy survive repair, or was it carrying that conclusion the whole time? And if the trial is the wrong home, is the complexity-theoretic analogy from the paper a better one, or is there a third thing that fits better — a referee checking a proof they could not have written, a grading rubric, peer review, an audit — and what does each of those import that the others do not? Above all, I would like your view on the question underneath all of this: whether any of these pictures says anything about how the setup degrades as the capability gap widens, or whether there is a threshold past which the analogy stops describing anything at all, and if there is, whether anything in the protocol tells us where it falls.
