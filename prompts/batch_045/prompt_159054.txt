I teach a third-year seminar in contemporary political philosophy and I am setting the final take-home, due in seventy-two hours, length 1500 words. The students have worked through justice as fairness in detail: the original position and the veil of ignorance, the argument for maximin, the list of social primary goods, and the difference principle. I want one question that a student who has memorised all of it still cannot answer well without thinking, and I would rather it not be a question answerable by rehearsing the familiar egalitarian complaints about the view: the objection that the talented could not sincerely affirm the incentives the principle ends up licensing, the expensive-tastes line of criticism, luck-egalitarian scruples in general. The crux should be internal to the machinery: something that goes wrong, or is left undetermined, even granting the apparatus as it is standardly set up.

The case I have in mind, which you should use or sharpen if you can make the crux starker, is what I have been calling the bifurcation problem. In society S the least-advantaged stratum splits into two equal-sized subgroups. L are low-productivity workers with no special needs: whatever income they receive converts one-for-one into the advantages income normally buys. H have a chronic, expensive health condition, unchosen and not a matter of taste; managing it costs four units of income-equivalent, and if it goes unmanaged they function far below L at the same income, whereas if it is managed they function as L does. Two policies are feasible. Under P the bottom stratum receives a cash income of ten units and there is no in-kind provision: measured in income and wealth, the least advantaged get ten, and in effective terms L stand at ten and H at six. Under Q the bottom stratum receives a cash income of eight and the needed care is provided universally and in kind, funded out of general taxation: measured in income and wealth the least advantaged get eight, and in effective terms L and H both stand at eight. Read one way, Q is forbidden, because it lowers the share of primary goods held by the least advantaged from ten to eight. Read the other way, Q is mandatory, because it raises the position of whoever is worst off from six to eight. The two readings yield opposite deontic verdicts, and I want the question to make students feel the force of both.

So the question should press at least these things. First, how the least advantaged are to be identified: by an index of primary goods, or by what that share actually secures for a person given her circumstances. Second, whether the choice between those two ways of identifying them is itself a choice the parties in the original position can make, given that they know general facts about human psychology and sociology but not their own conception of the good, and given that primary goods are supposed to be all-purpose means whose value does not depend on any particular plan of life. Third, that the usual reason for indexing by resources rather than welfare, namely that people are responsible for their ends and justice should not compensate costly preferences, does not settle this case, since H need is neither chosen nor a preference; and that letting needs into the index seems to reintroduce exactly the dependence on a conception of the good that primary goods were meant to avoid, with the further complication that health is not on the list. The seminar has throughout adopted the stipulation that the parties represent normal cooperating members over a complete life and that disability and special medical need are bracketed as a problem to be handled later, at the legislative stage; a strong answer should say whether that bracketing is doing load-bearing work here and whether it can honestly be maintained.

Please give me: the question as it would appear on the paper, including whatever setup it needs and running to no more than 250 words; a model answer of 700 to 800 words; and a marking scheme out of twenty built around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each of them wrong. Add a short explicit statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer on this question, naming the move the A makes that the B does not.

On the model answer I want commitment, not survey. It must come down on one side: either a needs-sensitive or conversion-sensitive reading is correct and Q is what justice requires, or the apparatus has the resources to resolve the case without leaving the primary-goods index and Q is required for that reason, or the case is genuinely undetermined and the difference principle has to be supplemented from outside, in which case say precisely what a supplementary principle would have to supply and why the bracketing cannot be lifted. Whichever it is, it should defend that verdict against the strongest version of the rival reading rather than noting that the rival exists, and it should close by naming in one sentence what the verdict costs, that is, which attractive feature of the view has to be given up.

Two constraints. No citations, no secondary literature, no reading lists: everything has to be arguable from the setup in the question, and the model answer should reason from the case rather than from what anyone has written about Rawls, though it may describe the standard moves and objections in its own terms. And if you introduce any device of your own, a principle, a refinement of the index, a stipulated variant of the veil, name it as your own rather than as something the seminar has read.
