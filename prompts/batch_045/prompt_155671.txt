I'm putting together a reading-group session on immigration — about twelve people, mostly engineers plus one lawyer, no philosophers — and I want to end it with a 'which position do you actually hold?' quiz instead of another hour of people repeating the words 'open borders' at each other. Can you build me one?

What I want is a decision tree over the logical space, not a list of -isms: start from the questions that actually split people, then locate the real positions in the space those questions open up. The splits I'd start with are: whether there is a moral right to cross a border at all; whether such a right, if it exists, includes access to the welfare state or only residence and work; whether the case for keeping borders closed is fundamentally about protecting redistributive institutions or about something cultural, national or associational; whether admission may be conditioned on integration and how thick that conditioning gets to be; whether numbers matter as such — thresholds, tipping points, institutional capacity — or only through their consequences; and whether the effects on the countries people leave are part of the argument at all. Add splits I've missed, and drop any of mine that turns out not to separate anything.

I definately want these leaves, whatever you end up calling them: full open borders with equal access to benefits; open borders to residence and work but migrants mostly excluded from the welfare state for a long period; the view that a political community may decide its own membership but owes generous admission and serious refugee duties anyway; restrictionism whose whole case is that generous redistribution needs bounded membership; the 'club' or freedom-of-association version; the consequentialist version where the objection runs through effects on sending countries and brain drain rather than through anything the host country is owed; integration-conditional admission, split into thick and thin versions if that distinction earns its place on the tree; and whatever is left for a 'no states, no borders' position. I'd also like a leaf for a scheme I made up for the session and can't find anyone defending: entitlements attached to persons rather than territories, so a migrant keeps drawing a reduced version of their origin country's benefits for, say, ten years, funded by a surcharge on their host-country earnings, and opening the border therefore doesn't automatically mean drawing on the host's redistribution. If that cell really is empty, say whether it's incoherent, administratively hopeless, or merely unoccupied. Give each leaf a descriptive label — only attach a thinker's name if you're confident ('roughly the view defended by X' is fine), don't guess — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one case it handles badly.

Set every case in a made-up country: Vandia, rich, 12 million people, generous welfare state, ageing population, so we don't spend the evening arguing about real politics. Each question should be a concrete case a non-philosopher can answer without knowing any labels, and I want at least these among them: an unskilled 55-year-old from a much poorer country who will be a net recipient for the rest of his life; a wealthy retiree who will never claim anything; a surgeon leaving a country with a severe doctor shortage who also sends money home to fund a clinic; a guest worker admitted for five years with no vote, no benefits and no route to citizenship; a migrant who obeys every law but rejects the host society's norms outright and lives in a linguistic enclave; a family-reunification case where the citizen's spouse comes from the poorer country; an unauthorized migrant who has lived and worked there for fifteen years; and a counterfactual in which Vandia has no welfare state at all — is the border still justified? I'd also like two questions that aren't about individual people: one where the flow jumps from 50,000 a year to 2 million overnight (does any leaf that said yes at 50,000 have to change its answer, and if so is that a threshold effect or plain consequentialism?), and one about whether admission may be sold.

Finish with a scoring key that routes patterns of answers to leaves — nothing should ask the reader which label they like; the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, with your diagnosis of why; the pairs of leaves your questions can't actually separate, together with the extra case that would separate them; and a short note on where the quiz is weakest, i.e. the place where two very different-sounding positions collapse into the same answers. Nine or ten questions is about right, keep the prose plain, and if a question only works for someone who already knows the literature, cut it.
