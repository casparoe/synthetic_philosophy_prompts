I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on epistemology — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, a whiteboard, index cards, and a projector I would rather not have to use. Roughly half of them have worked through the opening remarks of Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*; the other half are enrolled because the module required it and have read nothing at all. I want a structured activity, not a lecture, that makes them feel the force of the hinge thought before anybody names it: the words "hinge," "riverbed," "*On Certainty*," and "Wittgenstein" should not be spoken until the debrief.

What I have in mind, roughly: set them arguing about some ordinary, apparently contestable empirical claim — I will leave the choice to you, but it should be something about which real disagreement is possible and evidence genuinely relevant, not a Cartesian demon and not a brain in a vat — and run a regimented regress. If you challenge a claim you must supply a reason; a respondent may then demand a reason for that reason; no claim already in the chain may be reused; and when the chain stalls, the group may, by unanimous vote, declare the stalled claim load-bearing, write it on the board, and thereafter may not use it as a reason for anything else. On top of that I want a second mechanism: a few students privately receive a card instructing them to genuinely doubt one of the claims the group has written up and to keep participating normally while doing so. My hope is that they discover, by doing it, that their doubt has nowhere to go — that it is not answered but also not raisable, and that the group's response is not an argument but a kind of refusal. Then a swap: groups exchange boards and are asked to run the same argument using the other group's load-bearing claims while treating their own as unavailable.

Three things I would like you to solve rather than hand back to me. First, the grammar. At some point participants should be made to describe their own load-bearing claims in the first person, and the available forms should misfit: "I know that..." implies grounds and the possibility of error; "I believe that..." implies that it could be otherwise and that one has reasons; "I am certain that..." implies a doubt that has been overcome. Design the mechanism so they feel this squeeze in their own mouths rather than being told about it. Second, the incentives. I once tried a version with credibility tokens — demanding a justification cost the respondent a token if they stalled, declaring a claim load-bearing cost three — and it backfired: students concluded that load-bearing claims are simply the cheap exit from the game of reasons, a strategic way to stop arguing, which is nearly the opposite of the point. If the activity has any scoring or cost structure at all, engineer it so the opposite lesson is unavoidable; if the right design has no economy whatsoever, tell me why. Third, the ending. I would like them to leave having produced something — one sentence each, perhaps, sorted into piles — but I do not want a tidy taxonomy that lets them file hinge propositions as merely a curious species of belief.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto specific features of the idea, and should say what will predictably go wrong when I run it. I expect at least one student to reconstruct the whole exercise as foundationalism, one to withdraw into silence, and one to try to win; tell me how to handle each. Please also be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce. My particular worry is that by making participants articulate and vote on their load-bearing claims, the activity makes hinge-taking look like a deliberate, propositional, optional act, when the point is that we absorb these things long before we can formulate them and that they are manifest in what we do rather than in what we are prepared to assert. If the activity cannot be made to show that, say so plainly and tell me what I will have to supply myself. Assume a room with no web access and no assigned reading beyond what participants happen to have done: no citations, no bibliography, no further-reading list, and no need to quote Wittgenstein verbatim — paraphrase is fine, and probably safer.
