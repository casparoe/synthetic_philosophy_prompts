I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the indeterminacy of radical translation, and I'd rather start from my verdicts on cases than from a theory, because the theories I know all seem to me to buy their neatness by lying about one case or another. The question I care about is narrow: when, if ever, is there a fact of the matter about what a speaker's term means, and what makes it a fact?

Here are my verdicts. In the base case — the familiar field-linguist scenario, a community whose members say *gavagai* when a rabbit scurries past and dissent from it in the presence of everything else I can think to test — I think there is no fact of the matter among *rabbit*, *undetached rabbit part*, *temporal stage of a rabbit*, and *the scattered fusion of all rabbits*. And I mean this metaphysically, not epistemically: I don't want to say that there is a truth we can't reach. I want to say that nothing in the world settles it.

Second case. The community has a full counting apparatus — numerals, plurals, a distinction between one and many — and they count three animals in a clearing as three *gavagai*, call the same animal a year later the same *gavagai*, deny *gavagai* of a severed leg, and say of a roadkilled animal that it *was gavagai*. Here my gut says the tie is broken: the word means *rabbit*, the persisting animal. I know the obvious rejoinder — that each of these verdicts about counting and reidentification can be absorbed by adjusting the auxiliary apparatus rather than the term — and I still don't believe it.

Third case. A community whose grammar for the term is exclusively mass-like: no numerals, only measure phrases, more and less; they assent to *gavagai* when I point at a tail, and treat two animals' pelts heaped together as the same *gavagai* again. My verdict: determinate, and it means rabbit-stuff rather than rabbit.

Fourth case. Two communities, Alpha and Beta, indistinguishable by any test on speech dispositions I can devise, including the counting and reidentification tests above. But we have neural readouts: in Alpha the term's production is triggered by a whole-animal detector with identity-tracking routines; in Beta, by a temporal-slice detector with no such routines. My verdict: determinate, and they mean different things — Alpha means *rabbit*, Beta means *rabbit-stage*. I recognize that this makes me an internalist about meaning in this case.

Fifth case. A community, Gamma, whose readouts show a single detector that fires indiscriminately for rabbits and for the local hares, with nothing downstream that distinguishes them, and whose usage covers both without distinction. My verdict: determinate, and what it means is the coarse property — the local lagomorph, rabbit-or-hare. Note that I am not saying "indeterminate between *rabbit* and *hare*." I want determinacy of the coarse reading, not indeterminacy between the fine ones.

Sixth case. A community internally and behaviorally identical to Alpha, but situated where the local rabbit-lookalikes are a wholly separate lineage — convergent mimics, different ancestry and chemistry, no interbreeding with our rabbits. My verdict, held with much less confidence: their word means the mimics, not rabbits. I notice that this sits badly with my verdict in the fourth case, and I don't know how to have both.

Seventh case. A community half of whom are hooked up whole-animal-wise and half stage-wise, distributed so that no behavioral evidence could ever reveal it, because the difference never makes a difference, and with no social fact about who counts as an authority on the word. I am genuinely torn here between "the term is indeterminate" and "the term is homonymous — two terms that happen to sound alike," and I lean toward the first without being able to say what distinguishes the two.

My tempting one-liner is: a term's meaning is determinate just in case some fact about the speakers — internal or environmental — discriminates among the candidate interpretations; where nothing discriminates, there is no fact, not merely an unknown one. I suspect this one-liner is either trivial or false, and that cases one, four and six are where it breaks.

What I'd like from you: state the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, precisely, and say which clause of it each of my cases is doing the work of motivating. Then tell me what it predicts about the cases I haven't listed — about *rabbit* in my own mouth, where the same arguments seem to apply and where I have a strong verdict anyway; about a trained artificial system that emits some internal label for rabbit images, for hare images, and for rabbit-shaped shadows, where the label's causal history is fully known to us and partly known to nothing; and about translating into a target language that has no count nouns and no singular terms at all, where I want to know whether indeterminacy is a property of the pair of languages or of the source term alone. Say which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn, and say honestly whether what you end up with is a thesis about meaning, about reference, or only about translation manuals — and, if it is the last, what the linguist should actually write down.
