I write the interpretive text for the natural history galleries of a medium-sized regional museum. That means roughly forty object labels a year, sixty words or fewer each, every one of them signed off by our curator of geology, who has no training in evolutionary biology and who is, on principle, hostile to any sentence that sounds hedged. The labels are permanent, they are read by around two hundred thousand people a year, and our funders particularly like the panels that say what a trait is *for*. A complaint from a visiting evolutionary biologist — that a good half of our why-it-evolved panels were speculative stories dressed up as findings — has been upheld by the director, and I have been asked to produce a written procedure that my team can run on any proposed trait-and-function claim before it goes into a label.

Please write that procedure for me. What I need is something runnable in about fifteen minutes per label by someone with school-level biology, working from whatever brief accompanies the specimen: a sequence of questions asked in a fixed order, what counts as an acceptable answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops and returns not established, and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question has become one of values rather than fact.

The conceptual material I want it answerable to is the critique of the adaptationist programme associated with Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper — not as a slogan I can cite, but as something that changes what ends up on the wall. Specifically, I want it to do the following.

Interrogate the individuation of the trait before anything else. Most of our bad panels, I think, fail here: the trait is picked out by the very effect it is supposed to explain, so the claim cannot be tested and cannot fail. Give me a question that catches this, phrased so that a non-specialist can apply it, and say what we do with a proposal whose trait dissolves under it.

Make the alternatives explicit and symmetric. A proposal should not be accepted unless at least one non-adaptive alternative has been stated with comparable specificity: an architecturally necessary by-product of some other feature, the co-option of an existing structure for a new function, inheritance from an ancestor with no current function, a developmental or allometric consequence of body size or growth pattern, drift, or the possibility that the trait has no non-arbitrary boundaries at all. Give me this as a menu with a rule for how many alternatives must be written down and how specific they must be.

Grade the kinds of evidence that can discriminate between them, and say what each kind can and cannot establish: correlation between independent origins of the trait and the supposed selective regime across related lineages, experimental manipulation of the trait or of the environment, direct measurement of selection in the field, and fit to an engineering or optimality model. Include what to do when the brief contains none of these and only reports what a handbook says.

Separate three claims that our current labels all collapse into one: that the trait originated by selection for function F, that it is currently maintained by selection for F, and that it is currently useful for F although it arose for some other reason or for no reason at all. Each should license a different wording, and I want the wording rule stated, together with two or three model sentences of agnostic copy that my curator will actually pass.

Operate inside sixty words. Where the honest verdict is that we do not know, tell me what the label should say instead of the explanation it cannot give.

Include a check on the set as well as on the item. Individually defensible labels can still add up to a gallery that presents organisms as collections of optimally engineered devices, and that is the substance of the complaint against us. Give me a way to audit a batch of labels: how to sample or count them, what pattern triggers a revision, and what the revision consists of.

Mark clearly what the curator is being asked to certify at each kind of outcome, and where the procedure must stop because the question has stopped being factual — in particular where the risk of overclaiming to a quarter of a million visitors has to be weighed against the cost of a hedge that nobody reads.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, a label on the dark form of the peppered moth saying it evolved to match soot-blackened bark, with a brief noting that dark forms appeared independently in many industrial regions. Second, a label on the human chin, with two competing briefs: one saying it evolved to withstand the mechanical stresses of chewing, one saying it evolved as a signal in mate choice. Third, the case I will not like: our most successful temporary format, a Survival gallery of twenty-five specimens in which each one gets a why-it-evolved panel, researched to the standard of a popular science book and much loved by the funders. Apply the procedure to the format as a whole and tell me what it does to it.

Finally, say honestly what the procedure cannot do: the cases where the underlying biology is unsettled in ways a fifteen-minute check will never reach, and the whole regions of our collection — early hominins, deep time, behaviour that leaves no fossil trace — where the procedure will systematically return not established and the gallery will end up saying nothing at all.

Do not give me references, citations or a reading list; my team has no library access and cannot follow them up, and the specimen brief is all we will ever have. Please write the whole answer in British English.
