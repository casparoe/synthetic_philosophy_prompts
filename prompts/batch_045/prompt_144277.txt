I would like the 1938 CBS broadcast of *The War of the Worlds* — Orson Welles's *Mercury Theatre on the Air* adaptation of H. G. Wells, aired live at 8 pm on October 30, scripted by Howard Koch, its first half cast as news bulletins interrupting dance-band music, its first commercial break coming roughly thirty minutes in, and its close broken by an out-of-character sign-off — analyzed through the three-part hierarchy of messages that Hofstadter develops in the "Location of Meaning" chapter of *Gödel, Escher, Bach*: the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message, where the frame tells a receiver that there is a message here and to look for a decoder, the outer message supplies the conventions and machinery needed to decode, and the inner message is the content thereby recovered.

Two cautions before the analysis. First, I know the standard story about mass panic and the standard revisionism: the scale of the panic is genuinely disputed, and the popular explanation that listeners had switched over from a more popular NBC program mid-musical-interlude and thereby missed the introduction is now thought to account for very few cases. I do not want the analysis to rest on either claim. Treat the episode as a documented case in which a nontrivial number of receivers, entering a signal at different points, assigned it to the wrong frame, and analyze the structure of that misassignment rather than its demographics. Second, if you think any fact I have asserted above is wrong, say so explicitly rather than building on it.

What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions, and I care much less about a verdict on Welles than about what the case does to the theory. Specifically:

**The mapping.** Identify, item by item, what in and around the broadcast does frame work, what does outer-message work, and what the inner message is. My candidates for frame carriers include the opening monologue adapted from the novel, the printed radio listings for that evening, the standing identity of a weekly dramatic anthology series, the network and time slot, the acoustic and grammatical conventions of the bulletin format itself, and the sign-off. Rank these by how much frame load each actually bears and say which are doing no work at all. Then say precisely what went wrong, in the theory's own terms: was this a failure to receive the frame message, a failure of the outer message, or something the three-layer scheme has no name for?

**The split I suspect the case forces.** On Hofstadter's telling, the frame message is the one that is understood automatically when you encounter an object with intelligible structure — you cannot miss *that* there is a message to decode, though you may lack the machinery to decode it. But the broadcast seems to show that the automatic part is only the bare existence claim, while the claim about *which* frame governs the object is not automatic at all and is exactly what failed. In his central examples — an inscription, a phonograph record recovered from a ruin — the two coincide, which I suspect is why the conflation is invisible. Please tell me whether the case really does force a distinction between a frame-existence message and a frame-governance message, whether that distinction can be made to do any work, or whether it collapses back into the outer message on inspection. If you think my way of drawing the split is wrong, draw it better.

**Frame capture.** The bulletins were engineered to reproduce, as inner content, precisely the cues that ordinarily carry the outer message in radio: the interruption convention, the register of official announcement, the names of real towns and real institutions. The consequence is that the disclaimer travels on the same channel, through the same machinery, as the material it is meant to frame, and is therefore absorbable as content — a receiver who has already taken the bulletins as news can hear the framing announcement as itself part of the drama, as an announcement read under duress or as official reassurance. Does this show that the frame message is not a message in the signal at all but a state of the decoder, pushing Hofstadter's own record-player point further than he pushes it, and what does that commit us to about where meaning is located? Can a frame be constructed that resists capture, or must any frame share a channel with what it frames and therefore remain capturable in principle?

**What the theory recommends and where it is silent.** Given that a frame must reach each receiver at the point where that receiver enters, what does the theory say the broadcasters should have done — where should the frame have been placed, and how often? If the theory has no resources for a design recommendation like this, say that it is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

**What the case does to the theory.** I have a candidate repair rather than a refutation: that frame stability is a function of the frame's being carried in channels distinct from the content and repeated at every entry point, so that a single top-of-program paratext is the weakest possible design. Assess whether that repair is consistent with Hofstadter's conclusion that meaning resides in the message plus the decoding machinery, or whether it quietly costs him the claim that the frame message is automatic. Then tell me what the three-layer model cannot say here: whether it has any vocabulary for a signal deliberately engineered to defeat frame assignment, for a population of decoders with heterogeneous machinery and different entry times, for graded rather than binary frame uptake, or for the way a wrongly assigned frame becomes self-sealing, absorbing subsequent counter-evidence as content instead of as correction. Note also the asymmetry the theory must explain: most receivers got the frame right. Finally, if the diagnosis generalizes, say briefly what a frame-captured genome would look like, given that Hofstadter extends the same apparatus to the cell reading its own instructions — and mark plainly where you are extrapolating beyond what the case supports.
