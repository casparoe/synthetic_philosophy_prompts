I am trying to state precisely a view I keep defending in arguments about suffering, and I want to know whether my own verdicts on cases actually add up to it. The view is a lexical threshold form of negative utilitarianism: every experience has a hedonic magnitude, pleasures positive and pains negative, and magnitudes of the two kinds are comparable; but there is a threshold T such that no finite collection of experiences each individually below T, however numerous and however distributed, can outweigh a single pain at or above T. I also want to keep three further commitments: an asymmetry below the threshold (a pain of magnitude x is not fully offset by a pleasure of magnitude x); a ceiling on pleasure (no pleasure, however intense, compensates an at-threshold pain); and the claim that what matters is the intrinsic character of each experience, not its causal history. Below are the cases on which my verdicts feel settled. I suspect at least two of them cannot both survive, and I want to know which one goes.

1. Ten million people each enjoy one genuinely good minute on a summer afternoon. One person spends forty seconds in full-thickness burn pain before analgesia takes effect. Verdict: the burn is worse; no number of good minutes compensates it. Confident.

2. The same forty-second burn versus ten million people each with a mild headache for an hour (2/10, no nausea, no photophobia). Verdict: the burn is still worse, but I hold it far less confidently, and I notice that if the number were a trillion I would begin to wobble. I am unsure whether that wobble is a confusion or a sign that T is doing all the work in case 1 as well.

3. Concentration. A: one person, ten minutes of pain at intensity 9.5 (an unreduced fractured femur, no analgesia). B: six hundred people, each one second of pain at the same intensity 9.5, then nothing. Verdict: A is worse, and worse by more than the ratio of duration-times-person explains; I would pay a great deal more to prevent A than to prevent B, and the two do not feel like different quantities of the same thing.

4. One person, one second at intensity 9.5, versus the same six hundred people at one second each. Verdict: the six hundred case is plainly much worse; I count seconds and multiply without hesitation, and if I extend the single person from one second toward ten minutes I cross over B somewhere. So I aggregate within the lexical tier and also charge a premium for concentration. Those two moves pull against each other, and I suspect the resolution is that a one-second pain of nominally high intensity was never really at T at all, in which case my intensity-at-an-instant currency is the wrong one.

5. Relief. An itch at 2/10, scratched after ninety seconds, the scratch being a pleasure at 3/10. Verdict: the scratch genuinely cancels the itch, leaving the episode mildly positive. Now the same 3/10 pleasure arriving as a cool breeze to someone who was never itchy: verdict, it cancels nothing, because nothing is there to be cancelled. And comparing a person who itches and never scratches with a person who never itches and gets the breeze: verdict, the first is worse off, and the breeze does no offsetting work whatsoever, even though the scratch and the breeze are, I stipulate, identical as pleasures in quality and magnitude. Cancellation therefore seems to require that the pleasure be counterfactually dependent on the discomfort, which sits badly with my commitment to intrinsic phenomenal magnitudes.

6. Whose pleasure. Three hours of a 3/10 toothache. If the same person had a lovely afternoon of comparable magnitude earlier that day, I feel some real partial offset, and I am willing to call the day acceptable. If a stranger had that afternoon, I feel no offset at all. The intrinsic experiences are identical in the two arrangements. Confident in both verdicts, unsure they are coherent.

7. Engineered pleasure. A subject wired to a stimulator that produces an hour of pleasure at an intensity beyond anything natural, say 20/10 on a scale where the burn is 9.5. Verdict: I still will not let any quantity of it compensate the forty-second burn, and I would prevent the burn in preference to providing the pleasure. But that suggests the discontinuity is not a comparison of magnitudes with a cliff at T; it looks more like an absolute ceiling on the compensating power of pleasure, a different structure that merely agrees with the threshold clause in ordinary cases.

The case I go back and forth on: a 1 percent chance of the forty-second burn versus certainty of a 6/10 pain for three days. Expected-magnitude reasoning says the second is far worse. A lexical view seems to make any nonzero probability of crossing T catastrophic, which I do not accept; but I also do not want to say the 1 percent chance barely counts, since at some probability it clearly dominates. Relatedly, I do not know what to say about a 6.9/10 pain lasting an hour with brief spikes to 7.1/10, given T at 7: do the spikes convert the whole hour into a lexical item, or only the spikes themselves?

What I want from you:

First, one or two principles stated precisely, clause by clause, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, including which of my verdicts each clause is there to save.

Second, an honest accounting: which of my verdicts each candidate preserves, which it forces me to give up, and the cost in complexity of the version that saves everything. I would rather give up a verdict than accept a five-clause principle, but I want to see both options side by side.

Third, whether pleasures and pains remain on a single scale once the ceiling on pleasure is in place, or whether the honest conclusion is that they are commensurable only within a bounded region and incomparable outside it, and if so what becomes of the hedonic zero and of the asymmetry clause.

Fourth, what the account says about two cases I have not listed: many people each at exactly T versus one person far above T; and a ten-minute agony the subject knows will end in ten minutes versus an identical ten-minute agony the subject believes will continue for years.

Fifth, what T is a threshold in: instantaneous intensity, episode-level badness, the subject's counterfactual preferences, or something else, and whether it is plausibly a single point or a band.
