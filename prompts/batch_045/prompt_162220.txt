I do cat modelling at a reinsurer — I build the stochastic models that price hurricane and earthquake tail risk. Basically my whole working life is aggregate exceedance curves, return periods, a Pareto tail index, and the awkward fact that once that index gets near 1 the mean of the fitted loss distribution blows up and stops being something anyone will underwrite to, so we switch to quantiles, spectral risk measures, ruin probabilities. And when there's genuine model uncertainty — vendors disagreeing about the physics, say — we don't pretend it's just parameter noise; we credibility-weight across the models, and if the spread is bad enough we decline to price off the mean at all.

I've been reading aobut the longtermist side of effective altruism, and I want it in my language. The claim as I understand it: for basically any intervention you could fund, the expected value is dominated by its effect on the astronomically many people who might exist in the far future, so reducing existential risk swamps everything else by orders of magnitude, and you should be kind of fanatical about it. Then the pushback I half-recall from a comment thread: the simulation argument (the one with three disjuncts, where either we die out before running ancestor-simulations, or nobody runs many, or we're almost certainly in one) is supposed to take a bite out of that — if we're probably simulated, then maybe there's no real far future for us to influence, or our sim just gets switched off, and also apparently most simulated minds might live in short cheap simulations that don't have much of a future at all, so the probability that there's an astronomical future we can actually steer is meaningfully below 1 and the expected value gets multiplied down. Someone also said it cuts the other way, since if sims are cheap then most minds are simulated minds and there are way more moral patients than I assumed.

Can you restate both the longtermist argument and the simulation-based dampener as faithfully as you can as if they were two structural models sitting in a cat modelling suite — event sets, exceedance curves, mixture weights, credibility weighting, tail behaviour, capital allocation, portfolio effects — and then give me the residue report. Be honest about where you had to fudge; don't just glue my vocabulary onto their conclusion and call it a translation.

Three things I specifically want in the residue report. First: my instinct is that this is just model uncertainty, put a mixture weight on simulated vs base reality, take the posterior mean, done — but I have a feeling that's smuggling something, because what's being updated isn't a model of how the world generates losses, it's a claim about which world in the ensemble I'm standing inside. Is mixture weight the wrong object, and does that break the analogy or break their argument? Second: in my job I've got a few thousand risks in a portfolio and diversification plus the law of large numbers plus reinsurance saves me from ever really living in the tail — here there's exactly one future, one shot, no cedent to share it with. Does anything in their reasoning secretly depend on a portfolio that doesn't exist? Third: what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? The thing that keeps bugging me is that writing a new life and preventing a death are completely different lines in my models, but the far future is mostly people who don't exist yet, so I can't tell whether an uncreated person is exposure I failed to protect or new business I never wrote. Same for whatever this moral realism thing is that everyone seems to quietly assume. Is that a gap in my framework, or a sign their claims are murkier than the expected-value spreadsheet makes them look?
