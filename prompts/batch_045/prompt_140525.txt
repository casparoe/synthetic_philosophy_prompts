I'm revising a paper on whether death can be bad for the person who dies, and before I commit myself to the package in print I'd like someone to audit it. Individually the pieces strike me as defensible; I can't tell whether they're jointly, and my supervisor and I disagree about where the fault is (she thinks it's in (3), I think it's in (7), and I suspect we're both looking in the wrong place). Here is the position as I would state it:

(1) I accept Epicurus's negative premise in full. Being dead is not a condition that anyone is ever in, and nothing can be good or bad for a subject who does not exist. So I reject any account that locates the evil of death in what it is like — or in what it would be like — to be dead.

(2) I accept deprivationism in its objective form: what makes death bad for the one who dies is the goods of continued life of which it deprives her — the experiences, projects, relationships and achievements she would have had — and not the frustration of any desire of hers. I also want this badness to be scalar and additive: dying at thirty is worse for you than dying at eighty because more good life is taken away, and the difference in badness is nothing over and above the difference in the goods.

(3) I accept Lucretius's symmetry argument. The age before I existed and the age after I cease to exist are not, considered in themselves, relevantly different, so the asymmetry has to come from somewhere else, and I don't think the familiar candidates supply it. In particular I reject the desire-direction reply — that our desires simply run forward in time and not backward — since that seems to me to explain a feature of our psychology rather than the fact I want explained. My own proposal, which I call the interruption account, is that my death is bad for me and my prenatal nonexistence is not because my death is an event in the history of somebody: it stops a life that has already got under way, and it is the sort of thing a person can foresee, act to prevent, be wronged by, or compare her situation to. There was no one for my not yet having been born to happen to, and in that case nothing got cut short. On this picture, the badness of death is the badness of a life's being interrupted.

(4) Some case verdicts I don't want to surrender: (a) a man killed instantly and painlessly by a falling meteorite, with no warning and no opportunity to do anything about it, is gravely harmed by his death; (b) a two-year-old with severe cognitive disabilities, who has no conception of a future and no desires about one, is gravely harmed by dying, and more gravely harmed than an eighty-year-old who dies at the same moment; (c) it is not a misfortune to me — not to any degree — that I was conceived ten years later than I might have been, even if I would then have had ten additional years of good life.

(5) I want to keep something like the Epicurean practical conclusion: dread of one's own death is, in some important sense, to be cured rather than indulged. And I want to keep this while conceding that death really is bad for the person who dies.

(6) I reject the standard reconciliation of (1) and (2), the ante-mortem harm reply. I deny that the harm of death has to be located at a time at all, and I specifically reject the ante-mortem account because it makes the magnitude of the harm hostage to the mental life of the living person — as though death were less bad for someone who never learns it is coming, or for someone who has stopped caring whether she goes on.

(7) I would also like to say that dying young is worse than the lost years alone explain. A life cut off in the middle of its undertakings is a worse loss than one that has run its course, even holding fixed how much good life remains, and this is part of why the death of the young strikes us as a catastrophe of a different order.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like it; rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; for each, say precisely what I would have to give up and what the cheapest revision would be; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the mess at once. Where a tension is only apparent, say what distinction dissolves it, and be explicit if keeping my resolution of it requires me to pay for it somewhere else on the list.
