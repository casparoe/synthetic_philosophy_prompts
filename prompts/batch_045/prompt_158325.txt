You are a political theorist who has published on democratic reform and who also writes satirical pieces for a magazine of ideas. Please work carefully, and start with a short private brainstorming pass before you draft: list eight to ten genuine, widely discussed features of lottery-selected deliberative bodies — how members are drawn, who sets the question, how evidence reaches them, how disagreement among them is handled, what becomes of the recommendations — and beside each, in one line, name the objection that feature invites. Use that list only as scaffolding. None of those objections may be stated anywhere in the document you then write.

The commission:

Three weeks ago I was drawn in the first lottery for the Sunbury Citizens' Chamber, my state's replacement for the upper house, and I take my seat in six weeks. The orientation packet that arrived is written in the cheerful register of corporate human resources, and reading it has convinced me that the interesting thing about a lottery legislature is not the lottery. I would like you to write a satirical version of that packet: the "Questions Newly Selected Members Ask Most Often" section of the Chamber's Member Handbook, 800–1,000 words, with a letterhead, a two-sentence preamble, and ten to twelve numbered question-and-answer items whose headings are phrased as the questions a new member actually asks — "What will I be deciding?", "What if I already know something about this?", "Can I talk to the others between sessions?", "What happens afterwards?"

The venue is an official plain-language guide; the voice is warm, competent, and entirely unembarrassed. No one in the document should be a villain, and nothing should be a scandal: the dread should come from the fact that every arrangement described is reasonable and well administered. Keep the deadpan absolute — no winking, no ironic italics, no aside to the reader, no closing twist that breaks character. It should make a reader who knows the sortition literature laugh in recognition and still be legible and funny to a reader who has never heard of sortition: do not explain sortition anywhere, name no real assembly, organization, or person, and quote no real document.

Ground the piece in the following actual features of our Chamber. You may exaggerate them and you should add two or three inventions of your own in the same spirit, but do not replace them:

1. Members are drawn by stratified lot from the electoral roll — 180 seats, three overlapping cohorts of sixty, eighteen months each, non-renewable. The sample's demographic legibility is treated as a formal property of the Chamber: an absence unbalances it, so an excused member is replaced by a demographically matched reserve.
2. The questions the Chamber may address are set by an appointed Convening Panel, in the exact form posed. The Chamber may add no question of its own, and a schedule of Matters of Reserved Competence — taxation, treaties, anything with a budget line above a stated threshold — is withdrawn from its remit.
3. All evidence reaches members through the Official Briefing. Members sign a Frame Integrity Undertaking not to consult outside material bearing on the question; suspected breaches go to Frame Contamination Review. The Office of Neutral Framing writes the Briefing, pre-clears the options that may appear on the ballot, and costs them.
4. Expert witnesses appear in matched pairs. Where no counter-witness exists, one is commissioned; where none can be commissioned, the testimony is designated Single-Testimony and members are instructed to weigh it with correspondingly reduced confidence.
5. Members who have professional expertise bearing on the question are, in order to protect the ordinariness of the sample, recused from substantive participation and reassigned to the Witness Liaison tier.
6. Because sortition is meant to end careerism, a member may not stand for election for six years after service. The Alumni Register and the Certified Deliberator credential, however, permit a former member to return as a paid facilitator, briefing reviewer, or Civic Ambassador. Attendance, recusals, and Deliberative Integrity Scores all bear on standing in the Register.
7. Deliberation is measured. Each member completes a values inventory at the opening and close of each question; the Deliberative Integrity Score combines opinion movement, recorded as "openness," with airtime equity and fidelity to Briefing material. Members whose views do not move are flagged as non-deliberative and receive additional facilitation. Speaking time is allocated per member and may not be yielded, pooled, or transferred. Persuasion outside scheduled sessions is Prohibited Deliberative Contact. Tables are re-sorted weekly, and members may not sit with anyone they knew before selection.
8. Recommendations go to the Convening Panel, which must respond in writing within twelve months with one of three dispositions: accept, accept in part, or note.

Two further constraints. First, the target is the practice and its rhetoric, not any person: the piece should land the standing worries about lottery government — where the power goes once the members are random, who frames the question and the options, what competence is and who is disqualified for having it, whether accountability has been abolished or merely relocated, whether randomization ends careerism or professionalizes it differently, and what a recommendation is worth — by embodying each in a procedure rather than mentioning any of them. Second, give the piece no narrative: no member rebels, no facilitator sneers, nothing is resolved. It is a document, and it should end as a document ends — with, say, an invitation to contact the Member Experience Team.
