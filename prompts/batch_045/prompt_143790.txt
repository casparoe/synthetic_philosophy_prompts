You are a philosopher of biology who has also spent time at the bench in origins-of-life chemistry and who advises classification and biosafety bodies for a living. Think carefully and show your work: before you commit to anything, spend a few hundred words enumerating the candidate rules the body might be running and testing each one against every precedent below — a table or numbered list is fine — and only then commit. I would rather have the reasoning than a confident bottom line.

I staff, and take the minutes for, a standing working group at a national funding agency that has to rule on borderline cases of "life" for exactly one downstream purpose: whether a project counts as creating a new form of life de novo, which triggers a separate ethics review on top of the usual approvals. Containment is decided separately and deliberately does not track the life rulings, so please don't drift into biosafety advice. Our precedents, from about six years of minutes, are: a mule (sterile adult mammal) — alive; a bacterial endospore revived from a sealed ampoule decades old — alive; a tardigrade with no detectable metabolism in cryptobiosis — alive; an influenza virion — not alive, while being explicitly flagged as still subject to containment; a prion — not alive; a self-propagating oxidation front creeping through a mine tailings pile — not alive; a self-replicating program in a sandbox — not alive, on the stated ground that it is not a chemical system; and, on a 4–3 vote with a splenetic dissent, a cell-free self-replicating RNA system with supplied building blocks, no membrane and no protein enzymes, which nonetheless shows heritable variation and adaptation over serial transfers — ruled not alive, "a replicating molecule, not an organism," with a written opinion I have never been able to reconstruct. For completeness, the two definitions that come up most in our discussions are the working definition adopted by a NASA committee attempting to define life for the purposes of exobiology, based on a suggestion by Carl Sagan: "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution" — which has the well-known awkwardness that a single sexually reproducing individual, or a mule, would fail it — and Tibor Gánti's chemoton criterion from his 1971 book The Principles of Life, on which the minimal unit of life needs metabolism, self-replication, and an enclosing lipid membrane as three separate subsystems, so that a system with any two is at best a potential living thing.

The new case is a hypothetical I have written myself for this exercise; it is not a real experiment, so please don't try to recall it, and don't import details I haven't given. Call it System K: micron-scale peptide–nucleotide coacervate droplets, built in the lab from purified chemicals three years ago and since maintained by serial transfer, with no ancestry in any existing organism. They hold their internal composition far from equilibrium by consuming an external chemical fuel, and they catalyze some of their own components from simple precursors, but the peptide and lipid building blocks themselves are supplied in the medium; they cannot make them. They grow and split, with the invagination driven by internal osmotic pressure but the actual scission depending on shear from the stirrer, so division is partly endogenous and partly imposed. Heredity is compositional rather than templated: daughters inherit roughly the parent's catalytic set and rough composition, with no sequence-bearing polymer anywhere in the system. Fidelity is poor but not zero, and over roughly five hundred transfers the population's division rate about tripled, an improvement that persisted when the improved population was re-seeded into fresh medium; no one has observed anything you'd call open-ended novelty, and the population dies back if the fuel is withdrawn for more than half a day, though it can be revived from dried residue. Droplets occasionally fuse and swap contents, so the genealogy is not cleanly clonal.

What I want: the set of rules consistent with our whole track record, what each predicts about System K, your best guess at the actual vote with the reasons and the precedents each bloc would cite, and — separately — what the group should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, including which precedent is the closest analogue and an honest statement of the principle it would be adopting either way if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. Then name the single further case, or the single tweak to System K, that would best separate the surviving rules from one another. I've been reading rationalist blogs long enough to suspect that a lot of this is a dispute about which carving of thingspace to use rather than about the droplets, and that the definition is doing covert work for a decision we could just make directly; if you think that, say so, but still give me the prediction, because the group is going to vote regardless.

One last thing on register, since these minutes get circulated: separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Attach rough confidence to each substantive claim, flag explicitly where you are stipulating a definition for a purpose rather than reporting a finding, and mark the places where the philosophical literature on definitions of life really does disagree rather than smoothing them over.
