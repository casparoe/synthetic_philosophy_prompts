I retired a few years ago (surface science, 35 years in the same industrial lab) and last autumn I started sitting in on a graduate seminar on agency at the university in town, mostly for the pleasure of arguing with people younger than me. I've got stuck on one thing and I'd like to know whether my reactions to a set of cases add up to any single principle, or whether I'm just having inconsistent gut feelings and dressing them up.

The thing I keep chewing on is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. My notes have it roughly like this:

1. you do what you do, in any given situation, because of the way you are
2. to be ultimately responsible for what you do, you have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are — at least in those mental respects that issued in the action
3. you can't be ultimately responsible for the way you are, because for you to have made yourself a certain way you'd already have to have been a certain way, and that requires the same thing again, and so on
4. so nobody is ever ultimately responsible for anything

The part I find most annoying is the claim that it doesn't matter whether determinism is true: if the world is deterministic you're carried along by how you already are, and if it isn't, then whatever isn't settled by how you are is chance, and chance isn't you either.

(While I'm here: there are two Strawsons, father and son, and I have it in my head that the father is the one who wrote about reactive attitudes and resentment. Is that right, or have I got it backwards?)

Here are my verdicts, going on gut.

Case 1, "remote control": a team of neuroscientists with an implanted stimulator switches on a violent desire in a man over lunch; he kills a coworker at 12:10. Not responsible. Obviously.

Case 2, "programmed in advance": same team, no remote control. They arrange things while he's an infant — hormones, conditioning, careful placement of foster homes — so that the desire and the killing turn up when he's 40 with nobody touching anything at the time. I still say not responsible.

Case 3, "ordinary bad start": same shape of story, but the causal history is an alcoholic mother, a violent stepfather, a rough school, no scientists anywhere. I do want to say responsible. Cases 2 and 3 are the embarassing pair, because as far as I can see the causal chains are the same kind of thing, and the only differences are who was doing the shaping and what they intended.

Case 4, "ordinary, deterministic": decent upbringing, ordinary life, and stipulate the universe is deterministic in the Laplace sense. He embezzles from his employer after six weeks of going back and forth about it. Responsible.

Case 5, "ordinary, indeterministic": same man, same life, but now stipulate the physics is indeterministic and the final decision is settled by which way a quantum event in his brain goes. I find myself saying less responsible, not more. That surprised me — I thought this was the case that was supposed to rescue free will.

Case 6, "self-training": a woman who for years deliberately worked on being less sharp-tongued — reading, practice, a bit of therapy — and then, at a moment that mattered, said something cruel to her sister anyway. More responsible than someone who never tried. But I can hear Strawson saying the trying itself came from how she already was.

Case 7, which I made up myself and most want comments on, "the convergent loop": at 20 she has character C0. She decides to make herself into a certain sort of person. Round one: she inspects her current dispositions, picks which to strengthen and which to weaken, applies drills, practice, whatever, and ends up with C1. Round two: the same procedure applied to C1, giving C2. And so on. The changes get smaller each round, and after a few hundred rounds she is at C*, where further rounds change nothing measurable. Then she acts from C*. So she has, about as well as a finite being could, made herself into what she is: the regress in step 3 got cut off by a process that converged instead of running forever. My gut says responsible, more clearly than in any of cases 1 to 3. My worry: C* is determined by C0 — different starting points would in general land on different fixed points — and she didn't choose C0.

Case 8: an engineer builds a second agent directly with character C*, no loop. Same character, same action. My gut says not responsible. Which means I think the history matters and not only the machinery, and that is exactly what sits badly with 2 versus 3.

My own attempt at a principle: an agent is responsible for an action iff the action issued from a mechanism in her that tracks reasons in the right way, and the history of how that mechanism got there matters only insofar as it affects how the mechanism works now. That gets cases 1, 4, 5 and more or less 6. It also makes 2 and 3 come out the same, and 7 and 8 come out the same, and both of those go against my gut.

So: is there a principle that keeps more of my verdicts? I'd like two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I can test them, each with the case that motivates each of its clauses, and I want you to tell me honestly which of my verdicts each one forces me to give up. Please also say what each candidate predicts for two cases I haven't decided about: (a) the loop agent from case 7, except that C0 was installed in her by the neuroscientists from case 2; (b) an agent whose loop never converges — the self-modifications keep changing her by roughly the same amount, indefinitely — and who acts at time t out of whatever character she happens to have at t.

And: does the Basic Argument as I've written it actually tell against a principle like mine, or only against some stronger, "ultimate" kind of responsibility that my principle was never trying to secure? I cant tell whether I've answered it or changed the subject. Is anything really resting on the "it doesn't matter whether determinism is true" move, or is that just rhetoric?

Please don't hand me a reading list — the library is a bus ride away and I've already got three books on the go. Just argue with me.
