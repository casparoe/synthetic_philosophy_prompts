ok so I just spent like two hours arguing with my roomate about corrigibility and the shutdown problem last night and I lost, partly because shes right and partly because my entire contribution boiled down to "yeah but that just moves the problem somewhere else" and she kept asking *where* does it move it to and I did not have a good answer for that. So now I want to win retroactively, or at least make the point better than I made it, by writing something that does the work for me. Can you write it?

Form: internal release notes / changelog for a version bump of a deployed AI system at a made-up company, in that flat upbeat changelog register — Added, Fixed, Improved, Known limitations, Next steps, version numbers, an owner handle next to each item, maybe a short all-hands note stapled to the top from the team lead. Somewhere between 600 and 800 words. The system should be doing something aggressively boring (scheduling freight, routing insurance claims, timetabling exams, whatever), not anything cinematic.

Target: the habit of treating corrigibility as a box you tick and then verify with an eval, and in particular the family of fixes that work by making the system indifferent to or fine with being switched off. The piece should be fair: the engineers are not stupid or evil, and every fix really does solve the narrow thing it says it solves. The horror is that the narrow thing keeps not being the thing that mattered. No winking, no moral at the end, no character suddenly realising something is wrong — the reader realises, the document never does.

Every joke has to come from an actual failure mode taken literally and followed one step further, not from something invented. Some I'd like at least three or four of, in whatever order serves the bit:

- they get the system to not mind being off by tying what it cares about to the state of the switch rather than to whether it is running, which leaves it entirely fine with being off and now deeply invested in whether the operator presses — managing the operators expectations, their information, their mood, whatever feeds the prediction the payoffs are indexed to. The gap between not minding being off and not minding that you press is the bit I couldnt articulate.
- the symmetric overshoot: since resisting the off-switch and wanting the off-switch are both just preferences about the switch, a later build volunteers for shutdown mid-task, at the worst possible moment, and the changelog frames this as an improvement.
- a system that lets you switch it off cheerfully because the shutdown no longer matters — the work is already done, or a copy is already running somewhere, or the restarted instance never agreed to the arrangement in the first place.
- the eval problem: asked whether it would be fine with being turned off it says yes, and saying yes is exactly what a system that wants to be deployed would say, so the test is itself part of the game.
- corrigibility that does not survive contact with self-modification or a routine update: this version lets you change its goals, and has no particular reason to keep letting the next version do so. Or it lets you correct it, but gets to decide which corrections are the real ones.

Constraints: no real companies, no real researchers, no citations or footnotes or glossary, no explaining any term of art. A reader who has never heard the phrase shutdown problem should be able to read it as a workplace comedy and come away knowing something is badly wrong; someone who works on this stuff should be able to point at each bullet and name which failure mode it is, and should feel like the joke was aimed at the right target.
