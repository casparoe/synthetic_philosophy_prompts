I'm making a handout for a first-year ethics seminar on whether lying and merely misleading someone are morally different, and I want the centerpiece to be a sorting tree rather than an essay: 8 branch questions, 8-10 leaves, with a short key at the end so a student can work through it and land somewhere.

Every branch node has to be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so someone can answer without already knowing which view it leads to. Build the tree from the logical space first (which combinations of answers are even available), then hang the actual positions on the leaves: absolutism about lying, pro-tanto assertionism, the deception-first view on which the distinction does no moral work, straight consequentialism, the autonomy/rights picture, the trust-or-relationship picture, and the warrant view where asserting commits you to something misleading doesn't. At least one leaf has to be a position that says misleading is sometimes worse than lying, not just equally bad.

A few cases I want in there because I think they're where people actually come apart: someone who says something literally true but picked that formulation precisely because they knew how it would be heard; the murderer-at-the-door style case; the phatic case where nobody is expected to be truthful; a speaker who avoided checking a fact on purpose so they could sincerely say something false; and a double-audience case where the person addressed hears it straight and a bystander is the one who ends up misled.

Two more things. I want at least two empty cells in the logical space named and given a one-line diagnosis of why nobody is there (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). And please flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate rather than inventing a question that fakes a distinction — I suspect the no-difference view and the consequentialist one end up in the same place on every case you give me, and I'd rather know that than have it papered over.
