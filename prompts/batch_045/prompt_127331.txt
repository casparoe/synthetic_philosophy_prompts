I've been going in circles with the old rationalist-blog discussions of van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the constraint that your credence in A now, conditional on your later self having credence x in A, should itself be x — and I've talked myself into thinking it can't be a genuine requirement of rationality. I'd like to know where that argument goes wrong.

Here is the case that convinced me. I'm deciding whether to fund a two-year trial of some intervention. I trust the trial's design completely: whatever final credence n it leaves me with, I will then move to n. Reflection looks like it says my credence today should already equal my expectation of n, which means I should skip the trial and just adopt that number now. And it gets worse. I expect the trial, if it does anything, to move me toward the truth — but Reflection seems to forbid me from expecting my credence to shift in any direction I can name in advance. Since I plainly should run the trial, and plainly should expect it to leave me better calibrated than I am now, I conclude that Reflection is either false, or else derived from conditionalization and so incapable of telling me anything I didn't already grant.

Which step fails: does Reflection really have the consequence that I cannot expect my credence to move in a direction I can specify now, and does that consequence really make the trial worthless? (I care partly because the same structure reappears when a system plans to hand off to a better-informed successor — a model that will defer to a retrained version of itself.) Please separate what is settled here from what is contested among people who actually work on diachronic rationality, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. Answer in prose, in at most 300 words.
