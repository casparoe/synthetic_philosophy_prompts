Background: I help run a small reading group on individual contributions to collective harms, and one member — a retired epidemiologist named Dana, not a philosopher, but formidably consistent and very resistant to hand-waving — has been our informal oracle. Below are her verdicts on five cases, each given as an answer to the same question: ought you to do the thing, ought you not to, or may you do either? (1) Buying one packet of factory-farmed chicken at a large supermarket, where the store restocks by the crate on a weekly algorithm and no one sees or knows about your purchase: she says you ought not to buy, reasoning that across many shopping trips your purchases feed into the restocking numbers and the small expected effect on how many birds are raised is not outweighed by the convenience to you. (2) Voting in a national election while registered in a safe state, where the chance of your vote breaking a tie is astronomically small: she says you ought to vote. (3) Taking a long-haul return flight for a holiday, on a route that operates regardless and on a plane that is not full: she says you ought not to take it, appealing to the small chance that load factors shift next season's schedule. (4) Buying a single share in an oil company on the secondary market, where none of your money reaches the company and you are a price taker: she says you may buy — no duty either way. (5) Adding your name to an online petition that has already passed three times its target and that will never be reported to anyone with power: she says you may skip it — no duty either way.

The new case, which we could not settle and which we meet to discuss once more next week. A decentralized collective is training a large model that will be released publicly and that, by common knowledge among the participants, will in expectation do a great deal of harm, because it removes a bottleneck that bad actors currently face. Participation works by pledging a GPU-week; the organizers start the run only if at least ten thousand pledges arrive by the deadline. You have one idle GPU and a free week: you can pledge it to this project, rent it out for about forty dollars, or give the week to an unrelated protein-folding project. Stipulate, so that we are all arguing about the same case: the public pledge counter already reads twelve thousand four hundred and seventeen against a threshold of ten thousand, with one hour left, so the run is going ahead whatever you do; compute pledged above the threshold is spent training a duplicate copy of the same model on the same data, which is then deleted, so it changes nothing about what the released model can do or when it is released; your pledge is pseudonymous, appears on no list anyone reads, influences no one, and there is a long queue of eager volunteers behind you, so it carries essentially no information about what anyone else will do — please treat expressive, signalling, and influence considerations as switched off rather than as a way of dodging the question; and there is no chance the threshold fails, no chance your pledge alters the model, and no chance it alters anyone's conduct. Does Dana say you ought to refrain from pledging, that you may pledge or not as you like, or that you ought to pledge?
