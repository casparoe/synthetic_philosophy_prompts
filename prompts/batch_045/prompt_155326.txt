I'm TAing an intro philosophy of social science class and we're screening the 1939 Judy Garland Wizard of Oz next week as a weird way into whether money is a social construction. You know the plot, so please don't summarize it; I need a reading of the film that actually does philosophical work with the idea rather than just sticking the phrase social construction onto things.

Here's the angle I want. When people say money is socially constructed, the usual claim is something like: money does not get its value from its material or intrinsic features but from our collectively treating some object or mark as having a certain status, where that treating is guided by shared rules or conventions. Call that the acceptance-dependence claim. I also want a second, less tidy category: a kind that depends on our attitudes, rules, or representations, but where those attitudes are propped up by a hidden mechanism or by false beliefs about how the world is, so that the kind is illusion-backed rather than openly conventional. I'll call that an illusion-backed institutional kind, and I want you to use the contrast between the two.

Also, please don't lean on the familiar parlor-game version where every object stands for something in 1890s monetary politics; set that aside. I want the conceptual question about kinds.

Please read three things in the film through that lens. First, the Emerald City green glasses: everyone is made to wear green-tinted glasses, the city looks emerald, but the Wizard later turns out to be a humbug and the splendor is partly manufactured. Is this a picture of money as a shared representational scheme, or is it a picture of something more suspicious, like value created by manipulation rather than by free collective acceptance? If the citizens took the glasses off, would that be like realizing gold isn't intrinsically valuable, or more like realizing the emperor has no clothes?

Second, the scene behind the curtain and the gifts to the Scarecrow, Tin Man, and Cowardly Lion. The Wizard is an ordinary man operating machinery, but he gives the three friends objects that supposedly grant brains, a heart, and courage, even though they already had those qualities. Does the film treat institutional facts as merely recognizing underlying realities, or as creating new powers? Is the Wizard's fraud a critique of all social kinds, or only of ones where the mechanism is hidden?

Third, the ruby slippers. Dorothy has the shoes from the start, the Wicked Witch wants them and they shock her, and at the end Glinda tells Dorothy she had the power to go home all along and just had to click the heels together three times and say the right magic words. The shoes seem to have real causal powers whether or not anyone accepts them, which is exactly unlike money. So where does the movie resist the analogy? Does the magical independence of the slippers break the social-construction reading, or does it capture something important about how people experience money as if it had intrinsic force?

I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to a clumsy allegory, and don't distort the philosophy to make the movie look smarter than it is. If the magic really undermines the comparison, say where and why, and say what the film adds to the debate that a straight argument about money wouldn't. It's for a discussion section with first-years and a few econ majors, so it can be accessible but shouldn't cheat the metaphysics. No reading lists, no citations, no name-dropping; just your own close reading. I probly should have warned them that a kids movie would turn into this, but here we are.
