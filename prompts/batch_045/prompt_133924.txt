I'm a TA for an undergrad seminar on ethics and AI — about 18 students, half of them CS majors who are only there because it fills a requirement, and I've got one 80-minute session next week on the question of whose values an aligned AI should serve. We did the liberal neutrality material last week and I think the room is split three ways: a chunk who think the answer is obviously "everyone's, aggregated somehow", a chunk who think that's incoherent and you have to just pick, and a chunk who think the whole thing is a fake question because the system will just inherit whatever its builders happened to care about. I don't want to resolve that by telling them; I want them to run into it.

So: design me an activity for that session where the problem shows up in what they do, not in what I say afterward. Rules, roles or factions, materials (I have index cards, a whiteboard, and no reliable laptop situation), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific moves in the argument. Ideally the mechanic itself should force the tradeoff rather than them just discussing it — some structure where they have to actually build or approve one system prompt / one charter / one ranking under time pressure and divided interests. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the phrases "value aggregation" or "liberal neutrality" until the debrief.

A few things I'd specifically like to come out of it, if you can make them happen rather than assert them. First: that "serve everyone's values" isn't one option, it's a whole family of options with wildly different profiles — averaging, veto rights, overlap-only, rotating control, carving up domains — and that choosing between them is doing the thing the question was supposed to postpone. Second, something I've started calling the dilution problem: as you add more constituencies to the aggregate, each one's share of influence shrinks toward nothing, so a system that "serves everyone" can end up substantively serving no one, and the people who cared most about a given thing are exactly the ones most shortchanged. The mirror image matters too — if you let people register how intensely they care, everyone has an incentive to scream maximum intensity, so maybe you can't aggregate without either ignoring intensity or inviting strategic inflation. And third: the constituency is endogenous. Whoever gets to decide who is in the denominator — future people, children, people who'd reject the whole process, people who'd rather opt out — has already settled most of the question before any aggregation happens.

Two honesty requests. Where are you actually confident that 18 undergrads will react the way your design needs them to, and where are you guessing? Give me the observable mid-session signal that would tell me your design is misfiring (something concretely watchable, not "they seem disengaged"), and what I should switch to if I see it. And be blunt about which part of the idea the activity just cannot reproduce and which I'll have to supply in the debrief.
