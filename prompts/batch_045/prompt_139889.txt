I am convening a two-session adversarial collaboration between two philosophers who have been arguing past each other about the procreation asymmetry for years, and I need the joint statement they would produce at the end of it: the document both could actually sign, with the residual disagreement stated exactly rather than papered over.

The asymmetry, in the two forms they will be arguing about. Axiological form: the existence of a person whose life is good is not better, other things equal, than that person's never existing, while the existence of a person whose life is bad is worse than that person's never existing. Deontic form: there is no duty, and perhaps no reason at all, to bring a person with a good life into existence, but there is a duty, or at least a decisive reason, not to bring a person with a bad life into existence. Both parties should also be pinned down on the weaker version, on which there is *some* reason to create a happy person that is nonetheless always outweighed or merely optional, versus the stronger version on which there is none.

The parties. The Asymmetrist holds that some version of the asymmetry is a fixed point that any adequate account of our reasons about future people must reproduce, and regards the familiar embarrassments of symmetric views as confirming evidence. The Symmetricist holds that reasons of beneficence have the same structure whether they bear on creating or on refraining from creating, and treats the asymmetry as a bundle of distinguishable errors: a person-afflicting consideration mixed with an impersonal one, the claim that no one is deprived mistaken for the claim that nothing is lost, and an empirical judgement about risk or about the quality of ordinary human life smuggled in as if it were a principle. Give each side the strongest version of its own view, in the wording that side would accept, and do not hedge with some people on this side say.

Shared background, not to be relitigated in the document: welfarism about what makes a life go well; no appeal to a duty to continue the species, to a deity, or to the intrinsic value of human life as such; creating a life that is nothing but agony is to be avoided; there is no duty to procreate; the non-identity problem is genuine and the options in the cases below never involve the same individual across alternatives. Both sides should also record whether they are treating the asymmetry as a thesis about value, about reasons, about permissibility, or about some combination, since a good deal of their past shouting may have crossed these.

Four cases on which both sides must take a definite position:

1. The Ten-Percent Child. A child whose life has a high chance of being clearly above the neutral level and a small chance of falling below it. Each side must state the probability at which creation becomes permissible, as a number, and say what makes the curve have the shape it has: in particular, whether the negative tail dominates solely because of the asymmetry's negative limb, or because of ordinary risk aversion, or because of something else. This is the case where I most want the predictions written down in advance, because the two sides should come apart quantitatively and not merely in tone.

2. The Substitute. Exactly one child will be created, and you choose between two possible children: one whose life is uniformly good, and one whose life is net-positive overall but contains a decade of severe suffering that nothing compensates and that leaves no lasting trace. Both sides will say there is a reason to choose the first. The question is what that reason consists in: does it come only from the avoidance of suffering, or does the securing of the greater happiness also contribute? And, relatedly, is the asymmetry's negative limb a claim about a life's net balance or about episodes within a life? Say which, and note that whichever they choose changes what the asymmetry forbids in ordinary cases of creation.

3. The Million and One. Creating a very large number of lives barely above the neutral level plus one life far below it, versus creating nobody. On a one-at-a-time reading of the asymmetry there is no reason for the many and a reason against the one, which most people find jarring. Is an aggregated n-fold version of the asymmetry the same thesis as the single-case version or a different and independently committed one? Does either side's view survive when the numbers are made very large, and if a limit version is what does the work, say so rather than hiding it in the intuition.

4. The Hour-Long Mind. A mind implemented in software, whose entire life is a single hour of joy followed by cessation, affecting no one else and leaving no trace. Does the asymmetry apply to creating it? The point of the case is diagnostic: I want to know whether each side's answer reveals the asymmetry to be a thesis about persons, a thesis about value, or a thesis about a social practice of procreation that happens to be what the intuition was trained on. If one side's answer here forces it to retreat to a narrower claim than it started with, record the narrower claim.

One further task for the collaboration. Either exhibit a cycle of three options, A over B, B over C, and C over A, generated by the asymmetry together with whatever principles both sides accept governing same-number-different-people and different-number choices, plus any transitivity or acyclicity requirement both accept; or, if no such construction exists, state exactly which premise blocks it and whether that premise is one the asymmetry is entitled to help itself to. If the blocking premise turns out to be the only interesting content of the asymmetry, say that too.

Format the document as follows. First, a numbered agreed-claims section of eight to twelve claims, each with a one-line note on what the other side gets out of signing it; I want claims that are load-bearing rather than courteous, and any that would be signed by a theorist who held a view neither party holds should be cut. Second, a disagreements table with a column for the issue, a column for the Asymmetrist's formulation in wording an Asymmetrist would accept, a column for the Symmetricist's formulation likewise, and a column recording whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive, with a one-line statement of what observation or argument would settle it. Third, three to five cruxes, each with the sub-question, why settling it would move at least one side, the case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance as a number, a direction, or a definite expectation about what the argument will show.

I want committed rather than survey-like work throughout. In particular: give a verdict on which of the disagreements are merely verbal and which are not; name the single crux you expect to be decisive and say which side you expect it to favour, with your reasons; and add a short closing note flagging the formulations in your own document that you suspect a real partisan of one side would refuse to sign, together with the one place where you had to choose between the parties' preferred framings and why you chose as you did. Argue from the cases and from distinctions stated in your own terms; no citations, no bibliographies, no ascriptions of views to named authors, since the document has to stand on its own for a reader with no library. Assume a careful non-specialist reader. Around 1200 to 1600 words.
