I'm a high school senior who runs our school's ethics club, and this term we're doing a unit on whether an individual has any obligation to do anything about large collective harms: climate change, industrial animal farming, elections in districts that are never close. I'd like a diagnostic quiz of at most ten questions that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by what they'd call themselves, together with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the actual positions and a decision tree in which every branch is labeled with the single claim that separates the views below it.

Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the named positions inside it; mark any combination nobody occupies and say briefly whether it's empty because incoherent or merely unattractive. The branches need to distinguish at least: an act's changing the expected amount of harm from its changing nothing about the outcome; "my act makes no difference" used as a claim about consequences from the same sentence used as a claim about fairness, participation, or what I could be blamed for; and views that ask what would happen if enough others acted as I do from views that hold everyone else's behavior fixed. Leaves should include act consequentialism with the tiny-chance-of-being-pivotal response, rule or universalization-style views, complicity or participation views, virtue-based views, and the view that there is simply no obligation.

Use everyday cases a teenager could face — one burger, one unvoted election, one petition signature. Two of them should be ones the standard discussions don't cover: one where my act leaves the expected harm unchanged but widens the spread of outcomes, and one where the harm is capped rather than open-ended, so a small chance of tipping it can't be multiplied by enormous stakes. Finally, tell me which positions the quiz can't separate and why.
