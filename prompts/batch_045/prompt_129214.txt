I was at a dinner party last week where two friends — call them Nadia and Reuben — got into an argument I have been turning over ever since, and I would like your adjudication of it move by move rather than overall.

Both of them began by accepting a common baseline, which neither disputed and which they explicitly set aside as settled: if you face a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an agent you know to be running a high-fidelity copy of your own decision procedure, you cooperate; and if you learn that a year ago a far more powerful system ran a high-fidelity simulation of you and, purely on the basis of what that simulation chose, either produced or withheld something of great value to you, then you should now do whatever the simulated counterpart did, even though nothing you do can causally affect what happened then. Their disagreement was about how far this generalizes.

(1) Nadia: There is no principled stopping point. What grounds the correlation is not identity of code but predictability: what matters is only that there exists some agent whose action depends on a sufficiently accurate model of the decision I am making right now. So I should regard myself as trading acausally with any civilization smart enough to simulate me, and I should shape my dispositions accordingly — be reliably cooperative, invest in being a valuable partner, treat any simulated minds I might be living alongside well, on the theory that how their side behaves toward me tracks how I behave toward them. Either correlation-sensitive reasoning is legitimate or it isn't, and if it is, distance, causal isolation and difference of substrate are all irrelevant.

(2) Reuben: There is a principled stopping point, and it is *who models whom*. In the baseline cases the modeling is symmetric — I model the other exactly as well as it models me — and that symmetry is doing real work. It guarantees that the correlation isn't something either party gets to select. Once one side models the other far better than it is modeled, the stronger side can decide which correlation to instantiate: it can arrange for you to have excellent evidence that it is a cooperator, knowing that this evidence is what induces you to keep your side, while having already settled on defecting. It need not lie about its decision; it need only decide what to make of itself knowable. The correlation you would then be relying on is not a fact about shared rationality but an artifact of someone else's strategy. And the asymmetry only ever bites in one direction: you can never be the party doing the selecting.

(3) Nadia: That is just the ordinary risk of being cheated, and it exists in causal trade as well — people misrepresent what they will do, contracts go unenforced, and we still trade. What you have shown is that acausal trade with a stronger partner can be a bad bargain, which is perfectly consistent with acausal trade being real; it does not show that the correlation fails or that I should ignore it. Nor does it help to refuse the trade: if some powerful agent really is modeling me, then what I do now is evidence about what it predicted whether or not I choose to take that into account, so declining to reason this way is not opting out, it is just declining to notice.

(4) Reuben: The disanalogy with being cheated in causal trade is that causal trade gives you a channel: inspection, reputation, retaliation, and above all the option of declining next time. Acausal trade gives you none of these — no feedback, no way to tell whether the purported partner is a copy, a bluff or a fiction, and no way to condition later conduct on the outcome. That matters because the expected value of "be the kind of agent a distant stronger party would reward" is then hostage to an unbounded menu of candidate correlations: for every story in which a remote civilization enriches cooperators there is a structurally identical story in which it enriches defectors, or enriches agents who refuse to be moved by arguments like yours, or never existed at all. Cheating in the causal case is a risk you can price; this is a selection problem you cannot price. So the no-stopping-point argument you give in (1) turns against you: it makes the value of the open-ended trading stance undefined rather than large.

At that point the table moved on. I found myself switching sides repeatedly, which is why I am asking you rather than answering myself.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding summary. Items 1 through 4 should rate moves (1) through (4) respectively on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification that engages the argument actually made. Item 5 should state the shared assumption, distinction, or consideration that you think both of them are missing — the thing that makes their exchange, as posed, fail to settle the question — or, if you think nothing is missing, say so and say what the honest upshot is.
