I've been reading about animal minds in my spare time (I'm not a philosopher, I do data engineering) and I keep tripping over the same shape of argument whenever insects come up. Let me give two instances instead of describing it abstractly.

Instance one. Someone says: an injured insect's behaviour doesn't seem to be modulated by anything else going on, there's no sign of an enduring state that carries over, the whole nervous system is tiny — so I'd put maybe 5–10% on there being anything it's like to be that insect. Fine. Two paragraphs later the same person does the arithmetic that's supposed to show this is the biggest thing in the world: absurd numbers of insects alive at any one time (quintillions), times that probability, times how bad a unit of suffering is — and for that last factor they use more or less the number they'd use for a chicken, maybe shaved down a bit. But if the reason for the 5–10% is that the machinery is minimal, that same reason ought to tell you that *conditional on* there being something it's like, it'll be a thin, small thing rather than a full-blooded pain. The consideration gets used once and then quietly dropped.

Instance two, from someone arguing the other side. He says: suppose insects are conscious, fine, but what they'd have is a bare flash of something unpleasant, a fraction of a second, no memory and no anticipation — so the amount of welfare at stake per insect is negligible. Then when he's asked how likely it is that insects are conscious at all, he uses a very permissive test, where any valenced state whatsoever counts, which pushes the probability up. So he has probability high and magnitude low. The first person has probability low and magnitude high. It looks like the same evidence — how simple the system is — gets read as bearing on one dial and declared irrelevant to the other, and which dial it gets applied to tracks which conclusion the person already wanted. My suspicion is that wherever you set the bar for what counts, the dials move in opposite directions and more or less cancel, so the resulting expected number barely moves and the actual disagreement is somewhere else. But I'm not confident that's right either.

Is there a name for this? The nearby things I've found don't fit. It's not plain correlation neglect, I think, because the complaint isn't that people are ignoring a statistical correlation between two things out in the world — it's that the two quantities look like one question split in two, so it isn't obvious they can vary independently at all. It's not double counting either: nobody is counting the same consideration twice, they're counting it once and then refusing to count it the second time. And it's not the usual worry about enormous numbers multiplied by tiny probabilities, because that worry is about the size of the answer, whereas mine is about whether the thing is assembled consistently.

So: is there an established concept, or a bit of literature, on this exact configuration? If there is, I want to know how close the fit is and what's left over. If there isn't, I want it named and properly carved — and I don't mean a label, I mean what exactly the phenomenon is, what the mechanism is, whether the cancellation I half-believe in actually holds, and which cases fall outside it (I can think of situations where the two dials genuinely should move sepearately, and I'd want those excluded rather than swallowed).

Could you write it up in four sections: "Closest concepts in the literature" (about 250 words) on what's already out there that this resembles; "How each one falls short" (about 250 words), covering the three I ruled out above as well as anything else you'd nominate; "What's actually going on" (about 300 words) on the mechanism, whether the two factors really are entangled, and whether the cancellation goes through; and "Name, definition, and boundary cases" (about 200 words) with a term, a definition tight enough to apply to a new case, and two cases that look similar but should be excluded.
