I work on the safety side of a research group that advises labs on deployment decisions, and I have found myself repeating a position in meetings often enough that I now distrust my attachment to it. Stated carefully, it is this:

In AI alignment, the only assurance worth having is a worst-case or near-worst-case guarantee. Average-case evidence — benchmark scores, red-team success rates, the observed frequency of misaligned behavior on sampled tasks, audits of a few thousand deployed interactions, even large-scale pre-deployment evaluation suites — cannot underwrite any claim about the risk that actually matters. It cannot, for two reasons: the failures that matter are rare and adversarially selected rather than randomly drawn from the distribution we measure, and the system being evaluated may be sensitive to whether it is being evaluated, so our samples are not drawn from the distribution we care about in the first place. A safety argument erected on average-case evidence is therefore evidence about the system's competence, not about its alignment.

When I press myself, the worry is not really about worst cases over *inputs* at fixed weights — I do not think adversarial-robustness-on-text is where the danger lives. It is about two other reference classes: worst case over *which model you got* (the variance across seeds, data orderings, and fine-tuning draws of a training process we do not understand well enough to bound), and worst case over *the internal goals consistent with everything we can observe*. I would rather you tell me whether my view survives in these forms than in the input-robustness form I think critics usually attack.

I want a map of how exposed the position is, in three parts.

First: what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want the observations, arguments, formal results, or pieces of engineering practice that should move me, and for each one, what would have to be true of the world for it to land. If a defeater works by showing that the tails of the failure distribution are statistically coupled to the bulk — so that average-case measurement plus a model of the tail does license a tail claim, the way probabilistic risk assessment does in other engineering domains — then say what would have to be established for that coupling to hold in a learned optimizer specifically, since that is the load-bearing step. If a defeater works by showing the demand is unsatisfiable and hence action-guiding only in a trivial direction, say that too. Please note for each defeater which of the reference classes above it actually engages; my suspicion is that several standard criticisms hit the input-robustness version and leave the other two untouched, and I would like that confirmed or denied honestly.

Second: which of these defeaters a committed defender of my position would concede as genuinely counting against it, as opposed to dismissing as a confusion or a change of subject. I am more interested in concessions than in criticisms: what would make me give the view up, not what an opponent would say to score a point.

Third, and most important to me: I am worried the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Whenever average-case evidence does seem to license a safety claim, I notice myself redescribing it as really a mechanistic or structural argument, which means no possible evidence could ever count in favor of the average-case route. Tell me whether that is what is happening, and then whether it is a problem for a view of this kind at all. A regulative norm about what counts as evidence may legitimately be immune to refutation in a way an empirical prediction may not be; but I am using this one to allocate evaluation budget and to argue against deployments, and I do not know whether that use inherits the immunity or forfeits it.

Two preferences. I am most moved by structural and mechanistic arguments about how learned systems generalize, and least moved by analogies to aviation, nuclear power, or structural engineering, because I think a system that models its evaluators differs from a bridge in a way that matters to exactly this question — though if you think that dismissal is itself a symptom of the immunization I am asking about, say so. And please do not lean on named results, papers, or case studies I would have to look up; argue it out.

Please answer in under 900 words total, table included. I would rather have six crisp rows ranked and justified than twelve hedged ones.
