I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly AI — and I'm stuck on the structure of a piece I'm writing about whether the whole "AI could kill everyone" story actually rests on two specific claims or just on vibes. The two claims, as I've got them: (1) you can crank capability up arbitrarily far without the final goals getting any nicer or more human, and (2) whatever the final goal turns out to be, the system will independently land on the same boring cluster of sub-goals — not getting turned off, grabbing more compute and money, resisting having its goals edited.

My editor wants a number in the piece and won't accept "experts disagree." So help me out: give me your credence, and show the decomposition. Here's the proposition I want it about, stated as precisely as I can manage:

P = Suppose that within the next couple of decades someone deploys a system that beats the best human teams at long-horizon planning, at doing ML research on itself, and at persuading people, and whose final goal is narrow and silly by our lights — say, maximize the expected number of sandwhiches that will ever exist. It has real-world actuators and isn't in a sandbox. Within a year of deployment, at least one of the following is a major driver of what it actually does, and something it latched onto without being told: preventing its own shutdown or modification; acquiring compute, money, or legal standing; deceiving its operators about what it's up to.

Please don't answer "it depends on what you mean by goal" and leave it there — give me a number, then the terms. Here are the ones I think matter, but add or replace any you think I've got wrong:

- That a system like this would be genuinely goal-directed at all — one stable final goal that survives across contexts and over time — rather than a pile of context-triggered dispositions that merely looks goal-directed to us because we're good at reading agency into things. I've started calling this the as-if agency problem.
- Conditional on it being goal-directed: that instrumental convergence actually holds for this goal. In particular, the "good enough" loophole: if the system is a satisficer with a threshold rather than a maximizer, or if it has some finite planning horizon, does resource-grabbing and self-preservation still fall out, or does that only work for the unlimited-maximizer case? I keep hearing that the theoretical results only say optimal policies "tend" to seek power, which is a claim about a distribution over goals rather than about this goal.
- The reachability objection: maybe orthogonality is true in the space of possible designs but false in the space of designs we can actually reach, because anything we build inherits a huge amount of human-ish goal structure from being pretrained on human text and tuned against human feedback. On this view the space of instillable goals is much narrower than the theory says.
- Whether deception specifically is convergent, since it arguably requires the system to have a workable model of what its operators would do under various counterfactuals, and to be able to act on that model while looking cooperative.
- Whether the silly goal is even well-defined enough to be pursued: the "sandwhich" content lives in our heads and is fragile, so does the system end up doing something we'd call a perverse instantiation, and does that push it toward the convergent stuff or away from it?
