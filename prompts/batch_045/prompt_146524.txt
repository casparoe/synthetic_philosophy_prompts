I have no formal training in philosophy. Over the last couple of years I've been reading about weakness of will on my own — some popular books, some behavioural economics, and a lot of arguing with myself about my own evenings — and I've reached a point where I'd like to write the whole thing down and have someone check whether it actually hangs together. I don't want a survey of the literature and I don't want suggestions for what to read next; I want an audit of the position I've arrived at.

Here it is, as eight things I currently believe:

(1) Clear-eyed weakness of will is real and reasonably common. A person can, at the very moment of acting, take it to be the case that option B is better than option A all things considered, and still freely and intentionally choose A. This isn't compulsion and it isn't self-deception or forgotten information; the judgment is genuinely there and the action genuinely goes against it.

(2) At the same time, I think most of what people call weakness of will is really present bias. Rewards lose value very steeply as soon as they stop being immediate, so as a smaller-sooner option approaches, preference ordering flips: the thing that was worse at a distance becomes better up close. On this picture, at the moment of action the person isn't going against her own judgment at all — her preference really has changed.

(3) That flip is itself a genuine rational failure, not just a change of mind that we should note and move on from. Somebody whose plans reliably unravel as the moment gets near is criticisable in a way that isn't captured by saying her preferences changed and that's the end of it.

(4) There's no deep fact of the matter about which time-slice's preferences are the person's real preferences. The person who makes the plan at eight in the morning and the person who breaks it at eleven at night are equally her; neither one speaks for the whole life in a way the other doesn't.

(5) Nothing can be a reason for you to do something unless it connects up with something you actually care about. There's no standing list of things a person ought to want regardless of what she does want, and no way to criticise her ends from outside her own concerns.

(6) Binding yourself in advance is the rational response to all this, and using a device is not a failure or a confession of defeat: deleting the app, handing over the keys, automatic enrolment, telling other people what you intend so that backing out would be embarrassing. Tying yourself to the mast in advance is a win, not a loss.

(7) The difference between people who handle this well and people who don't is closer to a trained habit than to some extra faculty of will. It can be built up with practice, and it reliably gets worse with tiredness, stress and alcohol, which suggests it's a capacity that runs down rather than a truth you either see or don't.

(8) The clearest cases — the ones worth building a theory around — are the ones where the judgment is stable and long-standing: the smoker who has judged for years that she should stop, who in some real sense wants to be a non-smoker, and who then freely lights up anyway. The dessert-after-dinner cases are the unclear ones, not these.

What I'd like from you:

Go through these and tell me which of them actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me — specifically, which of my eight claims I would then no longer be able to make, and which piece of ordinary experience I'd be left unable to describe. Please rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than listing them all as equally serious, and please say plainly if any pairing that looks suspicious to me is in fact fine as it stands.

Two further requests. First, I have no settled view about which claim I'd abandon, so please don't assume I'll drop whichever one sounds most like jargon — argue for the cheapest revision, not the most fashionable one. Second, I suspect I've been sliding over at least one distinction that would dissolve some of the difficulty: something like the difference between what a person judges to be better and what she is moved to do, or between a failure of rationality and a failure of capacity, or between explaining why something happens and excusing it. If a tension of mine turns on a distinction I haven't drawn, name the distinction and tell me which side of it each of my claims is secretly committed to. And if, after all that, there's one revision that would clean up the most, tell me what it is.
