I am a teaching assistant for a second-year epistemology seminar, and our next unit is a two-week block on the epistemology of testimony. The through-line I want students to leave with is the question of whether accepting what someone tells you is a basic way of acquiring epistemic good, or whether it always borrows its warrant from other sources, and every year the same thing happens: students can state the reductionist/anti-reductionist contrast more or less correctly and then fall apart the moment they are given a case with an unusual speaker, an unusual chain of transmission, or an unusual hearer. So rather than another taxonomy-by-names handout, I want a classification instrument: a decision tree whose branch nodes are questions about concrete cases, phrased so that a student who has done the reading for the first time that morning can answer them, and whose leaves are the positions in the space. Please do not put any theoretical vocabulary in the questions themselves — no reductionism, entitlement, calibration, transmission, assurance, internalism or externalism may appear in a branch node; that vocabulary belongs on the leaves and in the commentary. Give me eight to ten branch nodes, each with two or three answer options, ordered so that the tree can be walked from a single starting commitment to a leaf.

Before you draw the tree, I want the space rather than the roll-call: lay out the three or four axes that actually do the separating work (whether the hearer needs positive reasons, whether those reasons are things the hearer has in mind or facts about reliability, whether the reasons have to be about this speaker and this statement or may be a general track record, whether the good under discussion at that node is justification or knowledge, and whether the distinctive reason is a second-personal one that depends on the speaker taking responsibility or merely an indicator of truth), state how many combinations the axes allow, and then locate the positions in that space, marking empty cells and diagnosing each one — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. The leaves must include at least: a broadly Humean global reductionism; a local reductionism on which the hearer must make per-speaker and per-domain credibility judgments; a Reidian or Coady-style anti-reductionism on which testimony is a basic source; a bare-presumption or default-acceptability view; an externalist process-reliabilist view on which calibration with a reliable source does the work; a trust or assurance view on which the speaker's taking responsibility for the truth of what she says matters in a way that evidence does not capture; a non-evidentialist or entitlement view on which we may accept without positive reasons but not because testimony is itself a basic source; and a view about knowledge from testimony that handles merely transmissive cases. If you are not confident that any actual theorist occupies a cell, write unnamed rather than guessing at an attribution — I would rather have an honest gap than a wrong name, and I do not want any bibliographic or reading-list material since the handout has to work in a room without internet.

The cases I need the instrument to handle, because these are the ones that break my students, are: a stranger in a railway station who tells you the time; a source that is entirely reliable in its outputs but whose outputs reach you only when they are favourable, so that you never see the disconfirming ones; a chain of a hundred speakers each of whom simply repeats the previous one, where each link is individually reliable but the chain as a whole is not; a teacher who sincerely and carefully asserts a scientific claim she privately believes to be false, teaching from a curriculum she does not endorse; an anonymous account with a long, independently verified track record on precisely this kind of question; two speakers with identical measured reliability, one of them a stranger and one of them a person you have trusted for years, saying the same thing; a speaker who warns you in advance that she is unreliable about this topic and then tells you something about it; a group report issued in an institution's name where no individual member of the group endorses the claim; an output from a translation system or a large language model that has been right about comparable questions; and a speaker whose reliability in the past is partly the result of your having believed her. Several of these are deliberately cross-cutting rather than branch-shaped, so where a case does not fit the tree, say so and say what it is doing — I would rather you flag that the instrument cannot separate two leaves, or that a case cuts across a branch, than force it into place. Finally, for each leaf give me one sentence on its most awkward commitment, a scoring note for the answer patterns that do not land cleanly on any leaf (the incoherent ones and the merely mixed ones), and a short closing note on what a tree like this cannot tell me about a student — for instance, whether someone's verdicts come from a view at all rather than from case-by-case intuition.
