Posting this here because it came up in our work-in-progress seminar again today, and I realised I've now watched the same shape of argument go round maybe five times without having a word for it. I'm writing a chapter on the machine cases (the one where you're hooked up and get whatever experiences you'd like, plus the variants people keep inventing), and this is the thing I can't get a grip on.

The pattern, with two instances.

First, a case someone made up last week. Call it the Overdub. You live your actual life — you actually write the novel, actually show up for your friend, actually climb the mountain — but any time the experience of doing it would be tedious, frustrating, or grim, the machine ghosts you through that bit: your body keeps going, the world keeps responding to you, nobody notices, and from the inside it just feels like a smooth run of enjoyable activity. So the book exists, the friendship exists, and none of the effort was ever felt.

Second, the plain plug-in case. One person in the room says they'd refuse: they don't want to merely seem to have climbed the mountain, they want to have climbed it. Someone who defends the "a life goes well to the extent the person gets what they wanted" style of view says: fine — then what you wanted was the actual climbing, the machine doesn't give you that, so it's worse for you, which is exactly what the view predicts. Another person in the same room says they'd plug in happily, they only ever cared about how it felt. Same defender: fine — then what you wanted was the feeling, the machine gives you that, so it's better for you, exactly as predicted. Both people walk out feeling the theory handled their case.

What bugs me is that the theory ends up agreeing with whoever is speaking, and the only thing doing any work seems to be the reaction you fed it in the first place. And I think I see why: to apply it at all you first have to work out what the person wanted, or what it was like for them — and that's drawn from the same place as the judgement you were trying to test it against. So it always reproduces the verdict, and never says whether the verdict was right.

I've tried on a few labels and none fit. Not unfalsifiability exactly — it does produce verdicts, they just always match. Not motte-and-bailey, there's no strong claim and weak claim being swapped. Not really the bolt-on-auxiliary-hypothesis thing either, because nothing gets added after the fact: the absorbing move is built in and is the same move every time. It's also not quite the "oh, that's a different value, not well-being" dodge, though it's nearby — that one pushes the value outside well-being, this one keeps it inside and just re-describes it in the theory's own terms.

So: does this have a name, either in the well-being stuff or somewhere else (philosophy of science, argumentation theory)? And I don't mean a bare label — I need to know what precisely the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. Specifically: when is a theory legitimately allowed to say "of course it predicts that, that's just what the theory says", and when has it slid into this? Does an objectivist view that can always add another item to the list (say, contact with reality) have the same problem, or a relevantly different one? And is there any way to tell whether a given view is actually doing this or only looks like it? Please don't bother with a reading list or citations, I can chase those myself — I just want the thing carved properly, and if there's no term for it, coin one and give me the boundary cases.
