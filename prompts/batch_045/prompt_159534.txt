I am in my final year of school, writing an extended essay on responsibility for failing to act, and I have run into the same wall every time I sit down to write: I have confident verdicts about which failures to act actually cause the things that happen next, and I cannot state the rule I am using to get them. I would like your help extracting it, testing it, and telling me where it breaks.

By caused I mean whatever the ordinary word means in sentences like her failure to call the fire brigade caused the house to burn down — the sense that seems to matter for blame, not a technical sense.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A lifeguard on duty at a pool is reading her phone and does not notice a child struggling in the shallow end; she could have reached him in a few seconds. Verdict: her not going in caused the child's death.

2. A lifeguard, off duty, in ordinary clothes, sunbathing beside a different pool, sees a child struggling there. She is a strong swimmer, the water is calm, there is no risk to her, and she does not go in. Verdict: I still want to say she caused it, but this is the verdict I am least sure of, and it feels different from case 1 in a way I cannot articulate.

3. A tourist at the same pool who cannot swim and has no connection to anyone there, who also does not go in. Verdict: she did not cause the death, although she may have done something else wrong.

4. My housemate promised to water my plant while I was away, forgot, and the plant died. Verdict: she caused it.

5. A guest staying in the flat that week who saw the plant, knew it needed water, was never asked, and did not water it. Verdict: she did not cause it.

6. In tennis, a ball comes to my forehand. I see it, I have time, and I decide not to swing; I let it pass. It lands in. Verdict: my not swinging caused the ball to land in.

7. In January I decide not to give anything to charity this year. Somewhere abroad a child dies of an illness that a twenty dollar donation from me would have prevented. Verdict: I did not cause that death.

Cases 6 and 7 are the pair I cannot reconcile. In both, I could have acted, I did not, the outcome would not have happened if I had acted, and in 7 I even formed the intention in advance. The difference cannot be ability, foresight, or whether the outcome was avoidable. The only thing I can point to is that in 6 I was already inside an activity and the ball was mine to deal with, while in 7 I was never engaged in anything relevant at all. I have come across the suggestion that some omissions are really negative actions rather than bare absences, and that sounds like my distinction, but I cannot say what turns a bare absence into a negative action.

8. A machine raises an alarm only if two guards turn their keys at the same moment. Both are at their keys. Neither turns one. The fire spreads and the building burns. Neither guard alone could have made any difference. Verdict: both of them caused the fire to spread.

9. A machine raises an alarm if any one of twenty bystanders pulls it. None does. The building burns. Verdict: the group's collective failure caused it, but no individual bystander did — and I hold this even though, for each of them taken separately, the fire would not have spread if that person alone had pulled it.

So my questions. Is there one principle behind these verdicts, or am I using two? My own tentative rule is that an omission causes an outcome only when it is the non-performance of something within an activity the agent was already running, and cases 8 and 9 seem to add a separate requirement about the omission's standing in a set of omissions that jointly mattered. Please test that rule against all nine cases, state it back to me precisely, and tell me clause by clause which case forces each clause. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot keep — in particular whether case 2 has to be reassigned to case 3's verdict, and what I would be giving up if I did that.

I also need it to decide three cases I have not settled.

10. I set up an automated assistant to email our group's file to the teacher by midnight. I never entered the instruction. Nothing was sent. We received a zero. Did my omission cause the zero, did the assistant's omission cause it, or neither?

11. A school district never got around to installing carbon monoxide detectors in a boarding house; a leak kills a student. The official who would have signed the order left the post two years before. Whose omission, if anyone's, caused that death?

12. A town council voted down a flood barrier in 1970. In 2025 the river floods and two people die in a house that the barrier would have protected. Did the councillors' failure to build it cause those deaths?

Finally, give me one case of your own construction — one I have not listed — that your principle decides in a way you think I would find surprising, and say what it decides.
