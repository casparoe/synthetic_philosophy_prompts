I am seventeen, my mathematics stops at single-variable calculus, and for the past few months I have been reading on my own about Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity. There is one analogy that seems to be doing an enormous amount of work in that literature, and before I build anything on it I would like to know how much load it can actually carry.

The analogy is this: a scientific theory is like a compressed file. The observations are the raw file; the theory plus its initial conditions is the compressed archive; and the best theory is simply the one that shrinks the data most, the way the best compressor is the one that produces the smallest zip. On this picture Occam's razor is not a separate aesthetic principle that has to be argued for independently: it falls out of the mathematics, because the Solomonoff prior weights each candidate program by two to the minus its length, so the shortest program reproducing the data gets the most prior weight and its predictions come to dominate the mixture. The conclusions people then draw are that theory choice is objective, that there is a fact of the matter about which of two theories is simpler, that simplicity is the same virtue in physics and in biology, and that a machine which merely compressed well enough would eventually do science.

Here is what I cannot get past, and which I have started calling the translation tax. Any two universal languages can simulate one another, and the cost of the simulator is a constant independent of the data, which is supposed to make the choice of language irrelevant. But constant means independent of the input, not small, and it is paid on every single program. Consider two toy languages. Language A has, built in, a primitive that emits a sinusoid of given amplitude, period, and phase. Language B has only addition, multiplication, and a loop. Hand both a table of a few thousand numbers sampled from a real pendulum with some noise in it. In A, the shortest program says: sine, these three parameters, then the noise. In B, the shortest program either has to rebuild the sine out of arithmetic or, and this is what I suspect actually happens, just hardcodes the table, since once the data is shorter than the sine routine you would have to write, the shortest program that outputs the data is the data. In that case the winner on file size is a theory with no structure in it whatsoever, and it predicts that the pendulum stops moving.

So I would like an audit rather than a survey. Please give me the correspondence table explicitly: in the compression picture, what plays the role of the theory, the data, the decompressor, the file format, and the person who chooses it? Where does the mapping break? Which of the breaks matter for the three conclusions above and which are merely decorative? In particular I want to know what the analogy quietly imports that science does not have, whether the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity is the same problem as the language dependence or a different one, and whether the whole thing is being propped up by the fact that the decompressor is given away for free while in science the language of mathematics is itself something we had to discover.

And please commit to a verdict rather than arranging the options for me: does the file-size picture carry the conclusion that theory choice is objective, or is the objectivity an artifact of having fixed a language in advance? If the analogy can be repaired, say how; if it should be discarded, say what you would put in its place. Keep the notation light — I would rather have the argument than the formulas, though I can follow simple exponents — and flag the points where you are least confident and the places where you think my pendulum example is confused.
