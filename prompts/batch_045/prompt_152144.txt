I'm a junior majoring in cognitive science, and this semester I'm taking a seminar on predictive processing and active inference. Most of it I can follow at the level of the readings, but I keep getting stuck on the same thing: my professor insists that perception and action are just one minimisation seen from two directions, that everything hinges on how much precision a prediction carries, that expected free energy splits into a pragmatic and an epistemic part, and that the dark room objection is what stops the whole framework from predicting that agents should curl up somewhere unchanging. I believe all of that at arm's length, but I don't know what any of it would look like if you had to do it on purpose, in real time, with another person in the room.

The reason I want to know is that I've been a peer tutor in our writing center for three semesters and I'm training the new cohort in January. The work is repetitive in a way that a theory ought to help with: forty-five-minute drop-in appointments, six to ten a week, tutees arriving with everything from lab reports to personal statements, no authority over grades, a rule against editing the paper for them, a session note that goes back to the instructor, and end-of-semester evaluations that the center's funding partly depends on. My own instinct right now is to ask what the writer thinks is wrong before I say anything, and roughly half the time I get a shrug. What I'd like from you is a procedure I can actually hand to a new tutor, not a set of principles: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the tutor stops early and hands the session back to the writer, and where the procedure has to stop because the remaining question is one the writer has to answer for themselves. Please keep the theory doing real work in it — how a tutor would read the precision of a writer's expectations in forty-five minutes without any numbers, what the tutor should do differently when a writer's predictions are very confident versus very diffuse, when the right move is to get the writer to change their model versus to send them off to sample the page differently, and how to tell whether a session should be spent reducing uncertainty or just getting the thing finished. But be straight about the places where the translation fails: where precision is a metaphor I can't actually measure, where the story I tell afterwards would fit whatever happened, and which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing.

Then please run it on three cases, in American English, with the reasoning shown rather than skipped. First: a first-year who is completely sure his essay is about ambition in Macbeth and is wrong about that, confidently. Second: a multilingual graduate student who says everything is a problem and is right, in the sense that there are many surface errors and no single one to fix. Third, and this is the one I expect to dislike: a strong senior who books a slot every week with an already polished draft and mostly wants to be told it's fine, who is lovely, who rates me maximally, and who I suspect is using me the way the dark room objection says agents would use a dark room. If your procedure tells me to do something to that third person that will cost me her evaluations and possibly her feelings, say so plainly and tell me why the theory commits me to it — but also tell me where you think the theory is simply the wrong tool, and what a tutor should fall back on there. It needs to be teachable in an hour to someone with no cognitive science, and usable in a room where I can't say any of these words out loud.
