The committee I staff is deadlocked on a case and I have to draft the recommendation, so give me your read on our own record first, I've been staring at this to long to trust my own.

We run on a statute that was itself a compromise. Half the assembly thought we were buying truth with punishment and got the operative test written that way: amnesty may be granted where the value of what the applicant hands over exceeds the value of the punishment being given up. The other half couldn't kill that, so they attached a rider saying amnesty must not be granted for an act whose amnesty would defeat the purpose of the Act. Everyone in the room knew the two could collide and left it to us to work out, and the split is still there among us: half of us think we are running a trade, half think we are giving away something that was never ours to sell, and both halves voted for the statute.

Six decisions so far. A conscript who fired into a crowd on orders and killed two people, told us everything including who gave the order, said he would do it again: granted. The officer who gave that order, also full disclosure, also unrepentant: denied. A police interrogator who beat a detainee with a baton entirely on his own initiative, gave us a full account of how the interrogation room actually worked, and apologised to the family in the hearing: granted. A rebel fighter who shot a village headman she had decided was an informer, full disclosure, genuinely sorry, no orders: denied. A senior intelligence officer who authorised four disappearances and gave up two burial sites plus the names of the two men who carried them out, both long dead, said nothing about the other two and offered no apology: denied. A prison clerk who forged three death certificates so families were told their relatives had died of illness, handed over the whole paperwork chain, cried through his testimony: granted with a condition that he repeat it in public in his own district.

The one I have to write up: a man who for two years kept the secret register at the regional prison, who never struck anyone and never gave an order, and who three times deleted an entry when the interrogators asked, so that three men who had been killed became men who had never arrived. He says he did it to keep his job and his pension. He has now produced his own copy of the complete register, which for the first time gives roughly a hundred and forty families an official record of what happened to their relatives. Everyone else involved is dead, already amnestied, or out of reach, so he is the only person who could still be tried, and what he actually faces is a few years for forgery and falsifying records, not for killing.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I can see more than one rule that fits all six of our cases about equally well and that come apart on this one, and I want them set out cleanly with what each predicts here, which precedent is the closest analog and why, and what principle we would be adopting either way. Two things I especially want a view on: whether the value of what he hands over should be counted as the record the families get or as what it lets us prosecute, since on these facts those two come completely apart, and whether it matters that his is the last punishment left in the whole ledger. Also tell me the one further fact I should try to establish in a supplementary interview, the one that would do the most to settle it. And write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullets or bold, just an email.
