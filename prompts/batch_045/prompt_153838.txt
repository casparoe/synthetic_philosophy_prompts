I want to use Wittgenstein's private language argument in the second half of it, but I've only got it second hand and I dont trust my notes. Here's roughly what the episode claimed (paraphrasing from memory, so some of the messiness is probably mine):

- The target is the idea of a language whose words stand for things only the speaker could ever be aware of. The example is a diary where you write the sign S every time a particular feeling comes up.

- You can't give S a meaning by concentrating on the feeling and, as it were, pointing at it inwardly. Ordinary pointing works only against a whole setup in which pointing can misfire; here there's nothing that counts as using S correctly or incorrectly, because the only test is whether it seems right to me the next time. And if whatever seems right is right, then there's no such thing as being right — so the sign never got a meaning, and a private language is impossible.

- The host added that Wittgenstein also blocks the obvious escape: you might say you remember what S stood for, but memory is unreliable, so you could be misremembering, and a definition you can't reliably recall isn't a definition.

- The takeaway he drew: meaning requires other people. Language is essentially a shared social practice, and the reason any word of mine means anything is that other people can in principle check whether I'm using it the way they do. Private language impossible, therefore language is irreducibly communal.

- Then the bit that made me sit up. He says the beetle-in-a-box thing shows that the private inner object is really nothing at all — what's in the box might as well be empty, and so Wittgenstein is basically denying that subjective experience exists in the sense people usually mean it, which makes him a kind of behaviourist.

- And then he applies it to neural implants. Suppose I have an electrode that reliably detects a neural pattern, call it N, and I write S whenever the implant fires; the readout goes to my phone, so it's public in the ordinary sense. Now, he says, the word finally has a public criterion, so it's no longer a private language at all — S just means N is firing, and the private feeling drops out as idle. Which is exactly Wittgenstein's own point, since at one stage he imagines hooking the private sign up to a manometer reading and then says that whether I got the sensation right becomes irrelevant. The host goes further: once we have good enough neural readouts we can finally settle the question the argument was stuck on — whether my S is the same feeling as your S — because we can just compare the data. So a mature neuroscience dissolves the private-language worry, and with it the hard problem of consciousness.

The last bit is where my spider sense goes off, because if the argument is that there's no fact of the matter about whether I got it right, I dont see how better instruments can then go on to *answer* whether we're feeling the same thing. It feels like he's quietly reinstalling the very private object he just said drops out. But maybe I'm the one who's missing something, and the first four bullets might be off too.
