I'm a journalist writing a long piece on whether contemporary philosophy has anything left to learn from classical Indian debates about perspective, and I've found myself stuck on a question I can't report my way out of. My working frame is the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness (anekāntavāda), together with the two apparatuses usually read off from it: syādvāda, the theory of conditioned predication, on which any claim about reality holds only under some qualifying respect, often expressed by attaching a particle meaning roughly in some respect or perhaps; and nayavāda, the theory of partial standpoints, on which each of our descriptions captures one aspect of a complex object. The piece needs a single sentence a general reader can carry away, and the candidates on my desk are: the Jains invented relativism, the Jains invented contextualism, and both of those are anachronistic projections onto what is really a doctrine about the limits of human knowledge. Those are very different sentences, and I'd like a number attached to the choice rather than a survey of readings.

So here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage: that the best reconstruction of this package treats the qualifying operator as doing semantic work — that is, as shifting the truth conditions of the claim it qualifies, so that standpoint-relativity is a feature of what is true and not merely a marker of the speaker's uncertainty or of the incompleteness of human evidence about a standpoint-independent reality. Call this the truth-conditional reading; count it as vindicated if a careful reconstruction of the syādvāda and nayavāda machinery, plus the sevenfold schema (saptabhaṅgī) that combines affirmation, denial, their combination, and a fourth mode usually rendered as indescribable, is better explained by standpoint-indexed truth than by an epistemic-hedge reading. Give me your headline number on that, and then decompose it. The sub-questions I have in mind, which you should feel free to re-cut or add to, are: the indexing question, whether the standpoint parameter is eliminable — whether there is, in principle, some complete standpoint-free description of reality, such that relativity is only our cognitive limitation, perhaps available to an omniscient being — or whether the parameter is constitutive, with no such description existing even in principle; the aggregation problem, whether the partial descriptions delivered by the several standpoints can be conjoined into one consistent total account, or whether they conflict irreducibly, in which case the system needs something like a paraconsistent or many-valued logic rather than just an index on the truth predicate; what the fourth mode actually is, whether it marks a truth-value gap, a failure of expressive resources, or a determinate truth we cannot state, since the answer to that question seems to decide whether the apparatus is a logic, a semantics, or a discipline of speech; whether the doctrine applies to itself, and whether the familiar move of exempting the second-order claim produces a regress, a contradiction, or nothing worse than the benign self-exemption that any contextualist needs; what I'll call the toleration gap, whether non-absolutism about truth yields intellectual toleration or nonviolence in belief on its own, or whether that popular reading needs an extra normative premise the texts do not obviously supply; and, underneath all of these, whether anekāntavāda even names a single stable doctrine across its sources rather than a family of loosely related ones, which would cap how confident any reconstruction can be. A number and one line of justification for each, then say how they combine — I'd especially like to know which terms are not independent, since several look like they move together — and then tell me which single term your headline number is most sensitive to.

Two more things, both of which matter for how I use this in print. First, for calibration: the analytic philosopher I interviewed, who works on relativism and gradable adjectives, puts the truth-conditional reading at about 0.7; a scholar of Jain philosophy I spoke to puts it nearer 0.15, on the grounds that the semantic reading is a twentieth-century artifact. If those numbers are both defensible, I want to know which sub-question their disagreement most likely lives in, not just that reasonable people differ. Second, be explicit about your own exposure: flag which of the sub-questions are really questions about what the Sanskrit sources say, and so not resolvable without textual work you can't do here, as against questions of philosophical reconstruction that stand or fall on argument; tell me which term you would abandon first under pressure; tell me what single argument or piece of evidence would move your headline number by at least 0.2 in either direction; and mark where you suspect your own reconstruction is anachronistic or is quietly assuming a modern distinction the material doesn't make. I'll do the sourcing myself, so please don't spend the answer on bibliography or on telling me who to read — I want the reasoning, with its weak points labelled.
