I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly print with some audio — and once a month I host a live night in the back room of a bar: short talks for a general audience, then a discussion bit. Around 25 people, mixed ages, mostly not philosophy people, they've usually had a drink or two. I've got 45 minutes and I want to do the private language thing with them: the idea of a word whose meaning is supposedly fixed by pointing, inwardly, at something only you can get at. Not a lecture. I want them to bump into it.

Hard constraints: no jargon up front, and don't say Wittgenstein's name until the last five minutes. What they should walk out feeling is why "it seems right to me now" isn't the same as "I got it right," and why adding more people to the room doesn't automatically fix that.

Here's the wrinkle I care most about, and it's the reason I'm asking you rather than just doing the obvious version: I don't want the standard setup where private = hopeless and public = fine, because that isn't the interesting part and it's a bit of a rigged demo. So build the activity around a case where a private signal and a noisy public proxy both exist. Concretely: each person has access to some inner thing only they can feel, AND to a readout that correlates with it but imperfectly — call it 70% reliable, and they're told the number. They have to coin a name for the inner thing and then actually use it across several rounds. What I want them to feel is (a) the private part gives them no way to distinguish remembering-right from seeming-to-remember-right; (b) the noisy public readout looks like it rescues them, but it can't straightforwardly, because they can't calibrate it against the thing it's a proxy for without already having the private word working — and then they're stuck choosing whether to trust the readout or their own sense when the two disagree, with no settled fact about who's right.

Design me the actual runnable thing: rules, what people physically do, roles if any, materials I can carry in a backpack, a minute-by-minute shape for the 45 minutes, and at least one round with small real stakes (points, or something silly-but-actually-counted) so the choices aren't costless. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific claims, and tells me where to shut up and let them argue versus where I have to supply something.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong. I can already see two: someone will say "but I know when I'm in pain," and someone will turn it into a thing about qualia and I'll loose the room. Give me a way to handle those plus whatever else you expect to blow up. And be straight about what the activity can't do — which part of the argument can't be staged in a bar and has to come out of my mouth in a sentence or two, and how to say that without it sounding like I've started lecturing.

Last bit, and it matters for my purposes: I'm going to write this up afterwards, so in your answer keep a clear seperation between what's settled among people who actually work on this and what's genuinely fought over. I mean things like whether Wittgenstein is offering an argument at all, what exactly he's denying, whether the beetle-in-the-box bit is doing the work people assume it does, and whether any of this threatens ordinary talk about sensations. Don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the standard line — I'd rather have the fights named and left open than smoothed over — and flag anywhere the activity itself depends on a reading I'd have to pick sides on.
