I am applying for a twelve-month research fellowship from my faculty's humanities research board, and the application calls for a two-page proposal, assessed on three criteria: the significance of the question, the precision with which it is stated, and the feasibility of the method within the period. Reviewers are philosophers but mostly not metaphysicians, so the proposal has to be intelligible to an outsider while remaining sharp enough to be judged. I would like you to draft it.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a project. The dispute between endurantism and perdurantism is, I suspect, not one dispute but a tangle of separable theses that the literature habitually runs together. Roughly: (i) a mereological thesis about whether persisting things have proper temporal parts; (ii) a thesis about what persistence consists in — whether it is identity across times, or some counterpart- or continuity-theoretic relation among things at different times; (iii) a thesis about which things are the subjects of persistence claims, that is, about what our quantifiers range over when we say that something persists. Survey treatments generally present (i) as the whole dispute and assume the other two travel with it. But they do not obviously travel together: the view on which there are only momentary things and persistence is a matter of relations among them, with no long-lived aggregates over and above the stages, is standardly classified as a version of perdurantism even though it apparently denies that anything has temporal parts of anything.

My conjecture is that once the three theses are pulled apart, the considerations supposed to settle the dispute stop pointing the same way. The argument from temporary intrinsics; the worry that the perdurantist has only exchanged intrinsic properties for time-indexed relations; the problem of how many things there are when something persists; the difference between the two views' treatment of fission and fusion cases — these seem to me to bear on different dimensions, and some may be neutral between the positions as ordinarily stated. If that is right, the debate has been miscut, and at least one of the cells in the reconstructed space is unoccupied. The one I keep returning to is the combination of denying temporal parts with denying that persistence is identity; I cannot tell whether this is a coherent position that has been classified out of existence by assumption, or whether it collapses on inspection into something familiar.

What I need from the proposal:

A precise statement of the question, phrased so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than a gesture at a research area. If my conjecture needs to be weakened to become tractable, weaken it and say what has been given up.

An argument that the existing positions leave the question open — what the standard framing presupposes, and why separating the theses is not simply a change of notation.

The method, in phases I could actually follow over twelve months: what I would do to establish that the dimensions really are independent, what kind of case or formal apparatus would test their independence, and how I would decide whether a residual disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, which I take to be arguing directly for one of the traditional positions, or attempting a general theory of identity over time.

The possible outcomes and what would decide between them — including the outcome that the recut space turns out to contain no new viable position.

An honest statement of what would count as the project failing, distinguishing failure as an uninteresting true result from failure as not having produced a result at all.

A realistic scope for twelve months, including which parts of the topic I should fix by assumption rather than defend — the background theory of time, the restriction to material objects, the treatment of persons — and what I would cut first if the project outgrew the year.

A short closing paragraph on why the question matters to anyone who does not work on persistence: what it would show about identity over time generally, about composition, or about how one ought to argue that a philosophical dispute is verbal.

Please keep it to proposal length and do not pad it with a literature survey or a bibliography; name positions and arguments rather than papers, and I will supply the references myself.
