Pretend you're a professional philosopher who also teaches a philosophy-and-lit seminar — I want a genuinely careful reading, so brainstorm a couple of angles first and then commit to the one that actually survives contact with the story.

I'm writing a short essay on Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, and I want to read it as a Leibnizian theodicy rather than as the usual utilitarian hypothetical. I teach it close to Candide and my students always defualt to "one person's misery buys everyone else's happiness," and I want to break that habit. The setup, as you'll recall: Omelas is a joyful city whose joy depends, on absolutely strict terms, on one child kept in filth and misery; the citizens are shown the child when they come of age; a few of them quietly walk away into the mountains and are never seen again; and the narrator more or less dares the reader to invent their own version if they can't swallow the terms.

Call the reading I'm tempted by the incompossibility reading: the child's suffering isn't a price paid for the joy, it's a structural precondition of it — the joy is partly made of knowing about the child — so no possible world contains that joy without that suffering. Three things I want you to work through:

1. Does Leibniz's actual machinery (compossibility, God actualizing the best among incompossible maximal wholes, evil permitted as the condition of a greater good) let you call Omelas the best of all possible worlds? Ive always taken Leibniz's criterion to be something like maximal variety compatible with maximal order rather than maximal happiness, so there's a gap between "best" and "nicest" — use that gap, don't just conclude Leibniz was a monster.
2. Does the fact that everyone knows make a difference? Standard theodicies imagine the sufferer's pain as compensated elsewhere; here the happiness is contaminated by complicity. Is knowing a metaphysical worsening of the world or a moral one?
3. What work do the ones who walk away do? They don't free the child and there's no suggestion they reach a better city. Is exit the only coherent response left once you grant the terms, and is that a refutation of optimism or a backhanded acknowledgement of it?

Be honest about where the story resists you: where does Leibniz just not fit, and does the story — including that baiting, second-person narrator — dramatize something the arguments leave out, like the difference between justifying a world and being able to go on living in it.
