I run a twice-yearly professional-development workshop for about twenty mid-career civil servants in a government policy unit, in a 100-minute slot. These are people who will themselves write or commission choice-architecture guidance in the coming year; they arrive split between thinking that this kind of work is obviously just common sense and thinking that it is obviously manipulation, and both camps are articulate. Available materials: a room with movable chairs and one door, a projector I would rather not switch on before minute 60, index cards, sticky dots, two clearly different snacks, paper sign-up sheets, and pens. No phones, no laptops, no money, and no pre-reading. I want an activity, not a lecture, and I want the technical vocabulary — paternalism, nudge, default, manipulation, autonomy — to appear for the first time only in the last fifteen minutes.

The substance is the ethics of influencing other people's choices by arranging their options rather than by changing their incentives or forbearing anything. Three distinctions matter to me, and I want the participants to collide with each one before it is named.

The first: some influences keep working on a target who has been told, in plain language, exactly what the influence is and what it is for; others lose most of their force the moment they are made explicit. Call the first kind disclosure-stable and the second disclosure-fragile. This cuts across the usual classification of policy instruments — a subsidy, a plainly stated fee, or a mandatory reminder is disclosure-stable, while an option-ordering effect, a decoy, or a pre-ticked box that works through inertia is not — and I want participants to notice that the standard "freedom of choice is preserved because opting out costs nothing" defence seems to give the same verdict to both kinds, which is what makes them uneasy.

The second: the size of an intervention's immediate effect and the amount of a person's downstream life that it ends up determining are different quantities. A pre-ticked box that also fixes the terms of next year's automatic re-enrolment, or that sets the menu from which all later choices are made, has high leverage while looking trivial at the moment of choice. I want participants to feel that a nudge can be trivially easy to resist — one tick, one click — and still be the thing that settles the whole path, and that the cost of resisting is a cost of noticing, not a cost of effort.

The third: designing for targets who know that someone is designing for them. In the later part of the session, tell the groups that a substantial and growing minority of the population they are designing for has read a plain-language guide to these techniques, recognises them, and resents them. I want them to have to revise their design under that condition, and to discover that some of what they were happy to do to the trusting they are unwilling to do to the suspicious — or vice versa — and to have to say why.

Concretely, I'd like: a design brief with a multi-stage decision that exhibits leverage (enrolment at stage one, automatic escalation at stage two, a fund or provider choice at stage three, reconfirmation at stage four) and a described population that is heterogeneous in a way that matters — procrastinators, people with principled objections to the outcome, people who distrust the department, people for whom the default is genuinely bad; two rounds of small-group design with a mid-point change of condition; and at least one real, low-stakes choice architecture imposed on the participants themselves during the session without their prior knowledge, arranged so that they experience being on the receiving end while they are theorising about doing it to others. Please say plainly how you would handle the fact that imposing that on them is itself the thing under discussion, and whether, when, and how you would get their consent for it.

Deliverables I need, in runnable detail: roles and group sizes; rules; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes including setup, the moment of revelation, and the transition to explicit vocabulary; what participants are asked to write down and hand in; how the room is arranged and when it is rearranged; and any scoring or payoff structure if one helps. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three distinctions above, that uses the participants' own words and disagreements rather than my formulations, and that shows where the "it was for their own good" condition becomes indeterminate because the room disagrees about whose good and about who counts as a competent judge of it. Finally, be honest about three things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it; which participants will refuse to play and what I should say to them; and which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce at all, so that I will simply have to state it myself.
