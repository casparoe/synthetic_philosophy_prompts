A popular book on rationality that I've been reading presents what it calls "the master argument" for expected utility maximization, and I want to know how much of it to trust. Its version runs roughly like this:

"If your degrees of confidence don't obey the probability calculus — say your odds on A and on not-A don't add up to even odds, or you're more confident of A than of A-or-B — then a bookie who knows your odds can offer you a set of bets, each of which you yourself judge fair or better, that loses you money in every possible outcome. That's a Dutch book. The same idea works for preferences: if your choices cycle, a money pump sells you A for B plus a penny, B for C plus a penny, and C for A plus a penny, leaving you with what you started with minus three cents. And if you violate independence, as in the Allais case, a pump can be built the same way. Nothing here depends on any empirical claim about the world: it's arithmetic plus the fact that a sure loss is worse than no loss. So expected utility maximization isn't a preference or a piece of advice about how to live; violating it is logically incoherent, on a par with believing both P and not-P. Nor can you escape by resolving in advance to stick with one option, since the bookie can offer you the whole sequence of trades as a single package, and because you'd accept each trade in it, you must accept the package — which is a guaranteed loss. Hence the only coherent decision rule is to maximize expected utility, and preferences of the Allais and Ellsberg shapes are simply mistakes."

Two things bother me, though I can't tell whether they're real problems. First, the book never says what the bookie is supposed to know about me, and it slides between calling a violator incoherent and calling a violator exploitable, which look like different claims; my instinct is that I could simply decline to trade with anyone who seems to be reading my mind. Second, the package move at the end feels as though it assumes what it needs to prove, but I can't say where.

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually defends these arguments would endorse, what is a standard simplification, and what is subtly wrong or question-begging. Specifically:

- Does the Dutch book and money pump apparatus really deliver expected utility maximization, or something weaker? If weaker, what has to be added to get the rest, and where does the added premise come from?
- Is "nothing beyond arithmetic" right? Which substantive premises are doing work behind the scenes?
- Does the argument work for the Allais or Ellsberg cases in the same way it works for intransitivity, or is that an overstatement?
- Does the single-package reply dispose of the resolute-chooser objection, or does it smuggle in an assumption about how a bundle of trades is evaluated?
- What is the actual relationship between the charge of incoherence and the charge of exploitability — is one reducible to the other?
- Does the argument as stated establish countable additivity, and does it pin down a unique probability function and a unique utility function, or leave something undetermined?
- Once the inaccurate parts are corrected, does the book's final conclusion survive?

If there's a cleaner way to state what these arguments do establish, give that formulation at the end.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — how much of the summary a specialist would accept — then give the claim-by-claim reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
