You are a professional philosopher who specialises in value theory and in the practical ethics of commemoration, and who has spent time advising memorial commissions and curriculum committees. Take this seriously and work carefully: before you write the final answer, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming candidate requirements freely, including ones you then reject, so I can see what you considered and cut. Then give me the finished specification.

The situation: I have no academic background in philosophy, only a long private interest, and I have ended up on a volunteer committee with an unenviable job. We have a fixed budget, a fixed amount of physical space, and a fixed number of teaching hours, and we have to divide all three among several historical atrocities we are being asked to commemorate: cases on the order of the Nazi genocide, the transatlantic slave trade, and the deliberate starvation of a colonised population. Whatever we do will be public, and descendants of the victims and survivors will read our reasons. So ranking is being forced on us whether or not ranking is a decent thing to do, and I would like a specification before I read or invent any particular ranking method.

Write me the desiderata that a theory of comparative magnitude for atrocities would have to meet to be good enough for a decision of exactly this kind: a committee with limited resources, no research staff, and an obligation to give reasons in public. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and why that case earns it a place on the list. Then mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable for adequacy and which are really preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements.

Here are the puzzles I keep circling, which I want your list to speak to rather than skirt. Please treat them as the raw material for the requirements, and tell me frankly if any of them is a bad test:

The currency problem. A ranking has to say what is being compared: deaths, life-years lost, suffering, stolen futures, destroyed languages and kinship structures, terror, the corruption of the perpetrators, the damage done to everyone who watched and did nothing. If a theory needs conversion rates between these, say so and say what would justify one.

The replacement test, which is my own invention and the one I trust most. Suppose a people is destroyed and, a century later, an unrelated population lives in the same place, speaks a different language, and is on average no worse off than the destroyed people would have been. Has anything bad happened here beyond the killing itself, and how much? A theory that answers "almost nothing extra" has built in a very specific assumption, and I want that assumption surfaced as a requirement rather than left implicit.

Scale saturation. I notice that my own reaction to two hundred thousand deaths and to two million deaths is roughly the same, and I don't know whether that shows something about magnitude or only about me. A theory must say what it does when our sense of magnitude flattens out, and whether it is allowed to rank by a number that nobody can feel.

The survivor's remainder. Some of the harm of an atrocity is carried afterwards by the people who lived: the grief, the inherited poverty, the broken families, the fear passed down. This does not track the death count. Say whether a theory must include it, and what goes wrong if it does.

Manner and will. Hold the outcomes fixed: two events each kill the same number of people in the same amount of pain, but one is deliberate extermination and the other is indifference that foreseeably kills. Does the theory count the intention as extra badness, or only the outcome? Both answers cost something, and I want to know which cost is worse.

Kind versus degree. I have a stubborn intuition that destroying a whole people, language and all, is not merely a bigger quantity of the same thing as killing many people within a surviving people, but something different in kind. If a theory is purely additive it cannot say this. Tell me whether "different in kind" is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice, and what it costs to include.

The archive shadow. Perpetrators destroy records, and the best-documented atrocities are often the ones committed by losers or by literate bureaucracies. Any method we can actually run will therefore be biased toward the well-recorded. A requirement I care about: the theory must degrade honestly under missing evidence and must not quietly award high rank to whoever kept the best paperwork. Tell me how to state this so it binds.

The obscenity question, which I refuse to drop. Many people say that ranking evils at all demotes the victims of whichever event comes second. I want this handled inside the specification rather than as an objection to it: a theory should say which verdicts it permits — greater, lesser, equal, on a par and incomparable, or silence — and should not collapse "equal in magnitude" with "cannot be compared." It should also distinguish ranking because a decision requires it from ranking for its own sake, and say something about who is permitted to do the ranking, since our committee is not composed of victims.

Please also do the part I most want to see: identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and be explicit that the list then becomes a result rather than a shopping list. I suspect at least two collisions — between demanding a verdict for every pair and allowing genuine incomparability, and between using only evidence the archives can bear and being able to rank badly documented atrocities at all — but find the ones I have missed.

Finally, two short things. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive approaches, since that tells me where the real argument is. And then, briefly, score three or four families of approach against your own list: ranking by death toll or lost life-years, ranking by the intention and meaning of the act, the view that refuses verdicts and permits only parity, and the view that ranks by what is still reparable or by who still has a live claim on us. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard is for orientation only.
