You are a professional philosopher who specialises in moral psychology and the ethics of responsibility, and who also advises organisations on how to handle incidents and blame. Please think about this carefully before you answer: begin with a few lines of private brainstorming in which you list the distinctions you intend to rely on and, just as importantly, the ones you are tempted to blur, and only then give me your answer. Write in British English throughout.

Here is my situation. I manage a small engineering team. Two of my engineers, on the same afternoon, each pushed a change to a payments service without the manual review step our process requires. For the purposes of this question, please treat everything I would want to hold fixed as held fixed: they had the same kind of reason (a deadline), the same quality of evidence that the change was low-risk, the same level of care, the same intentions, the same track record. The only difference is how things turned out. One of them corrupted several hundred customer bills over a bank holiday weekend and we spent a fortnight cleaning it up. The other's change did nothing, and would have done nothing, because of a traffic-routing quirk that nobody could have predicted: the dangerous path in the code was only reachable under a load pattern that happened not to occur.

Three complications, which are the parts I actually care about.

First: we have since found that a third engineer made exactly this kind of unreviewed change six months ago, and that his change could never have caused any harm at all. The feature it touched had been silently switched off by a flag months earlier, and he had no way of knowing. So there was apparent risk but no real risk. From the standpoint of what each engineer had reason to expect at the time, he and my lucky engineer look identical; looking back, one of them rolled a die and won and the other was never rolling anything. Does that difference have any moral cash value, or is it metaphysics?

Second: we only learned about the harmful change because a customer complained. Our monitoring would have caught perhaps one incident in five of this kind. So whether an engineer ends up in the position of the unlucky one or the lucky one depends in part on whether we happen to find out. That seems to make blame hostage to something even further from the agent than the outcome was. But there is a counter-thought I cannot dismiss: if we detect only a fifth of the harm, outcome-based consequences may be the only incentive we have, even a badly noisy one.

Third: my own proposal, which I want you to test rather than to flatter. I want to split our response into two things — a backward-looking judgement about how blameworthy someone is, which I think should not depend on outcomes, and a forward-looking practice of accountability, which I think may legitimately depend on outcomes because it shapes future behaviour, signals what the team cares about, and is what the people affected by an incident need from us. I like this distinction, but I cannot tell whether it is an answer or a way of changing the subject. In particular, does the second half quietly smuggle back in everything I was trying to keep out of the first? And if the two really can come apart, what do I say to the unlucky engineer, who will very likely feel guilty in a way the lucky one does not, and who will certainly notice whether we treat the two of them differently or pretend not to?

I do not know whether philosophy has a name for this pattern of problem, and I do not need the label or a reading list. What I need is this: (a) what we should actually do on Monday — whether the unlucky engineer gets a written warning, whether the lucky one gets nothing, whether the third engineer is in a different category, and how the incident review should be worded; (b) how I should think about my own case, since twenty years ago I did precisely what my unlucky engineer did, nothing happened, and I have never quite stopped noticing that; and (c) which of the distinctions above is load-bearing and which is merely cosmetic. If the philosophically cleanest answer and the answer you would act on as a manager come apart, say so and say which one you would act on.

One more thing about how I want the answer pitched. Please separate clearly, as you go, between what is settled among people who work on questions like this and what is genuinely contested between competent people who know the material. Do not present a contested view as the consensus, and do not present your own preferred view as though it were simply what everyone thinks. Where you are relying on a distinction that some people in the field reject outright, flag it as such. And tell me which single consideration, if it turned out to be mistaken, would most change the advice you have given me.
