Im writing a novel and ive backed myself into a philosophical corner that I dont have the vocabulary for. Setup: my protagonist is a paintings conservator who got pushed out of a museum job, and she now makes money by reconstructing paintings that were destroyed — works that definitely existed, listed in inventories and in one case recorded in a mezzotint made after it, but that nobody alive has seen. Her versions are, as far as anyone can tell from the surviving record, exactly right; she sells them as the real thing. In the third act one of them is hailed as a masterpiece, and the experts start saying it's finer than anything else surviving from that workshop, possibly finer than the lost original ever was.

Two characters argue about whether what she did is wrong, and both of them reach for an analogy. The critic who made her career says a forgery is just a lie told in paint: the wrong of it is entirely in the deception, and deception needs someone who could be deceived. Nobody can be deceived about a canvas that vanished in the 19th century, and what she produced is as good as, maybe better than, what was lost — so on his view there's no wrong worth the name and she's closer to a restorer than a forger. The dealer who's been quietly buying her output says the opposite: an original is like an autograph. What you're paying for is the causal chain back to the hand that made it, so a visually identical object is a photocopy of a signature, worth roughly nothing however good it looks. That, he says, is exactly why her work is only valuable while everyone believes it's real, and why exhibiting the same canvases openly as reconstructions would destroy the price rather than redeem her. As a novelist that second one makes me uneasy, because nobody thinks a reprint of a novel is a forgery or that a band covering a song is faking it — so the autograph picture can't be the whole story of what an original is, and I have a vague memory of a distinction (Goodman?) between arts where any exact copy counts as an instance of the work and arts where it doesn't. Please don't give me the reference or a reading list; I just want to know if the thought is right and where it leaves painting.

What I'd like is an audit of both analogies. Give me the explicit mapping for each — in the lie picture, what plays the role of the speaker, the hearer, the false proposition, the intent to deceive; in the autograph picture, what plays the role of the signature, the photocopy, the signer, the chain of custody — and then where each one breaks, with the breaks sorted into ones that matter for the conclusion each man is trying to draw and ones that are just noise. The critic wants no victim therefore no wrong, arguably a service to art history; the dealer wants provenance is the whole of the value, always. And I especially want the two wrinkles my plot hinges on: that she was replacing something that really existed rather than inventing a new old master out of whole cloth, and that her version may be the better picture. Does either conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, is there a better analogy for whatever is genuinely wrong (if anything is) with what she did, and what should my protagonist end up believing?
