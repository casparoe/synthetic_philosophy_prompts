so I've been poking around conservation stuff lately and I keep hitting the fact that basically the whole machinery runs on species, and I can't tell whether that's a deep philosophical commitment or just beurocratic inertia. Here's the bundle of things I mean:

- The lists of things worth protecting are lists of species (plus, in the US at least, subspecies and even mere "distinct population segments" of a species).
- Biodiversity gets reported as a count of species, and money, recovery plans and staff time get allocated per listed species.
- To get on the list you generally have to be a *named* species, and to get named you need a published description with a physical type specimen sitting in a museum somewhere (or these days sometimes a sequence from one). A population everyone agrees is genetically weird and ecologically on its own doesn't get anything until somebody does the paperwork.
- When taxonomists split one species into three, the number of protected things goes up overnight without a single animal changing; when they lump three into one, it goes down.
- But the same system seems to treat hybrids as a *loss*. As far as I can tell mixed animals often aren't protected, and managers sometimes remove them to keep a listed lineage's genome clean — even though interbreeding is sometimes exactly how the lineage is avoiding extinction.

Now the bit that got me thinking. A big funder I follow has switched, or is trialling, a rule where the units of conservation aren't named species at all: they cluster genome sequences at a fixed cutoff and fund the resulting clusters, recomputing them every year as more samples come in. So a unit can merge into its neighbour and vanish purely because someone sequenced more animals, and people are furious that the funder is "deleting species" while the actual animals are still there and still rare. Under the old rule that couldn't happen, because the unit was pinned to a name and a type specimen.

So: what does the species-based practice presuppose about what species are? Does spending money on species-as-units require some kind of realism — species as real lineages, branches on the tree of life, historical individuals with a beginning and an end — or could a thoroughly instrumentalist story (species as convenient bookkeeping, a grid we lay down for our own purposes) license the exact same practice? Does the type-specimen business fit the lineage picture at all, or does it quietly commit you to something more like an exemplar or cluster view, and is that an inconsistency in the practice or just harmless?

Same question about the two asymmetries: why does fine-grained within-species structure count as something to protect, while admixture between species counts as something to prevent? What has to be true of species for both of those to be sensible at once? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out — an ecological or functional account where a hybrid doing the same job in the same niche is no loss at all, or a view on which only individual organisms and their lives matter? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where would several quite different theories all sign off on it?

Last thing: if we actually dropped species as the accounting unit and funded by ecological function, or by assemblages, or by individual animals and their prospects, what would concretely have to change in the practice, and what would we lose — I'm thinking about things like whether anyone could be held to account, or whether the numbers would even be stable enough to budget with. I don't need names of theories or who said what, I just want the implicit story reconstructed, what it strictly needs versus what it merely goes well with, and where it's underdetermined.
