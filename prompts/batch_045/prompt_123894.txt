I am giving a colloquium talk next month—forty-five minutes with twenty minutes for questions—to a joint session of my department's Cognition and Mind seminar, and what I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defence of the view itself (the paper is written; I need to be ready for the room).

The talk is about Aunt Hillary, the talking ant colony in Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach*. It defends two claims.

T1: Aunt Hillary is not a metaphor. The colony literally has beliefs and desires, and the individual ants do not; the colony, not the ant, is the subject of the intentional states expressed in her signals.

T2: In the variant colonies I construct, there is frequently no determinate answer to how many colony-level minds are present, or which of two overlapping colonies is the subject of a given signal. This indeterminacy is not a reductio of the view but a consequence of taking composition seriously: our practice of counting minds is calibrated to organisms with skins and bottlenecks, not to a joint in nature.

My argument for T1 runs in three steps. First, a signal's content is fixed by its role in a colony-wide economy of signal production and consumption, not by what any ant is in when she emits it; a signal means what it does because of what it reliably does to downstream signal traffic. Second, no single ant is in a state that carries that content—an ant's own states (route memory, odour template, satiety) are about her route, her odour, her hunger, and are not about food, the nest or the weather in the way the colony's signals are, so there is no double counting and no competition between the colony's contents and the ants'. Third, the colony satisfies what I call the closure condition: its signals regulate the production of further signals of the same kind, including signals whose function is to respond to the failure or absence of other signals, so the system is self-maintaining at exactly the level of description at which I want to say it has content.

Two invented cases do the heavy lifting, and the audience will not have seen either.

The mute-reader colony: an intervention leaves every ant able to detect and respond to a class of signals while unable to produce them (the emitting gland is blocked, the receptors are intact). The colony continues to act appropriately on signals it can no longer emit—comprehension without production. I use this to argue that the vehicles of Aunt Hillary's thoughts are not the ants' emissions.

The one-way bridge: two colonies joined by a corridor treated so that trail signals can be laid in one direction only. Colony A's foragers recruit colony B's workers, never the reverse; the two share part of one signal economy and not the rest. I use this to argue that in partially fused colonies the question of how many minds there are has no answer, although everything about the underlying causal story is perfectly determinate—the indeterminacy lives in the mind-counting, not in the facts.

The room will be about thirty people. Roughly: two behavioural ecologists and one insect neuroscientist from the animal behaviour group, who work on individual learning, path integration and private information, and who regard colony-level talk as a convenient modelling shorthand; one philosopher of the 4E and dynamical-systems persuasion, sympathetic to anti-individualism and distributed cognition but hostile to positing content-bearing states at all; one analytically trained representationalist who is happy in principle with sub-personal content but will demand an account of misrepresentation and error; one animal ethicist working on invertebrate welfare and pest-control policy, who will press on what follows practically if individual ants are not subjects but colonies are; and three or four multi-agent-systems and swarm-robotics researchers, who will want to know whether any of this is empirically testable and whether a swarm of identical agents running fixed policies would count. The chair is methodologically conservative and fond of Hofstadter. Nobody present is a Hofstadter scholar; perhaps a third have read the book, and most know the ant-colony-as-brain analogy only by reputation.

What I would like:

The eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by combined likelihood and damage, each attributed to the kind of person who will raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it. I want to hear the ecologists' vocabulary of confounds, controls and alternative explanations at the individual level; the 4E theorist's coupling, affordances and scepticism about representations; the representationalist's correctness conditions and the possibility of a false signal; the ethicist's welfare subjects and precaution; the swarm researchers' ablations and metrics. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is soft, and whether there is something in it I should concede up front rather than be forced into.

Then, separately: the one objection I should concede wholesale rather than fight; the single reply that has to land if the talk is to survive; the question I am hoping nobody asks; and a suggestion for how to use the first ninety seconds of the question period, since I expect the first question to set the register for everything after it.

Please write in British English. Do not give me citations, references, reading lists or suggestions for further reading—I have no library access where I am going and cannot follow anything up, so everything you give me has to stand on its own.
