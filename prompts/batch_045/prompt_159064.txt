I'm writing a seminar paper at the intersection of early modern metaphysics and monetary theory, and I've gotten into an argument with a friend who works in payments policy, so I'd like you to construct the strongest case you can for one side of it. The position I want defended is this: Berkeley's immaterialism is not merely incidentally compatible with his monetary writings — it commits its adherents to a nominalist rather than a metallist theory of money, and it does so while leaving the metallist no fallback of anchoring money's value in a mind-independent natural kind.

The textual premise is The Querist, published in 1735, a long series of numbered rhetorical questions about the Irish economy in which Berkeley also sketched a plan for a national bank. Query 23 asks whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter? Query 25 then asks whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof? Set against this, define the metallist as holding that money is, or ought to be, a commodity whose value would survive the collapse of convention because it is grounded in the physical properties of a scarce substance; define the nominalist as holding that money is essentially a token, a unit of account, and a transferable claim whose value is constituted by conventions, expectations, and institutional regularities.

What I want is argument, not exposition, so let me say where I think the inference is fragile. First, my friend concedes that immaterialism rules out intrinsic value in any naive sense, but insists it can still preserve a mind-independent distinction between a substance whose supply is fixed by nature and a token whose supply is fixed by agreement: gold's scarcity is a fact about the world, whereas the scarcity of a purely digital token — say, a cryptocurrency with a hard-coded supply cap and a difficulty-adjusted issuance schedule — is only a fact about what people have agreed to enforce. I don't see why an immaterialist gets to keep that contrast, since on that metaphysics the fixity of gold's supply is itself only an observed regularity in the order of ideas, sustained by the same divine legislator as everything else; my friend's reply is that regularities differ in robustness, and that this is a difference that makes all the difference. Second, and relatedly: if money is only a ticket or counter, and the value of things is a compounded proportion of demand and plenty, then it looks as though a Berkeleyan has no principled objection to the sovereign simply minting more tickets — yet Berkeley clearly did care about credit, industry, and public order, and presumably did not think his bank should issue credit without limit.

Please argue for the position. Assume immaterialism as a premise rather than defending or reconstructing it, and spend your effort on the monetary argument; I've supplied the only passages I care about, so don't cite or appeal to secondary literature or to other primary sources. A strong answer will probably do four things: identify precisely which metallist premise fails once matter-independent substance is denied; settle whether the natural-versus-conventional contrast collapses or merely gets reinterpreted; apply the result to the digital-token case, ideally in a way that says something non-obvious about whether such a token is a better or worse instance of a Berkeleyan counter than gold; and answer the debasement objection by explaining what, if anything, replaces the metallist's external anchor as a constraint on issuing more tickets. Steelman the metallist reply before rebutting it, keep the whole thing to roughly 800 to 1,200 words, and end by committing to the position rather than by balancing considerations.
