I work as a psychometrician at a large professional assessment body. My working life is item response modelling, differential item functioning, measurement invariance across candidate groups, differential prediction studies, standard setting, and the validation of the cut scores that turn a continuous score into a pass/fail decision. A governance committee has asked me to explain why the fairness literature in machine learning keeps saying that its own criteria are mutually unsatisfiable, and I would rather translate that claim into the vocabulary my colleagues and I already use than import theirs. Assume I am fluent in my own vocabulary — do not explain DIF, invariance hierarchies, attenuation or range restriction to me — and that I have read perhaps a dozen papers on the other side: enough to follow the algebra, not enough to know what is being argued about.

Here are the results as I have them. First, for a binary decision rule with a fixed threshold: within-group calibration (among candidates in a given group assigned the same risk value, the proportion who actually turn out positive equals that value), equal false positive rates across groups, and equal false negative rates across groups cannot all hold, except where the groups have the same prevalence of the outcome or the score separates the outcomes perfectly. Second, for the score itself rather than a threshold: within-group calibration, equality across groups of the mean score among those who turn out positive, and equality across groups of the mean score among those who turn out negative cannot all hold, apart from the same degenerate cases. Third — and this may be my own reconstruction rather than anyone's stated theorem — every criterion of that kind is a function of the joint distribution of group, score and outcome only, so causal structures that differ in whether group membership acts on the outcome, on the score, or on both can generate identical joint distributions and cannot be told apart by any such criterion.

Translate all three into the vocabulary of psychometrics: latent trait, item and test characteristic curves, test information, uniform and non-uniform DIF, configural/metric/scalar invariance, differential prediction in intercept and slope, criterion unreliability and attenuation, range restriction, judgemental standard setting, classification accuracy and consistency, the expected utility of a pass/fail rule, construct validity, and the position that the social consequences of test use belong to validity. For each result, tell me what carries over exactly, what carries over only if the claim is quietly altered to fit, and what cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report: for each leftover, say whether it is a defect of my framework — something psychometrics has never needed to say because of the uses it was built for — or a sign that the fairness literature is relying on a distinction it has not made explicit. Do not smooth this over by treating the target vocabulary's habits as neutral translation.

Two features of my setting that I want handled rather than idealised away. First, the groups we are pressed to report on are not given: they are pathway variables — which route into training a candidate took, whether they previously sat the assessment — and those are partly downstream of earlier use of the same test, so 'within group' is not a partition the measuring instrument had no hand in making. Second, our criterion is observed only for candidates who were admitted, and it is a supervisor rating made by someone who has seen the score. Say what becomes of the impossibilities under those two conditions.

I do not need references or a reading list. What I would like, though, is an audit of your own confidence: mark which translations you consider safe, which are your reconstruction of a literature you are recalling, correct me if any of the three results as I have stated them is not the result as it is usually stated, and finish by naming the single point in your answer you are least sure of and why.
