I've been trying on and off for a couple of years to write down what actually makes an utterance of a slur offensive, and every principle I come up with fits most of my cases and then breaks on one. I'd like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work properly, with my verdicts as the data.

Throughout, write S for a well-known ethnic slur for some group G — I'd rather not type it out, and the choice doesn't matter; assume a fairly bad one. My working principle is this:

**P.** A use of a slur is offensive just in case it is a public act that (i) invokes a derogatory stereotype supposed to hold of the members of a group G in virtue of *what they are*, and (ii) is directed at, or about, someone presented as a member of G. Whether the speaker endorses the stereotype or feels any contempt is irrelevant, and so is whether the stereotype is true. What separates slurs from ordinary pejoratives — *jerk*, *coward*, *bully* — is that the discrediting property is meant to attach to the target in virtue of what they are rather than what they have done.

My verdicts:

1. Reclamation. Two members of G greet each other with S, warmly, both having been called S by outsiders for years. Fine; actively good.
2. Cross-group borrowing. Someone outside G but from another group with a comparable history uses S the same warm way to a member of G, who laughs and isn't hurt. Still not fine, though clearly less bad than hostile out-group use.
3. An actor, in a film about racism, says S in character as a bigot, in a scene that makes the bigotry the target. Permissible; I still wince.
4. A linguistics lecturer utters S aloud as a phonetic example to two hundred students, some of them members of G, with a content warning first. Fine.
5a. Mutual ignorance. A non-native speaker picked S up from a song and believes it means something like *mate*; she uses it affectionately to a friend from G, who also doesn't know the word; nobody else hears. Nothing wrong happened — an accident, not an offence.
5b. Same utterance, but a colleague from G overhears and is hurt. Now something bad has happened and an apology is owed; the utterance was offensive.
6. A man alone in his room writes S in his private diary about a colleague from G. Nobody is wronged and no offensive act occurs, but this is much stronger evidence about his character than 5a, which tells me almost nothing.
7a. Salem, 1692: a woman hurls at her neighbour the period's term for someone who rides at night with the devil. Everyone in the speech community thinks the kind is real and abominable; we think it's empty. This was a real, offensive insult, and wrong.
7b. The identical word today, as a joke, at an eccentric neighbour, where neither speaker nor hearer believes in witches. Not offensive — comic, even affectionate.
8. Pejoratives for voluntarily joined or socially dominant groups: *glory-hunter* at a rival club's fans, *toff* at a hereditary peer, *Nazi* at an actual neo-Nazi. All fine, and I don't think any of them is a slur in the relevant sense. But *scab* at someone who crossed a picket line makes me uneasy in a way the others don't, and S is of course not fine at all.
9. The case I go back and forth on. In a forum I read, people have started calling language models *tinskins*, contemptuously. My verdict: nothing is wronged, no offence. But the same people have started calling humans who write in a flat, corporate way *tinskins* — and that feels genuinely nastier, not just metaphorically. Same string, same speaker attitude, same hearers, different verdict depending on whether the target is a person.

The pair I can't get P to hold together is 5a/5b, which makes offensiveness depend on who happens to be listening, against 6, where the fullest possible derogatory attitude with no audience leaves the act blameless while telling me the most about the speaker. P as stated gets 6 right as an act and 5a wrong; any P that keys on the speaker's attitude gets 5a right and 3, 4 and 7b wrong. And 7a/7b suggests the question is what the surrounding speech community takes the kind to be, not whether it exists.

What I want from you:

- A principle — or, if P can't be repaired, a small set of two or three principles answering different questions: when an utterance is offensive, what it licenses hearers to do, what it reveals about the speaker — stated precisely enough that I can apply it to a new case myself. For each clause, name the case above that forces it, and say explicitly which clause is carrying which verdict.
- Say plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which one you think I should give up, and which you'd let me keep at the cost of an extra clause. I'd rather have the trade-off stated than papered over.
- Predictions on two cases I haven't settled: (a) a slur invented for a fictional species in a novel, adopted by fans and then turned on real people who resemble the fictional kind in some resented way; (b) a group that is subordinate in one country and dominant in another, with the same slur used in both.
- One sentence on which broad family of account your principle belongs to — derogation as part of what is said, as a conventional but non-truth-conditional feature of the word, or as something the use *does* — and where within it. Please don't give me a reading list or citations; I have no way to follow them up, and I don't want the literature surveyed.
- Epistemic: mark how confident you are in each clause separately, tell me which single consideration or case would most change your answer, and say where your reasoning is most likely to be failing — in particular whether you think you've produced a principle or just a redescription of my intuitions in more technical vocabulary.

Please write in British English.
