Give a concrete numerical example that isolates a conceptual issue about the bearing of the standard impossibility results for algorithmic fairness on deployments with selective labels, and then comment on what the example shows.

The setting. A jurisdiction assigns each defendant a score s from a small ordered set of levels and detains everyone scoring above a fixed threshold, releasing the rest. The score is meant to predict Y = 1, where Y = 1 means that the defendant would be rearrested before trial if released. Two groups, A and B, are of concern.

The feature of the setting I want you to exploit is that Y is observed only for defendants who are released: a detained defendant's outcome is never observed, because detention removes the opportunity. So let Y* be, for each defendant, the value Y would have taken had they been released. Stipulate that Y* is well defined, that Y* = Y for released defendants, and that within each group P(Y* = 1 | s) is nondecreasing in s. Nothing else constrains Y* in the detained region. Call an assignment of P(Y* = 1 | s, g) to the detained score cells a completion, and call two completions observationally equivalent when they are consistent with the same classifier, the same threshold and the same observed data. Note what the observed data fix — the joint distribution of score and outcome over the released cells, and the marginal distribution of the score, hence the group detention rates — and what they do not: the base rates P(Y* = 1 | g), calibration in the detained cells, the positive predictive value, and both the false-negative and the false-positive rates. The asymmetries are worth recording: the numerator of the false-negative rate is identified but its denominator is not, for the false-positive rate neither is, and the negative predictive value is identified while the positive predictive value is not.

The familiar result I want brought into contact with this setting: a score that is calibrated within groups — same score, same probability of Y = 1, in every group — cannot also have equal false-negative and equal false-positive rates across groups, unless the groups' base rates are equal or the score separates the outcomes perfectly. The moral usually drawn is that one must choose which condition to relax, and that this choice settles which group bears which kind of error.

What I want from the example:

1. Specify, with explicit counts, a score with four or five ordered levels, a threshold placed so that the top two levels are detained, two groups of a few hundred defendants each, the observed joint distribution of score and outcome over the released cells, and the full score distribution within each group. Keep the arithmetic checkable.

2. Exhibit two observationally equivalent completions, each satisfying the monotonicity constraint, under which the direction of the error disparities reverses: under the first, group A has the higher false-negative rate and the lower false-positive rate; under the second, group A has the lower false-negative rate and the higher false-positive rate. For each completion, verify that it reproduces the observed counts exactly and respects monotonicity, and report the resulting base rates, false-negative rates, false-positive rates and detention rates.

3. State whether the score is calibrated within groups under each completion. If your numbers already refute calibration in the observed released cells, say so, and explain why that asymmetry — refutable by the observed record, never verifiable by it — is itself significant.

Then comment, briefly but substantively, on the following:

4. Which of the familiar criteria are completion-robust, in the sense of returning the same verdict, including the direction of any disparity, under every admissible completion? Is any criterion robust precisely because it quantifies only over decisions rather than outcomes? And is the accuracy side of the fairness–accuracy trade-off itself identified here?

5. What follows for how the impossibility results are to be read in this setting? If a theorem tells us that a choice between error disparities must be made, while the observed record does not determine which choice we are making, is the theorem a constraint on policy or only a constraint on a joint distribution that no one observes? Say what should guide the choice — worst-case reasoning over the set of admissible completions, randomized release of a small audit sample in order to restore identification, or something else — and what each of these costs.

6. Finally, investigate whether the underdetermination survives when the two groups' detention rates are equal. If it does not, try to identify which feature of the observed data then pins down the direction of the disparity, and comment on why an observable quantity should have that power.
