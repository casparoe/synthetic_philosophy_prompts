I've just come out of a long argument with a friend and would like a third opinion. We disagree about whether the spread of social media has, on net, degraded the quality of democratic decision-making; I'm at roughly 70%, he's at roughly 25%, and after several hours neither of us has moved. He invokes Aumann's agreement theorem: we have each stated our number to the other plainly, so our posteriors are common knowledge, and he says we should simply grant the common-prior assumption for the sake of argument, on the grounds that people raised in the same broad epistemic culture would assign the same probabilities to any question neither of them had evidence about. From this he concludes that the disagreement is itself a symptom — one of us must be updating badly on his private evidence, or reporting something other than his real credence.

I find that conclusion unacceptable, but the alternative I proposed is that the theorem's real lesson is remedial: because neither of us can fully transmit his private evidence to the other, rationality requires meeting in the middle, so I should come down toward the midpoint and he should come up toward it. Which of us is using the result correctly? If everything relevant is on the table and the numbers still differ, does it really follow that one of us is irrational or less than fully honest — or can the theorem's antecedents fail in ways that leave us both blameless? And if they can, is there anything the result does tell us about which direction either of us should move?
