I'm TA-ing for an intro cog-sci course this term and we just did the week on Dennett's intentional stance, and the students all wanted to know whether modern chatbots have beliefs. I found I had a pile of gut reactions and no principle behind them, which is awkward because I have to run the same discussion section again on thursday. I know the official Dennett line is that taking the stance is a predictive strategy and not a claim about inner states, but that doesn't settle anything for me: it tells me what belief-talk is *for*, not when I should be willing to use it.

So here are my verdicts, going on gut alone. A chess engine that sacs a knight to open a file: I'm happy to say it thinks the sacrifice is good, but I don't feel I'm committing to much. A mid-size company that issues a statement saying demand will recover next quarter: totally comfortable saying the company believes that, even in a case where I know no individual employee believes it. An ant colony rerouting around a broken bridge: no. A robot vacuum that keeps bumping the same table leg: no, though the word "thinks" keeps escaping my mouth anyway. A pocket calculator: no, obviously not — even though it has exactly the right dispositions, which makes me think dispositions alone can't be the test. A language model that says the capital of Australia is Sydney and then, the moment I push back, agrees that it's Canberra: my gut says it believes neither thing, and weirdly the fact that it folds so fast is evidence *against* belief rather than for it — which feels backwards, because being responsive to correction is normally my best evidence for belief in a person. The same model answering something I happen to know well, fluently and with the right qualifications: I catch myself saying it "knows" that, and I hate it, because I'm perfectly willing to attribute *false* beliefs to my students, so truth shouldn't be entering into this at all.

And here are two that I think are a matched pair, because I definately can't see what's supposed to differ between them. Take two systems that are behaviorally identical — same outputs to every input you could give them — except that one was trained on first-person avowals ("I think the meeting is at three") and the other on third-person descriptions ("the meeting is at three"). I feel more inclined to attribute beliefs to the first one, and I can't justify that, and I suspect the feeling is an artifact. Relatedly: if I find out an engineer deliberately built a system so that its internals would be interpretable in belief-desire terms, I'm more inclined to belief-talk than if I find out the belief-talk was just a shorthand someone slapped on afterwards to a black box — but by my own lights that shouldn't matter one bit if the stance is only a tool for prediction.

My tentative rule is: attribute to a system the belief that P just in case (1) it's disposed, across a wide enough range of circumstances, to do and say things that would serve its purposes if P and frustrate them if not-P; (2) when it goes wrong, it goes wrong in the ways a believer with a slightly different belief would go wrong — the errors are shaped by the content rather than just being noise; and (3) it doesn't matter how it was built or what anyone intended it for. But (1) hands beliefs to my calculator, (2) looks like it's secretly doing all the work and I can't spell it out, and (3) is flatly inconsistent with the matched pair. Can you fix this into something I could actually defend? Two more things I want out of it: can belief-attribution come in pieces — my daily-use assistant is sharp about phrasing and hopeless at multi-step arithmetic, and I want to say it has beliefs in one domain and not the other — or is it all-or-nothing per system? And what does whatever principle you end up with say about a system that never says anything at all but reliably acts as if P, versus one that reports P flawlessly but never acts?
