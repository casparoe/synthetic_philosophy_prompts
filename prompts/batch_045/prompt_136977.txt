I sit in on a game-theory reading group in my department, and last term two of its members got thoroughly stuck on the centipede game. I've reconstructed their exchange below as faithfully as I can; I have no stake in who wins and genuinely couldn't tell.

The game they were discussing has six decision nodes: P1 moves at the first, third and fifth, P2 at the second, fourth and sixth. The pot starts at 4 and doubles after every pass (so 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256 if nobody ever takes). A player who takes gets three-quarters of the pot at that node; the other player gets one quarter. If both pass at all six nodes, the final pot of 256 is divided in the same three-to-one ratio, but in P1's favour: 192 to P1 and 64 to P2. The possible outcomes are therefore (3, 1), (2, 6), (12, 4), (8, 24), (48, 16), (32, 96), and (192, 64) at the end.

Backward induction gives (3, 1): at the sixth node 96 beats 64, so P2 takes; at the fifth, 48 beats the 32 that P1 would get by passing, so P1 takes; and so on back to the first node. Both players would be enormously better off if they could reach the end.

Their exchange:

(1) Owen: The induction is valid, and the fact that its verdict is bad for both players is not an objection to it. That rationality can be collectively self-defeating is the moral of the Prisoner's Dilemma, and there is nothing special here. Anyone who passes at the first node is either confused, altruistic, or maximising something other than her own monetary payoff.

(2) Lena: The phrase "and so on back" hides the only interesting step. Every link in the chain reasons about a node that the previous link has just shown will never be reached. The argument concludes that the second node never occurs, and it gets there by using a claim about what would happen at the second node. A chain of counterfactuals whose antecedents the argument itself rules out needs a semantics, and common knowledge of rationality does not obviously supply one. So the induction is not, at any rate, a plain deductive consequence of "everyone is rational."

(3) Owen: It supplies the ordinary one. To be rational is to have a complete plan, and a plan says what you would do at nodes you do not expect to reach; the induction is a proof about plans, not about visits. There is a standard theorem to the effect that common knowledge of rationality in a finite game of perfect information yields the backward-induction outcome. So if (2) is to bite, it has to claim something stronger: not that the reasoning is invalid, but that the reasoning, however valid, tells you nothing at a node you have actually reached.

(4) Lena: I will claim exactly that. Suppose I am P2 at the fourth node, having watched P1 pass at the first and third. Common knowledge of rationality is now straightforwardly false — I have direct evidence that P1 is not a backward inductor. The induction's verdict at the fourth node was derived from premises I now know to be false, and nothing in the argument tells me what to do instead. A theory whose advice evaporates precisely when you need it is not giving advice; "take" survives only as a reflex.

(5) Owen: But the evidence does not say what you want it to. A P1 who passes at the first and third nodes might be a cooperator; she might equally be someone who passes early in order to induce you to pass at the fourth and then takes at the fifth. The mimicking hypothesis is available to you only because the cooperative one is. So you cannot read "she passed" as "she will keep passing." You need a distribution over the kinds of non-inductor she might be, and once you have one, the recommendation comes entirely from that distribution and not from rationality at all.

(6) Lena: Agreed — and that is my point in a sharper form, because the numbers show how little work rationality is doing. At the first node, let s be my probability that P2 will pass at the second. Even on the pessimistic assumption that I myself grab at the third node, taking is worth 3 and passing is worth 2(1 − s) + 12s = 2 + 10s, so passing wins as soon as s > 0.1. The backward-induction verdict therefore requires better than nine-to-one confidence that P2 will grab at the second node. And the quantity the theory needs is the probability that P2 passes at the second node *given that the second node has been reached* — a conditional probability on an event to which the theory itself assigns probability zero. The claim that P1 should take rests on a degree of belief the theory can neither supply nor, on its own terms, ever acquire evidence about.

(7) Owen: Undefined is not the same as wrong. Every equilibrium refinement has that structure; it is not a special disease of backward induction. And your own calculation locates the content in the right place — in a prior over opponents, which is an empirical matter. Put in a plausible prior, with a small admixture of altruists, cooperators, shallow reasoners and people who misread the screen, and the pattern people report from experiments — passing early, unraveling near the end, average payoffs well above (3, 1) — is just what rational players would do. So the data do not contradict rational choice theory. They contradict the auxiliary assumption that each side knows the other to be a pure money-maximiser.

(8) Afterward, a third member proposed a variant: leave the tree, the doubling and the three-quarter rule untouched, and change only the terminal division — split the final pot evenly, (128, 128), instead of (192, 64). Owen said this settles it in his favour: run the identical induction and it now prescribes passing everywhere, for 128 each; a theory that changes its answer when the game changes is a theory that is working.

(9) Lena said it settles it in hers: the identical reasoning, with common knowledge of rationality fully intact and with no altruism and no uncertainty about types at all, now delivers full cooperation. So whatever produced (3, 1) in the original game was never rationality and never common knowledge. It was one fact about one cell — that the last mover's share of the final pot is smaller than the three-quarters she can grab for herself. Change that and the "paradoxical" outcome disappears without touching any assumption about the players.

(10) Owen: No one ever claimed that rationality does badly in every game; the claim was that the induction is valid, and the variant confirms it. Lena: if that is all the claim amounts to, then the paradox was never about rationality, and we have spent a term arguing about a convention for filling in the last cell of a payoff table.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the ten moves, say where it falls on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and why — not merely who wins overall. In addition:

(a) Check Lena's arithmetic in (6), and say what her threshold becomes if she drops the pessimistic assumption about her own play at the third node.

(b) Settle the variant: does it decide anything, and for whom? Is the even split a fair test of the two positions or a gerrymander — does it alter a rule, or only a payoff?

(c) Say whether there is a general threshold, expressed as the last mover's share of the final pot, at which the entire outcome flips from total collapse to full cooperation, and give it. Say also what the existence of such a threshold shows: is the backward-induction verdict robust or knife-edge, and in what respect?

(d) Say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and what each would have to concede to the other.

(e) Finally, say whether, with all of this on the table, anything survives of the "paradox" as a thesis about rationality and common knowledge, as opposed to a thesis about the geometry of a payoff table — and if something does survive, state it as precisely as you can.
