The set-up. Fix a finite horizon N, and let a world be a binary sequence of length N. A method takes any history of observed bits and outputs a prediction for the next bit; score a method by the fraction of the N coordinates it gets right. Nothing turns on the details: this sequential version reproduces the mechanism behind the familiar no-free-lunch theorems, where one averages performance over a class of worlds closed under the relevant symmetries and finds that every method ties.

Two facts I take as established.

Lemma 1. If the prior over worlds is uniform over all 2^N sequences, then conditional on any history the next bit is a fair coin flip. So every method has expected accuracy exactly one half, and no possible observation can ever favor one method over another: the tie is not an artifact of averaging across worlds but holds step by step, inside every history.

Lemma 2. Among priors that assign positive probability to every sequence, the uniform prior is the only one that makes every unobserved coordinate a coin flip conditional on every history — the condition that does the work in Lemma 1. The argument is an induction from the last coordinate backward: pinning the conditional at step N forces the two leaves under each history to be equal, which forces the four leaves under each shorter history to be equal, and so on down to the root.

A wrinkle I cannot fit in. Suppose instead that we average uniformly over the smaller class of sequences with odd parity. Steps 1 through N−1 are still coin flips. But the last coordinate is fixed by the parity of what has already been observed: a method that tracks the running parity is right about it with certainty, while no method blind to the constraint can do better than chance there. So the tie is knife-edge. A single bit of global structure — which no one would call an inductive bias, and which leaves every local regularity untouched — is enough to hand the advantage to one particular method, and the method that wins is not "expect the future to resemble the past" but "keep the total consistent." I cannot tell whether this is a curiosity of my toy set-up or the key to the whole thing.

The argument.

(1) [Tie] Under the neutral prior, every method has the same expected accuracy, and no evidence could break the tie (Lemma 1).

(2) [Neutrality] The uniform prior over the space of all possible worlds is the neutral, information-free starting point: it encodes no substantive assumption about the world, and it is therefore the prior one should use in the absence of evidence.

(3) [Indifference] If two methods tie in expected accuracy under the prior one should use, and no evidence could break the tie, then there is no epistemic reason to prefer either. Hence there is no reason to prefer "the future will resemble the past," or simplicity, or persistence, to "expect the opposite of what has been observed," or to any other method whatever.

(4) [Bias requirement] Any justification for induction must therefore come from outside the data: from a substantive assumption that the world is orderly, or that unobserved cases resemble observed ones, or that it is generated by some low-complexity process.

(5) [No non-circular ground] No such assumption can be justified. Not a priori, since the only a priori neutral prior is the uniform one (by (2)), and that is precisely the prior under which induction enjoys no advantage. Not a posteriori, since "induction has worked so far, so it will keep working" is induction defending itself. And there is no third route.

(6) [Collapse] So no method of forming expectations about the unobserved is preferable to any other. For any method you favor there is a mirror method, equally well supported, that recommends the opposite expectations, and choosing between them is arbitrary. There are no reasons — as opposed to causes — for any expectation about what you have not observed.

(7) [Discharge] The conclusion generalizes in two directions. First, the ban on circular justification in (5) would also prohibit justifying a rule of inference by using that rule, so deductive reasoning falls together with inductive reasoning. Second, (2)'s choice of the uniform measure is itself a substantive assumption about the world — one among infinitely many — which by the argument's own standards cannot be justified, and yet (1) rests on it.

What I want kept, if anything can be. I would like to keep (4): the claim that induction cannot be grounded in the data alone, that it needs a substantive assumption about the world, seems to me true and worth the trouble. I would also like to keep (1) as mathematics. What I cannot accept is (6), that every method is equally arbitrary, or the spectacle in (7) of an argument consuming its own premises.
