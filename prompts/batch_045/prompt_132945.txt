I'm a science journalist and I ended up at one of those long dinners after a workshop on embryo screening last month — mostly people in the field plus me, and two of them got properly stuck into each other and wouldn't let it go. I want to use some of it in a piece for a weekend paper, so I need to know who's actually ahead rather than just who sounded better over pudding. Dan (decision theory, broadly sympathetic to selection) versus Miriam (disability and ethics, broadly not). I've tried to reconstruct it as accuratly as I could from notes on my phone, so forgive any gaps; I've kept myself out of it, I didn't take a side at the table and I'm not going to now.

1. Dan: if you're already going to have a child and you've got several embryos in front of you, you should pick the one expected to have the best life. It costs you nothing extra, you have the information, and the alternative is deliberately choosing a worse-off child for no reason at all. (I think this is the thing people call procreative beneficence.)

2. Miriam: the non-identity point kills the "should". Whichever embryo you pick, that child isn't better off then they'd otherwise have been — the alternative for them wasn't a worse life, it was non-existence, or being a numerically different child entirely. So you can't be doing it for the child's sake. To keep the conclusion you have to go impersonal: a duty to bring about the best-off person you could. Which is a much shakier thing, and it isn't what Dan originally said.

3. Dan: fine, impersonal then — and it doesn't only switch on when a lab hands you a menu. We already accept reasons about which child comes into existence: don't drink through pregnancy, take folic acid, don't conceive when you're in no position to raise anyone. The reason exists whether or not the technology makes it actionable, so "this duty only appears because screening does" is a dud objection. The seatbelt existed before you could buy one.

4. Miriam: that's a switch. With the folic acid there's a person who ends up better off; here there isn't one. And taken seriously the impersonal version doesn't stop at the clinic: it says fertile couples have reason to time conception to catch a luckier sperm, with indefinitely many possible children in the frame, so the duty either attaches to no one in particular or demandingly to everyone. It also quietly assumes you're always choosing between the same number of children, which screening isn't — it's tangled up with how many embryos you transfer. And what these scores actually predict is mostly population-level stuff about schooling and height, and most of that signal washes out inside a single family, so "expected best life" is really "highest number on a proxy". Those are different things to be maximising.

5. Dan: noise is an practical problem, not a moral one. If the signal is weak but positive you should still act on it — expected value is expected value, and it beats choosing at random. And demandingness doesn't cancel a reason, it just means the reason can be outweighed by what compliance costs you. Here it costs roughly nothing, once you're in IVF anyway.

6. Miriam: then take the objection I actually care about. The wrong isn't to the child at all, it's the message to people who already exist. Selecting against deafness is something you do in front of the Deaf community, and the complaint is about what ranking lives like that says about them, not about the child you end up with. That's why this critique doesn't need to solve the non-identity problem first.

7. Dan: but if the wrong is the message, it's audience-relative. A couple who screen and tell nobody have done nothing wrong, and a couple who screen and then spend thirty years campaigning for Deaf rights have somehow cancelled it out. Wrongness that vanishes when nobody's watching, or that you can undo with a press release, is the wrong shape for a moral objection — which suggests the message isn't doing independent work. You need a prior story about why the trait is being treated as a disqualifier, and the message is a symptom of that, not the argument for it.
