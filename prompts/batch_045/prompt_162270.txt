I'm in the final term of an MA seminar on the ethics of reproduction and selection, and our professor has each of us contribute one question to a shared practice exam. The format is fixed: a 90-minute essay question worth 20 marks, a model answer of roughly 900–1,000 words, and a marking scheme. We have just finished the unit on the Principle of Procreative Beneficence (PPB) — the claim that, other things being equal, prospective parents have a significant moral reason to select, from among the children they could have, the one expected to have the best life.

The question I want has to be one that cannot be answered by running through the objections on the syllabus. So it should stipulate those away: assume for the purposes of the question that the non-identity objection, the expressive objection (that selecting against disability-related traits devalues people who already have those traits), and the objection from the child's future autonomy or from "designing" people have all been met, and that a reliable, cheap, widely available method of estimating an embryo's expected well-being from its genome is in place. What I want to test is whether a candidate can do something *with* the principle rather than only against it.

The case I have been building it around is this. Suppose a society in which a fixed number of places — elite university admissions, a fixed set of high-status career entry points — does a great deal of the work in determining how well a person's life goes, and suppose that embryo selection for the traits that predict success in competition for those places is nearly universal. Call a trait *positionally loaded* when most of its contribution to a child's expected well-being comes from how he or she compares with others rather than from anything that makes life go better in absolute terms: competitiveness, or height in a society where height is rewarded largely because it is above average. Contrast traits like robust health or low trait-anxiety, which seem to improve a life whether or not anyone else has them. My worry is that when parents follow PPB and select for positionally loaded traits, each of them does better by their own child and yet the practice as a whole buys nothing: the places are fixed, so the gains cancel, and the resources spent on selection are pure waste.

I would like the question to force candidates to say what, exactly, this shows. Please have it close by requiring candidates to endorse exactly one of four diagnoses and defend it against the others: REDUCTIO — the case shows PPB is self-defeating, since a principle of beneficence cannot require acts whose universal performance produces no benefit, so it must be given up or restricted to non-positional traits; COORDINATION — the reason PPB gives an individual parent survives untouched, and the case shows only that the practice needs collective restraint; DISSOLUTION — the case shows only that PPB must be stated over a non-comparative conception of well-being, after which there is no arms race and nothing to restrain; CATEGORY ERROR — there is no arms race at all, because selecting one embryo rather than another never changes the traits of anyone else's child. I have argued myself into all four at different points, so I want the model answer to commit to one and to say what partial credit the other three deserve.

Deliverables, please: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper; the model answer; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around the specific moves a top answer must make, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. In particular, I want the scheme to say what separates an A from a B rather than just counting points — I suspect the dividing line is whether a candidate notices that the verdict flips depending on whether PPB is grounded in parental partiality or in impartial beneficence, and whether they separate the positional from the non-positional component of a trait's expected benefit instead of treating the case as all-or-nothing. Please also note, in the scheme, that a candidate who answers by reciting the objections I stipulated away has not engaged with the question, and say what such an answer should be capped at.

Keep everything self-contained: no citations, no reading lists, no paper titles, and no appeal to anything outside the case as stated and the principle as I have described it.

Finish with a single final line of the form \answer{LABEL — phrase of at most twelve words}, naming the diagnosis the model answer endorses and the one qualification it turns on.
