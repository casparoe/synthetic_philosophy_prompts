I work in contracts and performance oversight for a county human-services department. We pay nonprofit providers partly on whether they hit numbers, and my team writes somewhere between fifteen and twenty contracts a year, each with three to six performance metrics. I get maybe two hours per metric to pressure-test it before it goes to our finance director for sign-off, and he mostly cares whether a number can be audited from records we already keep — which pushes everything toward metrics that are easy to verify, and those are exactly the ones I've watched fail. I have no training in any of this, I've just been doing it six years and seeing the same four or five failures repeat: providers quietly selecting the easiest clients, relabeling a bad outcome into a category nobody counts, pouring everything into the one measured line while the rest of the service rots, and turning in numbers that climb while the thing we actually care about doesn't move. I keep coming back to that thing everyone says about a measure ceasing to be a good measure once you start paying for it — I don't remember who said it and I don't care, so please don't spend any words on the history or on who coined what, and don't cite anything because I can't look it up. It's a warning, not a procedure, and what I need is a procedure my team can actually run.

Two rough ideas I've been using on my own, which you can keep or throw out. First, I distinguish what I call gaming (someone deliberately works the number — reclassifying, cherry-picking, closing cases early) from what I call capture (no one is cheating at all; the staff just sincerely internalize the metric and stop noticing the parts of the mission that aren't in it). They feel like they need different tests, but I'm not sure they do. Second, I've started slipping cheap "ghost measures" into contracts — a number we collect quietly that isn't tied to any payment and that the provider barely notices, chosen so that it ought to move together with the paid metric; if the paid metric improves and the ghost doesn't, something's off. I want you to tell me whether that trick is actually sound or whether I'm fooling myself, and where it breaks. A third thing I'd like handled: our director wants each year's target to be last year's actual plus a bit, which I think punishes whoever performed well this year. And there's a pressure I can't name properly — the metrics that survive auditing tend to be true of the paperwork rather than true of the world, so a provider can hit them perfectly while nothing changes for anyone.

What I want is a written procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about what we value rather than what's happening. It has to be runnable in about two hours by someone with no theory background, using records we already have, and it should say what to do when a number simply can't be gotten. If you use a technical term, define it in one clause. Then walk it through three metrics from my world, in this order: (1) average days from referral to first appointment, which is my favorite because it's trivially auditable and I suspect you're going to tell me to drop it; (2) percent of people placed in housing who are still in the same housing six months later; (3) a proposed metric on serving the hardest cases, where I think the procedure will stall and a human will have to decide something about who we owe what. Please also tell me straight out which parts of this are judgment calls dressed up as steps.

Sections, in this order, with rough lengths: "The procedure" (700 words, ordered steps with pass/fail/hand-off at each), "Walkthrough: referral-to-appointment time" (200 words), "Walkthrough: housing at six months" (250 words), "Walkthrough: the hardest-cases metric" (200 words), "Where a person has to decide" (200 words), "What this procedure can't tell me" (150 words).
