I run a small reading group on theories of wellbeing, and last week we spent an entire evening on tranquilism — the view that, as far as a person's welfare is concerned, the only things that matter fundamentally are the absence of craving (restless or unsatisfied desire) and the absence of suffering, with everything we ordinarily count as a good — achievement, knowledge, love, art, autonomy — mattering at most derivatively, insofar as it bears on those two. Half of us found the austerity of it bracing. The other half kept arriving at the same two places: that the view seems to make wellbeing alarmingly cheap to produce, and that if the absence of craving and suffering is the whole story, then a life free of both is not obviously doing *well* rather than merely not doing badly — which would leave a government that took the view seriously with no way of ranking any two adequately calm options, and no way of saying that anyone is flourishing.

I'd like you to write the document that a government which had actually adopted this view would eventually have to write. Specifically: a satirical supplementary appraisal guidance note of roughly 700–900 words, in the deadpan, acronym-laden, relentlessly euphemistic house style of British central-government policy guidance, issued by an invented body (call it the Office for Tranquillity) and introducing a mandatory Craving Impact Assessment that all departments must complete before any domestic policy may proceed. It should carry a ministerial foreword setting out what has been decided and why, numbered sections in the manner of Treasury Green Book guidance, at least one fully worked cost-effectiveness calculation using an invented metric (Craving-Adjusted Life Years, or whatever you prefer), and a closing annex of Frequently Asked Questions. Please write it in British English, use invented but plausible acronyms throughout, name no real persons, and include no citations or footnotes.

The comedy has to come from taking the theory seriously and following the arithmetic, not from anyone wincing on the reader's behalf: nothing in the document should say that this is callous, and no section should quietly smuggle in the thought that achievement or knowledge or love matters in its own right. The objections I want landed should instead appear as administrative problems that have been *solved*. Please make sure the note covers: how departments are to score goods like schools, galleries and friendships, which on the official view come out as welfare-neutral and so must be justified on other grounds; the awkwardness that the cheapest intervention per unit of craving averted will be a pharmacological one, and that the cheapest of all may be to prevent the capacity for craving from developing; the ranking problem raised above, under some such heading as the Neutral Point Convention, including guidance on what to do when every available option leaves everyone exactly at zero, and on how to score the deeply sleeping or permanently sedated; and the case of a population that is perfectly content, perfectly still and has produced nothing, which by the official arithmetic is a triumph. The only dissent I want to permit inside the document is a civil servant's minute objecting to one of these measures on presentational rather than welfare grounds. It should be legible and funny to an intelligent reader who has never opened a philosophy journal, and it should make someone who has read the literature wince in recognition — including at the point where the reader realises that, on the view being administered, no policy can ever make anyone better off, only less badly off.
