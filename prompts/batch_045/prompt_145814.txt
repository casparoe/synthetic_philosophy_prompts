I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether a larger population is, in itself, a better thing, and I built an argument that I now think works too well. I'd like you to tell me which step to reject — and to commit to one diagnosis and defend it, rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to choose.

To keep the usual objections off the table, imagine the additional people live on a causally isolated archipelago — call it the Far Side — with its own resources, no contact with us, no crowding, no emissions, no fiscal burden, no effect of any kind on anyone already alive. Everyone stipulates that each life there would be worth living. I also want to avoid relying on total utilitarianism: as far as I can tell the argument runs on person-affecting premises alone.

(1) A life worth living is a benefit to the person whose life it is. She is glad to have it, it is good for her, and since she would not otherwise have existed, it is not the case that she would have been better off without it.

(2) If an act benefits someone and harms no one, and violates no side-constraint, then, other things equal, performing it is morally better than not performing it.

(3) Creating one more person on the Far Side benefits that person (by 1) and harms no one: no one here is affected, and no one there is deprived, displaced, or made worse off by being joined.

(4) So, other things equal, each additional worthwhile life on the Far Side makes the outcome better than it would otherwise have been.

(5) Additions are independent. Whether adding a worthwhile life improves the outcome does not depend on which other lives the outcome already contains, because on the Far Side nothing interacts with anything: no feature of the existing population can turn an improvement into a non-improvement.

(6) Hence for any population P of worthwhile lives and any larger population P+ of worthwhile lives, P+ is better than P — no matter how far the quality of life in P+ falls below that in P, provided each life in P+ is still worth living.

(7) So ten billion ecstatic lives are worse than a hundred trillion lives each barely above the neutrality line, and worse again than a population a hundred times larger still with lives a hair above it. This has the familiar repugnant shape, but I don't think invoking the label refutes anything.

(8) And since (2) yields a reason for action and not merely a ranking, the argument also says: a couple who stop at two children when they could lovingly raise six have acted wrongly; lifelong childlessness is a serious moral failure; every dollar spent improving the lives of existing children is a dollar that should instead have gone to increasing their number, since that creates new beneficiaries rather than improving old ones; and a state that could raise fertility by compulsion and declines to do so is permitting a preventable loss on the order of the number of lives it failed to create.

I cannot accept (7) or (8). But I don't want to give up (1), (2), or (3): those are what made me think a larger population could be better in the first place, and if they go, I don't see what is left to say in favor of even a modest pronatalism. Two replies I've already set aside: that resources are finite (the Far Side removes that), and that the argument secretly assumes total utilitarianism (I don't see where, since (2) is person-affecting).

So: which step is the weak one, and why did it look sound? If you think the problem is not a false premise but a gap — that (7) and (8) don't actually follow as stated — then show me the gap, state the smallest additional premise that would close it, and tell me whether that premise is independently plausible. If you think the argument is valid and I should just swallow (7), argue for that instead. Either way, name your confidence, say what would change your mind, and be explicit about what your rejection costs me: in particular, whether I can still claim that a larger population is in any respect better, or whether the honest upshot is that the question has no determinate answer.
