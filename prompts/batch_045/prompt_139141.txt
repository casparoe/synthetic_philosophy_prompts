hey — I'm in a quantitative social science PhD program and in my department the exact same argument blows up about once a year whenever somebody posts a new heritability estimate for something like educational attainment, height, or BMI. It's not a literature i work in directly, but I end up moderating the thread every time, and it never goes anywhere. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict.

Here's the shape of it. Side A: heritability is just a ratio of variance components for one population in one environment; it's a bookkeeping fact about that population, it licenses nothing about individuals, nothing across populations, and nothing about what would happen if you changed the environment; and given how reliably people slide from high-heritability to nothing-to-be-done, publishing these numbers without a wall of caveats causes more harm than insight. Side B: it isn't nothing — high heritability tells you where to look for causal pathways, it predicts that purely environmental fixes will be hard to find and harder to sustain, it's part of why anyone went looking for molecular mechanisms at all, and the answer to misuse is better communication rather than suppression.

What drives me nuts is that I don't think they disagree about any statistic. Both grant that narrow heritability is additive genetic variance over phenotypic variance in a defined population; both grant the phenylketonuria case (fully genetic, managed by removing one thing from the diet) and the height case (very heritable, and populations also got markedly taller within a couple of generations off nutrition and disease load); both grant the gap between twin-based and measured-genotype-based estimates; both grant that gene-environment correlation mangles the decomposition. And then nobody moves an inch.

My hypothesis, which might be the lazy one, is that they're answering seperate questions with one number: a causal-attribution question (where do differences come from) and a decision question (what to do under uncertainty, and who eats the cost if the number gets misread). If so, no study settles it. But there are two rival readings I can't rule out: one where a hidden empirical question — how much gene-environment interaction there is in realistic ranges — is doing all the work, and one where the concept is just ill-posed, because what heritability refers to quietly shifts whenever the environment distribution shifts.

A thing I've been chewing on, possibly bullshit: a friend proposes replacing the question how heritable trait X is with something like menu-relative heritability — fix the set of interventions that are actually feasible and legal right now, equalize access to all of them, then ask how much of the remaining variance is genetic. Different quantity, explicitly depends on what we're willing to do, and it falls as interventions improve. Does that reframe help, or does it just relabel the same fight?

So: diagnose it. Verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, hidden empirical question, or something else — and which bits of each side's actual argument point that way. Please test your diagnosis against one stipulation: suppose tomorrow we got clean estimates showing heritability is basically constant across every environment anyone cares about and interaction effects are negligible. Who, if anyone, has to concede something? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a redefinition, a specific empirical result, a change of question — or whether expecting resolution is just a mistake, and why. And be honest about whether my hypothesis is the lazy version and what I'd have to look at to tell.
