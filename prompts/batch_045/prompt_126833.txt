Doing a consistency check on my own position before I present it at the department work-in-progress next month and I'd really like someone to poke holes in it, because I can't locate the weak joint myself. Context: I'm a political theory PhD student writing on class and moral culture — roughly, the rural/working-class outlook versus the urban/professional-class one — and here's where I've landed.

(a) I'm not a relativist. Some values really are better than others, and we can sometimes be right about which.

(b) But I reject the deficit model: I don't think the rural working-class orientation toward concrete obligations — family, place, the trade, the people actually in front of you — is a stunted or less reflective version of the professional-class orientation toward self-authored, chosen, mobile lives. I think it's a different structuring of a life, not a failure to structure one.

(c) I think the prestige of the professional-class outlook is partly an artifact. It's the outlook of the people who staff universities, write policy and control gatekeeping, and the fact that it fits the credentialed labour market is not evidence that it's true about how to live.

(d) I explain the difference materially: moral outlooks track occupational structure, exposure to risk, whether your living depends on being trusted by people you'll see again for thirty years, whether your livelihood is tied to a specific place.

(e) And I think explanations of that shape are not debunking explanations. That a commitment is socially caused doesn't show it's unjustified.

(f) Still, I want to condemn specific things without flinching: the code against cooperating with outsiders and authorities, the gender traditionalism, the homophobia. I don't want to be caught saying their just different but equally fine.

(g) I also want to say that some changes usually described as professional-class morality spreading outward are genuine moral improvements rather than mere class ascendancy — the decline of hitting children as discipline is my stock example.

(h) I think it's a real cultural loss when these places thin out and the outlook goes with them, and I think place-based policy — keeping the local school open, agricultural support, that kind of thing — is partly defensible for that reason. At the same time I'm officially committed to state neutrality among conceptions of the good, and I don't think anyone who leaves should be pressured to stay.

(i) In my own chapters I keep leaning on something I call the exit-cost asymmetry: the two outlooks are not equally costly to exit, because professional-class norms are already the default in schools, HR departments and law, so a state that treats both symmetrically is not in fact neutral between them.

So: which of these are actually in tension, and which only look like it? i keep going back and forth on whether (d) and (a) really clash — a friend in the department says a thoroughgoing causal story about value commitments leaves nothing for (a) to be about, and I think she's wrong but I can't say precisely why. Meanwhile I have a suspicion that (c) and (e) are doing something illegitimate between them, since I apply the causal/genetic move aggressively to one class's values and then insist it doesn't touch the other's, but maybe there's a principled asymmetry available there. And I can't tell whether (f) is compatible with (b) or whether it quietly guts it, given that the practices I want to condemn aren't accidental accretions on that outlook.

What I want is an audit, not an overview: the real tensions ranked by severity, for each one what exactly would have to give and what giving it would cost me, and an explicit note on any pair that dissolves on inspection. Don't tell me which commitment you'd keep — I haven't decided either, and I'd rather not be steered. If it helps to test things on a case: I grew up in a town of about three thousand, my dad was a machinist and my grandfather logged, and I genuinely don't know whether I'd want my own kids raised inside that outlook or outside it, or what my position so far commits me to saying about that. Also tell me whether (h) survives if I drop (i).
