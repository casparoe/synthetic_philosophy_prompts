Folk version: Good's theorem — that for an expected-utility maximizer, free information has non-negative expected value ex ante — gets quoted around these parts as a blanket license: when information is free, take it. I want to know how much weight that license can bear. Here are six ways the setup might fail to describe the situation I'm actually in:

(a) Leaky agents. Call an agent leaky if its updated state is readable by others — in its face, its bids, its later testimony, its weights — so that ignorance was doing work as a commitment: what you don't know you can neither reveal nor be made to reveal.

(b) Signal-in-utility. The observation itself is something I have preferences about; finding out would hurt for reasons not exhausted by what finding out lets me do.

(c) Ambiguity. I don't have one prior but a set of priors, and I choose the action with the best worst case.

(d) The pedant's oracle. A channel that never lies but chooses which true fact to hand me, optimizing for the fact whose optimal use is most computationally expensive for me. Still free, still truthful.

(e) Successor drift. Whoever acts at t+1 shares my prior and my memories but not quite my utility function.

(f) The yes-channel. A source that always reports whichever hypothesis I currently favor most, and I know this about it.

Sort them into tiers, from "this genuinely kills the maxim" down to "the theorem absorbs this without strain," with one sentence per item naming the clause of the theorem that does the work in your verdict. If one of them — I have a suspicion about (f) — only looks like a threat because it exploits an agent who updates wrongly, put it in its own bottom tier and say what's wrong with it. Then give the maxim "when information is free, take it" a single survivability score from 0 to 10. Please answer in American English.
