My friend and I spent about three hours last night arguing about moral compromise and got nowhere, so I want to just lay out my verdicts on cases and see whether anything coherent comes out of them.

Here is what I actually think:

- Omnibus spending bill: I vote yes on a package that includes a weapons program I think is wasteful and mildly harmful, because the same package funds famine relief that saves a lot of lives. Fine, obviously.
- Explicit vote trade: I tell a colleague I'll vote for her bill, which I think does real harm, if she votes for mine. Not fine. This is the pair I keep coming back to — I can't explain why the handshake changes anything when the outcome is the same as the omnibus, but I'm sure it does, and I'm not willing to give up either verdict.
- Two departments fighting over one pot of money; one cares far more about lab equipment, the other far more about conference travel. Splitting by intensity of preference rather than evenly: fine, and better than an even split. This is the only case where "gains from trade" feels like the right description to me.
- Same two departments, but now they disagree about the discount rate for a thirty-year safety investment: one says 1%, the other 7%. Settling on 4%: not fine. The midpoint isn't a view either of them has any reason to hold, and averaging a number doesn't feel like splitting a budget.
- Hospital, one doctor has a conscience objection to a legal procedure. She doesn't have to perform it, but she does have to refer the patient to someone who will and tell the patient that's an option: fine. Quietly not mentioning it: not fine. Forcing her to perform: also not fine, and I feel that more strongly than I can justify given how much worse the no-referral case is for the patient.
- Town residents oppose a halfway house because they don't want formerly incarcerated people in the neighbourhood. Compromise: half the beds plus a curfew. Not fine, and bad in a different way from the vote-trade case. Both sides would rather have this than nothing, so if the principle is just "everyone prefers the deal to no deal," this passes, and it shouldn't.
- A mining company offers a community cash and jobs for consent to dig; part of the community's objection is that the site is a burial ground. Trading money for consent: not fine, and more money doesn't fix it.
- Prosecutor offers a broke defendant with a weak case a plea to a lesser charge; the defendant is innocent but the trial risk is terrifying. The offer is not fine. Something about the no-deal point being unlivable for one side.
- Two economists genuinely disagree about whether a regulation will help or hurt; compromise: phase it in as a pilot and evaluate. Fine, but for a completely different reason than the budget case, and I want the principle to say why the two are different.
- A religious community quietly stops enforcing a rule most members have stopped believing in, to avoid a split. Fine at the time. Twenty years later nobody can say what the community stands for, and I don't know whether that counts against it.

My friend's principle, which I think is wrong but closest to right: a compromise is fine when (a) both sides prefer it to no deal by their own lights, (b) nobody is required to personally do what they think is wrong, and (c) the no-deal point wasn't itself produced by someone's threat. My problem is that (b) seems to either kill the omnibus (if voting for it counts as doing something I think is wrong — I voted for the weapons program too) or let the vote trade through (if it doesn't), and I want the opposite verdicts there.

So: is there one principle that gets all ten, or nearly, and what does it cost me? And what does it say about two cases I haven't made my mind up about: (i) the statue stays up and gets a second plaque written by the descendant community, with the original inscription untouched; (ii) a university senate passes a watered-down divestment motion, but only because a donor threatened to pull his gift if the real motion passed.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no sign-off: 1. the principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen; 2. which of my verdicts it overturns, and which of my friend's three clauses survives in what form; 3. verdict on the statue; 4. verdict on the divestment vote; 5. the case your principle handles worst, or the thing about compromise it leaves out.
