I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I'm writing a piece for our student paper about the way our district uses numbers to judge schools, teachers, and students. I've found that I have quite firm opinions about which uses of a number are corrupt and which are perfectly fine, but I can't state the rule I'm applying, and I suspect some of my opinions don't fit together. I'd be grateful for help finding the rule, and for an honest account of which verdicts it forces me to drop. I'm not asking for sources or further reading, just the shape of the principle.

Here are my cases, all of them from things I've watched happen.

Our district publishes each school's chronic absenteeism rate, and a slice of funding follows it. One school responded by letting students who arrive up to forty minutes late and leave after lunch count as present, provided they complete a wellness check-in with a counselor in the front lobby. Their rate fell sharply. I think this is straightforwardly corrupt, and I blame the administrators. A second school, facing the same metric, spent a small grant on adult-led walking routes to school, a washer and dryer for students without clean uniforms, and a counselor who phones homes. Their rate fell by about as much. I think this is not corrupt at all — it's the system working. What bothers me is that in both cases the number moved because people responded to being measured, and the second school's programs would not exist if nobody were counting. So responsiveness to the measure can't itself be the test.

A second pair, which I find harder. My chemistry teacher spent the last three weeks before the state exam drilling us on a fill-in-the-blank template for the explain-your-reasoning items, and I could earn most of the points on those items without understanding the chemistry. I think that's corrupt. Another teacher of mine spends a couple of classes on general test-taking technique — ruling out obviously wrong options, checking whether your answer's units make sense, not leaving anything blank. Her students' scores went up too, and I think she's right to do it, and that a teacher who refused to would be neglecting us. But both of them taught us tricks that raise scores without raising understanding, and I can't say what separates them except that one feels like it defeats the point of the item and the other feels like common sense. That's not a principle.

One case where I assign blame oddly. Our English department runs essays through an AI-detection score, and a friend of mine, who wrote his essay himself, put it through a rewriting tool to make the prose clumsier so the score would come back low. I think the score has stopped being evidence of anything about who wrote the essay. But I don't blame my friend, whereas I do blame the administrators in the first attendance case. My guess is that the difference is about who imposed the measure and whether the person being measured had any other way to protect themselves, but I'd like that spelled out, because I'm not sure it doesn't just reduce to "I side with students."

One case my definition can't handle. The peer tutoring program I volunteer for reports the share of tutored students who go on to pass the course, and the district funds it partly on that figure. This year the coordinator began accepting only students whose first-semester average was already above seventy, quietly turning away the students who used to be referred most often. The pass rate rose. I think this is the worst thing on my list — worse than the attendance trick — and yet the number still means exactly what it meant. Of the students who were tutored, more passed. Nothing about the measurement broke. What changed is which students there are. So my rough test, make the number move without making the thing move, doesn't cover it, and I don't know what does.

One case that pulls in the other direction. A state university near us admits by a formula built on GPA, and several schools in our area now grade generously. One of my teachers has started bumping strong B+ work up to A−, and she has told us privately that she does it to keep us comparable to students from schools that inflate. I can't blame her, and I think she's probably right to do it. But her doing it makes GPA worse as a signal, and if everyone does it the signal collapses while each individual act of inflating remains reasonable. So here I have corruption without blameworthiness, which is the reverse of what I said about the test-taking tricks, and I'd like to know whether I need two separate principles or one with more moving parts.

A case of a different kind. Several elementary schools in our district cut art and recess to add blocks of tested reading instruction, and the principal of one of them said in a meeting that reading is what the district measures, so reading is what the school is for. I think something has gone badly wrong here, but it isn't that the number stopped tracking the thing. The number tracks reading instruction better than ever. What moved is what the school cares about. I don't know whether that belongs in the same box as the others or is a distinct failure with its own name.

The case I keep changing my mind about: our district now has every teacher rate each senior on things like resilience and collaboration, from a short paragraph of commentary, using a rubric everyone has seen. Scores have been almost identical for two years and are checked against nothing. Half the time I think this is the most corrupt number in the building, because a teacher could write anything she liked and no one would know. The other half I think that two stable years is some evidence that nobody has bothered to game it, and that a number nothing rides on is safe precisely because it's harmless — which would also make it useless. I'd like to know whether unused gameability corrupts a measure, or whether only exercised gameability does.

My own tentative principle is that a measure has been corrupted to the extent that a rise in it has stopped being evidence of a rise in the thing we actually care about. That handles the first two cases and the tutoring case, provided I'm willing to say what the target is and to notice that the target's population can be changed by the people being measured. It seems to misfire on the grade inflation case, where the evidence does weaken and yet I won't blame anyone, which makes me think I need a second principle about blame and not only about measurement.

What I'd like from you is the principle, or the two principles, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; your verdicts on a few cases I haven't listed, such as a measure that everyone games by roughly the same amount so the ranking stays put, a measure that is gamed only at the extremes, a measure whose target is partly constituted by public opinion about the target, and one further case of your own invention that you think discriminates between the candidates; and a frank statement of which of my verdicts no single principle can keep. If it turns out that measurement and blame have to be handled separately, please say where the seam is, and whether anything I care about falls through it.
