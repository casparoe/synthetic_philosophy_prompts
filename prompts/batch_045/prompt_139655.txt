I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about duties of rescue, and arguing from first principles has not helped me: every principle I pick up either swallows more than I can accept or lets me off the hook in cases where I plainly am not off the hook. So I want to start from my verdicts on a spread of cases and see what, if anything, captures them. The case everyone reaches for is Singer's drowning child in the shallow pond, and I have put a version of it first; the rest are variations I have been turning over. I have recorded the verdicts as I actually have them, including the ones I am not proud of.

1. A child is face down in an ornamental pond a few metres from where I am walking. I can wade in and lift her out. I will ruin a suit worth about six hundred dollars and be an hour late. Verdict: I must go in. Someone who walked on would deserve severe condemnation.

2. The same pond, but wading in means missing a job interview that would very likely change the course of my life. Verdict: I still think I must go in. But I notice that I would describe someone who walked on as having done something terrible rather than something unthinkable, and I would not think he should be punished or that his friends should treat him differently afterwards.

3. An email appeal from a verified charity: four thousand dollars will, with high reliability, save the life of a child I will never meet on another continent. I could give it without anything I would call hardship. Verdict: I think I ought to give. I do not give. I do not feel like a monster, and I judge myself far less harshly than I judge the man in case 1 — which I cannot square with 1 and 2 unless there is a difference between what I owe and how much blame I incur.

4. Forwarding a one-line message to a colleague that would spare her a small but real loss. Cost to me: seconds. Verdict: I ought to; if I forget, I am careless rather than culpable. Contrast: a one-line message that would prevent someone's serious injury. Verdict: I must send it, and failing to is close to the pond case. I do not know whether the difference here is just the size of the benefit, or whether there is a cliff between the trivial and the serious that proportionality cannot generate.

5. A surgery fund must reach fifty thousand dollars by Friday. An anonymous donor has pledged to cover any shortfall at the close of the appeal, so my twenty dollars will not change whether the surgery happens; it will only change how much the donor pays. Verdict: I feel released. I also feel, on reflection, that this is the case where I am most likely to be reasoning badly.

6. Twenty dollars to a vaccination programme with roughly a one-in-three-thousand chance of saving a life — no threshold, no top-up donor. Verdict: here I feel a genuine, if faint, pull, and I think it would be better to give. So my verdicts in 5 and 6 come apart even though the money is the same size, and I want to know whether the right principle is tracking expected difference-making, and what it does with the offsetting in 5, where my money changes nothing about the outcome and only rearranges whose money pays.

7. A neighbour's toddler wanders onto my land and is trapped in the decorative pond I chose to build and could have fenced. I did nothing careless and broke no rule. Verdict: my duty is stronger than a passer-by's, and I owe more in the way of cost and risk. But I resist saying I owe as much as someone who pushed the child in. Is there a third category between having caused the danger and merely being present at it, or does my verdict here just record a dislike of drawing lines?

8. Twenty people on the bank, all able to swim, any one of them sufficient. Verdict: no one is released by the presence of the others — I cannot excuse myself by pointing at them. But I also think the ones who hang back owe me something, not the child: if I go in alone, they owe me a share of what I gave up. That feeling is real and I do not know what kind of thing it is.

9. The water is freezing and there is roughly a one-in-twenty chance that going in kills me too. Verdict: I am permitted to stay on the bank, and I would admire rather than condemn the person who goes in. At one-in-a-thousand I think I must go in. I cannot say where between those numbers the change happens, or whether there is a point rather than a slope.

There is also a structural worry that sits on top of all this. If the principle is of the form "prevent something very bad whenever the cost to you is small compared with the bad averted," then there is no natural stopping point: satisfying it in one case simply reveals the next, and the aggregate is a life of substantial sacrifice. I reject that greedy reading, and I do not think my rejection is mere weakness of will. But I cannot tell whether the limit I want is a ceiling on total sacrifice, a prerogative to pursue my own projects, or something about the demands arriving from many separate sources — and whichever it is, it has to leave case 2 intact.

The collisions I most want addressed are 1 against 3, and the limit I want in the structural worry against cases 2 and 9.

My tentative principle, such as it is: (i) I owe a rescue when, and to the degree that, I could avert something very bad at a cost to myself that is small in comparison with the badness averted; (ii) how much blame I incur for failing is a separate matter, fixed by whether the need was present to my senses or had to be inferred from statistics and testimony.

What I would like: test that principle against the nine cases, clause by clause, saying which case forces each clause and where the clause has to be repaired or abandoned; state its verdicts on cases I have not listed, in particular a victim who will not exist for sixty years, an animal whose suffering is comparable to a child's, and a rescue needing exactly one of a thousand bystanders; and tell me plainly which of my verdicts a defensible principle cannot keep. If there is a cheaper principle that overturns two of my verdicts and a more complicated one that saves them all, give me both and say which you would adopt. Finally, I want the result stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking again, and I want to know whether it should be framed as a principle about what I owe, or as a principle about reasons plus a separate account of blameworthiness — my 1-versus-3 split is what makes me doubt that "duty" is the right single word here.
