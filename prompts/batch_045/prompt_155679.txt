I volunteer with a small grantmaking group that funds speculative long-termist projects, and after two funding rounds I've noticed that my gut reactions to cases are badly out of joint with each other. I'd like to know whether there is a single principle behind them, or whether I simply have to give some up. Here is where I land:

(a) An engineering project whose risk model I broadly trust puts it at roughly a one-in-a-million chance of averting a catastrophe that would kill everyone alive today; the cost to us is about $200,000. I fund it without much hesitation.

(b) Same shape, much larger numbers: a proposal our physicist member estimates at a one-in-a-trillion chance of preserving a civilization of 10^30 people. The expected value dwarfs (a), and I want to say no.

(c) The mugging case: a stranger emails us saying that $200,000 will let him, by means he won't specify, save 10^100 lives. I refuse instantly. What troubles me is that I feel roughly the same reluctance in (b) and (c), even though in (b) I accept the number and in (c) I don't believe it at all — so my resistance in (b) can't be the usual move of dismissing the probability estimate, which is how people normally get out of this.

(d) Bundling: ten thousand independent projects, each with a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a very large good, each cheap. I would happily fund the whole portfolio, and yet asked about any single one of them — including the marginal one, dropping which lowers the portfolio's chance of success by one part in ten thousand — I want to say no. Is that straightforwardly incoherent, or could a defensible principle be irreducibly about portfolios rather than about individual acts?

(e) Uncertain magnitude: a project with a one-in-a-billion chance of a good whose size we can't pin down — our best guess is that every order of magnitude between 10^10 and 10^50 lives is equally likely. The expected value here is far above (b), and I recoil more than I do in (b). Something in me penalizes not knowing how big the payoff is, over and above the effect that uncertainty has on the expectation.

(f) Harms: a one-in-a-million chance of causing 10^20 units of suffering. I feel far more strongly that we must avoid this than that we must pursue the mirror-image good. A friend says this is just loss aversion and that I should drop it; I can't tell whether she's right.

(g) Costless: a button that genuinely costs us nothing, with a one-in-a-trillion chance of a 10^30-person payoff. Here I flip and press it, and I notice I would press it even if the chance were far smaller. That makes me suspect my reluctance in (b) was entirely about what else the money could do, and hence that I don't really have a problem with fanaticism at all — but that conclusion feels too cheap, and it sits badly with (c), where the money matters and I refuse anyway.

My tentative principle, such as it is: I seem to deflate stated probabilities by a factor that grows with the size of the claimed payoff, roughly as if the effective probability were the stated one divided by the logarithm of the payoff. That keeps (a) and gets (b) and (e) right. But someone in the group pointed out that it doesn't do the work I wanted, since the product still grows without bound as the payoff does: I would remain committed to chasing a one-in-a-googol chance of a good that is large enough, which is exactly what I was trying to avoid.

So: can you give me one principle, or failing that a short list of candidates, that captures as much of this pattern as possible? For each, please say which clause is forced by which of my verdicts, what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on — whether I should buy a thousand, or a billion, independent tickets of the (b) type, given that my bundling instinct in (d) pulls me toward yes while my verdict in (b) pulls toward no — and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If some verdicts have to go, I would rather be told plainly which are the weakest and why than have all seven preserved by a principle with a separate bolted-on exception for each.
