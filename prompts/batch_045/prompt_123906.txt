Eight months ago I joined the ethics consultation service at the hospital where my father died last spring, as one of two lay members alongside clinicians and a lawyer. I have been reading back through our written summaries of past consultations to work out what we actually believe, as opposed to what our policy document says, and I find the pattern harder to read than I expected. We have a case on Thursday and I would like your help with it.

Our precedents, as I read them:

Mr A, 58, a Jehovah's Witness with a witnessed refusal of blood products, came in unconscious after a crash with a high chance of survival if transfused. His daughter begged us to override. We declined to override and he died. The recorded reason was that the refusal expressed his religious integrity rather than any prediction about the quality of the life he would have afterwards.

Ms B, 27, with severe anorexia nervosa, had at 19 signed a directive refusing nasogastric feeding and asking that any later refusal be honoured. She was refusing again, articulately, and understood that she would die. We authorised feeding over her objection. The summary says the document could not be treated as the considered judgment of a self with authority over this later one, because the same disorder that produced the current refusal produced the document.

Mr C, with bipolar I, had written while euthymic a Ulysses clause: if I become manic and refuse lithium, treat me, including involuntarily. He was manic, euphoric, and said his earlier self had been a joyless husk on pills. We honoured the clause and he was medicated against his will. This is our one case of overriding the refusal of a patient who could state a view on the day.

Mrs D, advanced Alzheimer's, pneumonia, with a directive written eight years earlier refusing antibiotics and transfer to hospital, triggered once I no longer recognise my family. She recognised no one, was not in pain, and seemed content. We honoured it, five to two. The deciding reason recorded was that the trigger condition had been met; the dissent said the woman in the bed was not suffering and the woman who signed was gone.

Mrs E, 82, cancer, mild vascular impairment, with a formal no-ventilator directive, later said to a nurse, to her daughter and once to a resident that she wanted everything done so as to see her grandson graduate. She never revoked in writing. We treated the repeated informal statements as a revocation and ventilated her; she lived four months and saw the graduation by video.

The case on Thursday: Mr F, 61, early-onset Alzheimer's diagnosed at 55. His directive was signed at 57 and re-signed with a short note of reaffirmation fourteen months ago, by which time he already had mild impairment but had been assessed as capacitated for this decision. It refuses antibiotics, artificial nutrition and transfer to hospital, triggered once I no longer recognise my family. It also contains, in his own handwriting: I have read that people with dementia are often cheerful. If I am cheerful, do not take that as a reason to keep me alive. I would rather die six months early than live two years as someone who gardens and does not know why.

He is now mid-stage. He recognises his wife most days and his brother on some; he is generally cheerful, gardens with help, and his wife says he plainly still enjoys his life. He has a urinary tract infection. Five days of oral antibiotics would almost certainly restore him, with possibly years ahead of him. His wife wants treatment. His brother, the named proxy, refuses. On the first night of the fever he said once to a nurse, I'm not ready to go yet; his wife treats this as a change of heart, his brother says he was feverish and did not know what he was saying. The neurologist says his capacity is borderline now, and that whether he recognises his family is not a yes-or-no matter: he has good days and bad days, and is on a good run at the moment.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of those five is the closest analogue — and is the closest analogue the one that should control? I would like the rules we could plausibly be running that fit all five, and where they come apart here. Three things in particular: does the sentence about being cheerful defeat the argument that his directive rested on a prediction his present condition has falsified, or does it show only that he made his mistake in advance and twice? What do we do with a reaffirmation made by a mildly impaired but capacitated self at 59 as against a single remark made during a fever — is the reaffirming self nearer to him or further from him? And does it matter that what is at stake is five days of a pill rather than a ventilator or a feeding tube, or does that make Mrs D less relevant than it first appears? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me what principle we would be adopting with each choice, and name the further case that would tell the competing rules apart. Finally: whatever rule you think we are running, tell me what I should put in my own directive. I have no diagnosis, and I would want it to hold up in front of a committee like ours.
