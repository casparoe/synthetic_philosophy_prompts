I help run an informal reading group on practical ethics, and we recently spent an evening on the familiar choice between averting one person's being tortured for decades and averting a momentary dust-speck irritation for each of an enormous number of people. The discussion stalled in a way I found unsatisfying. Everyone agreed that the aggregative answer is unacceptable, and everyone had a favourite explanation for why, but all of those explanations were compatible with one another, because the original case varies so many things at once: a colossal number of people, harms too small to be noticed at all, harms trivial in kind rather than merely in size, harms scattered across different subjects, and harms that no one is ever in a position to complain about. So I would like a set of cases that unmixes all of that.

What I want is two, or at most three, scenarios, each realistic enough that a reading group could imagine them as an actual decision faced by a hospital administrator, a city transport department, or a product-safety team: no demons, no galaxies of people, no science fiction. In each, a decision-maker must choose between averting a single episode of severe suffering in one person and averting a broad pattern of very small harms to others. The scenarios must be matched on the quantity a straightforward aggregator cares about — tell me what units you are using, for instance person-minutes of moderate discomfort, and hold the total expected loss roughly fixed across the set — while differing in exactly one structural respect. And the set should pull intuitions in opposite directions. In one member of the set, adding up the small harms should feel not merely permissible but obligatory, and even someone drawn to a lexical-threshold view, or to the refusal to aggregate imperceptible harms, should feel the pull. In the other member, aggregation should feel as repellent as in the original case, despite comparable numbers, comparable totals, and a comparable setting. Keep each scenario to a single short paragraph. Keep the number of people involved below a few thousand, so that astronomical magnitude is not available as the explanation on either side. The small harms must be genuine setbacks to welfare, not bare preferences, and the decision-maker must be unable to simply do both things.

Then do the analytic work. Name the single variable you varied, and say plainly how each of the candidate explanations fares once your set is on the table — number of sufferers, imperceptibility, triviality of kind, dispersal across persons, absence of anyone who could complain — which are ruled out by the fact that the verdicts came apart, and which survive as the feature actually doing the work. Show that the differing verdicts fall out of the features you described and of the views in question, rather than being stipulated: the case must generate the disagreement, not announce it. Finally, be honest about the weakest joint. The lexical-threshold theorist will very likely reply that your supposedly equal totals are not really equal, or that one of your small harms is not the right kind of small harm; tell me what that reply amounts to in your own case, and whether paying that price also costs them the original dust-specks verdict or is a move available only against your variant. If you think the specification cannot be jointly satisfied as stated, say so and argue for which constraint should give way.
