I've been working through Kant's ethics on my own — no classes, just a beat-up used copy of the Groundwork, some free lecture transcripts, and way too many browser tabs — and I keep stumbling into the exact same argument in every thread I find about it. It's about the part where you're supposed to check whether you could will your maxim to become a universal law. After about a year of poking at this, I can't tell whether anyone has actually moved.

Here's the shape of it. One camp reads the formula more or less as "what if everybody did that": you picture a world where everyone breaks promises whenever it's convenient, notice that promising stops working in that world, and say the maxim fails — which makes it sound like a coordination test, or a consequences test with a German accent. The other camp says no, absolutely not: Kant says contradiction, not outcomes, and if your test reaches its verdict by totting up what goes wrong in the imagined world, you've reinvented rule utilitarianism and put a wig on it. Then a third group says the whole thing is either empty or circular — either the "contradiction" got manufactured by picking a description of the maxim that guarantees the verdict you wanted, or there just isn't one, because a world where everyone lies is perfectly easy to conceive, it's merely horrible.

Three things I keep noticing that I haven't seen dealt with head-on, and I want to know whether they're where the real disagreement lives or whether I'm chasing noise.

First, what I call "descriptive slack." Take something like deceiving someone who asks me a question. Described thinly — I withhold or distort information when it suits me — it looks like it can't be universalized. Described thickly — I mislead an official of a murderous government who is asking where my neighbour is hiding — it looks like it might universalize just fine, or at least the verdict gets murky. Both descriptions are mine; both are honest. So who picks the description, and how fine-grained is it allowed to be? Every argument I read seems to slide the description around until the test outputs the answer the author already had.

Second, what I've started calling necessarily exclusive maxims. Some things just can't be done by everyone, not because they're wrong but because of what they are: showing up at the trailhead first to get the quiet campsite, putting in the offer before the open house, being the person who gets the last seat. Run these through the formula and they fail every time — the "universal" version is nonsense — but they don't feel like the same kind of failure as the promise case at all. If the test can't tell those apart, something's off.

Third, I think there might be three different failure modes getting run together under one label: the practice destroys itself (the institution you were exploiting stops existing), the world can't accommodate it (everyone doing it is physically or conceptually impossible), and something like "I couldn't will that as part of a world I'm a member of," which seems to be about what I can rationally want rather than about contradiction or consequences. People quote all three as if they were one thing.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't a fight about Kant at all. It's three seperate questions wearing one name: whether the formula is supposed to be a procedure a person can actually run when deciding what to do; what kind of failure is meant to show up (logical, conceptual, practical, consequential); and who owns the description of the action — the agent, or whoever is grading the agent. If that's right, the talking-past-each-other is structural and no amount of inventing new examples will fix it. But I might just be inventing a tidy diagnosis because I'm frustrated, so push back on it if it's wrong.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on Kant. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, a case of people answering different questions under one heading, or is a hidden question — about what a maxim even is, or how actions get individuated, which is really philosophy of action rather than ethics — doing all the work? What does each side actually say that makes them dig in, rather than a list of the positions? And given whatever you think is going on, what would count as progress here: a real account of how maxims get individuated, some formal result about what "willing a world" can and can't be, a distinction that splits the three failure modes cleanly, or dropping the idea that this is a decision procedure at all? Or is this one of those questions where I should expect no resolution — and if so, can you say why rather than just gesturing at how long it's been going?
