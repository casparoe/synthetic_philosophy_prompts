The argument was between two people at a conference dinner — Marta, who helps run a small platform for what's called moral trade (the idea being that two people with different moral views can each do something the other one values, in exchange for something they value: Marta is a global-welfarist who thinks farmed animal suffering is the priority, and her pending counterparty is a localist Catholic donor who thinks obligations run first to the poor near him and that her cage-free campaign stuff is optional at best — he'd give to his parish food bank and she'd give to the campaign, both in escrow, both conditional on the other), and Dev, a political philosopher who thinks the whole thing is corrupt. It went roughly like this, and I've numbered the moves so you can score them:

(1) Dev: Morality isn't a market. If you think the food bank is worth funding, fund it for that reason. What the platform does is turn moral reasons into prices, and it asks each side to do something they themselves think is the worse thing, in order to buy the other side's compliance. Conviction that's for sale isn't conviction.

(2) Marta: That is just what trade is. I'd rather have his money going to the campaign than mine going to the food bank and he's the reverse — comparative advantage in doing good. And the comparison isn't everyone doing the best thing by their own lights; it's the no-trade baseline, where we each give to our own cause and neither of us produces anything the other thinks matters at all.

(3) Dev: Gains from trade come from differing costs, not differing beliefs. In a market both sides want the same thing — money — and trade because their costs differ. You two don't have different costs, you have different opinions about what's valuable. Your gain is manufactured out of disagreement itself, which is either magic or, more likely, you harvesting what you take to be his mistake. If he's wrong about local obligations, your deal is a way of profiting from his error.

(4) Marta: The gains come from differing leverage, not differing beliefs — he has money and no appetite for campaigning, I have time and no money, and people who agree completely about what's valuable can still gain from swapping. And the disagreement is exactly why I want to hedge: I'm not certain my welfarist view is the right one, and buying outcomes that are good by his lights too is diversification. Expected value across moral views beats betting the lot on mine.

(5) Dev: If it's only hedging you don't need a market — a hedger quietly splits the donation and goes home. What the platform adds is conditionality and enforcement: I'll do this only if you do that. And that's where it rots. A trade is only worth doing if the other person wouldn't have done the thing anyway, and both of you have every incentive to insist that's true, so you've built a market in invented counterfactuals. Worse, the institution now has a stake in you both staying wrong, because a donor who converts to your view stops being a counterparty.

(6) Marta: The counterfactual problem is real but it's a design problem, not a refutation — disclosed baselines, escrow, one-shot blind matching, void the trade and send the deposit to a fallback charity if the action wasn't additional. Same issue the carbon-offset world has been grinding on for years. And your purity isn't free: if I walk away, the people who'd have got the money still don't get it, and they don't care which moral theory produced it.

(7) Dev: Even granting all that, look at which acts the trades select. Each side gives up whatever costs them least by their own lights, so the equilibrium sits at the floor of everyone's morality — people spending their moral effort on the acts they think are least worth doing, in exchange for credit. That's a machine for turning people into moral procurement officers.
