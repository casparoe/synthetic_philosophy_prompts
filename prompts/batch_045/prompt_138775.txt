Not a philosopher, so sorry if this comes out clumsy. I work in data — mostly hospitals merging their record systems after an acquisition — and a few months ago I got a letter about a follow-up to a procedure I never had, because someone else's chart had been stapled onto mine. So the question of when you can tell what your data is *about* has gotten personal for me, and a friend who did a philosophy degree walked me into the following argument, which I can't find the exit from. I've rewritten it in my own words, so blame me and not her.

Setup, so we mean the same thing: suppose a description of the world tells you only the *shape* — which positions there are, which things stand in which relations to which, what patterns those relations follow, what would happen if you poked which part — and never tells you the *filling*, i.e. what the things sitting in those positions are like in themselves. Call the positions roles and the things in them occupants. And say you've identified a description's ontology when you know *which* things the occupants are, not merely that the roles are filled by something or other.

(1) The filling is inaccessible. Everything we could ever aquire — every observation, measurement, or intervention — arrives as more shape. If you permuted the fillings while leaving every relation and every law exactly as it was, nothing we could detect would differ.

(2) Identifying the ontology requires knowing which things the occupants are. Knowing the shape gets you at most "some things or other fill these roles", and that is a claim about the shape, not about what there is.

(3) The any-junk-will-do lemma. Take any shape description S, and take any collection C of things you like — numbers, sense-data, table-shaped arrangements of dust, angels — as long as C has at least as many members as S has positions (and if C is infinite, as long as it's big enough in the right way). Then you can assign the members of C to the positions and choose relations on C so that C has exactly the shape S describes. In other words the shape never rules out any inventory that's merely big enough.

(4) So by (1) nothing accessible distinguishes candidate inventories, and by (3) any big enough inventory fits whatever shape we end up with. Ontology identification is impossible in principle. Not hard — impossible.

(5) And if a claim that we could never, even in principle, have any reason to accept or deny has no content, then there is no fact of the matter about what exists, over and above the shape. I've flagged this as a separate step because I suspect it's doing work it isn't entitled to.

Then the part I can't accept:

(6) Run (1)–(5) on my own problem. Everything in the two systems is relations: identifiers, timestamps, codes, links. So by (4) I can't identify the patients, and by (5) there's no fact of the matter whether this chart is about me. But that's nuts — I can walk down to the ward andlook at the person, or check my own body for a scar that isn't there.

(7) Worse, the argument itself talks about objects, relations, and positions, and by its own lights none of those are identified either, so it can't say what it's concluding.

(8) And the step in (5) is napalm: if never-having-any-reason is what empties a claim of content, then every claim about anything unobservable — other people's feelings, the deep past, what your partner meant by that text — is contentless too. I'm not swallowing that.

I've tried three patches and none of them worked. First: add intervention facts, "what happens if I set node 7 to x", on the thought that poking latches onto a particular occupant rather than a bare position. My friend says counterfactual profiles are just more shape and the lemma eats them too, and I think she's right. Second: anchor the whole thing in the few fillings we *are* acquainted with — my own headache, the red I'm seeing right now — and let identification spread outward along the relations from there. That does fix (6); the ward visit is an anchor. But it doesn't fix the case I actually care about, microscopic physics, where there's nothing to be acquainted with and you only ever get the shape. Third: make the relations part of what's held fixed instead of something the lemma gets to choose. My worry there is circularity — "the real relations" seems to smuggle in exactly the identification I was trying to get out of the argument — and whatever pinned them down would either be more shape or collapse back into acquaintance.

So: where does this go wrong? Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (6) false and keep the thought that physics hands you shape and not filling, and give up whatever makes (8) follow; if those two can't both survive, tell me which is cheaper to lose and why. My own guess, which I don't trust, is that (3) is fine as mathematics and the rot is either in (2) or in that extra step in (5) — but I can't tell which, and I can't tell what either rejection commits me to.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you're rejecting, or that the argument is sound and I should eat the conclusion — and then give me the reasoning and a price list for the alternatives. No preamble, please don't restate my argument back at me, and don't just point me at a literature: I want the diagnosis itself.
