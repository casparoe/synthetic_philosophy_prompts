I'm writing my term paper for a graduate seminar on collective responsibility and I've hit the point where I don't trust myself to pick a side yet. Before I defend any particular answer to the inefficacy objection — my own emission, my own vote, my own packet of chicken make no difference to anything, so why should I not do exactly as I like? — I want the specification. Please write me the desiderata: what an adequate theory of individual contribution to collective harm has to be able to do, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with each desideratum given a short label I can use as a section heading.

Two constraints on the exercise, so the list is aimed at something.

First, fix the purpose, and tell me if the purposes pull apart. I want a theory good enough for two jobs: (a) first-personal guidance for an agent standing at the till, or at the ballot box, or at the thermostat, who has to decide now with the knowledge such agents actually have; and (b) retrospective and second-personal assessment — blame, apology, demands for compensation, the question of whether I owe anything to anyone for what I've already done. If no single account can serve both, say so, because that would itself be the most interesting thing in my paper.

Second, no citations, no reading lists, no citations-by-author: I have the seminar bibliography already and I'm asking about the shape of the problem, not the literature. Refer to families of view by their structure, not by who holds them.

Here is the case material I want the requirements built out of. The central one I've been chewing on: a supplier ships chicken to a supermarket in crates of sixty; the store re-orders whenever its shelf counter hits zero; and there is an app that publishes the current counter, so a shopper can check it in the aisle. On Monday the counter reads one: your purchase triggers a re-order with certainty. On Tuesday it reads thirty-four: your purchase cannot trigger a re-order today, though it moves the counter to thirty-three. On Wednesday the app is broken and tells you only that the counter was reset at a random point this morning, so your chance of being the trigger is one in sixty. Same act, same bird, same supply chain, three quite different causal profiles — and two of the three are knowable at the point of decision. A variant: sixty people each have to press a button for the crate to be ordered, and no one's press does anything alone, so each contribution is necessary but insufficient. A further variant: the shopper knows that if they don't buy the pack, the next shopper will, and the crate would be ordered by Saturday either way. And a variant on the individuation of the act itself: one pack a week for a year, or a household of four that pools its shopping, or a buyers' club of sixty that orders together — whether the purchase makes a difference seems to depend on how finely we slice the act and whom we count as the agent.

So the cases the theory must get right, and the distinctions it must not collapse, include at least these: symmetric overdetermination with no marginal difference (the harmless-torturers structure, thousands of agents, an imperceptible increment each); the known-threshold case; the known-tiny-probability case; the necessary-but-insufficient case; the substitution case; and the grain-of-action case. It must keep apart, and say something about each of: causation, wrongness, culpability, blameworthiness, and what is to be done now. It must say whether making a difference to an intermediate variable — the counter — counts as making a difference to anything that matters, or whether only the outcome counts. It must handle the generalisation test: if my excuse is sound for me it had better be sound for every other shopper, and I need to know what accepting it does to me over forty years of shopping. It must be usable by an agent who cannot estimate a one-in-sixty chance of decisiveness, or explain why it is acceptable that it isn't. And it must not gerrymander: the same apparatus has to work for emissions, for meat, and for votes, and any place where it returns different verdicts for those three had better be traceable to a real structural difference between them rather than to an ad hoc exception.

Two more things I want, and they are the part I care most about.

Mark which of the desiderata are genuine and which are the prejudices of one camp — the demand that no expected-utility apparatus appear anywhere, for instance, or the demand that wrongness must always track actual causal difference, or the demand that the theory never exceed what common sense already believes about individual obligation. And identify any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument: I suspect there is a real conflict between wanting the theory to be exactly faithful to the causal structure and wanting it to be action-guiding for agents who cannot see that structure, another between refusing to let agents profit from their own ignorance and refusing to let the theory become fanatical about trivial acts, and possibly a third between cross-domain uniformity and getting the known-threshold case right. Then tell me which single requirement you think excludes the most candidate theories, and why.

Only if it fits without crowding the above, add a brief scorecard — three or four sentences each, no more — for the main families: expected-difference accounts, complicity or participation accounts, character-based or self-corruption accounts, accounts that begin from a collective duty and distribute it, and accounts built on the idea that one can help without making a difference. I can do the scoring myself in the essay if the criteria are right; the criteria are what I'm paying you for.
