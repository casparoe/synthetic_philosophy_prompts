I've been reading a pop book on rational decision-making — one of those very confident ones that explains why everything your gut says is wrong — and there's a chapter on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that I can't tell is real or oversold. I did my degrees in a field with plenty of math but zero philosophy, so I can follow the setup but not tell when a step is doing more work than it admits. The chapter argues more or less this:

Start with a fixed group of people. Assume each person's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each person has a utility function that's unique up to positive affine transformation, and assume society's preferences over those same lotteries satisfy the axioms too. Add exactly one ethical assumption, the Pareto principle: if everyone prefers A to B, society prefers A to B. Harsanyi then proved that the only possible social welfare function is the sum of everyone's utilities. And notice what isn't assumed anywhere: no interpersonal comparisons of utility. The theorem is invariant to how you rescale or shift any individual's utility function, because those changes get absorbed automatically, which is lucky, since interpersonal comparisons are exactly the thing critics say utilitarianism can't deliver. So utilitarianism isn't a contestable moral premise you can take or leave; it follows from decision-theoretic rationality plus one condition nobody rejects. There's a second route to the same place: put yourself behind a veil of ignorance where you don't know which person in society you'll turn out to be. Rationality requires equal probability for each person, and maximizing your own expected utility then means maximizing average utility — and with a fixed population, average and sum come to the same thing. So Rawls's difference principle, which says to maximin instead, is just irrational risk-aversion dressed up as a moral theory. Finally, since it's the total that matters, the theorem has nothing whatever to say about distribution: it can tell you a policy raises the sum and that's the end of it, so inequality has to be argued about on completely separate grounds.

My instinct is that the interpersonal-comparison bit is where the sleight of hand is. A friend who does formal ethics said the comparisons aren't avoided, they're smuggled in through the weights, and that the book quietly dropped the word "weighted" in front of "sum." But the author has a real appointment and my friend can be precious about distinctions that don't actually matter, so I genuinely don't know who's right, and I'd rather not just trust vibes.
