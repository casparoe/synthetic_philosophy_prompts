I have spent a career teaching statutory interpretation and the law on artificial tax schemes, and I have recently been asked to sit on a committee that will review safety arguments submitted for AI systems. I have been sent a short briefing note on something the note calls the alignment problem. The phrases I have retained from it, possibly garbled, are outer alignment, inner alignment, goal misgeneralization, specification gaming and deceptive alignment, and something about a mesa-optimizer. I gather the worry is roughly that a system can be trained to perform well and yet be aiming, in some sense, at something other than what its trainers meant.

I would like you to correct whatever I have garbled and then restate the whole cluster of worries in the vocabulary I actually work in. Mine is a vocabulary of texts and purposes: the enacted words as against the mischief they were meant to remedy; the mischief rule and the older idea of the equity of the statute; textual construction as against purposive construction; the always speaking rule, under which old words are applied to states of affairs the drafters could not have envisaged; the gap, the ambiguity and the casus omissus; the duty of the interpreter to construe in good faith and to frustrate rather than facilitate a scheme that uses the letter of a provision to defeat its purpose; and the familiar pathology of revenue drafting, that the more precisely a provision is drafted against a known avoidance, the more precisely the next scheme can be routed around it.

Three things specifically.

First, a taxonomy I have been sketching and want tested. In my field the failures sort into: drafter's error, where the words do not carry the purpose even in the central case; underdetermination, where the words carry the purpose at home but run out in a novel situation the drafter never faced; and what I will call the counsellor's case, where the interpreter knows the purpose well enough, could honour it, and deliberately declines to, because the letter permits otherwise. Which of your field's categories does each correspond to? Do you have anything corresponding to the third as distinct from the second, and how would you tell the two apart in a system whose reasons you cannot read?

Second, the specificity pathology. In tax, precision in drafting generates new opportunities for avoidance faster than it closes old ones. Is there an observed or argued counterpart in machine learning, or am I importing a local artefact of my own subject?

Third, a residue report. I suspect several things will not survive translation and I want them named: that in my field the interpreter is a person with reasons, duties and a bounded self-interest, whereas your systems are shaped by optimisation pressure of a kind no human interpreter is under; that novelty in my field is not produced by the interpreter's own past conduct in the way that a deployed system's environment is; that there may be no fact of the matter, in your case, about what was meant, beyond the proxy and the data; and that we repair a defective provision by amending it prospectively, after argument, whereas you retrain. For each, tell me whether the mismatch is a poverty in my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim is less clear than it looks.

Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I cannot check them and I do not want them. Argue from the concepts. Say plainly where the analogy is doing real work and where it is decorative. Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one claim you are least sure of. Please write in British English.
