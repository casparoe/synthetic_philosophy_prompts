I'm applying for an eighteen-month dissertation fellowship in philosophy of history, and I need a two-page proposal. The topic is the familiar claim that history is written by the victors, and whether that is the right form of claim to be making at all.

My half-formed idea: both the defenders and the debunkers of the slogan tend to treat it as a claim about which answers get accepted — victors' versions get repeated, losers' get lost, and the standard objectivist reply is that professional historians recover the lost sources and correct for the slant. But those two positions may be arguing about the least interesting of three losses. Distinguish: (a) record-loss, the causal claim about which documents and testimony survive at all; (b) answer-loss, the claim about which accounts get believed and transmitted; and (c) question-loss, the claim that what disappears is not facts or interpretations but the agendas that made certain questions worth asking — so that an intact archive of the defeated side can be sitting in a reading room and still fail to restore what was lost, because question-agendas are carried by institutions, techniques, and living practices of inquiry rather than by documents. I suspect (c) is where the real content lies, and that it is compatible with the archival record being in perfect shape. Call it the question-loss hypothesis.

Please turn this into a proposal that does the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and say which of the three losses is genuinely at issue. Explain why the existing relativist and objectivist framings leave it open — in particular why recovering a loser's archive does not by itself settle anything. Give a method: what I would actually do, month by month, and how I would get traction on question-loss when it is defined as the absence of something, so that its absence is hard to observe. Argue for that method against the obvious alternatives. Say what a positive result and a negative result would each look like, and what would count as the project failing rather than merely being difficult. Keep the scope honest for eighteen months, and say plainly why this is a philosophical project rather than an empirical-archival one, given that I will not be doing original archival work.

The part I most want help with is making the question-loss hypothesis testable. My current thought is a paired comparison: two episodes in which the defeated party's records largely survived and are accessible, but which differ in how far that party's institutions of inquiry — its schools, journals, learned societies, bureaucratic record-keeping practices, traditions of commentary — were destroyed, dispersed, or co-opted. If the surviving factual material is comparable in both cases but the range of questions historians ask decades later is markedly narrower in the case where the institutions were destroyed, that is evidence for question-loss over mere archival attrition; if the range of questions is the same, my hypothesis is in trouble. Please sharpen this design, name what would disconfirm it, and tell me candidly if it is doing less work than I think — for instance if the two cases would be too unlike in other ways for the comparison to mean anything, or if question-loss turns out to be unfalsifiable as I have set it up.

Do not include a bibliography, footnotes, or named secondary sources; I will add the citations myself, and I would rather have the argument than the references. Where you use historical examples to make a point, say in a phrase what work the example is doing rather than describing it at length. Finally, close with a short note on how confident you are in the framing you have given me, and single out the one claim in the proposal you would be least willing to defend if a skeptic pressed hard on it.
