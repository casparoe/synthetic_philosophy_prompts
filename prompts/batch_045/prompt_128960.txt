I'm a journalist — science-and-ideas column for a Sunday magazine — and I'm finishing a piece on the Fermi paradox, so what I need from you is a number, not a tour of the literature. No citations; I couldn't fact-check them and I'd rather print your own reasoning than your memory of somebody else's.

Here's the proposition. Call the filter *ahead* of us if what keeps the galaxy quiet is mostly something that hasn't happened to a civilization at our stage yet — collapse, stagnation, transformation into something that doesn't spread — and *behind* us if the real bottleneck is in getting from dead matter to something like us: abiogenesis, the jump to complex cells, whatever turns out to be the hard bit. Give me your credence that it's more ahead than behind, as one number.

Stipulations so the thing has a truth value. Resolution, roughly as my source framed it: if within the next couple of centuries we turn up unambiguous biosignatures on a decent number of independent worlds, the behind story is basically dead and you lose the point; if we keep finding nothing while covering a genuinely large share of the plausible signal space, or if we do ourselves in first, you win it. Don't argue with the stipulation, just use it.

Then the decomposition, which is the part I actually want to print:

- How strong a constraint the silence even is — your P(we would by now have detected a civilization at our stage, or a bit past it), given the searches actually performed and the ways we'd actually be visible. My hunch is this term does most of the work and nobody says so out loud.
- Your credence in the hard-steps picture, where the big evolutionary transitions each ate a large share of the time Earth had available, and we're most of the way through them already.
- Your credence that loud and expanding is the default for a technological civilization, versus the quiet kind: turning inward, powering down into low-energy equilibria, switching to tight-beam links and stopping the leakage. Note that observationally this is nearly indistinguishable from extinction, which strikes me as a problem for the whole debate and not just one term in it.
- One my source raised that I can't shake: the selection effect. If the galaxy does eventually get filled, then observers like us should expect to find themselves near the leading edge of that process, early, because worlds behind the edge have already been settled and aren't turning out biological observers anymore. That allegedly makes our being first much less surprising and deflates the silence considerably. Say what you think of the argument — I don't care who came up with it.
- And a wrinkle I haven't seen handled: if a big chunk of the ahead-filter risk is our own near-term technology — AI, biotech, pick your favorite — then "the filter is ahead" and "the filter is us" are the same event, and no ammount of telescope time can separate them. Does that move the number, or is it only a re-description?

Combine them, then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and which one observation would move it the most.
