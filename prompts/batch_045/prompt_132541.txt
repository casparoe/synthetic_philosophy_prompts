I'm giving the opening talk at a three-day colloquium for literary translators in York at the end of next month, and I'd like to arrive with a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than my usual hopeful improvisation. The thesis I've committed myself to is this: a translator should treat an author's intentions as evidence, not instruction. Where the source text underdetermines a choice, the right question is what the sentence can be made to do in the new language, not what I was trying to do when I wrote it; and a translation that secures my intended reading by collapsing an ambiguity is, I want to argue, a worse translation than one that preserves the ambiguity at the cost of my reading. I've been calling this the resolute ambiguity rule in my notes, and I want to defend it as a norm for the profession rather than as a personal preference. The room will be about forty practising literary translators, mostly working into and out of European languages, three or four of whom have translated my own novels — my Italian translator, with whom I disagreed in public for twenty minutes about a single sentence at a festival panel last spring, will be in the second row. Alongside them: two publishing lawyers, one in-house at my own publisher and one who acts for authors' estates and is interested in moral rights; a scholar who does experimental work on what readers actually take from texts; and a chair who translates from Arabic and has said in print that theory is the last refuge of writers who can't take an edit. I've read the usual essays everyone cites — the one about the intentional fallacy, the one about the death of the author, the one claiming that a translation is not written for readers of the original — but I don't want to be lectured at about them, and I don't want my preparation to be a restatement of those arguments.

The argument I'll give rests on three claims, and I'd rather the objections were aimed at these than at the thesis in general. First, an epistemic claim: nobody outside my head, including a translator who has lived with a book for a year, has access to my intentions beyond what the text and its circumstances make available, so appeals to intention are always appeals to something inferred. Second, a claim about authority: even where my intention is available, it isn't decisive — I have repeatedly discovered intentions in my own published work that I did not know I had, and on one occasion I insisted for months that a minor character was lying in a scene only to be shown three pages later, in my own typescript, that I had made her truthful. Third, a claim about the practice: fiction is interpreted as a shared public object, so the text, and not my account of it, is what a reading is answerable to. I'll illustrate with actual cruxes from my own books: in The Salt Year the narrator's sentence about her brother is written so that the English leaves open whether an occurrence was habitual or one-off, while the Czech forces a choice; the Italian edition of the same novel renders a character's name, Clement, as Clemente, which imports a genial, slightly comic colouration I never intended; and the final sentence of the book turns on a "we" that in French would have to decide whether the reader is included.

What I need from you is the prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get in that room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased in the vocabulary of whoever is raising it — the translators talking about the line, the ear, register, pace and what a reader in their language will hear; the lawyers talking about the integrity of the work, derivative works, and what a contract can and cannot settle; the scholar wanting to know what my "shared public object" predicts and how anyone would test it; the chair wanting to know what practical difference any of this makes to someone working to a deadline. For each objection: a reply I could give in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft or evasive, and a mark for whether I should concede the point up front rather than be dragged to it. Tell me which single reply has to land if the talk is to be worth giving at all, and what I should hold even if the rest goes badly. Then include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I should say, so that you can aim at it, that twice in my career I have asked a publisher to stop a translation I thought had misread me — once successfully — and that I wrote a two-page explanatory note for the American edition of my first novel; I'm aware these are the obvious places where my own practice seems to betray the rule I'll be defending, and I'd like to know how badly they expose me and what, if anything, I can honestly say about them from the platform.
