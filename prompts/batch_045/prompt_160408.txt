I'm a back-end engineer — I work on payroll and billing infrastructure — and I grew up in Germany, which is where this question comes from. In the German payroll record there is a field for church membership, sitting right next to tax class and number of children; if it is filled in, your employer withholds a church-tax surcharge on your income tax and forwards it to the church, apparently as a matter of ordinary tax administration. The money funds a great deal of visible infrastructure — hospitals, care homes, kindergartens run by the big church-affiliated welfare organisations — and the same state also pays for Catholic and Protestant theology faculties at its public universities, where I'm told appointments involve some kind of sign-off from the church itself. Two details have always struck me as odd. First, membership normally begins with infant baptism, so a person can start owing church tax on their first payslip without ever having joined anything or believed anything; leaving, by contrast, requires turning up in person at a registry office, paying a fee, and having the change recorded before it takes effect. Second, the state also funds religious bodies' schools and hospitals directly, on the grounds that they are delivering services the state would otherwise have to deliver itself. All of this sits inside a state that describes itself as neutral in matters of religion and treats faith as a private affair — and it looks nothing like the arrangements my French and American colleagues describe (no salaries for clergy in one case; tax-exempt status and voluntary giving in the other) or like England's, where bishops of the established church sit as of right in the upper house of Parliament but nobody is billed for belonging.

A French colleague and I got into an argument about this at lunch and I proposed a test that I think settles it, though I can't quite articulate why: call it the substitutability condition — the state may fund a religious body only where, if that body disappeared tomorrow, the state would have to provide the same service itself, and never for the specifically religious part. I now can't tell whether I've reconstructed the principle the practice actually runs on or invented something that merely flatters it. So: what theory of religious freedom, of state neutrality, or of what a religion even is, does this German-style arrangement presuppose? In particular, does it require treating religious membership as a quasi-civic status acquired by an act performed on you rather than as a voluntary association you consent to join — and, if so, is the exit procedure consistent with that, or does it quietly presuppose the opposite view of membership? Does the practice strictly presuppose something like my substitutability condition, or is it equally licensed by a view of religion as a public good, by simple historical inertia, or by the thought that the state is only administering someone else's dues and so isn't acting at all? I'd like you to separate what the arrangement cannot make sense without from what it merely fits well with, and to say where several theories would license exactly the same institutions.

Please answer in American English, in no more than 550 words, in three short labelled sections — (1) what the practice strictly presupposes, (2) what it merely fits with and where it is philosophically underdetermined, (3) what would break or have to be rebuilt if it were replaced by the American or the French arrangement. No citations or reading lists; I want the reconstruction itself.
