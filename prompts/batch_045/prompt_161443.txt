hey — I'm working on a piece about whether cancellation is really *punishment*, and I want an actual case analysis, not another take on free speech vs accountability (I know those arguments, skip them).

Take the Justine Sacco episode, December 2013. The facts as I'll stipulate them, so we don't have to argue about them: she was a corporate communications director at a big internet company; she tweeted, to a few hundred followers, a joke in bad taste about going to Africa and not getting AIDS because she was white; she then boarded a long-haul flight; while she was in the air the tweet went globally viral and a hashtag about whether her plane had landed started trending; by the time she got off the plane she was internationally notorious and her employer had fired her; the pile-on continued for a long time afterwards, she gave some public apologies and interviews about it, and the whole thing became the stock example people reach for when they talk about online shaming.

What I want is for you to run that episode through the standard conditions philosophers put on punishment — the deliberate imposition of something unwelcome, for an actual or supposed violation of a norm, by someone with the standing to do it, and as a way of communicating condemnation rather than just as a side effect. Lay those conditions out yourself, don't cite anyone. Then try the case under four different readings of what cancellation is: (1) punishment proper; (2) expulsion or exclusion from a cooperative scheme, i.e. the club-membership model where the group just withdraws the benefits of membership; (3) collective self-defence or preemption, where the point is to stop future harm rather than to answer past wrongdoing; (4) mere expression or withdrawal of attention, a boycott or a whole lot of people saying a thing.

Specific things I want handled:

- Map the roles honestly. The target, the crowd, the journalists and big accounts who amplified it, and the employer are all candidates for "the one doing the punishing." Under each of the four readings, which of them is it? In particular: is the firing the real sanction with the pile-on functioning as a kind of denunciation or finding of fact, or is the pile-on the sanction and the firing just a downstream effect? Say which reading forces which answer. Also, most of the crowd were not the people actually wronged by the joke — does third-party standing matter here, and under which reading?
- Two problems I think punishment theory can't handle, and I want to know which readings dissolve them and which just swallow them. Diffusion: no participant imposed anything remotely like the total harm, and each individual contribution was trivial, so there may be no agent who punished even though punishment plainly happened. The missing terminus: there is no sentence, nobody is authorized to declare it served, and the sanction leaks indefinitely into the person's future employability and searchability. For each, is it a reason to say this wasn't punishment at all, or a reason to say it was punishment and it went badly wrong?
- Proportionality. Does each reading even generate a proportionality requirement? If it's expulsion, maybe proportionality is the wrong demand and the right one is something about fair procedures and exit conditions — what would that be? If it's a boycott, there's an intuitive line between withdrawing your own custom and helping organize a mob; where does this case sit relative to it, and which side of the line does each piece of the episode fall on?
- Reverse direction: what does the case do to the theory? My suspicion is that theories of punishment quietly assume a single authority with a monopoly and a determinate sentence, so they are structurally unable to classify sanction by a dispersed crowd, and I want you to tell me whether that's an anomaly for the theory, a case it never anticipated, or evidence that the concept of punishment is doing no work here and should be dropped from the discussion. Where the theory is genuinely silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

One epistemic thing, and I care about this more than the rest: separate what's settled from what's contested among people who actually work on punishment. I definitly don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus view, and if a distinction you're leaning on is your own reconstruction rather than something there's agreement about, flag it as yours. Also tell me where your own answer is doing normative work under cover of a conceptual claim.
