Something in agricultural pest control I cant get philosophically straight, and I want the implicit theory behind it laid out rather than a verdict on whether it's OK.

The package of practices:

1. Whether an insect counts as a pest depends on where it is and what we happen to be growing there — same species is a beneficial pollinator we're told to protect in one crop and a target we're told to wipe out in the next.
2. How many you may kill is set by an economic threshold: if the damage they'd do costs less than the control, you're supposed to leave them alone. So the number of insects it's fine to kill moves with the market price of the crop.
3. Growers planting insect-resistant GM crops are required to keep a refuge of non-resistant host plants, specifically so susceptible insects survive and keep breeding.
4. For vertebrate pests there are standards about how fast the animal dies and how much distress is involved; for insects there's nothing comparable at all.

What does that package presuppose? I can see two candidate readings: that pest is a relational role an organism occupies in a human project rather than a property of the organism, and that the real unit of management is a population or a gene frequency rather than individuals. Tell me which parts of the practice each reading actually gets you, whether either of them forces you to deny insects have welfare or only to say individual welfare isn't the currency here, and whether someone who did take individual insect lives seriously could still endorse most of this or would have to rebuild it from scratch.
