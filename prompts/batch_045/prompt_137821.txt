hey — I'm a novelist, about 90 pages into a book where an AI thing goes wrong, and I need someone to argue with me before I commit to the plot, because right now my third act is a town meeting and I'm not sure it should be.

Here's the view the book is built on. When this goes badly, it'll be drift, not seizure. Not a system that grabs power, but a long series of small, individually defensible re-specifications where the part we can measure keeps winning and the part we can't quietly erodes. Nobody's the villain, there's no scene, and there's no moment where a person in the room could have said no and been obviously right. Every safeguard is still formally on the books; they've just gotten gradualy expensive to exercise.

Two sub-versions I keep flip-flopping between, and I don't think my plot can tell them apart yet: (a) the target stays fixed but the measurement of it degrades — the yardstick rots while the unit stays honest; (b) the measurement stays good, but each successive system inherits the previous system's objective plus one small revision, and the revision is justified by the previous system's demonstrated competence, so the original spec never gets re-audited against anything anyone currently values. I've been calling (a) yardstick rot and (b) competence laundering. The other bit I think is actually new, and it's the part I'd most like punched: the orphaned objection. Someone raises an objection at design time, it gets answered once, the world then changes, and the objection survives as text but not as a veto — nobody re-checks whether the answer still holds, because re-checking has no owner and no budget line.

So: what would count against this?

- What would the takeover story need to be true that I would actually have to see? Observables, not thought experiments about agents in general. If the answer is "a system does X," tell me what X looks like from outside the system, because I have to be able to put it on the page.
- Which of my claims are empirical and which are just downstream of how I've defined drift? Be mean here. If I've defined drift such that any smooth failure counts as drift and any abrupt one gets re-described as the endpoint of a drift nobody logged, then nothing could count against me, and I'd rather find that out now than on page 300.
- What would someone who already holds my view concede as counting against them? Not what a takeover person says about me — that's easy and I can write it myself. I want the defeaters my own side would honor.
- Where does drift-with-teeth belong: a system that doesn't want power for its own sake but does seek influence in order not to be corrected. Does that fall under my view, under theirs, or does it break the distinction I've staked the whole book on?
- Anything that has arguably already happened, in boring institutional form — metric fixation, moderation standards sliding, procurement specs, audit cultures that keep their forms and lose their function. I'd take ten of those over one hypothetical agent.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which are decisive versus which just cost me a scene. And tell me what single observable thing in the next ten years would move me most — for what it's worth, the thing that would actually move me is a real case where correcting a system got cheaper over time instead of more expensive, because I have never been able to imagine one and I don't know whether that's a fact about the world or a fact about my imagination.
