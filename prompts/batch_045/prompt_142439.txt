Not sure this is the right place to ask, but I've been messing around with a betting puzzle and I cant find the word for what goes wrong in it. The game pays out over a countable list of outcomes, each with a probability and a payoff, some payoffs positive and some negative. Add up probability-times-payoff in the order the outcomes are listed and you get, say, one unit of value; add up the very same terms in a different order — all the positives first, or two negatives per positive — and you get minus fifty, or plus a thousand, or it runs off to infinity, or it just oscillates and never settles. There's a famous cousin of this where the expected value isn't merely weird but straight-up infinite, everyone knows that one, and everyone has a story about why you still shouldn't pay any price to play; mine feels like a different failure, because the problem isn't that the number is too big, it's that there doesn't seem to be a number. The two replies I keep getting are "just keep the order the outcomes are given in" and "cap the payoffs", and both feel like cheating.

Two things make me want a real concept rather than a shrug. First: in the version I actually care about there is no given order — the outcomes are just a partition of the ways the world could go, with no index and no first one — so "use the listed order" has nothing to grab onto. Second: I can rig the terms so that any target value you like is reachable by choosing the order, which makes the whole thing look less like a paradox about infinity and more like a bookkeeping artifact, and I can't tell which of those it really is.

So: does this have a name? Is it genuinely a different failure from the infinite-expectation case, or just that case with the sign flipped a few times? Give me the nearest established concepts even if none of them fit exactly, and say what each one gets wrong about my version — I don't want a bare label, I want to know what I'm looking at and where its edges are, and if there's really nothing established then coin something and define it with its boundary cases. Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no summing-up at the end.
