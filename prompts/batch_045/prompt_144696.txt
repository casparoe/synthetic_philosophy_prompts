Here's my opening. The real cases feel under-described to me, so I want to push you with two variants I made up.

Variant A, "inversion": the full sensory profile is intact — accurate localization, intensity ratings that match control subjects, the same verbal reports of burning or crushing — but the affective response isn't absent, it's flipped. It feels good. The patient seeks the stimulation out, and if you took it away they'd report a loss. Call it hedonically inverted pain.

Variant B, "transposition": the unpleasantness is gone, but something negative is left over. The patient says the sensation is irritating in the way a wrong note in a song you know well is irritating — a kind of aesthetic or formal dislike, with no bodily "ouch", no urge to withdraw, but a real dislike all the same.

Now: if badness just is unpleasantness, you look committed to saying that in Variant A the patient is having pain that is positively *good* for them while quietly accumulating serious tissue damage. And that seems backwards to me. Asymbolia isn't treated as a gift by clinicians, it's treated as a hazard, becuase the patient gets hurt without being hurt. Which suggests the bad-making thing was never the sting — the sting is how you find out about something that's already bad for you. Pain starts to look like a perception of your body going badly, with the affect as the delivery mechanism rather than the badness itself. Variant B is there to block your fallback: I suspect you want to say any leftover dislike is just mild unpleasantness in disguise, but this dislike isn't bodily at all, and it still seems like a way the experience is bad for the subject.
