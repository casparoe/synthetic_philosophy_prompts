I'm a journalist working on a feature about how disputes in the human sciences get settled — or fail to get settled — when the evidence base is fixed, the events are unrepeatable, and no new experiment is possible. My case study is the argument over how to explain the behaviour of the German Order Police reservists of Reserve Police Battalion 101: men recruited in Hamburg, mostly middle-aged and from working- and lower-middle-class backgrounds, many judged unfit for regular military service, who in occupied Poland in 1942–43 rounded up and shot tens of thousands of Jewish civilians. The emblematic episode is the massacre at Józefów in July 1942, where their commander, Major Wilhelm Trapp, is reported to have told his men that anyone who found the work too difficult could step out, and fewer than a dozen men out of roughly five hundred did so.

Two families of explanation dominate. One, associated with Christopher Browning's 1992 study of the battalion, treats the killings as the product of situation: obedience to legitimate authority, career and comradeship incentives, gradual escalation, role adaptation, deference to expertise, peer pressure, and the sheer difficulty of exiting a killing process already underway. The other, associated with Daniel Goldhagen's 1996 book and his earlier polemic against that study, treats them as the product of a specifically German political culture in which a murderous, eliminationist form of antisemitism had become a shared cognitive frame — such that the men knew what they were doing, took it to be right, and in many cases did more than they were asked.

What I want from you is the document an adversarial collaboration between these two camps should have produced: something a committed situationalist and a committed culturalist could both sign. Please write it as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than diplomatic: claims about what the men did, about the range of individual behaviour within the battalion (evaders, reluctant compliers, volunteers, enthusiasts, men who sought out extra killing), about what both sides concede the other's evidence establishes, and about what a satisfactory explanation would have to account for. Include what each side grants the other — the culturalist's concession that situations matter and that some men resisted, the situationalist's concession that antisemitic ideology was pervasively present and did real work.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should be one crux, with the situationalist's position phrased as that side would phrase it and the culturalist's phrased as that side would phrase it — neither a caricature. For each crux, give the finding or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each predicts that finding will be, plus how much movement each admits it would produce in them.

3. A section on the cruxes I would most like clarified, using three stipulative distinctions of my own devising (they are my inventions, not the literature's, so define them as you go):
   - the *zeal margin*: the gap between what an order required and what a perpetrator actually did — volunteered extra shooting, gratuitous cruelty, self-initiated hunting of hiding Jews on days off. Which side needs the zeal margin to be large, and what would a small one show?
   - the *variance signature*: within a single unit, does excess and reluctance correlate with position in the peer network and with how far a man has already gone, or with measurable prior exposure to a radicalised milieu? What pattern would discriminate, and would either side accept the result as decisive?
   - the *three roles of ideology*: as motive, as post-hoc legitimation, and as perceptual framing that makes some courses of action visible as options at all. Are the two sides disagreeing about which of these roles antisemitism played, or about how much?

4. A short closing section on how much of the dispute is verbal — about the words "ordinary" and "willing," about whether "willing" contrasts with coerced or with enthusiastic, and about whether a cultural claim has to predict uniformity to be worth anything — and on which cruxes are genuinely empirical. If some crux is untestable with the sources historians actually have, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a test.

Two constraints. First, treat the two positions as held by competent people: if a crux as I have framed it only has one defensible answer, reframe it. Second, where a crux would be settled by evidence that does not exist in the form required, you may stipulate the evidence hypothetically — for instance individual personnel files linked to pre-1933 precinct-level voting returns and party-membership dates, a comparison battalion from a different region operating in the same district in the same month, or interrogation transcripts coded for whether antisemitic justifications were volunteered spontaneously or only under questioning — but flag it as hypothetical. I am not asking for citations, sources, or further reading; I know the rough shape of the literature and want the argumentative structure.
