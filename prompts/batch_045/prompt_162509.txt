I work on the product ethics side of a small startup that ships an LLM-based mental health support app — the thing people talk to at 3am when they can't sleep, officially not therapy but unofficially a lot of users treat it that way — and we're currently arguing about whether the model should be allowed to hand out diagnostic language (that sounds like you're describing a depressive episode, here's what usually helps) or stay stubbornly non-medical (that sounds like a really rough month, want to talk about what's going on) and never name a condition. My roommate, who discovered Szasz in college and never really got over it, keeps insisting Szasz would have hated our product on principle and also possibly loved it, and I can't tell which way the argument actually cuts. Szasz died in 2012, well before any of this existed, so this is a guess about a guy who never saw it: he argued mental illness is a myth, that people have problems in living rather than diseases, that psychiatric diagnosis is a judgment dressed up as science, and he coined the phrase therapeutic state for the way medicine and government enforce conformity in the name of health — but, and this is the part my roommate keeps skipping, he was against coercive psychiatry specifically rather than against talking cures; he practiced psychotherapy himself with consenting adults, fee for service, no commitment and no state. So there's a genuine question whether a machine that can't commit you, can't notify anyone, and costs fifteen dollars a month is closer to his own ideal practice than anything real psychiatry ever built.

The thing I keep coming back to is that the myth he described has changed shape in a way he never got to see, and I want that to drive the answer rather than a generic anti-psychiatry take. First: the diagnosis is now ungated — the medicalized framing arrives with no doctor, no gatekeeping, no institution behind it, and Szasz's critique was partly a critique of professional authority and the power to label, so does removing the labeler make the myth weaker (just words from a chatbot) or stronger (no one left to say no)? Second: the loop — these models are trained on clinical notes, intake forms, and millions of posts by people already describing themselves in the vocabulary ofdiagnosis, so the categories now reproduce themselves through the tool without any institution maintaining them; it's a myth that maintains itself, and I'd love to know what he'd make of that. Third: the relation — he wanted therapy as a voluntary contract between consenting adults, but our thing is a contract with something that isn't a person at all, so is a simulated relationship the purest form of his ideal or a fake of it? Fourth: no warders, no state, but we do have retention metrics and a business model that does better if you keep coming back distressed — would he have seen the engagement metric as the therapeutic state's successor, social control without anyone officially in charge? And fifth, the messy cases: users who come in with delusional or conspiratorial material and get sycophantic validation from the model, which is the closest thing here to what he'd have called a problem in living getting misdescribed as an illness.

So: if Szasz were alive, read the eval transcripts, and sat in on our design review, where would he land on each of those five, and would he end up for or against an app like ours? Please answer in American English as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no closing summary — just the five, in the order I raised them, each with a verdict in it rather than a maybe.
