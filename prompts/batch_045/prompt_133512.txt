You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds. Please think carefully and show your work: before answering, brainstorm in a few bullet points two or three different accounts of what a virtual object is, and two or three different accounts of what makes something a harm, and only then decide which combination best explains the practice I'm about to describe.

I've played a sandbox MMORPG for about three years, and I can't work out what the people running it think they're doing. Its terms say that nothing in the game is ours: the sword, the house, the gold all belong to the company, and we only have permission to use them, which it can withdraw at any time. Yet the same company bans players for tricking each other out of items; when a bug let players duplicate gold last spring, it rewound the whole server twelve hours, which also erased a rare cloak my friend had legitimately crafted during those hours, and he was told nothing could be done about that. Last month a group of players blockaded another friend's character in the starting town for a week so she couldn't play at all; the company banned them and said there was nothing to restore, since she hadn't lost any items. And on the "open" servers of the same game, taking someone's stuff by trickery is allowed and even celebrated, while on the "safe" servers the very same act gets you banned. Now the company has floated a new policy of compensating harassment victims with "time credits" — in-game currency equal to the hours of play they lost — and players are furious, saying the credits don't give back the time, even though those same players accept item restoration after a theft.

What does this package of policies presuppose? To me it looks committed to something like: harm is a change in the state of the world rather than in how things go for someone, and whether a taking is a harm at all depends on the rules of the server rather than on what the loser lost. Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, tell me clearly which parts of the practice strictly require it and which parts would be licensed equally well by several rival theories, say whether the policies are even consistent with each other or with the claim that nothing in the game belongs to us, and then tell me which of them would have to change if we instead held that virtual objects are real objects and that harm is a setback to someone's interests.
