Compare David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation with James Woodward's interventionist theory. In your answer you should: (a) state the basic idea behind each theory; (b) explain how each theory deals with a case of symmetric overdetermination, in which Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle at exactly the same moment, both rocks hit, and either rock on its own would have shattered the bottle; (c) identify one respect in which the two theories are not just the same theory written in different words. and heres the essay i've got to mark:

Lewis's theory starts from the idea that a cause is something that makes a difference: roughly, c causes e just in case, if c had not happened, e would not have happened. This simple test fails for early preemption, so Lewis defines causation as the transitive closure, the ancestral, of counterfactual dependence, which lets a cause be linked to its effect through a chain of intermediate events each of which depends on the one before it. In late preemption, where a backup is waiting to step in, Lewis gets the right answer by letting the counterfactual backtrack: you let the antecedent lead you back to earlier differences in the history and ask what would have happened then, which is how the theory still counts the first throw rather than the backup as the cause.

Woodward's interventionist theory is stated in terms of variables rather than events. An intervention on a variable is something a human experimenter could actually carry out, a manipulation that sets that variable to a value from outside the system being studied. This is also the limitation of the view: a cause nobody could ever manipulate, such as the gravitational pull of the moon on the tides, is not really a cause at all on Woodward's account, since no intervention on it is available to us.

In the Suzy and Billy case the plain counterfactual test gives the wrong answer, because if Suzy had not thrown the bottle would have shattered anyway. Lewis's reply is that the effect is a fragile event: the shattering that actually occurred, with both rocks arriving exactly when they did, would not have occurred without Suzy's throw, so dependence comes back. Woodward reaches the same verdict more directly, since his test asks whether there is some setting of the other variables under which an intervention on Suzy's throwing would change whether the bottle shatters; hold Billy's throw at not throwing and the intervention on Suzy's throw does make a difference.

The real strength of interventionism is that it separates genuine causal generalisations, which stay true under interventions, from merely accidental ones, which fall apart as soon as you intervene.

So in the end the interventionist theory is basically Lewis's theory restated with variables in place of events. Both of them reduce causation to the very same counterfactual test, just described in different language, which means the two theories can never actually disagree about any case.
