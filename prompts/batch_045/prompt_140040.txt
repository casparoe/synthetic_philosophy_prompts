I'm in a historiography seminar this term, and we've spent the last few weeks on standpoint, archival power, and the old line that history is written by the victors — in particular on the gap between the weak, causal version of that claim (winners control the archives, fund the chairs, and build the monuments, so their account is the one that circulates) and the much stronger version (there is no fact of the matter about what happened beyond what some power has successfully imposed, so a suppressed account is not merely unpublished but in some sense not true). Our final assignment allows either a conventional paper or a piece of public-facing writing, and I'd like to attempt the latter seriously rather than glibly, because I think the strong version is easier to assert than to actually inhabit.

What I want is a piece of sustained satire in the form of an internal document — a compliance handbook, or a set of standard operating procedures — issued by a fictional state bureau in a country that has stopped treating the maxim as a cynical remark about how the world unfortunately works and has converted it into an ordinary administrative function, on a par with the census or the mint. Please write it in a deadpan civil-service register: flat, euphemistic, procedural, the style of real government guidance. No winking, no punchlines, and no final paragraph that drops the voice to tell the reader what the joke was; someone who misses the point should be able to read it as a genuine memo, and someone who gets it should be unable to stop seeing the point. Around 700–900 words, in numbered clauses or lettered subsections, with at least one invented but plausible worked case — a composite war is fine, and I'd rather it not hang on a real and contested episode — showing a file moving through intake, classification, allocation of narrative share, and periodic review.

The humour should come from taking the maxim literally and asking what a bureaucracy would actually have to do to operationalise it, and the piece should work as a reductio: by the end, the bureau should have been forced, in the ordinary course of its work, to rely on exactly the notion of a way-things-really-were that the strong version of the claim denies. Concretely, I'd like it to have to solve problems like these: who counts as the victor when a war ends in a negotiated settlement, a stalemate, or a coalition victory, and what happens when the victor's successor regime repudiates the war; how neutral observers' records, private diaries, and physical evidence that contradicts the approved account are to be handled (destroyed, reclassified, or merely unfunded); the timing problem, since the same war tends to get rewritten when power changes hands a generation later, so the bureau needs some kind of sunset or stable-narrative rule; the disposal question for whatever the losing side wrote before the peace treaty was signed; and the awkward status of an account that has been successfully suppressed — whether it has become false, or only unavailable. If it helps, you may invent one or two pieces of in-house jargon and define them in a footnote or an appendix of questions from regional offices; I was toying with something like reverse archival drift, the tendency of a defeated side's version to regain credibility as the generation that fought dies off, which the bureau would then have to budget and staff against, but feel free to use or replace it. The test I'm applying is whether the piece would make someone who has actually thought about standpoint and historical relativism laugh in recognition — because the exaggeration tracks a real feature of the position — while still being perfectly readable to my flatmate, who hasn't.
