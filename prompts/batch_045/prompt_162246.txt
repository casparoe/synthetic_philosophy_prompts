I am writing a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on aesthetic emotion, and I need a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on the paradox of tragedy: why, and in what sense, people seek out works that reliably make them unhappy.

Build the tree from four branch questions, each phrased so that someone who has never heard the labels can answer it, and keep it to at most ten leaves. The splits I care about are: (a) whether the distress is genuinely unpleasant or is better described as tender, moving or bittersweet; (b) whether the distress merely accompanies what is valuable in the experience, is transformed into it, or is simply outweighed by something separate; (c) whether what redeems the experience is an affective state or a non-affective achievement such as insight, the exercise of emotional capacities, or a sense of shared attention; (d) whether the account is specific to tragedy or generalises to horror, disgust and to non-fictional sorrow, with the consequence that tragedy turns out not to be special.

Leaves must include the conversion view, the meta-response view, straightforward compensation, capacity or achievement views, and a deflationary option on which there is no paradox because the distress in question is mild, virtuosic, or not quite displeasure. Name each leaf, give it one line on its most awkward commitment, and note which answer combinations the four questions permit but nobody occupies, with a word on why they are empty.

Reach every branch through concrete cases, not labels. Use at least these: a viewer who re-watches a film knowing exactly that it will devastate her; a reader who turns to tragedy in order to feel less alone or to understand something about grief; a horror reader who reports real disgust and no trace of enjoyment; and a case of my own design — a bereaved person who commissions a generative system to produce new recordings in the voice of someone who has died, plays them repeatedly, calls the experience sorrowful and valuable, and insists she does not enjoy it. Say which branch that last case forces and whether any leaf accommodates it without strain. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate on these cases, and describe one further case that would separate them.
