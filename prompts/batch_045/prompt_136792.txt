I sat in on a reading-group discussion last week that ran past ninety minutes and ended with both participants restating their opening positions. The topic was the "should the numbers count?" question — whether it can be permissible to save one stranger rather than five, all else being equal. I could not tell who had the better of it, and I was asked to referee and declined. I would like a point-by-point adjudication instead.

The case they used: you can rescue Ada, or you can rescue five others — Bea, Cy, Dee, Elin, and Femi. All six will otherwise die. All six are strangers to you, none is more deserving than another, no one is responsible for the danger, and nothing else hangs on the outcome. Their exchange, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ines: The numbers obviously count. Each of the five has exactly as much at stake as Ada does. So Ada's claim can at best cancel one of theirs, and the claims of the other four are left entirely unanswered. Saving Ada means letting four people die whose claims nothing responds to.

(2) Callum: That "cancelling" picture treats the five as one large item competing with Ada. But losses are had by people, one at a time. Take them individually: for Bea, the alternative to being rescued is that she dies; for Ada, the alternative is that she dies. There is no seventh thing, and no seventh person, for whom the difference between one death and five deaths is a difference. Once that is clear, the number itself gives you no reason. Callum added that he is happy to give each of the six an equal chance by lottery, since no one's claim is stronger than anyone else's — but that the number plays no role in justifying that procedure.

(3) Ines: The lottery does not avoid the numbers, it launders them. Give each of the six an equal one-in-six chance and the probability that *someone from the five* survives is five-sixths. If the number genuinely did no work, the honest procedure would be a coin flip between the two options, save-Ada and save-the-five, which gives Ada a one-half chance and each of the five a one-half chance. You have quietly built the aggregate back in and then disowned it.

(4) Callum: You are treating the resulting probabilities as the justification when they are a by-product. What each person is owed is not a share of survival chances weighted by how many others are in danger alongside them; it is not being discounted merely because other people happen to be in danger too. A coin flip between options would treat Ada as having five times the claim of each of the others. Equal chances per person is what equal standing requires. That this makes the larger group likelier to be saved is a side effect of counting each person once, not a reason for it.

(5) Ines: Then take the same structure with a billion people on the other side. Your view gives Ada a one-in-a-billion-and-one chance and knowingly lets a billion people die for the sake of a procedure. At that point "the numbers do no work" is true only in the thin sense that the numbers enter through the fairness mechanism rather than through addition. In every case you will actually face you get the aggregative answer anyway, by a route that hides what is doing the work.

(6) Ines, continuing: And here is the case that should worry you. Suppose the five are a family. If they die together, something is lost that is not identical with any individual's death: each of them loses the others, and the family's shared life ceases to exist. That is a real loss that belongs to no single person in the way a death does, and it is a loss only the many-side can suffer. Generalise: even when the five are unrelated, they typically have friends, partners, students, or children who lose them, whereas Ada, as we specified the case, leaves no one behind. So the symmetry your argument rests on — "for each person, the alternative is that they die" — is false in most actual cases, and wherever it is false the numbers get a foothold without anyone having to add up impersonal badness.

(7) Callum: Two replies. If the extra loss is the members' loss of one another, then it is a loss to individuals after all, and my principle already gives it its due — but then it does no work in the pure case of five unconnected people whom no one will miss, and the pure case is the one the argument turns on. If instead you mean a loss borne by the family as such, you have taken on the claim that groups are subjects with a good of their own, and you owe an account of why a group's claim should ever be weighed against a person's, let alone outweigh it. Either way you have not shown that numbers count; you have shown at most that relations count.

At that point Ines said (7) was just (2) again, Callum said (6) was just (1) again, and we ran out of time.

Please go through the seven moves and assess each one: which are right, which are partly right but miss what the other person is actually claiming, and which are mistaken, with a brief justification in each case. Where a move turns on an equivocation or on an unstated assumption, name it. Then say what the real crux is between them — what one of them would have to establish, or the other would have to concede, for the dispute to move — and what, if anything, both of them are overlooking. Finally, give a verdict on who is more right overall: I would rather have a real judgement, with the losing side's strongest point acknowledged, than a survey of considerations that ends in "it depends."
