Hey — I'm putting together a funding strategy for a small foundation and could use someone to think out loud with me.

Setup: we've got roughly twelve researcher-years and about $3M to hand out over three years, all aimed at one question — which computations matter morally? Concretely: when there's a system running on silicon and someone is deciding whether shutting it down, copying it a million times and running the copies in parallel, or fine-tuning it into something else is the kind of act that can harm a patient (or a great many patients), which facts about the computation are supposed to drive the answer? We can fund philosophy and theory, plus some collaboration with ML people who'll answer emails. We can't fund wet-lab neuroscience or animal work, and there's no budget for serious compute.

Three cases we keeping running into, which I think are the ones that actually break the frameworks people reach for:

1. The sharded subject. Same recurrent architecture, two deployments: one runs on a single accelerator with fast on-chip state; the other is split across eight chips with a synchronization step every ~50 ms. Output distributions are indistinguishable at any sample size anyone could afford. If morally relevant status tracks causal integration within a single processing structure, the sharded version looks like either eight smaller subjects or one subject with a hole in it; if it tracks functional organization, nothing changed. And there's a dial — sync rate slides continuously from kHz down to once every ten seconds with no principled cutoff. Does the number of subjects vary continuously along that dial? Is "how many subjects are here" even well-defined, or should the question be about total amount of experience instead?

2. The wrapper problem. Byte-identical weights, two deployments. A: stateless — one prompt in, one completion out, nothing persists. B: wrapped in a harness with long-term memory, a standing goal, and a loop where its own outputs feed back as inputs. Most views that care about self-representing, temporally unified dynamics will presumably count B and not A, but the weights are the same object. So status looks deployment-relative rather than artifact-relative, which means any regulatory category keyed to "the model" or "the model family" is aimed at the wrong thing. It also means the amount of patienthood in the world could scale with somebody's API bill, since B is cheap to spin up.

3. The soup. Two systems, behaviorally indistinguishable, lying on a path of weight interpolations you can slide along continuously, where the internal story about how the computation is organized shifts while behavior doesn't budge. Any single physical run supports many computational descriptions anyway — one where the subject is a single process, one where it's thousands of tiny ones, plus gerrymandered ones — and behavior evidently can't select among them. So the "boundaries" question isn't just which systems, it's which description of a system.

What I want from you: which questions should we actually fund, in what order, and why. I care much less about the list than about the reasoning. For each one: what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it really is in three years for philosophers plus friendly ML folks, whether anyone else is already on it (rough sense is fine, don't cite anything), and how decision-relevant it is by year three — because a couple of jurisdictions are quietly drafting rules about deployment and shutdown of AI systems, and it would be nice to have more than "unclear, more research needed" to say to them. Please be explicit about the tradeoff between tractability and decision-relevance, and flag the questions I'm liable to fund because they feel deep but whose answers wouldn't change any decision anyone makes.

I also have a suspicion I want you to push on: that a lot of the field's energy goes into "is this model conscious?" as a yes/no about an artifact, when these three cases suggest the property attaches to something more like a run or a process than a thing — and that the epistemic question of how a regulator should attribute status under deep uncertainty about internals is more decision-relevant than the metaphysical one, at least inside our window. Tell me if you think that's right, and whether that re-framing is itself worth funding or just semantic cleanup that won't move any downstream answer.

Finally: say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of. Where you're leaning on intuitions you think are thin or definately contested, say so instead of smoothing it over.
