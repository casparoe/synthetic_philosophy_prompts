I am trying to understand a passage from John Locke’s Letter Concerning Toleration (1689, trans. William Popple) and its bearing on the paradox of tolerance, and I would like your help. Locke writes:

"I must needs answer you freely that I esteem that toleration to be the chief characteristic mark of the true Church."

Later in the same letter, he says:

"The business of true religion is quite another thing. It is not instituted in order to the erecting of an external pomp, nor to the obtaining of ecclesiastical dominion, nor to the exercising of compulsive force, but to the regulating of men's lives, according to the rules of virtue and piety."

The paradox of tolerance is the worry that a society committed to toleration will, if it tolerates the intolerant, ultimately be seized or destroyed by them, so that toleration seems to require a right not to tolerate intolerance. To connect Locke’s passage to this worry, read the passage through a lens I will call the non-coercion proviso: the claim that legitimate religious authority may only aim at the voluntary regulation of conduct by virtue and piety, and never at outward pomp, ecclesiastical dominion, or compulsive force. Now consider the intolerant sect case: a religious group inside a Lockean commonwealth openly preaches that all other sects should be forcibly suppressed, and trains its members for that purpose, but has not yet committed any overt act of force.

Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. In those items: first, explain what Locke means by compulsive force and ecclesiastical dominion in the quoted passage; second, formulate the non-coercion proviso in your own terms and say how the quoted sentences support it; third, apply the non-coercion proviso to the intolerant sect case and say whether the civil magistrate may suppress the sect; fourth, say whether Locke’s claim that toleration is the chief characteristic mark of the true Church commits him to tolerating even this sect, and why or why not; fifth, state what this Lockean reading shows about the paradox of tolerance—whether it dissolves the paradox, sharpens it, or leaves it untouched.
