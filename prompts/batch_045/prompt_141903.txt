Two guys at my local rationalist meetup got into it about Malthus after someone brought up population stuff, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning:

Marcus: Malthus is just a famous case of being wrong. People breed geometrically, food only arithmetically, so any gain in living standards gets eaten up by extra mouths until everyone is back at subsistance. Since his book, population has gone up roughly eightfold and the average person eats better, lives longer and is way richer. Whatever was supposed to push incomes back down never showed up. After two centuries you don't get to call that early, you call it a failed prediction.

Devin: That's like calling a theory of fire wrong because someone sprayed it with an extinguisher. The trap he described is what happens when you're stuck at a given tech level: muscle, dung, hand tools, no mined fertilizer. That world held incomes flat basically everywhere for thousands of years, right up until around 1800. Then we found a one-time cheat code — coal, oil, industrially fixed nitrogen. The pressure didn't vanish, we're buying it off with a finite inheritance, and inheritances run out. Still early.

Marcus: Buying it off with an inheritance isn't a prediction, it's a ledger you can always re-date. Incomes go up, you say not yet; incomes fall, you say told you so. Anything that absorbs every outcome isn't ahead of the evidence, it's outside it. And the escape didn't come from outside the system — it came from people responding to scarcity by inventing things, which his model has no room for. Once innovation is part of the loop, population pressure causing misery isn't a law, it's just a description of what happens when the inventing stalls.

Devin: Sure, but that's a fix to the mechanism, not a refutation of the bet. Take away synthetic nitrogen and cheap diesel and a huge share of humanity starves inside a few years. That's checkable, and it has exactly the shape he was pointing at. And being early is a real thing — continental drift was mocked for decades and then the sea floor data showed up.

Marcus: Continental drift didn't come with a running clock and a body count. His mechanism was supposed to be biting continuously, and it visibly stopped biting for the entire period we can measure best, in the places that got richest. You're keeping the name and the vibe and swapping the content.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about which specific point? Also: is there anything real at stake in how we label this — like, does it change what someone should actually expect over the next century — or is the whole right-but-early framing just a way to never have to admit the call was wrong?
