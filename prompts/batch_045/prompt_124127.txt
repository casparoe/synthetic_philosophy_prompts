Pretend you're a professional moral philosopher who works specifically on animal ethics and population-level harm -- invertebrate welfare, wild animal suffering, that whole corner of the literature. Take your time, brainstorm the shape of the problem first, and then commit to something. I don't want a survey of positions with an "on the other hand" after every sentence.

Im a backend engineer. Zero philosophy background past one freshman requirement I sleptwalked through. I sit on the operations committee of a nonprofit that runs six urban market-garden sites plus a warehouse where we repack donated produce for food pantries. We have to make a call next Tuesday and I'm the one writing the memo, so: write me the memo. 800-1000 words, something I can circulate under my own name to five people who are all tired and half of whom have already made up their minds.

The case. We've got a stored-product pest problem in the warehouse -- pantry moths and grain beetles in the dry goods area. Our ops manager wants to renew the pest contract: four fogging treatments a year with a pyrethrin-based spray. His pitch is that pyrethrin comes from chrysanthemums and is allowed in organic production, so it's basically the natural option. A volunteer (philosophy undergrad, does our data entry) circulated a counter-proposal: we adopt a formal insect-welfare policy -- no broad-spectrum insecticides, we count and minimize insect deaths, and we accept up to about 15% loss of stored dry goods as the price of that. The committee is split roughly down the middle and the discussion is going in circles.

Things that are actually hard here, and that I need the memo to engage rather than gesture at:

- The volunteer built a spreadsheet. It has a "probability that a moth is sentient" field -- he put 5% -- multiplied by an estimate of deaths per fogging, and the resulting number dwarfs everything else in the model no matter what else I tweak. I write code for a living and this smells like a bug in the argument structure, not a result, but I can't say what the bug is. Is expected-value reasoning over a 5% credence and an enormous headcount genuinely decision-relevant here, or is something in that setup doing work it can't bear? And if the spreadsheet is right, doesn't it also imply we should dump the whole budget into insects instead of food for people, which literally nobody on the committee will accept?
- Leakage. If we eat 15% loss on dry goods, the pantry just buys replacement stock from conventional farms that spray. So our no-kill policy might only move the killing off-site and off our books. This feels like the strongest argument against the volunteer, and I want it taken seriously even if the memo ends up recommending against renewing the contract as-is.
- What we're counting. His policy counts deaths but not births prevented. The two other real options on the table are heat treatment (cook the warehouse for a day) and pheromone mating disruption, which mostly stops the next generation from existing rather than killing adults. If insect lives are on balance bad, preventing births looks like the good option; if they're on balance good, it looks like a loss. We need to pick a rule and say why we picked it.
- Consistency. We run snap traps for mice in the same building and nobody blinks, and we kill who knows how many bugs by tilling and mowing the garden sites without counting anything. Is that an inconsistency we should fix, or a real moral distinction between what we aim at and what we merely foresee?
- Mode of death. Neurotoxin convulsions versus heat versus sealing the building and letting what's inside starve outside. Does that matter morally, or are we just managing our own feelings?
- Practical: the conventional growers who donate us surplus produce will read an "insect welfare policy" as a statement about them, and that donation stream is most of our volume.

Give me a clear bottom line -- renew, don't renew, or renew with conditions -- a concrete timeline, the reasoning underneath it, and one honest paragraph on the strongest consideration on whichever side loses. Also say explicitly what evidence or change in circumstances would flip the recommendation, because I know someone will ask. Don't cite anything or point me at reading; I can't check it and neither can the committee, so reason it out in the memo itself.
