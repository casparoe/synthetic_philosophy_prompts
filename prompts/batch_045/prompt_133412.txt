I am writing a novel, and I need to know whether one of my scenes is load-bearing or merely decorative.

The setting is a port city whose magistrates sit alone under a rule I invented and have been calling record-purity: a verdict is void if the magistrate has knowingly read anything not entered into the record. There is no curative instruction, no substitution of a fresh magistrate once the hearing has opened, and no retrial for the offence in question — a void verdict discharges the accused for good.

My magistrate, Oduya, is handed by a clerk's error a single loose page: a signed statement the prosecution failed to enter, which she has no doubt is genuine and which settles the case. On the record alone she will have to acquit; with the page she would convict, and convicting is by her own lights the better outcome, provided the verdict stands. She may read the page or destroy it unread. No one will ever learn which she does. Reading costs her nothing in money, time, or attention. And she is not in the least afraid of being influenced: she is entirely confident that she could set the page out of her mind and decide on the record alone. She destroys it. Her reason in the scene is only that she has sworn to decide on the record and means to keep her oath.

I wrote out the case against her in six steps to see whether the scene survives it.

(1) There is a standard result — my clerk calls it Good's theorem and insists it is due to I. J. Good in the 1960s, though I have never seen the original and nothing in my scene turns on who proved it — to the effect that no expected-utility maximizer can lose, ex ante, by accepting a costless piece of information.
(2) The proof is two lines. Among the plans available to the agent who has seen the signal is the plan that ignores the signal and does exactly what she would have done without it; and the best plan drawn from a larger menu is never worse than the best plan drawn from a smaller one.
(3) The proof assumes nothing about the content of the signal, about the agent's preferences, or about how far the signal moves her beliefs. It does not require that she enjoy knowing, that the information be useful, that she be able to act on it, or even that acquiring it be without consequences: whatever follows from knowing is simply folded into the payoff of the plan, and the plan that ignores the signal is still on the menu.
(4) So a fully rational agent never declines free information. Every apparent case of rational ignorance must really be something else — akrasia, a taste for ignorance, an uncredited cost, a failure of nerve — and none of those is available here.
(5) Oduya declines free information.
(6) By the same reasoning, anyone who submits to blind review, seals a jury from inadmissible material, refuses to be told the name at the head of an application, exercises a legal right not to know her own prognosis, or arranges matters so that she can later swear truthfully that she never knew, is thereby failing a test of rationality. That is absurd: these strike me as among the more sensible things people do.

So I seem to have proved either that my magistrate is irrational or that a great deal of ordinary prudence is. I would much rather keep my magistrate and give up something in (1) through (4), but the two lines in (2) feel like arithmetic, and I do not see where a novelist is entitled to argue with arithmetic.

Tell me which step fails, and why it looked as though it held. Do not give me a survey of the candidate diagnoses — commit to one, say plainly what accepting it costs me, and say what actually goes wrong in the gap between "you can always do what you would have done" and "so you can never lose." And give me one sentence Oduya can say in the scene, in her own voice, that carries the point for a reader who has never heard of expected utility.
