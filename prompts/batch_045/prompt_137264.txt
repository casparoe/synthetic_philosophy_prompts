I teach a 16-person upper-year seminar on knowledge and skill. 75 minutes, mixed level: about half of them have done the Ryle-vs-intellectualists material, the other half haven't and I don't want to assume they have. I need an in-class game where they *run into* the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction rather than me announcing it.

The skeleton I keep failing to turn into something runnable: teams of three (A, B, C), and a skill gets passed down a chain. A can do the thing and has to get B able to do it; B then has to write it up so that C, who has never seen it, can do it. The skill should be something genuinely easy to show and horrible to describe, with a clean success/fail: I was thinking the one-handed card cut, or tying a bowline behind your back with one hand. Decks of cards and bits of rope are cheap. Whatever you pick has to be (a) learnable cold by most people from watching for under five minutes, (b) impossible to fake, and (c) something with a natural variant nobody has practised — other hand, mirror image, done fast, done with a different object.

Where I'm actually stuck is the payoff scheme. I want it rigged so several things happen on their own:

- A scores on B's performance on the *variant*, not on the thing B was shown — so A has to choose between spending the time explaining, or just demonstrating and letting B groove it.
- B scores on doing the original thing correctly, immediately — so A's and B's interests definately pull apart and they feel that.
- One round where the transmission is pure text: A writes the manual before meeting B, no feedback, it has to work for a stranger. I also want a "no pointing words" rule on that manual — no left/right/up/towards you/like this — because i suspect that's where the propositional version really breaks, and I'd rather they discover that than hear it.
- A condition where B performs while saying out loud what they're doing at each step. I have a hunch this will wreck the people who learned it by feel and barely affect the people who learned it as a list of steps, and I want the room to notice that pattern before anyone explains it.

And I want a "parakeet check" built into the scoring: the person who can reproduce the action perfectly in the exact setup they learned it in and falls apart the moment anything is changed. Reproduce-vs-adapt is a different axis from can-explain-vs-can't, and I want the marks to show that up as two separate numbers rather than me saying it.

So: the actual rules, materials, how many rounds and how long each one is, the exact scoring arithmetic, what I say at the start (nothing that names the distinction), and what I physically hand each person. Then a debrief that maps specific moments — the failed manual, the narrate-aloud penalty, the parakeet cases — onto specific moves in the debate, including which side of the dispute each moment is evidence for and how someone on the other side would absorb it.

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm assuming at least one person just cannot do the skill at all, someone will try to film it on a phone and play it back in slow motion, and someone will write a manual that's basically "ask A". Say for each whether to allow it or block it and why.

Last thing — be honest about what the game can't reach. I know it doesn't get near the stuff about knowledge-how being a relation to propositions grasped under some practical mode of presentation, or the cases where you supposedly have all the relevant propositional knowledge and still can't do it. Tell me what I'll have to supply myself in the final ten minutes and what the shortest way in is.
