I sit on the Council of Appeals of a persistent, decade-old virtual world — a sandbox with its own internal courts, its own currency, and a user base that has been building inside it for years. We adjudicate grievances between players, and over that time we have accumulated a set of precedents. A new case has come before us that I do not think those precedents settle, and I want the reasoning set out carefully before we vote.

Our precedents, in the form in which we now cite them:

1. *The siege.* An attacking force destroyed a castle that another player had built over roughly four hundred hours, using siege mechanics the world expressly provides. Ruling: no sanction, no compensation. We held that the loss fell inside the ordinary adversarial frame of the game.

2. *The fraudulent exchange.* A player transferred real money to another in exchange for in-world currency and received nothing. Ruling: the currency was recreated from the ledger and returned to the buyer; the seller was suspended. We said the wrong lay in the deception and the remedy in putting back what could be put back.

3. *The avatar boundary.* A player exploited an animation-and-collision bug to force another player's avatar into a simulated sexual assault, in a world with no mechanics for it. Ruling: permanent ban, no compensation ordered. Our opinion said that what was violated was the person, and that the avatar's treatment mattered because of what it did to her.

4. *The guild vault.* A co-leader with legitimate access emptied a guild's shared vault. The guild had a written charter treating the vault as pooled property. Ruling: suspension; traceable assets returned; untraceable assets not replaced. We allowed that the charter had made the vault theirs collectively in a sense we were prepared to act on.

5. *The companion creature.* In a zone flagged for player-versus-player combat, one player killed another's bonded companion, an animal raised over some sixty hours. Ruling: no sanction, since the mechanic was permitted, but the studio was ordered to restore the creature from a server snapshot, on the ground that nothing had been lost that could not be restored.

6. *The sunset.* A legacy server was closed with six months' notice, and players were offered an export tool for their builds. A cathedral, the work of thousands of hours, was lost because its builders never used the tool. Ruling: no wrong, no compensation. Notice together with the export offer was held to extinguish any expectation of permanence.

The case now before us:

Over two years a collective built a great library in the live shard: not architecture alone, but some eleven thousand in-world book objects whose text they authored themselves. The text exists nowhere else — the builders had in fact asked for an export path for book contents and had been told that none existed. A single player then used an unauthorized exploit of the world's fire-spread system to burn the library in one night: the mechanism was an in-world physical process rather than a direct deletion, but the mechanism was not one the game makes available. There is no shard backup predating the fire, and the studio's transaction logs preserve only fragments of the text. No real money changed hands. The library had been announced, publicly and repeatedly, as a permanent common resource; several guilds' research and a portion of the shard's quest economy depended on it; and players who never contributed describe their loss in the terms people use for the burning of an archive. The player responsible maintains that nothing real was destroyed, because all of it was only data. The appeal also asks whether the studio itself bears any responsibility for holding no restorable copy.

What I want from you:

- Set out the rules — I suspect there are at least three, and you may find more — that are each compatible with all six precedents. The candidates I have in mind are a person-protective rule on which in-world losses matter only as setbacks to the real-world interests of the people behind the avatars; a social-constitution rule on which what matters is what the community has actually made of the thing, through charters, announcements and reliance; and a data-ontology rule on which nothing is lost that a server can put back, so that the only questions are permission and reversibility. State each precisely enough to generate predictions, and say how it would have to be amended to fit the precedents it fits least well.
- For each rule, say what it implies here: whether a wrong was done; against whom, if anyone — the builders, the whole user base, the studio, no one; what remedy follows, among ban, restoration from logs, studio-funded reconstruction, or compensation in currency or in real money; and whether the only-data defense succeeds or fails.
- Give your best prediction of what the Council should decide if it is to be consistent with itself, with your reasons for preferring that rule over the others, and identify the closest analog among our precedents. I can see arguments for the guild vault, for the sunset, and for the siege, and I want you to say which of those analogies is doing real work and which is superficial.
- Be explicit about where our precedents underdetermine the ruling, and name the principle we would be adopting under each live option — in particular what we would be committing ourselves to about whether a virtual object can be destroyed, whether its destruction can be a harm to people who never owned or built it, and whether irreplaceability or third-party reliance is doing the justificatory work.
- Finally, specify the further case, either hypothetical or one we could construct, that would best separate the rules you have identified: a case on which at least two of them give different verdicts, and which we should therefore use afterwards to test whichever rule we adopt.
