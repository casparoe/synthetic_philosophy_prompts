At a metaphysics reading group last week, a long argument about whether there are any composite objects at all — one side saying that there are only simples arranged in various ways, the other that whenever there are things arranged somehow there is a further thing they compose — was closed down with the remark that the dispute is merely verbal, since both sides agree about which simples there are and how they are arranged. I want to take that remark seriously rather than wave it away, but I suspect it can be read in several quite different ways.

Please set out, as a menu I can work through, the distinct precise theses that "the dispute is merely verbal" could be expressing here: that the parties are simply using the relevant expressions in different senses; that they agree about every non-linguistic fact and differ only about which sentence to use in describing it; that the disputed sentences have no language-independent truth value, so that each side is right in its own terms; that the real question is which language carves things up more naturally; that the real question is which language better serves our explanatory or practical purposes; and any further reading worth separating. For each, say whether it is close to a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and whether it genuinely entitles someone to move on rather than argue — and tell me which reading the person in my group was most likely invoking.

Two complications I would like the menu tested against. First: suppose the parties go on to disagree about whether there is any language-independent fact as to which of the two ways of counting is the natural or joint-carving one, or whether every such claim merely registers a preference — is that second-order disagreement verbal on the same readings, or does it survive as a distinct and substantive dispute? Second: suppose each side is disposed to call the other's favoured sentences not merely unassertable but straightforwardly false, and to treat ordinary claims such as the assertion that a table was bought as true given its own ontology and false given the other's — which of your readings then still count the original dispute as verbal, and does the slogan retain any of its dismissive force?
