I sit on the Conduct Review Panel of Meridian Health Partners, a mid-sized international NGO that funds and jointly staffs a network of about sixty locally run clinics across seven country programs. We meet in ten days to decide a whistleblower complaint filed against the director of our largest country program — I'll call the country Ambara, since it's really a composite of several settings and I'd rather not pin any of this on a real place — and I need to circulate a decision memo beforehand that the other five members can actually vote on. Two of those members are from the region and have told me separately that they disagree with each other about nearly all of it, which is why I don't want a document that restates our head-office line and calls the matter closed. Please draft that memo. Assume away the legal questions: counsel has cleared everything below under local law and under our donor's rules, so I want no case law, no citations, and no reading lists — just the normative reasoning, written so that a colleague who disagrees can see where to push back.

The complaint, from a junior Ambaran member of our field staff, is that the director has been buying access with money that should have gone to patients. The practices it lists are these. (a) He contributed roughly two weeks of his own salary toward the funeral costs of the district health officer's mother and attended the funeral; he says any person or organisation with standing in the district is expected to do this, and that declining would be read as a declaration that no relationship exists. (b) Each year at the end of Ramadan he delivers a goat to the district health officer and one to the traditional authority whose endorsement lets our outreach teams enter villages; this is reciprocated with public praise at district meetings and with help when we need permission quickly. (c) He paid one term of school fees for the daughter of a records clerk in the district office — the clerk who controls how fast our reimbursement paperwork moves. Nothing was hidden, but nothing was announced; his explanation to me was that the clerk asked, and that everyone in that office knows whom to ask. (d) Our trainings pay attending officials a per diem set above actual travel and meal costs; government travel budgets in Ambara are effectively unfunded, every other NGO and the ministry itself do the same, and if we stopped, officials would simply give their days to other organisations' trainings. (e) He gave the district health office a laptop, logged as office property, though the officer has in practice taken the last two such machines with him on transfer. Our current code is a flat rule: no gifts or payments of value to government officials or community leaders, other than modest hospitality. There are two complications I can't get past. First, the complainant filed three weeks after the director declined to hire his cousin; the hiring file shows the cousin wasn't shortlisted under a scored process the director didn't control. Second, HQ does some structurally identical things that nobody has ever called corruption: it flies officials business class and puts them in good hotels for our annual Geneva meeting, last year's regional coordinator hire was the daughter of a former health minister, and our donor relations team hosts dinners.

So I'd like the memo to do five things. Give a bottom-line recommendation up front. Then dispose of each of (a) through (e) as prohibited, permitted, or permitted with conditions, with a reason stated in terms a field officer could follow rather than in the vocabulary of the familiar sector formula about entrusted power and private gain. Then say whether the complaint should be upheld and what, if anything, should follow for the director — including how much weight the cousin episode should carry when it goes to the complainant's credibility but not, obviously, to whether the practices are what they are. Then propose the rule that should replace the flat ban: something a country director with no training in ethics can apply in five minutes, in the field, without a philosopher or a lawyer on the call — and explain why it isn't merely our Geneva standard wearing local costume. Finally, give the strongest version of whichever position you are not recommending, in a form the panel member who holds it would recognise as her own; my guess is that the hardest view to defeat is that if we forbid these things the relationship dissolves, the clinics lose their permissions and referrals, and the cost of our purity lands on patients rather than on us. Two things I keep going back and forth on and would like the memo to rule on: whether the openness of a practice should count in its favour, given that all five are entirely open inside the district elite and none is visible to the people the programme exists to serve; and whether the fact that this complaint came from local staff rather than from head office defuses the charge that anything we decide is just an imported Western standard. For what it's worth, my own stab at a principle has been that what makes a transfer corrupt isn't that value moves but that it redirects the judgment of someone supposed to be acting for others — and that a gift can do this more effectively than a bribe precisely because it produces a relationship instead of settling a transaction. I'm not sure it survives case (a), and I'd rather have your view than my own attachment to it.
