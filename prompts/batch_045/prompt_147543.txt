I am a clinical educator at an NHS ambulance trust, responsible for training the non-clinical call handlers who triage 999 calls. A handler has roughly ninety seconds in which to gather what a caller tells them and to decide, among other things, how much weight that account should bear: whether reported chest pain is being minimised, whether a fall was a fall, whether someone is safe to leave at home. That weighting is recorded as a single code on the patient's record and follows them through the rest of the system. From next quarter the triage software will also attach an automatically generated reliability summary to every call, derived from features of the caller's speech and of the call itself, and handlers will be expected to take it into account when they set the code.

Please write a decision procedure my handlers can actually run, in the order the questions arise, for deciding whether a low weighting of a caller's account — their own or the summary's — is tracking the reliability of what was said or is tracking something about who said it: accent, age, sex, class, distress, intoxication, or English as a second language. It must say what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, at what point the procedure stops and directs the handler to raise rather than lower the weighting, when the summary should be disregarded, and what exactly should go into the record when the procedure says the judgement is suspect. It should be usable by someone with no training in the relevant philosophy, say explicitly which part of the distinction philosophers draw between a credibility deficit caused by prejudice about the speaker and one caused by a gap in the concepts available for describing what has happened to you can survive into a ninety-second call and which cannot, and be honest about which steps are judgement wearing procedural clothing. Constraints: handlers work to scripted protocols and any change to a response category has to be countersigned by a clinician; I have about two minutes of a handler's attention during a call and ten minutes in debrief afterwards.

Then run it on three calls: an eighty-two-year-old woman with a quiet voice and a strong regional accent describing abdominal pain, logged as a poor historian; a fluent thirty-four-year-old man who gives a textbook account of cardiac symptoms he has evidently read about; and a caller who reports something no triage category fits and can only say that it is not confusion. I would like at least one of these to come out in a way I will not like and will have to defend to the trust's clinical lead. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and how it is most likely to be misused in practice.
