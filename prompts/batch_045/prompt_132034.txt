I'm working through a wrinkle in the standard Kripkean argument that identity statements involving rigid designators can be necessary while knowable only a posteriori, and I'd like a careful explanation of what is doing the work in a case I've constructed.

Call a name a *runner-up name* when it is introduced by a public stipulation of the following form:

> "N" is to denote the object that uniquely satisfies condition C, if there is exactly one such object; if there is none, "N" is to denote the object that comes closer to satisfying C than any other object does; if there is a tie for closest, "N" denotes nothing.

Now suppose a community of astronomers introduces "Vulcan" as a runner-up name, taking C to be the condition *being a planet interior to Mercury whose gravitation accounts for the residual advance of Mercury's perihelion*. Science subsequently delivers two results: first, no object uniquely satisfies C — the residual turns out to have a non-planetary explanation; second, there is nonetheless a small body interior to Mercury, too small to produce the residual, which comes closer to satisfying C than anything else does. So "Vulcan" denotes that body. Let "X" be an ordinary proper name for that same body, introduced by a perceptual baptism and transmitted in the usual causal-historical way.

What is the modal-epistemic status of the statement *Vulcan = X*?

Please explain rather than merely assert, and in the course of your explanation address at least the following:

1. Is "Vulcan" a rigid designator? Note that the clause doing the reference-fixing work here is comparative and degree-theoretic rather than categorical, and that it can plausibly be read either with or without an implicit actuality operator ("the object that *in fact* comes closest"). Show how the modal status of the identity turns on that choice, and say which reading you take to be the more natural one and why.

2. Contrast the case with (a) the standard Hesperus/Phosphorus-style case, in which each name is anchored to its bearer by independent perceptual contact, and (b) the Neptune-style case, in which a name is introduced by a description that is in fact uniquely satisfied. In particular, say whether the runner-up case shows that causal-historical anchoring is doing any work in these arguments over and above rigidity — and whether any necessity you arrive at is of the same *kind* as in the Hesperus/Phosphorus case, or only a de facto rigidity that the argument does not really need.

3. It is tempting to say that Vulcan might easily have failed to be X, since a slightly different body might have come closest to satisfying C. Explain where that intuition comes from, and whether it is genuine evidence against necessity here or merely the familiar "might not have satisfied the reference-fixing description" intuition in a new guise.

4. Briefly flag any respect in which you think the stipulation is simply indeterminate — for instance because closeness to satisfying C admits no determinate metric — and say what follows for the identity statement if it is.

Please do not give a literature survey, reading suggestions, or citations; I want the reasoning itself, in roughly 600–900 words of prose.

End with a final line, on its own, giving your verdict about *Vulcan = X* in exactly this form:

\answer{label}

where the label is exactly one of: necessary a posteriori; contingent a posteriori; contingent a priori; necessary a priori; indeterminate. If your verdict is sensitive to the reading discussed in point 1, choose the verdict you take to be correct on the most natural reading. Do not list multiple labels.
