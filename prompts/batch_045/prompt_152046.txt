I direct a small forecasting unit inside a policy institute. Four analysts. Each spring we issue twenty-five-year survival forecasts for roughly forty political entities — states, federations, supranational unions, standing alliances — and every forecast is logged in advance, scored against a ledger of resolved cases once the horizon closes or the entity ends, and signed off by me before publication. The budget is about one analyst-day per item, all sources must be unclassified, and the written rationale has to fit on a single page. My analysts are strong generalists with working probability but no formal survival analysis.

Our house method is the outside view: for each entity, assemble a reference class of comparable entities, read off the base rate of survival to the horizon, and treat any entity-specific story about why this one is different as an adjustment that has to be argued for rather than assumed. Two disagreements have made that method unreliable in practice, and I want them settled by procedure rather than re-litigated every spring. The first is what may count as a class. Half the team wants classes defined by an explicit mechanism — polities that extract revenue through a fiscal-military apparatus, unions whose members retain a unilateral exit clause — on the grounds that a shared mechanism is what lets a base rate travel. The other half wants classes defined by the label the entity would have used for itself, or by a broad type such as empire or federation, on the grounds that mechanism classes are causal theories smuggled in as bookkeeping and that classifying the mechanism already is the forecast. The second disagreement is statistical. The entities we forecast are, by construction, ones that exist now, and I have become convinced that this contaminates the numbers we read off: a class assembled from currently surviving members is not the same population as a cohort followed from its founding, and I suspect several of our published figures are inflated for that reason alone, in a way that no amount of entity-specific storytelling corrects.

Please write the procedure my team will run, as an ordered sequence of steps an analyst can complete in a day and a reviewer can audit in half an hour. For each step state what would count as a sufficient answer, what would count as a failure to answer, and what the analyst writes in the one-page rationale. The procedure has to include at least the following, in whatever order you think is right: a step that fixes the persistence criterion before any number is computed, since our forecasts are worth nothing until we have said whether continuity of constitutional order, continuity of ruling coalition, continuity of core territory, or continuity of function is what makes a later entity the same entity; a membership rule that decides class membership only from properties knowable on the forecast date, so that nothing about the outcome leaks into the class; an explicit decision on whether attempts that never consolidated — unions proclaimed but never ratified, states declared but never administered — belong in the denominator, together with the reasoning that settles it rather than a stipulation; a correction for the fact that our classes are drawn from the living, with a statement of when the correction is large enough to matter and when it is ignorable; a required declaration of hazard shape, increasing, flat, or decreasing over the entity's life, with a default for when the analyst has no evidence and the conditions under which the default may be overridden; a rule for what to do when two admissible classes give conflicting base rates, including whether they may be averaged; a rule for revising a standing forecast when an entity simply survives another year without visible decline; and a closing register of the two or three findings that would most change this forecast, so the reviewer knows where to push back. Mark every point at which the procedure hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact, and say which of those points I must sign off on personally rather than delegate.

Then apply the procedure to three items from this spring's list. First, a thirty-year-old continental union of twenty-seven member states, constituted by treaty, whose members hold a unilateral exit clause and whose constitutional changes require unanimity. Second, a two-hundred-and-forty-year-old federal republic whose founding charter contains no secession procedure, which last fought an armed internal conflict over its own continuity a hundred and sixty years ago, and whose component units have diverged economically over the last three decades. At least one of these two must end where I suspect one of them ends, with a band so wide that it is useless to the people who commission our work, and I want to see the procedure produce that result honestly and tell me how to publish it. Third, apply it to a donor's request to forecast the twenty-five-year survival of the security order in a region, where the donor means by that a pattern of alignment rather than any body with a charter, a membership, or a treasury; if the correct output there is a refusal to issue a number, show the procedure arriving at that refusal and telling me what minimal redefinition of the object would make a forecast possible.

Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: which of our failures it will not prevent, which of its steps are judgment dressed as method, and where an analyst who follows it exactly can still be badly wrong. Do not cite or name any literature, authors, or studies; I need the procedure, not references, and my team will not have access to anything beyond what is in the document you write.
