I'm a teaching assistant for an advanced metaethics seminar, and we have spent the last fortnight on the family of views that treat facts about what an agent has most reason to do as facts about the output of hypothetical deliberation — the ideal-observer and ideal-advisor style accounts, on which normativity is *indirect*: the fact that an agent has most reason to φ consists in a fact about a procedure she is not in fact running — as against views on which there is a normative standard (the balance of objective reasons, say, or the expected value of outcomes under the correct weighting of interests) that holds independently of any procedure, with idealization serving only as our best epistemic handle on it. Every time we discuss this, the seminar produces the same two stock moves: the constructivist is asked which idealization is the privileged one, and the realist is asked why ideal deliberation should be expected to track the standard at all. I would like material that gets past that rut, so I have been trying to build a case that pushes both positions somewhere less rehearsed, and I would like a dialogue built around it.

The case is this. Rowan has a sum of money that she can either spend on reconstructive surgery for her sister or invest in a friend's start-up. Two features of her actual psychology are relevant. First, she has a false belief: she thinks the surgery has a success rate of roughly one in five, when it is in fact better than four in five. Second, the strength of her concern for her sister is, as a matter of contingent psychological fact, largely driven by imagined scenes of her sister's suffering, and such scenes grip her far more powerfully when she takes that suffering to be ongoing. Now consider two sequences of improvement, each of which seems to satisfy every condition anyone has proposed for counting as idealizing from Rowan's starting point — each step removes a genuine defect, and each sequence terminates in a state stable under further reflection. Sequence one corrects her beliefs first and then gives her full imaginative acquaintance with everything relevant: once she knows the surgery works, the imagined scenes lose their grip, her sister-directed concern stays at its current modest level, and her standing interest in the start-up wins. Sequence two gives her full imaginative acquaintance first and only then corrects her beliefs: the imagined scenes strengthen her concern dramatically, and learning the true success rate does not undo that but merely makes her hopeful, so she pays for the surgery. I call the general phenomenon the failure of the commutativity condition: the operations that make up idealization do not commute, so the verdict of idealized deliberation is path-dependent, and nothing in Rowan's actual situation appears to select a path. There is a second wrinkle I want used as well. Suppose the constructivist responds holistically — replacing a sequence with a single joint operation whose output is a reflective equilibrium or fixed point. Then two further questions arise: whether that fixed point is unique (it need not be; the dynamics could be bistable), and whether it is reachable from Rowan's actual starting point (path-independence is one thing, sitting in the right basin of attraction is another). I want both of those pressed, not just asserted.

Please write the dialogue between two philosophers of equal ability — call them Nadia, who defends the idealized-deliberation view, and Marcus, who defends the standard-first realist view — each of whom knows the other's literature and arguments well and neither of whom is a foil. Some constraints: at least twice in the conversation, the speaker who is responding should first reformulate the other's challenge in their own vocabulary, and the other should be able to recognise the reformulation as what they meant; each speaker should make one concession that is genuinely costly but not fatal to their position; Nadia should not dispose of Rowan's case by simply stipulating an admissible order or by stipulating that one sequence was not really an idealization, but should try the joint fixed-point or holistic-response move, with Marcus pressing on uniqueness, on basins of attraction, and on whether the notion of a defect being removed is quietly defined by reference to the very standard he wants; and Marcus should not answer the reliability challenge by bare assertion of a correlation, but should address the dilemma that the errors idealization removes are either identified independently of the normative standard or are not, and say which horn he takes and why. No speaker should resolve a point by appealing to bare intuition alone, and nobody should cite or quote any published work, name a paper, or make a bibliographic aside — I want the argument itself. Close without a winner, but with each speaker stating precisely what they now take the remaining disagreement to consist in.

Please organise the response with exactly these titled sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

- Cast — about 70 words: one sentence on each speaker's position and one on their dialectical style.
- Setting — about 60 words: where and why the two of them are talking.
- Opening — about 160 words: each states their view and says what they take the disagreement to hang on.
- Exchange I — Specification and Non-Commutativity — about 320 words: Rowan's case is introduced and each speaker gives their diagnosis.
- Exchange II — The Reliability Challenge Turned Around — about 260 words: Marcus is pressed on the tracking relation, and he responds to the dilemma about how idealization's errors are specified.
- Exchange III — Fixed Points, Uniqueness and Reachability — about 260 words: Nadia's holistic proposal and Marcus's reply about bistability and basins of attraction.
- Close — The Residual Disagreement — about 150 words: what each concedes, and the precise statement of what is still in dispute.
