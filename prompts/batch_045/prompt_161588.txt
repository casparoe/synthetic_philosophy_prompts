I'm the most junior member of our university's Committee on Research Integrity and Scholarly Attribution, and I've been asked to draft the decision memo that will circulate before next month's meeting on a complaint I think is genuinely hard; please write that memo for me. The facts, as the committee has provisionally found them: Dr. Marisa Okonkwo, who runs a small lab in regenerative neuroscience, hosted Dr. Tobias Renner, a postdoctoral fellow from the Halvorsen Institute, for a semester as a visiting researcher. At a lab meeting Renner attended, Okonkwo and her graduate student (who had spent two years building a novel calcium-imaging readout that was not yet published) sketched what they themselves called a shopping list of things we should try next year — chief among them the idea of applying an already-public perturbation protocol to a neglected model system and reading the result out with the new method. Nothing was written down beyond a slide deck with no data, no protocol, and no method in it. Renner returned to the Halvorsen Institute, and seven months later his group published exactly that experiment in a high-profile journal, crediting Okonkwo in an acknowledgments footnote for helpful discussions but not offering co-authorship to her or to the graduate student, who is now fourteen months from finishing and feels her project has been taken. Two further wrinkles: Okonkwo's own idea traces in part to a hallway conversation at a conference with a third group that had mentioned something similar in passing, and her lab posted a preprint describing the readout method three months after Renner's visit, at which point the combined idea became reconstructible by any competent group in the field. Our policy, R-14, covers tangible research materials, data, and unpublished manuscripts, and says nothing about ideas or research directions; Renner is not subject to our jurisdiction, so the only things we can actually do are decline to find a violation, find a violation and bar him from future visiting appointments while sending a letter of concern to his institution, or dismiss the complaint while recommending that the policy be amended. The Provost has also let it be known, off the record, that a formal finding would be awkward given a sponsored-research agreement with the Halvorsen Institute that is close to signing; I want the memo to treat that consideration as what it is, and not pretend it is a reason.

My own tentative view, which I've been turning over since a conversation with my former doctoral supervisor, is that the remedy here should be credit rather than control: that what was taken was a claim to be acknowledged as a co-author and not a right to block or enjoin the work, and that our committee should say this explicitly even if we dismiss. Two senior members disagree and I don't have a good answer to them: they say that a dismissal with no finding tells every future visitor that anything said in a lab meeting is free for the taking, that labs will respond by going quiet in exactly the settings where openness is most valuable, and that the value of the idea depended on tacit scaffolding — the graduate student's know-how, the unwritten calibration tricks, the sense of which negative results mattered — that Renner could not have built or bought elsewhere and that never appears in the published paper. I'd also like the memo to work through, at least briefly, how much weight to give the fact that Okonkwo's own origination is partly derivative, whether the preprint publication should extinguish any claim she has (and how to think about a case where each piece is public but the combination isn't), and whether the desert here should be apportioned between the person who generated the idea and the people who did the expensive work of executing it, given that without the execution nothing of value existed.

What I need is a memo of roughly nine hundred to eleven hundred words that the committee could act on: a clear recommendation among the three courses open to us (or a fourth you think better), with the reasoning, and with any remedy or referral stated concretely enough to be carried out, including who does what and by when. If you recommend a policy amendment, please draft the operative language you'd want in R-14 and say what it should cover: what counts as a disclosed research direction, whether it must be recorded in some durable form to be protected, how long protection should last, and whether the remedy is attribution only or also some restriction on use. Please also give an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side — I'd rather our memo be the one that names the best version of the argument we're rejecting than the one that quietly reframes it — and keep it in a register that Okonkwo, Renner's department chair, and the Provost could all read without wincing.
