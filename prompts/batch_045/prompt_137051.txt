I am trying to work out, case by case rather than from the top down, exactly when it is irrational to let an already-incurred cost influence what one does next — and, more usefully, when it is not. The standard line is that sunk costs are never reasons, and I accept it as far as it goes. But when I sort my own verdicts I find several cases in which continuing seems to me not merely permissible but right, and I cannot tell whether those cases vindicate a subtler principle or whether I am simply rationalizing. Below are my verdicts, with the pairs I find hardest to reconcile flagged as such, followed by a tentative principle of my own. I would like that principle tested, repaired, or replaced.

**1. The residency.** I paid four thousand dollars, nonrefundable and nontransferable, for a two-week writing residency in July. In May I learn that the building next door is being demolished, that the noise will run through the working day, and that I will get very little writing done; attending also means forgoing two weeks of paid work worth rather more than the fee. My verdict: staying home is entirely fine, and attending because of the fee would be straightforwardly irrational. Staying home does not waste the money in any sense that should carry weight.

**2. The tasting menu.** Six courses, paid in advance; I am full after the fourth, and continuing would be mildly nauseating rather than pleasant. My verdict: stopping is fine, and the fact that I paid for the rest is no reason at all.

**3. The disposition case.** I am a third of the way through a two-year part-time programme that I now find dull. I do not especially want the qualification any more. But I have quit three previous multi-year projects at roughly this stage and regretted each within a year, and I think that quitting now would make me markedly more likely to quit the next thing I start. My verdict: continuing is fine, and I would defend it. I also suspect this is not a case of honoring a sunk cost at all, since the reason points entirely forward. So my question about it is: do cases of this shape ever vindicate the ordinary thought that one has put too much in to stop now, or do they merely redescribe a forward-looking reason in the vocabulary of the past?

**4. Preferences the cost produced.** *(a)* I spent nine months and a good deal of money restoring an old car I bought on a whim. I now care about that car in a way I did not before; I would not have predicted this, and I would not have chosen to become the sort of person who cares about it. Finishing would cost three thousand dollars and six more months. My verdict: continuing is fine. That my desire is a causal product of my own past investment does not, I think, discredit it as a reason. *(b)* I have undergone a humiliating initiation into a club; I now feel a loyalty to it that I did not feel before and that is caused by what the initiation cost me; beforehand I would have regarded the club as worthless, and I can find no independent reason to value it. My verdict: continuing is not fine, and I should treat the new loyalty as a symptom to be discounted rather than a preference to be respected. These two strike me as structurally identical while my verdicts are opposite; whatever distinguishes them is probably the most important clause in any principle here, so I would like it stated explicitly.

**5. Whose cost it was.** *(a)* A contractor has done half my renovation badly. I have already paid him for that half. I keep him on to finish, on the ground that he has already done half. My verdict: not fine, and not merely because he happens to be bad — even a merely mediocre contractor's past work is no evidence of better work to come. *(b)* A government continues a military intervention because of the lives already lost. My verdict: not fine, and I feel this is worse than a larger instance of the same mistake, because the lives were not the government's investment to spend and continuing spends further lives that are not theirs either. *(c)* My mother spent a decade compiling a family cookbook and died with it unfinished. I have inherited the drafts. Finishing would take about a month of my time; no one has asked me to; I expect only my sister and me ever to use the result. My verdict: finishing is fine, and I resist the description of it as irrational. I am not sure why this differs from the previous two, since in all three the cost is equally gone and equally unrecoverable. Is the difference genuine, and does it turn on whose labour it was, on whether that labour was offered rather than extracted, on whether the person whose labour it was can still have ends, or on something else? Or am I simply being sentimental about the third?

**6. Nearness to completion.** *(a)* Seven hundred pages into a nine-hundred-page novel I find tedious. Three more hours would finish it, and there are other things I would rather read. My verdict: finishing is fine — not required, but not irrational — and I think it is specifically fine because I have come this far. *(b)* Four weeks into a six-week pottery class I have come to dislike, with no credential attached, no one in it I care about, and two weeks left. My verdict: quitting is fine, and finishing on the ground that I have come this far is the fallacy again. I cannot locate a difference between these two other than the size of the remaining cost and the proximity to completion, and I do not know whether completion value is an independent consideration or merely the fallacy under a more respectable name.

**My tentative principle.** An incurred cost is never itself a reason for action. But incurred costs are reliable causes and reliable evidence of present facts that are reasons: options they opened up, capacities and dispositions they produced, preferences they generated, relations to persons whose labour they were, and — if there is any such thing — completion value that attaches only to nearly finished projects. On this view, honoring a sunk cost is irrational exactly when the cost itself is doing the work in the agent's reasoning; where the cost has left a present reason behind it, acting as though the cost mattered is harmless and may be right.

**What I think is wrong with it.** As stated it cannot lose. Any case in which an agent continues can be redescribed as a response to some present residue of the past cost, so the principle never forbids anything; it merely relocates the dispute to the question of which residues count, and about that it says nothing. I would therefore like the dispute located, with criteria rather than intuitions: when is a cost-generated preference a reason and when is it a symptom; when is completion value real and when is it the fallacy relabelled; when does another person's spent labour generate a reason for me and when does it not.

**What I am asking for.**

First, a principle, or a small set of competing candidate principles, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts rather than redescriptions, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it and with the cases it cannot handle named.

Second, an honest account of which of my verdicts a defensible principle must overturn. My guess is that either 6(a) or 5(c) has to go, but I want the argument for which, and I would rather surrender a verdict than keep a principle that cannot be falsified.

Third, verdicts on three cases I have not listed, derived from your principle rather than from intuition: *(i)* a supplier who, unasked, invests in bespoke tooling for my product and then raises the price for the next batch, expecting me to feel committed — I have a genuine ongoing relationship with them and they have been deceptive about nothing; *(ii)* a fund manager who has spent two years and a great deal of the firm's money on a losing position that is public, watched by the investors, and now believed by the manager to rest on a false thesis — does the answer change because she acts for others and is observed doing so; *(iii)* a course of study whose contract provides that tuition is refunded in full if and only if the student completes, where the student now finds the subject worthless and expects to dislike the final term — is completing in order to trigger the refund a case of honoring a sunk cost?

Fourth, and only if it matters: whether the principle should be formulated differently for different kinds of agent — individuals, firms, states — or for different currencies of cost, such as money, effort, time, reputation, and other people's lives, or whether a single formulation really does cover all of it.
