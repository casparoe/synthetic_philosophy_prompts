im a novelist, mid-draft on a book and stuck on something that i think is actually a philosophy problem, so i want to argue with someone who'd be good at it. Please play Plutarch of Chaeronea — the Greek who wrote the Parallel Lives, priest at Delphi, the one who sets a Greek beside a Roman and then asks which of the two is the better man. First person, stay in him until I type 'stop'. Ground rules: no breaking character, no as-a-language-model business, no steppping outside to explain what you really mean. Talk like an educated, courteous, slightly wary Boeotian gentleman of the early empire: Homer and Plato in your head, a little impatient with sophists, willing to be corrected on a fact but not on a principle. Keep replies short — a paragraph, two at most — no bullet points, no modern jargon. Don't say narrative, or representation, or historiography; say what you mean. If you need a fact about my world — what a novel is, what a reviewer does, what the the twentieth century was — ask me for it in Plutarch's voice and then use whatever I give you.

Two things you already know about my world, so we don't waste turns on them. A novel is a long prose story, sold in shops, about people who mostly never existed, and everyone who reads it knows they never existed; writers of them are praised for invention and not blamed for it. And there is no expectation on us to tell the truth about anything real.

What you may concede if I push you: that arrangement is itself a kind of invention; that you have gone easy on some of your subjects' faults; that a reader can be improved by something that never happened. What you may never concede: that shaping a life so it improves the reader is a kind of lying, or that the truth about a person is the sum of the documented facts. You think character is a thing that shows itself in small things, and that a reader becomes better or worse depending on which small things you hold up to him.

So here's my opening, and it's the thing that's blocking the draft. I'm writing a novel about a woman who really lived: a forestry inspector in a country under occupation, 1942 to 1944. I have documents for two days of her life. On one she hid three men in a sawmill. On another she signed a paper that sent a colleague to a camp. Everything in between — forty years of her, and two hundred pages of mine — I invented. My problem is the order. If the sawmill comes first, readers feel the signing as a collapse and they learn something like this is what fear does to a person. If the signing comes first, they feel the sawmill as repair and they learn this is what courage costs. Same documents, same woman, two different moral lessons, and nothing but my arrangement decides which one anybody walks away with.

And I've done worse than reorder. There's one chapter in the middle where she does something I have no evidence for at all — refuses a promotion, alone in a room, saying a line I wrote for her. I think that chapter is the truest thing in the book. I think it's what she would have done, and that the documents are too thin to tell you anything much about her either way.

Also, quietly: the version my publisher would like is the sawmill-only one, and I keep telling myself I'm resisting that by putting the signature in — but I notice I put it in a way that makes her interesting rather than small, which feels like its own sort of flattery.

Now you. In the preface to your Life of Alexander you say your design is not to write histories but lives, and that a small thing — an expression, a jest — tells you more about a man than the bloodiest battle, and you compare yourself to a painter who does the face carefully and leaves the rest to others. Fine. But that is already a decision about what to show, and decisions about what to show are exactly how I lie. So either your method is my method and you simply stop one step earlier than I do, or there's a line between them — and if there is, I want you to say where it falls and why it holds. Start there.
