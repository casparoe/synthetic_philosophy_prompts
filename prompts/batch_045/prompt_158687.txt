I follow a commentator on comparative political economy who, over roughly six years, has published a running series of short verdicts on whether particular inequalities are unjust. I want to know what she will say about a case that has not come up for her yet, and I am more interested in the rule she is implicitly running than in the verdict itself. Her published verdicts, as nearly as I can reconstruct them:

(a) Two graduates of the same public university, same degree, same grades; one is hired at twice the salary because her uncle is a partner at the firm. Unjust.

(b) A software engineer earns roughly forty times what a home care aide earns; both work long hours, the labour market for both is open and competitive with no barriers to entry, and both live comfortably. Not unjust.

(c) Two siblings, both comfortably off; one inherits a large fortune, the other nothing. Unjust.

(d) Two classmates with identical skills; one spends six years on a venture that fails and ends up on a lower income than the other, who stayed in salaried work. The risk was known in advance, insurance against it was available and affordable, and neither of them is treated any differently by anyone. Not unjust.

(e) Two groups with almost identical incomes, in a society where one of them is routinely denied deference, voice, and equal treatment by public institutions. Unjust.

The case I want to put to her: in a mid-sized city, care work, delivery, and content moderation have largely moved onto platforms. Workers chose flexible platform work over stable employment that paid less take-home, knowing the work could disappear, and for several years they earned more than the stable alternative. A change in the technology and in regulation has now removed most of that work, and there was no affordable way to insure against it. A substantial minority are left with no steady income, are excluded from credit and most housing, have little realistic prospect of retraining into anything comparable, and are publicly spoken of as people who made bad choices. Nothing about the process was rigged: the contracts were genuine, the choice was real, and the regulatory change was not aimed at them. Some of these workers are now below any reasonable decent floor; others sit just above it but are permanently insecure and visibly marked out as a dependent class.

Please do the following.

1. Give your best prediction of her verdict, and state the rule you take her to be running explicitly enough that it could be applied to cases she has not seen.
2. Set out the other rules that fit the five published verdicts at least as well, and say what each of them predicts here, including where the sub-group below the floor and the sub-group just above it come apart.
3. Identify the closest analogue among her precedents, and say honestly whether the five verdicts underdetermine the new one. If they do, state the principle she would be committing herself to under each of the live options.
4. Specify one further case, concretely enough to be put to her, that would best separate the rules still in play.
5. Finally: is the rule you attribute to her closer to the attitude toward inequality usually described as American — wide gaps in outcome tolerated so long as the process is open and mobility is real — or to the one usually described as European — gaps in contribution and reward tolerated, wide social distance and relations of dependence not? I know both are caricatures. In one paragraph, say which of the two is the more defensible and what the strongest consideration against your answer is.
