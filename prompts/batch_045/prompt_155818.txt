Me and two friends have been going back and forth in a group chat about Roko's basilisk for like a week, and I think I've worked out why the argument never goes anywhere: people either grant the original setup — a superintelligence with a near-perfect model of you, deciding whether to torture a simulation of you based on whether you helped create it — and then argue about decision theory, or they refuse to grant it and say the whole thing is an artifact of perfect predictors and sci-fi stakes. I want to argue, in a blog post, that the structure is actually out there in perfectly ordinary human institutions and that the three standard dismissals don't kill it: the "no rational punisher would bother carrying out a costly threat" move, the "nobody can predict you anywhere near that well" move, and the "that's just deterrence with extra steps" move. So I need one case, and it has to be a case a hostile reader can't wave away.

What I need from you is a scenario, a paragraph and a half at most, meeting these constraints: no artificial intelligence, nothing smarter than a shrewd human doing the predicting (a retired claims adjuster, a dean reading old files, a trustee going through letters, whatever — nobody simulates anybody), no exotic physics or acausal magic, and stakes a normal person could actually face. Structurally it has to be genuinely basilisk-shaped rather than deterrence in costume: the payoff has to turn on a counterfactual fact about the agent — what they would have done without the incentive — not on what they in fact do; and the agent's current choice has to be causally shut off from whoever decides the payoff, or the whole thing collapses back into ordinary deterrence and I've gained nothing. My half-formed idea for doing that is a kind of look-back clause: the decision is made only from the record as it stood before the agent ever heard of the scheme, which is exactly the sort of anti-gaming provision real institutions really do write. At the same time the choice still has to be real evidence about the counterfactual fact, which means the agent is genuinely unsure what sort of person they are — and I want that uncertainty to fall out of the situation rather than being announced by the narrator ("Dana was 50/50 about her own character" is cheating, I'll spot it and so will readers).

Then the check: causal decision theory should say the choice makes no difference and isn't worth the cost, evidential decision theory should say do the thing, and functional decision theory should also say don't bother. I think I see a knob for that last one — something like the record having been produced by a person who is relevantly no longer the agent running now, so the subjunctive dependence FDT wants is missing even though the evidential correlation survives — but if there's a cleaner way to get FDT to peel off while keeping the predictor merely human, use it and tell me why it's cleaner. Please also state the action, the evidence and the probabilities you're assuming, and deal head-on with the screening-off objection: the one where, since the agent already knows her own disposition, her current action is a mere symptom of it and EDT's evidential boost evaporates. I need to know why that doesn't defuse the case here, or, if it does, that my three verdicts can't be had together. Give me two ablations — what happens if the record is allowed to include post-announcement conduct, and what happens if the predictor is swapped for something that models the agent's current decision procedure — and say which theory moves in each and why. Finally, half a paragraph on what a causal decision theorist would say to stop this from counting as a basilisk at all, and whether that objection is fair. No references needed, and if one of my constraints can only be met by stipulating a verdict rather than generating it, tell me plainly instead of papering over it.
