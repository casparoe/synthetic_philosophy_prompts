Hey — hoping you can help me turn something from class into something I can actually use.

I'm a junior taking a seminar this term on constitutions and the problem of the dead hand, and I also somehow got stuck on my student government's charter and bylaws committee. We vet every proposed amendment before it goes to the full assembly: about ten or twelve proposals a term, we meet every other Sunday for two hours, and I'm the person who writes the one-page recommendation that goes to the floor and then to the Dean's office, which has veto power but mostly rubber-stamps whatever we send. The committee is seven people who will argue about anything for forty minutes, so what I need is something that closes arguments down, not something that opens new ones.

Here's the thing: nearly everything that crosses my desk is the same move wearing different clothes. The people writing the rule now are making it harder for the people who come after them to do something. Cooling-off periods before a divestment vote. Supermajority requirements for raising the activity fee. A five-year lock on the sustainability fund. A clause banning the assembly from taking positions on national elections. In seminar we spent a while on Ulysses contracts — binding yourself now so that later, weakened you can't do the thing — and on the claim that a constitution is partly a precommitment device for a political body rather than just a list of rules. I get the appeal in the abstract. I cannot turn it into something I can run on a proposal on a Sunday afternoon.

What I want is a written procedure my committee could actually work through:

- A short ordered list of questions (six or seven max), each with what counts as a passing answer and what counts as a failing one, and with an explicit "stop here, recommend no" point. It has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by a first-year who joined last week and has never taken a philosophy class, and it has to rely only on things we can actually get — old assembly minutes, the text of the clause, maybe a budget number. We can't run surveys or commission research.
- Where a question isn't a factual one but a disagreement about values, say so explicitly and say who decides. I want the hand-off points marked, not smoothed over.
- Two half-formed tests I keep reaching for — please either fix them or tell me to drop them. (1) A direction-of-ignorance test: that tying a future assembly's hands is only ever justified by a failure of *will* it will predictably have (panic, short-termism, cycling between factions, getting lobbied during a crisis), never by a failure of *information*, because a future assembly will normally know more than we do, not less. So any clause whose real justification is that we know better what's good for them should fail. (2) A mirror test: would the majority proposing this accept the identical constraint if the other side held the seats? If not, it's factional entrenchment in precommitment costume. There's probably a third test I'm missing about how long the handcuff should last versus how long the diagnosed failure is expected to last.
- Then run the whole thing on three proposals I've got coming up: (a) a rule requiring 72 hours between the introduction and the final vote on any divestment resolution; (b) a two-thirds supermajority for *increases* to the student activity fee, with no equivalent requirement for decreases; (c) a clause locking in ten years of spending on a climate commitment, with amendment only by a three-quarters vote. Be aware going in that I personally think (c) is the best idea on the table, so if the procedure says no to it I want it to say no anyway and I want to see exactly where it breaks.
- Finally, an honest note on what this procedure structurally can't see — the failure modes I should expect after a term of using it.

American English, please, and please don't assume I can look anything up: we're undergrads, none of us has access to a law database, and I need something that works off the clause text and our own minutes.
