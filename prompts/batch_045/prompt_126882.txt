I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on what I call *commitment races*, and I have produced an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept and whose steps I cannot pull apart. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

By a commitment race I mean the following. There is a prize — partly a material payoff, partly the right to set the terms on which everyone else subsequently has to act — that goes to whichever of n ≥ 2 parties is first to make an irreversible, costly commitment to a specified course of action. The cost is sunk and is paid only by whoever commits. What the committed-to course of action is actually worth depends on a state of the world about which all parties are receiving the same kind of noisy evidence at the same rate. Assume the parties are symmetric expected-value maximizers. Assume also, to block one easy reply, that the race is not epistemically leaky: the first committer's choice is public, but it reveals nothing about the state beyond the bare fact that someone was willing to commit, so waiting cannot be defended as a way of harvesting information from the winner.

Model each party i by an evidence threshold τ_i: i commits at the first instant its accumulated evidence favors its preferred course at least as strongly as τ_i demands. Ties are broken at random, and the race ends when someone commits. The winner gets P − c; everyone else gets 0.

(1) The parties' evidence processes are continuous in time and almost surely cross any threshold whatever. So if τ_i < τ_j and the parties are otherwise identical, i commits first with probability one.
(2) Therefore, holding fixed everything else about a party, a lower evidence threshold yields a strictly higher probability of securing the prize.
(3) The winner's payoff is P − c and the loser's is 0, and P > c, since otherwise no one would enter at all. Expected value of entering is thus strictly increasing in the probability of winning.
(4) By (2) and (3), expected value is strictly decreasing in τ_i.
(5) So no threshold is optimal: for any τ_i one might adopt, a slightly lower one is strictly better.
(6) The thing to do, then, is to push τ as low as it goes — commit at the first instant, on no evidence at all. And since nothing in (1)–(5) turns on who you are, this holds for every party alike.
(7) Hence each party should prefer, ex ante, to be the sort of agent that evidence cannot move; a party that could disable its own capacity to gather and weigh evidence before the race begins ought to do so.
(8) But: (a) rationality cannot recommend disabling one's own responsiveness to evidence — that is close to the definition of irrationality; (b) if every party commits at the first instant, each wins with probability 1/n, exactly the coin flip they were trying to beat, except that they now pay c and choose their course of action blind; and (c) nothing in (1)–(6) used any feature special to commitment races, so the same reasoning shows that in any situation where another agent might act before you — submitting a paper, making an offer on a house, choosing a lane in traffic — you should discard your evidence and act at once.

Something has gone wrong and I cannot see what. My suspicion keeps settling on (2) or (5), but I do not trust it: (2) seems to follow straight from (1), and (5) straight from (4). I have been told that this is just a continuous-time pre-emption game and that the answer is mixed strategies over stopping times, but that strikes me as a redescription of the situation rather than an account of which of my steps fails. If I must choose, I would rather keep the modeling assumptions above and the claim that entrants are expected-value maximizers than accept (7), and I would rather keep (1) than give up the claim that commitment races are real and are sometimes rationally entered. But that may be mere prejudice.
