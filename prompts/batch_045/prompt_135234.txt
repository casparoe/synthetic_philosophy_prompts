I've drifted into defending a fairly hard-line version of cognitive impenetrability and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The version I hold: no genuinely cognitive state — a belief, a desire, an expectation, a bit of knowledge, a_valence attached to something you've learned — ever changes the phenomenal character of perceptual experience itself. What all the famous apparent cases show, on my reading, is effects on attention, on where you look, on how you map what you see onto a response scale, on memory, or on post-perceptual judgement. I'd rather hold the claim about *experience* than about early cortex, because I don't think "early cortex = perceptual" is doing any real work.

So: what would count against it? I want defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely embarrasing, and I want the ranking justified, not asserted.

A few things I specifically want you to wrestle with:

(1) I've been toying with a home-made test and I don't trust it. Roughly: train people that a novel shape is dangerous (or worth money), then stop asking them for verbal ratings or forced-choice judgements altogether and look for a *contingent aftereffect* instead — after adapting to that shape in the trained condition, a neutral test stimulus shows a tilt or colour aftereffect that transfers across retinal position and between the eyes. My thought is that an aftereffect is the behavioural signature of adaptation in sensory cortex, and no shifted decision criterion or response bias can manufacture one; it has to be built by the mechanism changing. Is that right? Would a clean positive result there actually be decisive, or is there a version where the aftereffect shows up and I'm still fine? And can the test be built so that it separates penetration-by-cognition from mere top-down attention, which I'd happily concede is everywhere — or is that distinction exactly what I'm not entitled to? What if it turns out the effect vanishes under a verbal-suppression or dual-task manipulation — does that kill it as evidence, or does it just tell us something about how the cognitive state has to be deployed?

(2) There are findings out there that are at least awkward for me even before any of that: hills reported as steeper by people carrying a heavy pack, targets looking bigger or closer to people who are currently performing well or holding a tool, objects of a known category seeming more saturated in their typical colour, multistable figures resolving differently depending on what you were primed with, the old results where kids who wanted coins more saw them as bigger, plus the whole expertise/perceptual-learning thing with radiologists and wine tasters. Don't name papers at me — I know the literature; describe the experiment type if you need to. What I want is: for each *kind* of finding, what exactly is it evidence of, how far up my ranking does it go, and what is my best reply? Which of these would a committed impenetrabilist concede as counting against them, and which should they dismiss as resting on a bad inference from a judgement measure to an experience claim?

(3) The unfalsifiability worry, honestly assessed. There's an obvious dodge available to me: anything that gets in and changes experience is by definition not perceptual, or not cognitive — pick whichever direction makes the claim come out true. How much of my view survives only because of that move? Is there a non-question-begging way to draw the perceptual/judgemental line that I can actually state in advance of seeing the data, and if not, is that a problem for a claim of this kind or just the normal fate of an architectural thesis? And what's the *reverse* side: what does my view predict that its rivals don't, apart from a long list of null results?

(4) What would make me give it up personally: I've always found bare introspective reports the weakest evidence and anything adaptation-shaped the strongest. Tell me if I've got that backwards.

Write it in British English please (colour, organisation, and so on). No citations, references or reading lists — I don't want to chase papers and you can't look anything up anyway, so if you're tempted to cite something, describe the experiment in a sentence instead. Prose, no headings longer than a few words, maybe 900 words or so, and don't flatter the view — if it's insulated to the point of being empty, say so.
