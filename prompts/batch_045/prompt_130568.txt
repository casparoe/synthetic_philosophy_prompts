I've got a second-year metaphysics seminar coming up in a fortnight — 16 students, one 75-minute slot, a whiteboard and a pile of index cards, no tech beyond that — and the topic is the special composition question (van Inwagen's version I think? the "when do some things add up to one thing" question, with the answer he gives in terms of life). I've tired to teach it before just by handing out a list of cases — two bonded particles, a molecule, a crystal, a rock, a table, a cat, a heap of scrap, my nose plus the Eiffel Tower — and asking "does composition happen here?". It died inside fifteen minutes: somebody says "well, it depends what you mean by object", somebody else says "the parts just are the object, there's nothing more to it", and nobody feels any pressure at all. So I want a structured thing where the pressure comes out of what they have to do, not out of me talking.

What I'm picturing — redesign it freely, I'm not attached — is some kind of certification panel or census office. Small groups, and each group has to hand in a single rule, one side of an index card, that a clerk could apply mechanically to a data sheet describing a candidate collection of parts (fields like relative positions, causal bonding, mass, whether anything's alive or conscious, whether the lot does anything as a unit, whatever you think belongs there) and get out a yes/no on whether it composes something. Constraints: no proper names, no indexicals, finite length, and one rule for all cases rather than a list of verdicts. Then groups swap rules and each gets a fixed budget of adversarial cases to throw at another group's rule, with small stakes — I can bring chocolate, or tokens they wager on whether their own rule survives. The bit I really want you to engineer is the squeeze: a round where, if a group has said yes to case A and no to case B, they have to locate the switch-over point along a series of intermediate cases you supply (near-life, near-contact, almost-a-mechanism, that sort of thing), and a round where they have to name the single respect in which A and B differ and then get handed a case that varies only that respect and only barely. And I want the case pool built so the comfortable middle — yes to organisms and tables, no to scattered junk — is the thing that starts to sag, not the extreme answers.

What they should walk out having felt is that the commonsense package is the unstable one and that each exit costs something they can name: yes to everything and you're stuck with the object made of my nose and the Eiffel Tower; no to everything and, well, no tables; or "each case is just a brute fact" — and I'd like that third one to come out feeling like a real option rather than a shrug, because I suspect a game will naturally make it look like laziness when it's an actual defended position. Keep it about composition at a time, not persistence over time — last year someone dragged in the ship of Theseus and we lost ten minutes — unless your design uses that confusion on purpose, in which case say so and I'll allow it.

Deliverable: rules and roles, materials, a rough minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, the case pool (12-16 cases with their data-sheet entries, or a schema I can generate more from), the scoring/wagering scheme, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular moves in the dialectic — this moment is supposed to make that move feel costly — plus what will predictably go wrong and how to handle it. My two bets: someone writes "it composes something iff it does something interesting" and I won't know what to do with that, and the group that writes the most gerrymandered disjunctive rule wins the game while learning nothing. Also be frank about which features of the question the activity just can't stage and I'll have to say out loud myself — whether composition could be vague, whether "how many objects are there" is well-posed, whether the whole thing is verbal in the quantifier-variance sense. Write it in British English please. And as you go, separate what's actually settled in the literature from what's contested among people who work on this: don't have me tell 16 second-years that everyone agrees composition must have a general answer if it's really one side of an argument, and flag any contested assumption your setup quietly leans on — that sums are ontologically innocent, that composition isn't vague, whatever — instead of burying it in the premise.
