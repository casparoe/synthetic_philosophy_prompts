I'm a journalist at a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I'm finishing a long feature on how old nations really are. For the sidebar I don't want another he-said/she-said. I want a real document: the joint statement that a modernist and an ethno-symbolist about nationalism could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

By the modernist I mean the line of argument associated with Hobsbawm's invention of tradition, Anderson's imagined communities and print capitalism, and Gellner's inversion of the common-sense story: that nationalism produces nations rather than the other way round; that the nation is a creature of the last two centuries, of standardized vernaculars, mass literacy, census and map, and of traditions that are often considerably younger than they claim. By the ethno-symbolist (or, in its stronger form, perennialist) I mean the position associated with Anthony Smith: that modern nations are built out of pre-modern ethnies — a name, a myth of common descent, shared memories, a cult or a sacred text, an association with a homeland — and that where those materials are thin or absent, nation-building stalls, which is why the modernists' mechanism cannot be the whole story.

Assume both participants have read the other side's strongest work, have agreed to stop asserting and start specifying, and are writing for an intelligent reader who knows the rough outlines of the debate but is not a specialist.

Please produce the document they would sign, with three parts.

Part 1: a numbered list of agreed claims. These must not be vacuous. I expect claims like: that no definition of nation fixed at the outset can settle the argument, because the definition is what is in dispute; that the modern state, mass schooling, print and vernacular standardization do real causal work and nobody denies it; that no nation has the unbroken pedigree of a family, and that some putatively ancient nations are demonstrably recent constructions (name at least two that both sides would concede); that pre-modern communities with names, descent myths and cults existed and were put to new uses after 1800; and that some inventions took while others were rejected or quietly forgotten. Each claim should be stated so that neither side would wince at the wording.

Part 2: a table of residual disagreements. For each row, give the disputed point in wording the modernist would accept for their own position, then in wording the ethno-symbolist would accept for theirs — and flag which rows are substantive and which are merely verbal, and why. If part of this fight is about how much continuity counts as continuity, or about which object the word nation properly names, say so explicitly rather than manufacturing a fake dispute.

Part 3: the cruxes, with tests and predictions registered in advance. I want at least these three.

(a) Continuity, made measurable. Suppose the two sides agree on a notional "continuity ledger": for a given nation, list which pre-modern materials were actually present before, say, 1750 (enduring ethnonym, chronicle or epic tradition, liturgy or sacred language, law code, cult of saints, remembered homeland, administrative border) and which of those were actively mobilized by nineteenth- or twentieth-century nation-builders, with the lag between material and mobilization recorded. Have the two sides agree on how the ledger should be scored, then say what each predicts the ledgers of, say, England, Greece, Armenia and the nineteenth-century Central European cases would show — and what pattern would count, for each side, as a result that hurts their own view.

(b) Failures and forgeries. Some projects of synthetic or civic nation-building came apart — I'm thinking of projects like Ottomanism, Czechoslovakism, Yugoslavism and the Soviet people — while others consolidated. And some of the "ancient" materials were outright forgeries, exposed in the same century that used them, yet did real nationalist work. Have both sides say which of those they concede as genuine failures, which they regard as unfinished rather than failed, and what each predicts about a further such case: whether failure is best explained by the absence of pre-modern materials, or by institutional and economic conditions that had nothing to do with them.

(c) The case I can't stop thinking about, which I'll call Ardania. I'm anonymizing a real place, so please reason about the type and not about whichever country you think it is. Ardania has: an ethnonym for itself recorded in a treaty of the early fifteenth century; a chronicle tradition that stops around 1520; a liturgy in its own language maintained continuously by a small clergy; essentially no secular writing in that language between roughly 1550 and 1780; a peasantry that, in the first trustworthy folklore collections of the 1830s, calls itself by the old name and tells stories about a medieval kingdom; and an independence movement in the 1990s that rebuilt a medieval festival, reprinted the chronicle, and taught the epic in schools. Ask both sides the same question: if a systematic search of village court records, tax registers and parish books from 1600 to 1800 were carried out, would ordinary people, and not only clergy, be found using the old ethnonym and telling the old stories? What does each side predict, and what finding would each count as damaging to its own position? If they predict the same thing, say so and explain why the case doesn't discriminate.

Constraints. Keep the whole document under about fifteen hundred words, and write it as prose and tables, not as notes. I don't want footnotes, citations or further reading — I've read the literature, and what I need from you is the argument. Explain any technical term the first time the document uses it. Where a crux turns on evidence that could not in principle be gathered, say that plainly instead of inventing a test; and where the two sides genuinely differ about what the archive would show, record the difference in a form that a future historian could check.
