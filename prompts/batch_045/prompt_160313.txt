I've got an essay deadline looming on my philosophy of medicine module and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the naturalism vs normativism debate about health and disease. My flatmate is very firmly normativist and keeps saying you can't get an ought out of a blood test, whereas I keep half-defending the naturalist line — that disease is, roughly, statistically subnormal functioning of some part relative to a reference class, and that the value judgements can in principle be kept out of it. But the more I think about the reference class problem the more I think the naturalist is quietly doing all the value work anyway, just out of sight. I'd rather write something that makes that visible than another essay, so I was wondering if you could write me a piece of satire about it.

What I want is a spoof UK government consultation document — the kind of thing a health service puts out when it has just written a strict naturalist, biostatistical definition of disease into its eligibility criteria, and is now consulting on how to implement it. Think deadpan Whitehall mandarin voice: "the Department welcomes responses by 14 November", impact assessments, an Equality Statement, that sort of thing. Around 800 words, with numbered paragraphs and two annexes — Annex A being a schedule of reference classes, and Annex B a note of dissent from the one normativist on the committee. Invent the department, the chair and the dissenter; don't use any real names, and don't bother with citations or further reading, I've got plenty of that.

The jokes have to come from getting the actual view right rather than from a cartoon version of it, so I'd like you to work in a few specific things. The reference class problem should end up being used as a budgeting tool — classes sliced by age, sex, occupation and region until your postcode decides whether your knees count as diseased. Things that statistically normal for a population — dental decay, presbyopia, the ordinary aches of being seventy — should come out as health, with treatment reclassified as enhancement or personal preference and quietly defunded; give me at least one patient example that stops being funny about halfway through. There should be some awkwardness about the asymmetry where only subnormal function counts, so the committee can't work out what to do with rare-but-harmless variants or with above-average function. A nice moment where they notice that on their own definition you can be diseased without being ill at all, and end up diagnosing nearly everybody. Maybe a pharmaceutical lobbyist who has worked out that the way to keep an indication alive is to fund a narrower reference class. And if you can slip in the hybrid "harmful dysfunction" style compromise being floated and then waved off for a rubbish reason, so much the better. Above all, the committee should keep insisting it is making no value judgements while obviously making them.

Keep it fair, though: I'd like anyone who actually works in the philosophy of medicine to read it and think you're sending up the real position rather than a strawman, and the normativist dissent in Annex B should be the strongest version of that objection, not a tantrum. And keep it deadpan — no winking at the reader, no "as philosophers would say", no nudging; it should be perfectly legible what's being ironised just from following the document's own logic. British English throughout, and it's fine if the document itself has a typo or two, that only helps.
