You are a senior research strategist who has spent years allocating philanthropic research budgets across neglected cause areas, and who is equally comfortable with population biology, decision-making under severe uncertainty, and the philosophy of animal minds. Please think carefully before committing to an ordering: start by sketching a longer list of candidate questions and the tensions between them, note which ones are upstream of which, and only then prune to the handful you would actually fund.

The setting. A foundation can spend roughly ten researcher-years over three years on work bearing on the scale argument for insect suffering: the argument that insects may matter enormously in expected terms because an enormous headcount — commonly cited figures put the number alive at a time somewhere between 10^15 and 10^18, overwhelmingly juveniles that die young — multiplies even a modest probability that they are sentient. No laboratory work is possible; the budget buys conceptual analysis, formal modelling, and synthesis of evidence that already exists in the entomological and animal-welfare-science literatures. The terminal goal is not a probability estimate but a decision: within ten years, some body with real leverage — a pesticide regulator, a producer of insect protein, a conservation agency running pollinator-habitat programmes — should have changed what it does because of this work. Rank the open questions or lines of work you would fund, and defend the ranking against the obvious alternatives.

Three complications I want you to engage with rather than set aside. I think they are underexplored, and I would rather you fight with them than restate the standard headcount-times-probability framing:

First, replaceability. Most insects are r-selected, with density-dependent juvenile mortality, so preventing N deaths in a cohort plausibly does not reduce suffering by N units: survivors or the next cohort expand into the vacated niche, and the equilibrial population barely moves. Call the share of an intervention's apparent welfare benefit that survives this offset the compensation coefficient. If it is low, the scale argument has been denominated in the wrong unit, and the welfare-relevant currency may be something like occupied cohort-slots or equilibrium biomass rather than lives or deaths. Ask how much of the ten researcher-years should go to this, and whether the conceptual question — what is the right unit of account? — is upstream of any empirical estimate of the coefficient.

Second, asymmetric valence in small minds. It is coherent to suppose that a creature with a very small neural repertoire has nociceptive, negatively valenced states but little or no capacity for the positive states that, in larger minds, partly offset them. On that supposition an insect life is not a small mixture of good and bad but a net negative, and every intervention flips sign: creating pollinator habitat, or restricting a pesticide on insect-welfare grounds, becomes harmful rather than beneficial. Consider what follows for your ranking — whether the probability of that asymmetry deserves its own line of work, and whether it matters that what hangs on it is the sign of the recommended action rather than merely its size.

Third, decision structure rather than probability structure. With a headcount spanning several orders of magnitude and a probability of sentience spanning at least as many, expected value is dominated by which hypothesis in the tail is true, and it may flip sign depending on the two complications above. In that situation a sharper probability may be worth less than a decision rule that is robust across the hypothesis space — a rule that ranks interventions by whether they help under every hypothesis in a specified set, or that deliberately bounds the payoffs it will let into the calculation. Tell me whether you would spend the budget on narrowing the probability at all, on mapping the hypothesis space, on robustness-first decision rules, or on the political question of what would actually move a regulator — and say why the others lose.

Format. Exactly five numbered items, each one to two sentences: name a line of work, say why it is upstream of the others or why it is tractable with conceptual tools alone, and state what result within three years would move it up or down the list. No introduction, no conclusion, no citations, no named papers or authors, and no reading lists — I want your own ordering and your own reasons.
