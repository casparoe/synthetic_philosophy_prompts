Our union at the hospital voted to authorize a strike last week — I'm a resident, so this is personally awkward — and going through it I've realized my gut verdicts about other people's strikes don't add up to anything. So here they are. Nurses at a private hospital give ten days' notice, keep the ER covered, walk out over staffing ratios: fine. Sanitation workers wildcat with no notice in a heat wave, in breach of a contract their own union signed: not fine. Warehouse workers striking purely to get the company to recognize the union at all: fine. Delivery drivers refusing to cross a picket line at a completely different company, with no grievance against their own boss: fine, but I feel shaky about it. Hotel staff stopping work for two hours at a time, at random, for a month: not fine, feels like getting paid to sabotage. Everyone doing work-to-rule instead — strictly the job description and nothing more, output falls off a cliff: fine, feels almost elegant. Transit workers walking out the morning of the city marathon after rejecting an offer they privately think is decent: not fine, hostage-taking. Engineers striking to force their employer to cancel a defense contract: not fine, and I can't say why that's so different from the picket-line case. Dialysis clinic staff striking in a way that will foreseeably kill patients: obviously not fine.

The only rule I've managed to articulate is that a strike is legitimate when the people eating most of the cost are the ones who can actually give you what you want. But that would greenlight the wildcat if it's mostly the city that suffers, and I said no to that. Is there a principle that gets most of this, what does it force me to drop, and what does it say about (a) me walking out as a resident with the union telling anyone who crosses that they're a scab, which I resent, and (b) three engineers at my brother's startup — the only three people who understand the deploy pipeline — all stopping work the morning of a launch?
