Hi — bit of context first so the questions land right. I'm 71, retired analytical chemist, thirty-odd years of mass specs and calibration curves, so I have spent a large part of my life being told that two numbers are "the same" when they plainly aren't, and that the difference only counts once it's bigger than the error. I've lately been auditing a philosophy reading group at the local university, and I do a small podcast for the group where I talk to one person for about half an hour. Next month's guest is a philosopher I'll call Dr. K, whose book manuscript defends the transitivity of "better than" against spectrum arguments — the ones where you lay out a chain of outcomes from A to Z, each link a tiny change, every neighbouring pair looking "roughly equally good" or "not worth arguing about," while the two ends differ enormously, so that if "at least as good as" transmits along the chain you get a verdict at the end nobody wants.

Her positive view, as I understand it, is that comparisons of betterness are resolution-relative: "A is better than B" is elliptical for something like "A is better than B at resolution r," where the resolution isn't a vague-predicate business but is fixed by what's at stake in the decision you're actually making — how much turns on getting it right. On this picture the spectrum cases equivocate: the pairwise verdicts are all true at the coarse resolutions at which we compare neighbours, the endpoint verdict is true at the fine resolution at which the whole span matters, and transitivity only ever had a grip across comparisons drawn at one and the same resolution. Which is enough, she says, because a chooser facing a decision faces one resolution. She also thinks the same machinery dissolves the chaining argument against incomparability (if two options really were incomparable, a tiny improvement to one shouldn't make them comparable, but enough tiny improvements obviously do).

I want eight or nine questions for the half hour, with a follow-up each keyed to what she'll most likely say back. Concretely:

- One that makes her say who or what fixes the resolution, and presses the worry that if the resolution can float, the account is vacuous — any verdict you like comes out true at some resolution, so "single-resolution transitivity" rules nothing out. (She tends to answer that stakes are objective features of the decision problem, not chosen by the agent; I want the follow-up to that.)
- My own hobbyhorse, which I'd like used rather than tidied up: in the lab, sub-resolution differences still accumulate. If each link in the chain is a real improvement, then the sum of them is a real improvement, and at a fine enough resolution the endpoint verdict is exactly what transitivity predicts. So has she answered the intransitivity argument, or relabelled its premise and changed the subject? She has a stock reply here — "that's just the tolerance premise in disguise" — and I'd like the question built so that reply doesn't settle it, including the case where the objector re-runs the argument with a much finer-grained chain.
- One on exploitation: the money-pump reasoning doesn't obviously respect a single deliberative context, and real salami-slicing cases (each trade individually trivial, the whole sequence ruinous, offered on different days by different people) look like they cross resolutions. Why can't the pump operator just work her over one small trade at a time?
- One on whether the resolution story also dissolves the incomparability chaining argument, and whether it then over-generates — does "incomparable" collapse into "indistinguishable at the resolution in play," and is anything left of the idea that some options are genuinely incomparable?
- One on whether this is just contextualism about a gradable adjective with a normative costume on, and what the cost is if betterness turns out to be indexed to a context: does whether a policy is better, or whether an option is choiceworthy, vary with how much is at stake for whoever is doing the deliberating?
