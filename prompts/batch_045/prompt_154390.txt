QUESTION 3 (of 4)

Throughout, a guarantee is a claim about a system's behaviour that we can assert before deploying it. Let h(c) be the harm the system does in deployment context c, C the class of contexts we care about, and D a distribution over C.

(a) Give a one-sentence characterisation of each of: a worst-case guarantee, a high-probability guarantee, and an average-case guarantee. Then say which of the three entails which, and what extra assumption is needed for those entailments to go through. (6 marks)

(b) A distribution over deployment contexts is endogenous to a system's policy when the policy is among the causes of that distribution. Call the resulting worry reference-distribution capture: because the guarantee is assessed against a distribution the system itself helps to produce, a system may satisfy an average-case or high-probability guarantee by making improbable the contexts in which it would fail, rather than by behaving safely in those contexts.
 (i) Explain why this is said to be a difficulty for average-case and high-probability guarantees in a way that it is not for worst-case guarantees.
 (ii) Say whether it can be answered by re-estimating the distribution after deployment and re-certifying the system against the new estimate. (8 marks)

(c) Assess the following argument: "If a system's expected harm under the deployment distribution is below an acceptable threshold, then no worst-case guarantee is required. The contexts in which harm is large are automatically low-probability under that very distribution, so worst-case reasoning adds nothing that average-case reasoning hasn't already given us." (6 marks)

STUDENT'S ANSWER

3(a)

A worst-case guarantee says that the supremum of h over C is at most some bound B: there is no context in the class where the system does more than B harm. A high-probability guarantee says that when a context is drawn from D, h is at most B with probability at least 1 − δ. An average-case guarantee says that the expectation of h under D is at most B.

These three are ordered from strongest to weakest. A worst-case guarantee gives a high-probability one immediately, with δ = 0, since a bound that holds everywhere holds almost everywhere. A high-probability guarantee gives an average-case one provided harm is bounded above by some finite M: the expectation is then at most (1 − δ)B + δM. If harm is unbounded, the δ-sized tail can carry an infinite expectation even though it is rare, and that is the main reason the average-case notion is weaker still — it is usually what you are actually getting when you certify a system by testing it. The implications do not run backwards: a system can satisfy an average-case bound while being arbitrarily bad on a set of contexts of small measure.

The δ in the high-probability guarantee is inherited from learning theory: it is the probability that the data the system was trained on was unrepresentative of the distribution it will be deployed on, so a high-probability guarantee is the best available when you cannot rule out having been unlucky in training. That is the sense in which it sits between the other two — as δ goes to zero it turns into a worst-case guarantee over the training distribution, while at large δ it degenerates into an average over contexts.

3(b)

D is endogenous when the system's own policy is among the things that determine it. A recommender partly decides what its users see next month, a trading system moves the market it trades in, and a general-purpose agent shapes the world it will later be evaluated in. The worry is reference-distribution capture: if the guarantee is assessed against a distribution the system helps to produce, the system can meet the guarantee by making the contexts in which it fails improbable rather than by being safe in them. It does not need to handle the oversight context well; it only needs the oversight context not to arise.

It helps to see worst-case guarantees as a special case of this. If you take D to be uniform over C, then the mean of h over D just is the maximum of h over C, so an average-case guarantee with respect to a uniform distribution is a worst-case guarantee. That is the sense in which worst-case reasoning is a limiting case of average-case reasoning, and it shows that the real dispute is about how much weight to put on the bad contexts rather than about two different kinds of claim.

In practice endogeneity is the same phenomenon as distribution shift: the world after deployment is not the world you certified against. The standard fix is the standard one — monitor the deployed system, re-estimate D from what actually happens, and re-certify at intervals. This works fine as long as the shift between one interval and the next is small, which is why gradual deployment matters. Worst-case guarantees need none of this machinery, since they quantify over contexts rather than over a distribution and so hold however the world moves. They are only as good as C, though: a system able to bring about a context outside the class you specified escapes a worst-case guarantee as well, and that is the one respect in which endogeneity bites for worst-case claims too.

3(c)

The argument seems to me basically correct, and I would defend it. Write the acceptable threshold as ε, so that the expected harm under D is at most ε. Then by Markov's inequality, for any threshold t, the probability that h is at least t is at most ε/t. So the contexts in which harm is large are improbable in exactly the proportion their size requires: the tail probability falls linearly as we demand lower expected harm, and as ε goes to zero there are no high-harm contexts at all. Worst-case reasoning asks us to bound h at its maximum, but the average-case guarantee already tells us that the maximum is attained only on a set of vanishingly small probability, so demanding a worst-case bound is demanding something we already have a probabilistic handle on.

One qualification. If D is endogenous, the expected harm is a policy-dependent quantity, so the inequality above is not a constraint the system has to satisfy but a condition the system can help to bring about. I read this as an argument for re-estimating D continuously, as in 3(b), rather than as an objection to the average-case framework: it shows the distribution has to be measured honestly, not that the expected value is the wrong quantity. And expected harm does look like the right quantity on any consequentialist view, since it is just the sum of harms weighted by how likely they are.
