I'm on a joint philosophy/CS degree and I go to this very informal Sunday reading group — last week two of the regulars, Nadia and Callum, got stuck into each other about the original-versus-derived-intentionality business and went round in circles for about forty minutes while the rest of us ate crisps. I couldnt tell who was winning and I still can't, so I'd like you to referee.

The background they were arguing over: everyone agrees that the words in a book, or the settings on a thermostat, mean what they mean only because some agent outside them reads them or built them that way — that's the derived case. The fight is about whether there's also the other kind, aboutness that isn't borrowed from anybody, and if there is, whether a machine could ever have it. Nadia was defending the distinction (she'd been reading the Chinese Room argument), Callum was attacking it using Dennett's example of a "two-bitser" — a gadget with only two bits of internal state, basically a glorified thermostat, which you can perfectly well describe as believing the room is too hot. Then it went like this:

(1) Callum: start with the two-bitser and add bits. Two, three, a thousand, a trillion, plus sensors, effectors and a learning rule. There's no point on that slope where you can say "here real aboutness begins" without it sounding arbitrary. So either the two-bitser already has it or the trillion-bit robot doesn't, and the second option is absurd, so the distinction Searle wants doesn't exist.

(2) Nadia: that's just a sorites. Heap and bald are vague too, and there really are heaps and bald people — a fuzzy border doesn't show there's no difference. And complexity was never the issue anyway. The issue is whether the aboutness is borrowed. Every gadget on your slope still gets its meaning from an external interpreter who has the real thing, so the borrowing relation holds all the way up and the derivation has to be grounded somewhere outside the machine.

(3) Callum: then tell me what the grounding consists in. If you say brains have the right causal powers, that's either an empirical claim a machine could in principle satisfy or it's a fancy way of saying "made of the right sort of meat", which isn't philosophy. And your external-interpreter move cuts against you: your own concepts were handed to you by a language community — someone with no language and no community doesn't have beliefs about interest rates or tort law — so if borrowing from others disqualifies a system, it disqualifies you.

(4) Nadia: it disqualifies the system from having derived intentionality, not from having any. My point is just that the regress of interpreters has to stop somewhere, and the only place it can stop is a creature whose states are about things in their own right. I know from the inside that mine are, and no amount of "you can describe anything as if it had beliefs" gets you from as-if to really.

(5) Callum: introspection can't tell you that. It tells you you're having thoughts; it doesn't tell you whether their aboutness is intrinsic or partly inherited. Which matters, because take this case: some engineers build a symbol-manipulating machine with a codebook, then everyone who knew the codebook dies, the codebook burns, and the machine carries on and even retrains itself, so its current symbol-mappings were never stipulated by anybody. If meaning needs a living assigner, the machine now means nothing at all; if meaning depends on what the system actually does, it means something and always did. So your distinction is tracking a fact about the machine's history rather than anything in the machine now — which is a wierd thing for a semantic property to do.

(6) Nadia: not that wierd. Whether a mark is a word, or a scar is a scar, or a noise is a promise, depends on how it got there — semantic properties are often historical. Two machines could be doing exactly the same thing right now, one built by a community that assigned meanings to its symbols and one assembled by blind drift, and it's not crazy to say only the first one means anything.

At which point someone else at the table mumbled that "derived" might mean two different things — causally dependent on somebody's purposes versus only interpretable relative to somebody's take — and then we ran out of crisps and it dissolved.

Can you go through the six moves and say who's right about what at each point, not just who wins overall? In particular: does Nadia's sorites reply actually answer Callum's first move or does it miss his point; is Callum's orphan-machine case a real problem for the distinction or a change of subject; which moves are doing genuine work and which are just rhetoric; and what, if anything, are both of them missing? If those two readings of "derived" really are seperate claims, say so and say who needs which one. Please write in British English, and keep it readable for someone who's only done one phil of mind module.
