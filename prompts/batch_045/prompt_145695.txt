So: what does the package of practices I'm about to describe actually presuppose about responsibility and desert — is there one coherent view underneath it, or is it just a contradiction we've all agreed not to look at?

The setup at my company (about 400 engineers, and I'm pretty sure this is bog standard across tech). Three things, all official:

- Blameless postmortems. Any incident above a certain severity gets a writeup, and the hard rule is that we never name an individual as the cause. We look for contributing conditions instead. People are explicitly told they won't be punished for what they did, so they should be totally candid about it. The justification we say out loud is basically: the person who ran the migration at 4pm was operating inside the system we all built, with the information and incentives they had, and any of us would have done the same thing in their chair.
- Impact-based promotion. The top performance rating is essentially "impact," assessed on shipped outcomes. Concretely, last cycle two engineers on neighbouring teams made more or less the same change — swapping a synchronous dependency call for a queue. One happened to ship two weeks before a marketing push nobody had warned her about, the queue absorbed a 10x traffic spike, and it's now in a VP's slide deck as having "saved the launch." The other shipped into a service that got deprioritized a month later, so his change is sitting there doing nothing anyone can measure. As far as I can tell, same skill, same care, same review process. She got promoted; he got "meets expectations," and his manager told him privately that the work was good but "didn't land."
- A no-heroes norm, which is the one that really confuses me. Incident review actively discourages praising whoever fixed the outage at 3am. We're told not to celebrate that person, on the grounds that being the one who got to be the hero is mostly a matter of who was on call that week and which service happened to break — and that rewarding it incentivizes people to keep systems fragile. So we refuse to hand out credit for a good outcome when the *opportunity* to produce it was luck, while at the same time we hand out promotions based on outcomes.

Here's what I can't work out. The postmortem policy sounds like the thing I was reading about, the control principle-ish idea that you can't be blameworthy for what wasn't up to you — but a friend of mine says that's a misreading, and that blameless postmortems are just epistemic humility (in a system that complicated we can't reliably trace outcomes back to individual choices, so we decline to punish even if the person genuinely deserves it) and have nothing to do with luck as such. And she says the same move might save the promotion system too: maybe we grade on outcomes not because outcomes are deserved but because grading on anything else is unmeasurable, or because we're setting incentives rather than handing out desert. I sort of think she's right about the second one but not the first, but I genuinely can't tell, and I also can't tell whether the no-heroes rule is the deepest clue here or a red herring — it seems to care about luck one step earlier than the promotion system does, and I don't know if that makes it consistent with the postmortem policy or consistent with nothing.
