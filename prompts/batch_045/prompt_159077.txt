I teach a first-year seminar (~24 students, mostly non-majors, one 75-minute slot on a Thursday afternoon when they're half asleep) on truth, media and polarization, and I want one whole session built around the thing I couldn't explain to my roommate last week: he said an echo chamber is just an epistemic bubble with wifi, and I could tell he was wrong but I fumbled the explanation. The distinction I have in mind is roughly the one from C. Thi Nguyen's work — in a bubble you just never hear the other side, so exposure fixes it, while in an echo chamber you *have* heard the other side and the group has pre-loaded you with reasons to discount anyone who says it, so exposure makes things worse. But I also want a third mechanism in the mix that I don't think has a standard name, and I've started calling it evidential laundering: the group doesn't deny the outsider's fact and doesn't even attack the source, it *admits* the fact and then redescribes it in the group's own vocabulary until it stops counting as evidence against them and starts counting as confirmation of them (the report says the water is contaminated — sure, and isn't it obvious that they'd plant exactly that story to distract from what's really going on). That one seems to me to be the hardest to break from outside, since neither adding evidence nor rehabilitating the source's credibility does anything.

So what I need is a runnable activity for the full 75 minutes with all 24 doing something, not a lecture with a discussion tacked on — no slide with the definitions, and nobody should hear the words bubble, echo chamber or laundering until the last 15 minutes. My constraints: the subject matter has to be invented and not political (last year I tried it with a real controversy and we lost 25 minutes to students arguing about the actual issue), and it needs a determinate truth that I know and they don't, revealed at the end from a sealed envelope, so the class can *see* that one group ended up wrong in a way another group didn't. I have index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard, one projector, and a coffee-shop voucher I'm willing to hand out — no cash and nothing that needs phones. Give me: the setup and materials; a small-stakes payoff scheme (quiz points or the voucher) where going along with the group's discrediting move is individually rational and collectively costly; roles or rotations so the room splits into conditions without anyone feeling singled out (three simultaneous groups of eight, or one staged case the whole class moves through — tell me which you'd pick and why); and minute-by-minute timings. If there's a role for observers or a spectator row, I like that, since I can't watch three groups at once.

Then the debrief, which matters more than the game: map specific moments that will actually happen in the room onto the three mechanisms, including what I should say when a student in the bubble condition updates instantly and a student in the echo-chamber condition digs in harder the more evidence I hand them. I also need an honest answer on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about the sharp student who clocks the setup in the first five minutes and meta-games it, the one who refuses to play along because it feels like I'm manipulating them (one student said something like that last year and I handled it badly), and the irony that I, standing at the front, am structurally the outside authority the activity teaches them to distrust, so my own credibility is a variable in the game. And be frank about what the exercise can't reproduce: real echo chambers are built up over years, they're tied to identity and to real costs for leaving, and none of that fits in a classroom, plus algorithmic curation isn't something I can stage — so tell me which parts of the idea I'll just have to state out loud, and give me one or two diagnostic questions students could take away for telling the three mechanisms apart from the outside.
