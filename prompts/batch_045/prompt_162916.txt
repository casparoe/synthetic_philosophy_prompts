hey — I keep catching myself having opinions about killing vs letting die that I can't fit together, and I want to see whether my gut on a list of cases actually adds up to anything. Bit of context: I was a volunteer firefighter for years, which is where a couple of these come from (loosely), and I'm not asking what to do on a call — I want the principle underneath, if there is one.

Here's what I think, going case by case.

1. Runaway trolley, five workers on the track, I can flip a switch and send it onto a siding where one guy is working. Flipping: fine.
2. Same setup, I don't flip, the five die. Also fine. Sad, but I don't think I was required to, and I don't think anyone can blame me.
3. No siding this time. The only way to stop it is to shove a heavy guy off the footbridge onto the track; his body stops it, he dies. Not fine. I've never managed to argue myself out of this one.
4. Same as 3 except I don't touch him — there's a trapdoor under the platform he's standing on and I press a button. Not fine either. So it isn't about bodily contact or pushing with my own hands.
5. Divert onto the siding, but the trolley doesn't hit the worker: it clips a support, a scaffold collapses, and he dies of that about a minute later. I think this is fine, same as 1. Length of the causal chain does nothing for me.
6. I've been pumping water out of a flooded basement where a guy is trapped; ten more minutes and he's out. I get a call that five people need the pump somewhere else and moving it saves them. I stop pumping and leave; he drowns. Not fine — and here's the part I can't square: it feels like killing to me, even though everything I did after the call was not-doing. Compare with 2, where not-doing was fine.
7. Terminal patient, in pain, asks me to stop. I unplug the ventilator. Fine. I don't lose sleep over it.
8. Same patient, same request, I give a lethal injection instead. Not fine. I know these two are supposed to be equivalent in every respect that matters, and I still react differently, so maybe one has to go.
9. Trolley heading for five; the guy on the siding has already been crushed by a fallen girder and will die in two minutes whatever I do. Diverting: fine, and much easier than 1 — he isn't losing anything he had.
10. Numbers. Two on the track, one on the siding: fine. One on the track, one on the siding: not fine — well, "pointless" — but I think I'd also say it's wrong, not just a waste of effort. I can't tell you where between two and one it flips, or whether there even is a line rather than a blur.
11. The one I go back and forth on: trolley heading for five innocents, and the guy on the siding is the guy who accidentally set the trolley rolling in the first place — no malice, pure mistake. Diverting onto him: sometimes I think yes, easily, he caused the mess; sometimes I think that's just punishment dressed up as rescue.

The rule I've been trying to work with, such as it is: you may steer an already-existing lethal threat, including onto other people, but you may not create a new threat or turn someone into equipment, and you can't withdraw a rescue you've already started. That seems to get 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6. But it's silent on 7 versus 8, silent on 11, and it has no opinion at all on 10, which bothers me because 10 is where I'd actually want help.

So: can that be repaired into something that covers most of the list, or is there a better single principle? Please state it precisely enough that I can run it on a case I make up later. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save and what it costs to save them — I'd rather have something slightly ugly that gets nine than something clean that gets six. Also give me two or three cases it decides that aren't on my list, ideally ones where I'd squirm: something where a machine does it for me, something where the only way to save the five is to kill one of the five, that sort of thing. And say plainly whether the ventilator/injection pair can be respected at all or whether one of those two reactions is just noise I should drop.

Oh, and don't give me a reading list or name a pile of papers — I want the principle, not a syllabus.
