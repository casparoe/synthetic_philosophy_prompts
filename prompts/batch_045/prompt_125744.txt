I have been working through the selection-based account of mental content and I have built an argument whose conclusion I do not accept. I would like to know which step to give up, and what giving that step up costs me.

The target is the formulation I was given. A state S of a system represents that F just in case S has the proper function of bringing about some effect E in a consumer system, where E's being beneficial — fitness-enhancing, or need-satisfying for that consumer — is explained by F's obtaining when S occurs. Proper function is fixed by selection history. Misrepresentation is then explained as S's occurring when its proper function is not performed, whether through malfunction or through abnormal conditions.

The argument:

(1) The explanatory relation in this formulation is a causal-explanatory one. Nothing in it requires F to be a natural, projectible, law-involving, observable, or cognitively accessible property. It requires only that the obtaining of F be that in virtue of which E is beneficial.

(2) So, for a given S, the candidate contents are all and only those conditions whose obtaining causally explains the beneficialness of E on the occasions on which S performs its function.

(3) Consider a forager in an environment containing exactly two nutritious items, A-items and B-items. They are visually and chemically dissimilar, equally nutritious, and across the forager's entire selective history they have always co-occurred in the same patches in a stable ratio, so no selection pressure has ever distinguished tracking A from tracking B. The forager has a single detector state D, caused about equally often by A-items and by B-items, which causes approach-and-consume; the response is equally beneficial whichever item is present.

(4) In that case the conditions 'an A-item is present', 'a B-item is present', 'an A-item or a B-item is present', and 'a nutritious item is present' each satisfy (2): on any occasion on which D does its job, the beneficialness of approach is explained by each of them. So all four are candidates, and the formulation singles out none.

(5) The standard tie-breakers do not help. Taking the most specific candidate leaves 'A' and 'B', neither of which is correct for every tokening, with nothing to choose between them. Appealing to what the state would indicate under Normal conditions returns the disjunction or the thicker 'nutritious item', since Normal conditions contain both items together. Appealing to what the consumer needs returns nutrition, which either disjunct serves. Call this the symmetry lemma: where candidate contents are perfectly correlated across the selective history and across Normal conditions, the account has no selectional or modal ground for preferring one.

(6) But the construction in (3) is not confined to that case. Take my visual state as of a cup on the desk, and let C be 'a cup is on the desk' and C* be 'a cup is on the desk, or (a saucer is on the desk and the room's oxygen is below nineteen per cent)'. The second disjunct of C* never arises in any condition the system can actually encounter, so the two candidates are correlated across the actual history and across Normal conditions. Whenever my state tokens because of a cup, C* obtains, and the beneficialness of reaching is explained by C* obtaining exactly as much as by C obtaining. By (2), C* is a candidate content alongside C.

(7) So by the symmetry lemma the account has no ground for preferring C to C* — and the construction is general: for any ascription of content whatever, a symmetric rival can be built by conjoining a second disjunct whose trigger lies outside the space of reachable conditions. If the account withholds a verdict in the forager case, it must withhold one everywhere, including for the states in which the account itself is formulated and for the beliefs I take myself to have about the desk.

(8) The only resource I can see for restoring determinacy is to require that the content be an eligible or natural property, or a property the device is a detector for, or one fixed by the mechanism's transduction rather than by its downstream effects. But each of those looks like an addition to the causal-explanatory relation in (1), and I cannot tell whether the addition is still a selectional notion, whether it makes content depend on facts about properties rather than facts about organisms, or whether it quietly presupposes the aboutness it was meant to explain.

I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, and I am happy to accept that the forager's state is indeterminate, or that it is really two state types rather than one, if that is where the argument pushes. What I cannot accept is (7)'s global conclusion, or the claim that the theory manufactures determinacy it has no right to in the ordinary case.

Which step do you reject? I want three things: why the step looked sound when I wrote it down; what the account has to give up in order to reject it; and how the resulting position handles the forager and the cup-and-saucer case differently, if it does.
