I am an autodidact with no academic background — I work in logistics — and for the past two years I have been reading on my own about Maxwell's demon and about the claim, which I have seen called Landauer's principle, that the cost of a demon's sorting is not in the measuring but in the wiping of its notes. I run a monthly "physics for curious adults" evening at my public library. I would like you to design an activity for it.

The setting: a library meeting room, folding tables, 14 adults (ages roughly 18 to 70, mixed backgrounds, none of them physicists, a few of them hostile to algebra), one 90-minute session, a budget of about fifty dollars in craft and hardware-store materials, no sinks and no mains power at the tables. Assume nothing beyond counting and, if it helps, the observation that halving the number of possibilities is "one bit" — which I would want the activity to teach rather than presuppose, by working a tiny case (two, then four, then eight objects) where the group can literally list the arrangements and count them.

What I want the participants to run into, in this order and without being told any of it in advance: that sorting objects by a property you cannot see requires you to *record* something about each object; that any record-keeping device you can actually carry around fills up; that clearing it is a real, countable, physical transaction and not a bookkeeping formality; and that the ledger, kept honestly in a single unit, never shows a profit. I also want them to try, and to break their own teeth on, the obvious loophole: a passive one-way device — a flap or trapdoor that lets the "fast" ones through and not the "slow" ones — which needs no notes at all. And I want a round where the sorted arrangement is allowed to drift back toward mixed, so that participants feel that holding onto order is a continuing expense rather than a one-time payment.

So please design:

1. The physical apparatus, from cheap materials. My own sketch, which you should improve or replace: a shallow tray divided by a partition with a shutter; two dozen identical balls whose "speed" is a hidden property (a colour, or a weight, that the sorters cannot perceive directly and must determine by some allowed procedure); a curtain or screen so the sorters work blind and can act only on what is written down; a "tally board" with a strictly limited number of slots; an "erase station" placed deliberately far from the table, where a slot can only be cleared by doing something physically effortful and countable (cranking a stiff spring-loaded lever, carrying a tile across the room, pumping a hand pump) which drops a token into a jar I call the ash bin; and a flap with an adjustable rubber band for the trapdoor round. Tell me exactly how many slots, how many balls, and what the effort should be, and why those numbers.

2. Roles, with rotation. At minimum: two blind sorters who work only from the tally board; an auditor who polices cheating (including the cheating I expect most: a sorter using their own memory instead of the board, or clearing a slot with their sleeve instead of walking to the erase station); and a ledger-keeper who computes the accounting in bits. Give each role a rule card written as instructions, not as theory.

3. The accounting. I want the group to convert the order they produced into bits (roughly: how many arrangements consistent with what they knew before, divided by after) and compare it against the number of slots they cleared. Give me the worked tiny cases, give me the formula for the big case in plain arithmetic, and tell me how to handle the fact that real groups will produce partial, messy order rather than perfect sorting.

4. A round structure and a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, including setup and teardown, and marking which rounds are droppable if we run long. Please include: a warm-up round where they sort with the curtain open and feel that it is trivially easy; the blind-no-notes round; the tally-board round; the trapdoor round; a "lazy demon" round in which they may deliberately record less (a coarse threshold, or every other ball) and pay less, so they have to hunt for the best trade-off rather than maximize either quantity alone; and an "archive demon" round, in which they are given unlimited fresh tape and told they may never erase anything, only copy notes into a permanent archive and draw new tape.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific claims: which action was the measurement, which was the recording, which was the erasure, where the ash came from, why the open-curtain round felt free, and why it wasn't. Include the point I find most interesting and most often skipped — that a sorter who cheats by remembering things in their head has not escaped the cost, they have merely moved it into their own metabolism, and that this is paid for by their breakfast.

6. Handling of my own candidate loophole, honestly. Here it is: it seems to me that the entire argument depends on the demon having a finite memory that must be reused. But suppose it never erases: it writes its notes, copies nothing, discards nothing, and simply draws a fresh, blank slate from an endless supply, leaving a permanent archive behind it. Erasure never happens, so the cost never comes due. I suspect the answer involves the archive being a growing physical object and the "endless" supply not being endless, but I want the activity to let a participant make this move and discover for themselves where the bill reappears — including, if it is true, the awkward part: that the cost can be *deferred indefinitely* and the second law still holds at every finite stage.

7. An honest list of what will predictably go wrong: the cheating attempts, the trapdoor round stalling because the apparatus doesn't fail in the right way, someone tying the rubber band so tight nothing passes at all, the erase station becoming a bottleneck that people route around, a participant who insists the whole thing is circular because I rigged the rules, and a participant who asks why a spring-loaded lever has anything to do with heat.

8. Finally, be blunt about what this activity cannot show and what I will have to state in my own words at the end. I assume I cannot reproduce: that the per-bit cost has a specific value fixed by temperature; that the relevant notion of information is the thermodynamic one and not some intuitive notion of "useful knowledge"; that with ~10^23 objects the second law is statistical rather than enforced by my auditor; and that the argument does not establish that *measurement* is free, only that it need not be. Tell me what else is missing, and tell me honestly whether the walk-to-the-erase-station is a genuine model of dissipation or merely a metaphor that happens to be pedagogically useful.
