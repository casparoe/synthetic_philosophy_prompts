I'm in the lower sixth, and there's an incident from last term that two people in my year have been arguing about ever since. They've both asked me to referee it and I can't, because I can't tell which of them is using the concepts properly. I want to write the thing up for our school philosophy magazine, so I need to know who is right about what, not just who wins.

The incident: a girl in my year, Amara, told our head of year that a visiting sports coach had made a racist remark to her during a training session. The head of year said he accepted she was upset, but he did not open a formal complaint. He was overheard telling a colleague that eleven of the last fourteen complaints made by pupils against visiting staff had gone unsubstantiated, and that he had to be careful not to overreact to a single account.

Here is the argument, compressed but faithful to the moves they actually made.

(1) Callum: This is straightforwardly a testimonial injustice. Amara was given less credibility than she was owed because of what she is — a pupil, and a Black girl — and the distinctive wrong here isn't the bad outcome or the hurt feelings, it is being downgraded as a knower, as someone who can tell other people how things are. That wrong is done whether or not the hearer meant anything by it and whether or not anything bad follows.

(2) Nadia: On the standard account, the credibility deficit has to be caused by identity prejudice. The head of year wasn't prejudiced; he was using a base rate — a real log of fourteen cases, most of them unsubstantiated. Reasoning from evidence about a group isn't prejudice just because the group happens to be picked out by identity. So this is at most a harsh outcome, and maybe evidence that the school should investigate complaints better, but it isn't the distinctive wrong Callum is describing.

(3) Callum: The log is contaminated. Those earlier complaints went unsubstantiated partly because they weren't properly investigated: pupils' witnesses were other pupils, whose word was discounted in turn, and nobody pulled the session records or the staff rota. So a low substantiation rate was manufactured by the very habit of discounting pupils, and now the rate is used to justify more discounting. Call it a laundered credibility deficit — locally rational, genealogically tainted. The hearer reasons impeccably; the deficit he applies is inherited prejudice with the prejudice filtered out of it. Still an injustice, and Amara pays for a record she didn't write.

(4) Nadia: Two replies. First: if the log is tainted, the people who tainted it are earlier staff and the school's own procedures. The head of year inherits a mess he didn't make. An injustice needs someone who wrongs someone, and here the most you have is a structural defect, which is a different charge and arguably not one you can press against him. Second, and more important: what happened to Amara was practical — she wasn't protected and the coach wasn't challenged. That is ordinary institutional unfairness. If we start calling that epistemic injustice, the term stops picking out anything special. Keep the concept for deficits caused by prejudice in the hearer.

(5) Callum: You keep assuming the wrong has to be caused by a prejudiced individual, and that's precisely what's in dispute. Look at what Amara now has to do in order to be believed: find adult witnesses, produce a written timeline, manage her tone, escalate past the head of year to someone else. Call it a credibility tax — extra epistemic labour she owes purely because of which group she files into. That is a wrong to her in her capacity as a knower, and it exists even if every individual hearer reasons flawlessly from the evidence in front of him.

(6) Nadia: The credibility tax is the best thing you've said, but it proves too much. Plenty of people owe extra epistemic labour — a research student has to cite sources, a specialist has to spell out her reasoning, anyone with a contested claim has to document it — and we don't call those injustices. What makes Amara's case different, if it is different, is that the tax tracks her identity. And the moment you build identity-tracking into the wrong, you are back to needing prejudice rather than mere base rates, unless you want to say that any identity-correlated credibility cost is an injustice, which would make ordinary statistical reasoning unjust. And for what it's worth I think the real failure here may not be testimonial at all: the school has no settled vocabulary for what happened to her, since the only categories on offer are banter, misunderstanding and overreaction. That is a failure of shared interpretive resources, which is the hermeneutical kind of case, not the testimonial one.

(7) Callum: That last move is wrong and it matters. Amara did have the concept: she called the remark racist, and she was understood perfectly well. The deficit arrived after she spoke, not before, so this isn't a hermeneutical case at all, and the testimonial category has to be able to stretch to laundered deficits. I'll grant you this much: if it stretches that far, it's a milder species of the wrong than the plain prejudiced case, and some of what we want to say belongs to the school as an institution rather than to the man in the office.

Please adjudicate this move by move. I am less interested in who wins than in which specific claims survive.

Give me the core of your answer as a markdown table, one row per move, with exactly these columns:

- Move — the number, (1) to (7)
- Speaker — Callum or Nadia
- Claim — the single central claim of the move, in one sentence of your own
- Verdict — one of exactly these five strings: clearly right, mostly right, half right, mostly wrong, clearly mistaken
- Why — two to three sentences justifying that verdict, addressed to me rather than to the specialists
- Stated or assumed — the one premise, stated or unstated, that the move depends on most, and whether you think that premise is true

Rules for the verdicts: judge the claim as made, not the best version of it; if a move rests on a factual or conceptual claim you think is false, say so in the Why column rather than quietly smoothing it over; and do not let the later moves retroactively repair the earlier ones.

After the table, add three short paragraphs of prose:

- Overall — who is more right overall, and what exactly they are more right about.
- What both are missing — the one distinction or question neither of them raised that you think decides more of the argument than anything they did raise.
- The hinge — which single feature of the case is doing the most work, and what happens to the verdict in (1) and in (5) if you remove it.

Please write in British English, and please don't give me a reading list, citations or bibliographies — I have no way of looking any of that up, and I only want the argument.
