I design discussion-based simulations for museums and upper-secondary classrooms. In the current one, a small group is placed in a premodern agrarian society in the years before a famine and a rebellion, and asked to apportion responsibility across human decisions (tax levels, grain reserves, a recent war, poor relief) and non-human ones (a novel pathogen, three failed harvests in a row, and a large tropical eruption whose aerosol veil cools the hemisphere for two growing seasons). I want the non-human causes treated as genuine participants rather than as scenery, but I can see two ways to botch it: the session becomes a determinism machine in which the weather decides and human choices are decoration, or the non-human causes get filed under background conditions and everyone argues only about the minister.

To steer between these I have been using two homemade devices. The trigger/terrain distinction: a non-human cause is a trigger where it sets the timing of an outcome whose shape was already fixed by human arrangements, and terrain where it also changes which outcomes were available to the people involved — what they could perceive, afford, or legitimately choose — and only the second, I have been claiming, earns the word actor. The substitution test: a cause is doing real historical work only if swapping in a nearby alternative (an ordinary harvest instead of a failed one, a milder strain) changes the kind of outcome rather than merely its date or its death toll, and I apply it symmetrically by swapping out human decisions as well.
