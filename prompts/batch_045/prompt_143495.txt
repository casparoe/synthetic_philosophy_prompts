I have been reading Orwell's essay on English prose alongside the novel, and I keep getting stuck on what looks like a flat contradiction between two things he seems to believe, so I want the conceptual issue isolated rather than the scholarship on it.

Call the first the paraphrase thesis. In the essay, the remedy for a bad passage is to translate it into plain, concrete English; the exercise presupposes that any genuinely held thought can be restated without the dying metaphors, the prefabricated phrases, and the abstract nouns, and that if the restatement yields nothing, the original contained no thought but only a verbal reflex. The second is the amputation thesis, which is Syme's case for Newspeak in the novel and the subject of the appendix on the language: shrink the vocabulary and you shrink the range of thought, so that certain attitudes become unavailable and not merely unspeakable — his illustration being a word for freedom retained only in the sense in which a dog is free of lice. The third is the apologetics thesis: political writing, on his account, is largely the defence of the indefensible, and must therefore consist of euphemism and vagueness, which is a matter of deliberately hiding something one has in full view.

The paraphrase thesis looks anti-Whorfian and the amputation thesis looks Whorfian, and the apologetics thesis seems to presuppose exactly the lucid, fully formed thought whose absence the diagnosis of prefabricated prose asserts. What I can't get clear on is how much of this is a real inconsistency rather than three claims operating at different levels, and which framework would let me tell the difference: the distinction between what cannot be said and what cannot be thought, the distinction between a logical impossibility and a cost in cognitive effort or in social coordination, and the distinction between the individual speaker's occurrent thought and what a whole speech community can keep available across generations.

Please do three things. First, explain what the underlying issue is — what exactly has to be true of the relation between thought and vocabulary for the amputation thesis to be more than a metaphor, and whether the paraphrase thesis rules that out or leaves it untouched. Second, say whether the distinction between being unable to think something, being unable to say it, and being unable to say it without penalty is enough to reconcile the two, or whether it merely relocates the contradiction. Third, apply the result: does Newspeak do any work in the novel that doublethink and the control of the past do not already do, or is it redundant machinery; and if a technical term such as entropy resists lossless translation into plain English, is that a counterexample to the paraphrase thesis or merely a case the thesis was never about? Flag any place where the disagreement is only verbal.
