hey — I keep going in circles on this and I want you to tell me which of my reactions has to go.

Setup, in case the thing I'm describing isn't standard under the name I know it by. The scheme: a hard question goes to a person who is allowed to break it into subquestions and put each one to a fresh copy of the same arrangement; that person may break those up too; and at some depth the buck stops with someone who just answers. Call that HCH-ish. I don't care about it as a proposal for anything practical; I'm interested in it as a picture of what good deliberation *is* — the idealized tree's answer is supposed to be what you'd conclude if you thought about the matter properly and at length, and I want to know exactly when that identification fails.

So here are my verdicts on when breaking a question up preserves the judgment and when it wrecks it.

1. Should I take this job offer. Splitting into: is the pay above market for this role and city, how bad is the commute in practice, what's the realistic path to promotion, how much do I like the people, what's the chance the company folds in eighteen months. The tree's answer I trust more than my gut. Good.

2. Is this poem any good. Imagery, meter, whether the theme hangs together, originality. The tree comes back smooth and confident and I don't believe it; one careful read is better. Bad.

3. Did the defendant's conduct cause the harm. Factual timeline, but-for, foreseeability, intervening cause. Tree wins, and each subquestion is itself splittable. Good.

4. Is this joke funny. The sub-answers (is the setup economical, is the turn a surprise, does it land twice) are each fine and the merged verdict is worthless. Bad, and worse than the poem case somehow, even though a joke is smaller.

5. Hiring, forty applicants to one. Scoring everyone on independent criteria is clearly better than me reading files; cutting to six that way is great. Then picking among the six by merging scores is worse than one person reading six files and choosing. Same question, good at one stage and bad at another. This is the one I keep coming back to.

6. Three-hundred-page contract, is the termination clause unusual for this deal type. Per-section digests, then the question about the clause. Works great, beats reading it cover to cover. Good.

7. Which of two research directions should the lab commit to. One node argues for A, one for B, parent reads both and decides. Very good — and specifically better when the parent reads both arguments rather than a summary of the debate, which is its own little puzzle.

8. Is this proof correct. Lemma by lemma. Good.

9. What to do about my father's care. The factual bits split beautifully (prognosis, costs, what he's said he wants, what the family can actually cover), but the merge comes out confident and slightly hollow and I trust it less than my own unsettled judgment. Fine at the leaves, bad at the merge.

10. Is this startup a good investment. Every sub-node produces a number, the parent combines, and the result is far more confident than it has any right to be, because the sub-nodes were all quietly assuming the same thing about the market. Bad — but bad in a different way from the poem: the poem case loses something that was there, this one manufactures something that wasn't.

11. Diagnosing a patient. The subquestion about the symptoms can't be answered well without the answer to the subquestion about the history, so sealing the nodes from each other is clearly wrong here. But in the contract case I want the section-readers sealed so they don't gossip and drift. Sometimes siblings should see each other's answers, sometimes they shouldn't, and I can't say what the difference is.

The principle I've been fumbling toward has two clauses: (a) a subquestion is safe when it can be answered well without already knowing the answer to the parent — I've been calling the bad ones leaky, and (b) the way the sub-answers combine has to be something you could state and hand to a node of its own, so the merge is itself a question the tree can ask. Tests I want run: does that actually separate the poem from the job offer, or is clause (a) just restating my reaction in fancier words? And here's the case that makes me think (a) is wrong or at least incomplete: evaluating a chess position. Material, king safety, pawn structure, piece activity — every one of those is leaky in my sense, you can't say whether the king is unsafe without already knowing whether the position is winning, and yet decomposing a position that way plus a search gives strictly better judgments than holistic human assessment. So either chess isn't a counterexample because the combination there is a search rather than a merge, or my clause is doing no work.

What I'd like: state the principle, or the two or three candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that forces each clause. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed — grading a stack of philosophy essays by splitting into states-a-thesis, answers-the-objection, argument-is-valid; deciding whether an unproved mathematical conjecture is probably true; choosing a name for a baby; judging whether a politician's apology was sincere. Say too whether the business about the leaves — that the bottom of the tree is somebody answering a small question fast, with no context, which seems to bias the whole thing toward whatever survives being said in a short answer — belongs inside the principle or is a seperate defect bolted onto it. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and would have me overturn.
