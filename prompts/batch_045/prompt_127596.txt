Slightly self-interested one: I've been pushing a relational reading of personhood in the Ubuntu literature — not just the slogan that a person is a person through other persons, but the stronger claim that personhood is a status constituted by being held in the right kind of relation, so its not something you could have all on your own. My own bit, which is what I actually care about: the relation doing the work isn't recognition or mattering-to-a-community, it's addressability — being someone who can be addressed as a you and who could in principle answer. Call it the address test. It handles the ancestors better than the recognition version does (you pour libation to them, you talk to them), and it's meant to dodge the slaveholder case without just stipulating that the slaveholder is wrong.

So what would count against it? Not the greatest hits I've already got referee reports on. I want the cases the test misdescribes: someone who can be addressed but can't answer (late-stage dementia, an anencephalic infant), the hermit nobody has ever spoken to, the last human alive, and the reverse case — someone who addresses and is never answered back, which on my test looks like they aren't a person until somebody replies. Which of those would a committed relationalist have to concede, and which are just changing the subject? I'd personally be most moved by a case where someone is a person beyond doubt and nothing could in principle address them. I'm also worried the test is doing less work than I think: say addressability just tracks a capacity and I'm a capacities theorist in relational drag; say it tracks worthiness of being addressed and I'm some kind of second-personal Kantian. Is there a version where the relation is genuinely constitutive and yet a community's getting it wrong is always and only the community's fault? Rank the defeaters, fatal down to merely awkward, and be straight with me if the whole view has been qualified into something nothing could touch — that's what my co-author keeps muttering.
