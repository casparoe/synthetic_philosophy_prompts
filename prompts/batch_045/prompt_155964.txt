I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I use the wrong vocab. I'm about 200 pages into a book about a paid home health aide — eleven years with the same client, knows her better than anyone in the family does, and has zero legal standing when the family shows up and starts making decisions. I keep circling the Terri Schiavo case as the thing my character's situation rhymes with, and I want you to run it through care ethics properly, because everything I've read about it online is either rights talk or cheap hand-wringing.

Here are the facts I'm working from, so you don't have to rummage: cardiac arrest in Feb 1990, oxygen deprivation, diagnosed a couple of months later as being in a persistent vegetative state; husband and legal guardian Michael Schiavo petitioned in 1998 to remove the feeding tube, saying she wouldn't have wanted to be kept going like that; her parents Robert and Mary Schindler fought him for seven years, disputed the diagnosis, wanted artificial nutrition and hydration continued; the tube came out and went back in in 2001, came out for good on March 18 2005, she died March 31; along the way the Florida legislature and Gov. Jeb Bush, then Congress and President Bush, got involved, there were a pile of appeals and the Supreme Court kept declining to hear it, and pro-life, right-to-die and disability-rights groups all made it their thing. Take all that as given.

What I actually want:

First, the mapping. Who is the one caring and who is the cared-for at each stage, and how does that assignment shift? What does care ethics say each party was responding to — not their stated principles, the actual relational facts on the ground? And what does the autonomy-and-substituted-judgment frame the courts were using miss or misdescribe, in care-ethics terms?

Second, I need you to separate three things that get lumped together under the name: (a) morality as fundamentally about sustaining and repairing relations of dependency, (b) the claim that attention to particulars is prior to principles in moral judgment, (c) the claim that the self is relational, so the individual agent in the autonomy frame is the wrong unit of concern. Which of those three does this case actually put pressure on, and how does each one would describe the Schindlers versus Michael?

Third, the bit nobody writes about: on some versions of care ethics a caring relation isn't complete until it's received or acknowledged by the cared-for. My character's analogue is never going to respond. Ever. So what happens to that requirement here — does the theory quietly smuggle in a substitute, does it break, or does it turn out never to have needed reciprocity? Say plainly if this is a case the literature just never anticipated.

Fourth, the thing that matters most for my book: two genuine, long-standing, loving care relations, husband on one side and parents on the other, pulling opposite ways. Say honestly whether care ethics has any way to adjudicate that, or whether it only has a vocabulary for describing the collision. Then run my aide as a probe — eleven years, daily, unpaid in the currency that counts, no legal relation at all. Does care ethics give her anything the law withheld, and if it does, does that show the theory needs some account of which relations count, or is it fine with counting all of them?

Please don't give me reading lists or citations — I can't check them anyway, and I don't want them. Just argue. Where the theory has nothing useful to say, say so instead of forcing it to speak. End with one last line, on its own: Verdict: <one of: confirmation | anomaly | case-the-theory-never-anticipated> — <six words max>.
