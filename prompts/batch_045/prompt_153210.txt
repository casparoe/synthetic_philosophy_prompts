I'm in a political theory course this term and we had exactly one week on Marx — one lecture on surplus value, one on the early manuscripts — and afterwards two people in my study group got into an argument over burritos that I could not referee. Marta said exploitation is the part of the critique that actually does work: workers produce more value than they're paid, the difference goes to whoever owns the means of production, and that gives you a precise structural claim plus somebody to blame. Alienation, she said, is the fuzzy psychological leftover from the unpublished 1844 notebooks, and the mature Marx largely drops the vocabulary even if the mood survives in places like the chapter on commodity fetishism. Dev said that's backwards on both counts: the word count thins out but the structure is all over Capital, and more importantly exploitation only looks like the deeper notion because we quietly smuggle in what it does to people. To make it vivid he offered two cases. First, a master furniture restorer employed by the owner of a small workshop: he has his own bench, picks his own jobs, works at his own pace, signs the finished pieces, would honestly do it for less, and is still paid less than the value he adds — exploited on the standard definition, but alienated from what, exactly? Second, a worker-owned call centre: no outside owner, the members elect the managers and split the surplus, but the work is scripted to the second, monitored, endlessly repeated, and the members describe it as something that happens to them rather than something they do — alienation, he said, with no exploiter in sight, and still exactly the sort of thing Marx is complaining about.

Marta's replies seemed strong to me. On the restorer: a structural claim isn't refuted by a contented worker — he can still be dismissed, the shop can be sold, the surplus he makes isn't his, and his enjoyment is a fact about one job rather than about the relation. On the call centre: a co-op inside a capitalist market doesn't abolish exploitation so much as internalise it, since the members have to drive themselves as hard as any boss would in order to stay solvent, and she said Marx himself basically expects co-ops to end up reproducing the defects of the system they sit inside. And even granting all that, she said, tedious work is bad isn't a Marxist claim — everybody thinks tedious work is bad — whereas the wage form hiding appropriation behind what looks like an equal exchange is Marx's actual discovery. Dev came back with three things: that if the members can be exploited by themselves, then one class appropriating another's labour isn't really doing the work anymore and exploitation has quietly become a synonym for working hard under market pressure; that alienation isn't just job dissatisfaction but a specific four-part structure — from the product, from the act of producing, from what he called species-being, from other people — and that structure is what makes both of his cases bad in a way pay can't capture; and that Marx more or less mocks talk of fair shares and equal right when he's arguing with other socialists, so making exploitation the moral core turns him into a liberal egalitarian with a labour theory of value, which he just isn't. He also threw in that on Marx's own categories some wage-workers produce no surplus value at all — domestic servants were his example — so they aren't exploited in the strict sense, yet it would be odd to say nothing is wrong with thier position. Marta's last move was that none of Dev's version gives you anything to act on: alienation is unfalsifiable, beacuse no workplace you could actually go visit counts as unalienated, so it can't pick out a reform or a culprit, while exploitation tells you who is taking what and where to push back. Dev's last move was that changing ownership is precisely the thing that was tried on a mass scale in the last century and it produced plenty of state factories full of alienated workers, so the exploitation-first reading has a track record problem. They both turned to me and I had nothing, and now I have an essay to write on this. Can you go through the exchange more or less move by move and tell me who is right about which specific point, which of the things they say about Marx are simply wrong or overstated, and what both of them are missing — in particular whether Dev's two cases really do pull exploitation and alienation apart the way he thinks they do?
