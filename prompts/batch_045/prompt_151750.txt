Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether humanity has actually become less violent. We disagreed about nearly everything: whether the decline is real, what ought to count as violence, and — the part we kept circling back to — whether a claim like "violence has declined" has a determinate truth value at all, or whether it simply dissolves once you specify a measure.

By an unlucky coincidence, I have a stack of take-home exams to grade on exactly this question, and after that conversation I don't trust my own judgment. I've pasted the question and one student's answer below. Please grade it out of 20 and give me the point-by-point comments you would actually write in the margin: where the answer earns its marks, where it loses them, and why. I need to be able to defend the mark to the student, so I want the reasoning behind each deduction rather than a general impression. Take the student's rough empirical figures at face value — I want the argument assessed, not the numbers.

**Question.** "Is the world becoming less violent?" Assess this claim. Your answer should (a) explain why the choice between absolute numbers of violent deaths and per-capita rates matters, and what each measure can and cannot establish; (b) discuss what ought to count as "violence," and how that choice affects the trend; (c) state whether you think there is a single fact of the matter about whether violence has declined.

**Student's answer.**

(a) The first thing to get clear is the difference between totals and rates. Because world population has grown enormously, the absolute number of people killed violently can rise while the risk to any individual falls: if one episode kills 100,000 people out of a population of 10 million, and another kills 1 million out of a population of 1 billion, the second is ten times larger in absolute terms but a tenth as lethal per person. Absolute counts therefore tell us little on their own; per-capita rates are what we want, since the question is whether a given person's prospects have improved. This is why the standard evidence is presented as deaths per 100,000 people per year. The data bear it out: in some pre-state societies roughly one death in six appears to have been violent, whereas across the twentieth century, even counting both world wars, only a few percent of all deaths were. A fall of that size in the share of deaths that are violent means the chance that a given person dies violently fell by roughly the same factor, and that is the sense in which the world has become less violent.

(b) Everything depends on the definition. The usual estimates of a long decline count homicide and battlefield deaths. But if we use a broader notion that also counts avoidable deaths from poverty, sanctions, or unsafe working conditions as "structural violence," the trend reverses, and the twentieth century looks worse than earlier centuries. This shows that the decline thesis is really a stipulation about what to count rather than an empirical discovery about the world. The choice matters even within the narrow definition: homicide rates and battlefield death rates have not moved together, so "violence" is not tracking a single quantity.

(c) There is no single fact of the matter, which is why the argument never gets resolved. "Violence" is a contestable concept, so different definitions and different measures yield different trend claims, and there is no further fact that settles which of them is the correct one. Once we fix a definition and a measure, the resulting claim is of course perfectly objective — the homicide rate either fell or it did not — but even then our evidence is uneven, since record-keeping is much better for recent centuries than for earlier ones. So "violence is declining" has no truth value over and above the choices we make.
