I'm a journalist on the ideas desk and I'm midway through a feature on the unwritten rules that make crowded cities work — queueing at a bus stop, keeping to one side of a busy pavement, standing on the right of an escalator. A few weeks ago I had dinner with a transport economist and a retired magistrate and we spent an hour talking past each other about the legal half of it, and I've been chewing on it ever since. What bothers me is this: the state criminalises breaches of some conventions and not others, and it does so in a way that looks very hard to justify by reference to harm. A driver who crosses onto the wrong side of the road at four in the morning on an empty stretch, with no other vehicle anywhere in sight, has harmed nobody and risked nothing, and is still guilty of an offence. A person who walks straight to the front of a queue imposes a small but real cost on a line of identifiable people, makes them angry, and the worst that happens to them is a dirty look. Both rules look to me like the same sort of object: an arbitrary selection from among several equally workable equilibria, kept in place only because each of us conforms conditionally on the others conforming. I half-remember that a philosopher — David Lewis, in a book on convention published at the end of the 1960s — offered an analysis along roughly those lines, and that on something like his account the hold a convention has on us comes from the coordination problem it solves rather than from anybody's authority or anybody's say-so.

So here is where I get stuck. First: what does the practice of backing an arbitrary equilibrium with criminal sanctions actually presuppose — that the law contributes a reason of its own, over and above the coordination reason, or merely that it secures expectations and makes the relevant common knowledge cheaper to come by? If the latter, why is a punishment needed at all; if the former, has the thing being enforced stopped being a convention in his sense, because people now conform out of fear of the penalty rather than conditionally on one another? Second: there is an asymmetry I cannot account for. The same legal system that prosecutes the harmless wrong-side driver leaves queues and escalators entirely to informal sanction; and, oddest of all, in civil cases the bare fact that someone broke the convention seems to settle liability almost irrespective of whether anyone was in fact harmed, whilst elsewhere the courts insist that we compensate harm rather than rule-breaking. What distinguishes the conventions that get enforced from those that do not — the size of the harm, the difficulty of detection, the fact that driving is already a licensed activity, or something about which equilibria are hardest to re-establish once they begin to wobble? And what does the occasional attempt to change a convention by decree tell us: if a country can announce a new side of the road and be obeyed, what exactly did the announcement have to latch onto in order to work?

The distinction I've started using to get a grip on this, and which I'd like you to test rather than humour, is between a first-order convention — the regularity itself, everyone drives on the left — and what I've been calling a deference convention: the second-order regularity of falling in with whatever the designated authority announces about which side to drive on, whatever it happens to announce. My hunch is that the criminal law protects the second-order regularity rather than the first, that the lone driver on the empty road is not a harmless eccentric but a defector from the practice of treating the ruling as decisive, and that their offence is closer to free-riding than to dangerous driving, which would explain why the absence of any harm is no defence. But I can equally see the deflationary reading: the law merely selects an equilibrium, and the deference story is redundant decoration. So please reconstruct the implicit theory of convention and of legal normativity that this pattern of practice presupposes; tell me which parts of it the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits well with, such that several rival accounts could license the very same practice; say whether my first-order/deference distinction is doing real work or collapses into something the standard account already handles; and tell me what would genuinely have to change, in doctrine or in behaviour, if the courts adopted the view that a convention's normativity is exhausted by its being an equilibrium, so that law can only select among equilibria and never add reasons of its own. I should say I'm filing this from a hotel room with no library access, so please argue it out rather than pointing me at the literature — I need the reasoning, not references. Give me exactly five numbered points, no preamble and no summing-up, each point two sentences at most.
