I'm a senior at a small college-prep school, and for our year-long senior thesis I'm writing about decision-making under limited time and information — specifically about whether the choices people actually make, when they can't look at everything and can't compute very much, are better described as imperfect versions of the "right" answer or as a different kind of answer altogether. My faculty advisor steered me toward Herbert Simon's ideas of bounded rationality and satisficing instead of the more fashionable behavioral-economics material, so I picked up a popular book on the subject — the kind with a cheerful cover, no footnotes, and a blurb from a well-known economist — and I've been treating one chapter as my working secondary source. I've now read enough in a couple of other places to suspect the chapter is smoothing something over, but I can't tell whether my suspicion is about the substance or just about the book's breezy tone. My school's article databases are blocked from home, so please don't send me off to look anything up or tell me whom to read; I need you to work from the ideas themselves, and I'm not asking you to verify editions, page numbers, or anyone's exact wording.

Here is the passage I've been leaning on, transcribed as carefully as I can: "Simon's point was that the rational-agent models economists use describe a creature that does not exist. A real person choosing an apartment, a job, or a breakfast cereal does not survey every alternative, attach a utility to each, and pick the best one: there are too many alternatives, information is expensive, and the human mind can hold only a handful of things at once. So rational choice theory is not merely unrealistic in its details — it is psychologically false, and every prediction that depends on maximizing is suspect. What people actually do, Simon argued, is satisfice. You set yourself an aspiration level — a salary you would accept, a rent you will not go above — and you search until you find something that clears it, and then you stop. Satisficing is therefore a compromise with our own limitations: it accepts a worse outcome in exchange for ending the search sooner, and Simon's recommendation was that we should do it deliberately, because in most real decisions the gain from continuing to look is smaller than the cost of looking. Notice, though, that this is a claim about how the answer is reached, not about which answer is reached. If you handed an unlimited optimizer the same search costs and told it to stop when the expected gain from one more look fell below the cost of looking, it would stop exactly where the satisficer stops and choose the very same thing. Satisficing and maximizing are thus two procedures with one output. The important consequence is that the interesting question is not which procedure is rational but how much search is worth paying for — and that is just an ordinary optimization problem with an extra line in the budget. Later work on fast-and-frugal heuristics sharpened this into a rebuke to Simon: simple rules of thumb often beat both maximizing and satisficing outright, which shows that the bounds on rationality are not really limits but adaptations, and that Simon's picture of a struggling, compromising decision-maker was too pessimistic."

Two things make me distrust it. First, the book never says where the aspiration level comes from; I've seen it claimed that in Simon's own account the level drifts upward when good options are easy to find and downward when they aren't, and if that's right then there is no fixed threshold for the book's hypothetical optimizer to be handed, so "two procedures with one output" may just be false rather than reassuring. Second, a friend and I tried a miniature version of this at lunch: we gave twelve classmates the same six phone plans, in two different orders, and asked them to pick one using a written rule ("go down the list and take the first plan that is at least as good as the bar we gave you"). The plan people ended up with changed with the ordering, and my friend says this proves the point, because a genuine optimizer wouldn't care about presentation order while a satisficer obviously does. I'm less sure: a rule that tells you when to stop looking might be order-sensitive too, in which case our little test shows nothing about the difference between the procedures, and I'd rather know now than put it in a thesis.
