I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree, and we've just had a fortnight on the ontological argument in my metaphysics module. The chapter I'm working through (one of those short introductory books, not our set text) summarises Gödel's modal version roughly as follows — I've copied it out more or less as it stands:

"Anselm's argument fails because it treats existence as a property, and Kant was right that existence is not a real predicate. Gödel's argument repairs exactly that defect: it nowhere says that God has a property of existing. Instead it defines a God-like being as one that possesses every positive property, and it counts necessary existence as one of those positive properties. From that definition the possibility of a God-like being follows by logic alone — the definition is consistent, and in S5 any consistent property is possibly instantiated — and then, since in S5 whatever is possibly necessary is necessary, it follows that a God-like being exists in every possible world and hence in ours. The only substantive assumption, then, is that the concept of a God-like being is coherent, which is far weaker than anything Anselm needed. The standard objection — that the axioms also entail that every truth is necessary, so that nothing is contingent — is not really a worry: if the premises entailed that, they would be inconsistent, and no one has shown that, so the argument stands."

I'd be grateful for an accuracy audit, since the chapter is confident but I can't tell how much of it to trust: which sentences would someone working on this accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are straightforwardly wrong? Three things in particular bother me, though I can't say why — the claim that the Kantian objection is simply inapplicable here, the suggestion that the possibility step costs nothing beyond the consistency of the definition, and the brisk dismissal of the modal-collapse objection. Finally, the chapter adds that because positivity is left as an undefined primitive and is never cashed out as goodness or greatness, the argument would go through equally well for any old partition of properties into two classes, so that all the real work is done by the modal logic rather than by the notion of a positive property. Is that a fair moral to draw, or does the weight sit somewhere else?
