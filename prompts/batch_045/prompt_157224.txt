I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a broadsheet, and for a forthcoming themed issue on the quantified life — sleep scores, step counts, continuous glucose monitors — I want a satirical piece of roughly 800–900 words aimed at accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory: the family of views on which the fundamental epistemic good is closeness to truth, credences are assessed (and vindicated) by scoring rules, and the norms of probabilism and conditionalization are derived as consequences of maximizing expected accuracy rather than from betting or pragmatic considerations.

The form I want is a leaked internal document: an extract from the draft statutory guidance of a fictional regulator — call it something like the Office for Epistemic Standards — launching a pilot scheme in which participating households must file a quarterly Credence Return, giving a number between 0 and 1 for every proposition on an Approved Question Schedule, scored by a strictly proper scoring rule, with a small rebate for good calibration. Set it in an invented town, format the guidance as numbered clauses in deadpan officialese, and append a specimen Credence Return with the auditor's marginal notes, plus at least one quotation from a departmental spokesperson and one from a resident or a certified credence auditor (all invented; no real person, living or dead, should be named anywhere in the piece, and there should be no citations, footnotes to the literature, or links).

The jokes have to come from getting the epistemology right, so that a formal epistemologist laughs rather than winces. I want the piece to work through, and stay faithful to, at least these real features: that under a strictly proper scoring rule it is optimal to report your actual credence, so the scheme can never detect insincerity and never needs to; the calibration-and-refinement decomposition, and the fact that an evasive citizen can score perfect calibration by returning 0.5 for everything, which forces the guidance to bolt on a sharpness rider; the accuracy-dominance vindication of coherence, so that holding inconsistent credences is an offence carrying no Dutch book and no loss of money, purely a loss of accuracy; the expected-accuracy argument for updating, which the guidance should codify as a duty to plan to conditionalize rather than a duty to end up coherent; and the equal treatment of subject matters, since — unless weights are legislated for, which is itself a departure — a well-calibrated 0.7 about the number of peas in a pod earns exactly what a well-calibrated 0.7 about a diagnosis earns. The framework's discomfort with suspension of judgment, and its silence about understanding, explanation, testimony, and about which questions are worth asking, should surface as administrative problems the guidance has to paper over: an appendix that keeps growing, a definition of "no view" that turns out to be a precise commitment after all.

Near the end I want the scheme's own logic to arrive somewhere the reader physically recoils — my suggestion is the Schedule obliging residents to record a precise credence on a proposition they have no business holding a view about at all, with the department's defence being that the score is indifferent to subject matter; if you have a better destination, take it. But the piece must not wink. No clause should be a straw man an accuracy-firster could dismiss in a sentence; where the guidance is unfair to the view, cut it, and let the unfairness that survives be the unfairness that is really there. It should be legible to a reader who has never heard of a scoring rule and recognizable as fair to one who has spent years with them.

Two things I need you to commit to rather than survey. First, pick one implication as the genuine target and make the whole piece an argument for it in the only way satire can argue: not the formalism, not the mathematics, not the people who hold the view. If, having thought about it, you conclude that accuracy-first epistemology is in fact in good shape and that the scheme is a travesty of it, write that piece instead and say so plainly — but choose, and don't split the difference by letting the ending undercut the rest. Second, after the piece, add a note of about 100 words, not for publication, stating where it leans: which reading of the view you have taken as canonical, which clause rests on a contested or scoring-rule-sensitive claim, and what a defender's strongest reply would be.
