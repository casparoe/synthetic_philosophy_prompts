hey, hoping someone here knows the philosophy-of-math side of this, because i keep running into the same shape of thing and I dont have a word for it.

So: there are a few different ways of telling whether two collections have the same number of things in them. You can try to pair everything in one with everything in the other with nothing left over. You can notice that one collection sits inside the other with stuff left over, and conclude there must be fewer. Or, where the things have a length or a weight, you can just measure. In every case I can actually check — piles of coins, guests in a hotel, whatever — all of these give the same answer, always. And it isnt a coincidence, I gather there's even a proof that if the collection finishes, they can't disagree.

But once the collections don't finish, they come apart, and each says something different: the full hotel can still take a new guest, because you can shuffle everyone up one room — except that the hotel was already full, and on the sits-inside test it can't take anyone. The even numbers are half of the whole numbers on one test and exactly as many on another. A line of length one can be matched point for point with a line of length two and still be half as long.

The part I want a word for isn't the surprise. I know about Galileo's paradox and Hilbert's hotel and I know Cantor's pairing answer basically wins once you decide that pairing is what "same number" means. What I keep noticing is the structure: several tests that provably agree everywhere in the range where we learn and use them, so nothing in that range can ever seperate them, and which then split the moment you cross into a new range — at which point everyone picks the one that felt more basic and calls the others "intuitions" or "only the finite case" or "a different notion of size". And after the split, the fight about which test is really about *number* doesn't behave like a normal factual disagreement, but it also doesn't behave like a purely verbal one, because people build real mathematics on each choice and the choices buy you different things.

Does this have a name? I don't think Galileo's paradox is it — that seems to be just the one case about parts and wholes, and it's usually presented as something to dissolve, not as a pattern with instances. If there's no name, coin me one, with a definition tight enough that I can tell what isn't an instance: a plain paradox, a plain mistake, a case where one of the tests was always only a rule of thumb, a case where the tests already disagree back inside the familiar range. And tell me honestly whether the right thing to say is that there were never one notion of size but several that happen to coincide while things are finite — if so I want it said in a way that doesn't collapse into "anything goes".

Answer as exactly five numbered lines, no intro, no conclusion, a couple of sentences each. Please also flag which parts of what you're saying are settled (I assume the math and the agreement-on-finite-collections bit are) and which are live disputes among people who actually work on this — I'd much rather be told "genuinely contested" than handed someone's house view as the consensus.
