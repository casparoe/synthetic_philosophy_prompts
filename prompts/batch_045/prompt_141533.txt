I'm putting together a small one-day workshop on philosophy of mathematics in the spring, and I want to open it by reading a poem aloud before the first talk — something that gets the room amused and then uneasy, not a versified literature review. I've decided it should be a sestina, and here's the reasoning, so tell me if the idea doesn't hold up.

The sestina's six end-words are the one thing that never changes while everything around them does. I want those six words to be: three, mark, true, outside, nothing, again. (Swap one if you've got a better candidate, but keep the set small and ordinary — no jargon, no "abstracta".) At some point the platonist should get to claim the recurrence as their evidence: the same three comes back to us again, survives every mark that carries it, is outside anything we do with it, and is what makes what we say true. And at some point the nominalist should get to answer that the recurrence is the rule of the form doing its work — the words come back because of how a sestina is generated, not because they're tracking anything; and if all the marks went, it wouldn't be that nothing had changed, it would be that there was nothing left to change.

The bits I want rendered properly, because a good half the room will be people who work on exactly this and I dont want them wincing:

- The platonist thesis isn't just "maths is useful" — it's that numbers are abstract, mind- and language-independent, outside space and time and causal reach, and that this is what makes our claims true.
- The epistemic worry in its sharp version: we're meant to know things about objects we have no causal contact with whatsoever. I'd like the poem to get at the version of that worry that comes from identifying numbers with sets. Under one standard reduction, 1 is a member of 3; under another, it isn't; and nothing seems to settle which reduction is the right one. Relatedly, nothing in the identification seems to settle whether Julius Caesar is the number three. If numbers really are objects of this kind, why is every question about what they are and aren't identical to like this?
- The argument that we're committed to them anyway, because our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects and we shouldn't accept a theory while refusing the commitments it can't do without.
- The nominalist reply to that: the programme of showing the maths is in principle dispensable — reformulating the science so it doesn't quantify over numbers, then showing that adding the mathematics back in lets you derive nothing new about the non-mathematical part, so the commitment was only ever instrumental.
- And if there's room, a stanza for the view whose name I can never remeber, on which every consistent mathematical theory is true of some realm or other, so the identification problem dissolves because there was never one right answer and there's room for all of them.

No resolution, please. Our reading group spent a term on this and ended up exactly nowhere, and I'd like the poem to end up nowhere too — the envoi (three lines, all six words again) should leave both readings live, the words still returning and still not telling us which story about them is right. Rough verse is fine, a tidy moral isn't; where a line has to choose between being pretty and being something a philosopher of maths would actually say, be accurate.

And just give me the poem: a title if you insist, then the six stanzas and the envoi. No headings, no bold, no bullets, no footnotes, and no paragraph beforehand about how you're approaching it. Two or three sentences of plain prose after the last line is fine if you want to flag a choice you made, but nothing formatted.
