Hi — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond lurking on a couple of blogs, and I'm running a lunchtime reading group at work where we're trying to produce one of those adversarial-collaboration documents instead of just arguing forever. I'm drafting both sides myself and it's going badly: they keep talking past each other. Please write the document they should have produced.

The two positions:

- Side A: at least some psychiatric categories pick out real kinds — stable clusters in nature (genetics, neural circuits, computational phenotypes, whatever the right level turns out to be) that we discover rather than invent, and the fact that the DSM is assembled by committees and votes doesn't touch that.
- Side B: the Hacking-style position — human kinds are interactive, the people classified react to being classified, the category is a moving target, so whatever we're doing it isn't like discovering a new element.

Deliverable: a numbered section of agreed claims — claims both could sign that aren't vacuous, i.e. where at least one side had to give something up — followed by a table of the residual disagreements, where each row states the disagreement in wording that side would accept as a fair statement of its own view (so two formulations per row, not one strained compromise), plus the cruxes, plus for each crux a case or study design both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Also: for each side, what it would count as evidence or argument against itself. And if part of the fight is only about words, say which rows and why, and what the substantive question is once the verbal bit is removed.

Things I specifically want in there:

1. My own job keeps intruding here. In production ML we have this constant problem: a classifier's output changes the thing it's classifying. Users adapt to the ranking, the labels you collect get contaminated by the model's own predictions, the distribution drifts, retraining on your own outputs degrades the model. My side-B friend says this is definately just a looping effect in Hacking's sense. My side-A friend says no — there's no self-conception in play, no one is told what kind of person they are, the recommender isn't in the business of giving people a description of themselves, so it's a different animal with a superficially similar shape. I want this settled in the document: is the production feedback loop an instance of looping, a genuine analogue, or something else entirely? Both sides have to say something non-hand-wavy, and the agreed-claims section should state plainly which parts of the two cases are agreed to be the same phenomenon and which aren't. If the honest answer is that it's a third thing, say that.

2. I'm sick of the standard examples (fugue, neurasthenia, the DSM-5 thing where Asperger's got folded into ASD, the older homosexuality decision). So here's a made-up one for us to reason with: a proposed new category for a hypothetical DSM-6, call it Persistent Notification-Checking Disorder — criteria along the lines of an inability to resist checking work messages or alerts, resulting in measurable impairment, present for six months, not better explained by an existing diagnosis. Nobody has it yet as an official category. Have both sides say:
   - what they predict happens to the prevalence, the presentation, and the population walking into clinics in the ten years after it's introduced;
   - whether the fact that the prevalence moves (if it does) tells us anything about whether there was a real kind there;
   - and what would happen if the same category were introduced in two medically similar countries with very different care and insurance systems — a divergence prediction from each side.

3. A crux I think matters: the interventionist/neuro move. If you reclassify by neural circuit or computational phenotype instead of by reported symptoms and distress, does looping stop, attenuate, or just relocate? Each side writes down a prediction, including the specific observation that would move them.

4. A crux about whether looping is all-or-nothing. There seem to be cases where classification barely feeds back at all (something clearly genetic and neurodegenerative) and cases where it feeds back hard. Is that a bright line between two sorts of kinds, or a gradient, and does the answer change anything about the dispute? Both sides have to answer for the middle cases, not just the endpoints.

5. My suspicion that a lot of this is about the word real — one side meaning mind-independent, the other meaning something like projectible or usable for prediction and intervention. If that's a verbal disagreement, tell me, and give me the real question it's hiding.
