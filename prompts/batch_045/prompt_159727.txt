I am applying for a six-month visiting fellowship at an institute that pairs philosophers with cultural-evolution modellers, and I need a two-page proposal I can send to the selection committee. I will fill in the references myself, so please don't spend words on citations or on re-explaining Lewis's analysis of convention — assume I know it, including the clause requiring that there be some alternative regularity which nobody could conform to at the same time as the actual one, the clause that is supposed to separate genuine coordination problems from mere habits.

Here is my half-formed idea. Lewis's analysis, and most of the dynamical work built on top of it, seems to me to presume that a convention has determinate, discrete content: there is the regularity, there are one or more alternative regularities, and change is a jump from one to another. But much of the normative change I actually care about doesn't look like that at all. Consider what counts as an acceptable level of eye contact in a seminar, or how much interruption is rude, or how a form of address becomes presumptuous and then insulting and then archaic. These shift over decades by increments so small that at no point does anyone seem to be deviating from what others expect, and yet the end state is a norm that nobody in the earlier period would recognise as the same norm. My modeller friends tell me the discreteness in their simulations is a technical convenience: you need a finite strategy set to write the game down, so of course you discretise, and nothing about the phenomenon hangs on it. I suspect that is wrong for Lewisian conventions specifically, because the common-knowledge condition does the real work in Lewis's account, and if the content of the regularity is graded or has blurred edges, it stops being clear what it is that everyone knows, and knows that everyone knows, about what others expect. Call this the drift problem. My supervisor's favoured reply is that drift is really a rapid sequence of tiny equilibrium switches, each of which has its own determinate alternative, so nothing needs to be added to the account; a second move is to say that drifting regularities were never conventions in the first place but something looser — habits, standards, practices, precedents — and so lie outside the theory's target; a third is to treat the whole thing as the vagueness problem in a new hat and hand it over to semantics. I don't think any of the three settles it, but I can't yet say why in a way that would convince a committee.

What I need from you is a proposal, not an essay. State the question sharply enough that it has a determinate answer in six months rather than being a licence to muse; say why the three moves above (and any better one you can think of) leave it open; lay out what I would actually do month by month, including what I would ask the modellers to do alongside me and why a philosopher's armchair work is the right method here rather than, say, running more simulations or doing a comparative survey of normative change; describe what a positive result would look like for both audiences and, equally important, what observable outcome would mean the project had failed; and give an honest assessment of whether this is genuinely a six-month project or secretly a dissertation. If it helps, one thing I would like the proposal to make room for is the possibility that the answer is asymmetric: that discreteness matters for how a convention gets started — salience needs a discrete menu to select from — but not for how it is sustained, or vice versa.
