just got nerd-sniped by the classic Molyneux setup and I think I've built an argument that proves way too much, so I want someone to find me the broken step.

Setup first, so we're arguing about the same case: person blind from birth (dense cataracts, say), surgery, bandages come off, and there on a table in front of him, behind glass so he can look but not touch, is a cube and a sphere — both of which he has handled hundreds of times and can name instantly by touch. Can he say which is which just by looking? I've been defending "no". Here's the argument a friend runs against me, and it ends somewhere I definately don't want to go.

(1) The task isn't discrimination. He can obviously see that the two things look different. The task is identification: judging, of the thing he sees now, that it is the thing he used to call "cube". Discrimination without identification gets you "those look different", never "that one's the cube". Everyone agrees on this.

(2) A cross-modal identification like that needs a link between the visual state and the tactile state: either the two are already in a common format (an amodal spatial representation, whatever exactly that amounts to), or experience has built a mapping between the two formats.

(3) The "no" answer just is the claim that neither is available to him at the moment of the test: no shared or innate format (that's the interesting reading of "no", the one that's supposed to tell us something about concepts or about sensory content), and no learned mapping either (he's never had the chance to correlate the two).

(4) So he fails the test. No. That's my position and I'd like to keep it.

(5) But he has the rest of his life. Either he can acquire the mapping from here, or he can't.

(6) To acquire it by experience, he has to notice that *this* visual state goes with *that* tactile state — that they're states of one and the same object. And noticing that is exactly the identification he couldn't perform in (4). So experience can never hand him the mapping: you can't bootstrap a cross-modal link out of nothing, because every candidate link has to be recognized as a link before it can start reinforcing itself.

(7) So either the link was innately specified all along — in which case the shared format was there at birth, the interesting reading of "no" is false, and the honest answer to the original question is basically yes-with-a-delay — or else cross-modal perceptual learning is impossible tout court, which is nuts, since coordinating senses is most of what infants do.

(8) So "no" isn't a stable answer at all. Any argument of this shape either collapses into "yes" or commits you to denying that anybody ever learns to coordinate two senses.

I don't buy (8), and I want to keep (4). But I can't locate the rot. My suspicion is a level-shift around (6): maybe "mapping" is doing two different jobs, one at the level of concepts and judgements ("that seen thing is the cube I felt") and one at some sub-personal level where a visual signal and a tactile signal merely co-occur and something statistical latches onto the covariation without anybody identifying anything. But if that's the diagnosis then I don't know what (2) and (3) were even claiming, and I worry the fix just relabels the mystery. Maybe the problem is really (5)'s dichotomy, or (2) offering a false choice between shared format and learned mapping.

Two probes that make me more suspicious rather than less:

Probe one, the simultaneous version. Same subject, same objects, but this time let him hold the thing in his hands while he looks at it. The argument seems to spit out the identical verdict, since (2) and (3) say nothing about timing — but surely that's absurd: if you can feel and see the same object at the same moment, the correlation is just handed to you. So either (2) and (3) are too strong, or they aren't doing the work the argument needs them to do.

Probe two, the invisible-difference pair. Swap the cube and sphere for two discs of the same size and color, one lead and one balsa, tellable apart by heft alone. Now (1) fails — the visual states don't differ — so the argument never gets started, and we get "no" for a boring reason: there's no information in the light. Fine. But that makes me want to know which premise was carrying the weight in the original case, and whether it was doing any real philosophical work or just restating that the test is hard.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (4) and the "no" verdict about those first minutes after the bandages come off than accept either horn of (7). If killing (6) means I owe you a story about what a mapping is and how one gets built, give me the story — just don't let it be one where the conclusion was smuggled in.

One more thing: be explicit about what's settled here and what people who actually work on this disagree about. I gather there's a live fight about what the "no" answer is even meant to show — something about amodal concepts, or about whether the senses share spatial content, or about whether spatial perception needs learning at all — and I'd much rather you flag a controversy as live than smooth it down into the boring consensus version. No citations needed; I want the diagnosis to work from the armchair and I can look up papers myself.
