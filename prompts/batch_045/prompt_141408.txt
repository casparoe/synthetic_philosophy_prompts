I'm giving a thirty-minute guest lecture to a room of second-year epidemiology residents, in their journal-club slot, on Simpson's paradox. They've all seen the kidney-stone and Berkeley-admissions examples and have cached the moral "aggregate numbers lie, disaggregate." I want to break exactly that: my claim is that the reversal is an arithmetic fact about ratios that has nothing to do with causality, that the more common professional failure among people who already know the paradox is stratifying when they should aggregate, and that "is this a confounder?" is downstream of a question they aren't yet in the habit of asking.

Plan the session — don't explain the paradox itself. I need: (1) the order the pieces have to go in and why. I'm tempted to open with a bare pair of 2x2 tables with no story attached at all, so the reversal first registers as arithmetic, before anyone can reach for a causal story; steelman starting with the vivid case instead. (2) One carrying example, medical enough not to feel imported, in which the stratifying variable is downstream of the exposure, so that the aggregate estimate is the right one and stratifying is the error — and why that example rather than the canonical ones, and what it must and must not contain. (3) The misconceptions in the order they'll form, and the point in the sequence where each gets defused: I expect "the stratified numbers are the truth," "a reversal means something is broken in the data," and a conflation of the reversal with non-collapsibility of the odds ratio. (4) Whether to introduce a causal graph at all in thirty minutes and, if so, where. (5) One end-of-session question that separates someone who got it from someone who has merely learned to distrust aggregate tables.

I've also been using a rule of thumb I don't trust to survive contact with experts: stratify on a variable only if it is a cause of the exposure rather than a consequence of it. Tell me where that rule breaks — I'm least sure about variables that proxy for an unmeasured confounder while also being affected by the exposure — and whether to teach the rule, teach the graph instead, or openly say I'm leaving the hard case out at this level.
