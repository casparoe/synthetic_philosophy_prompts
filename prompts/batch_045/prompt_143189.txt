I came to this literature by way of the rationalist blogosphere rather than a philosophy department, so I've tried to state my worry as a clean trilemma instead of wading through secondary sources — and I'd be grateful for a philosopher's eye on whether I've actually got one.

I've been reading about higher-order theories of consciousness, of the sort associated with Rosenthal. The core claim, as I understand it: what makes a mental state a conscious state is that the subject non-inferentially represents herself as being in it, and the difference between a conscious and an unconscious mental state is supposed to be exhausted by that relation. The objection I keep running into is the "no target" one: a higher-order representation with no first-order state to represent would seem to make its subject conscious of a state that isn't there, and in the limit to make something as inert as a rock conscious. The two replies I keep encountering are, first, that the theory is a claim about what makes *states* conscious rather than about what makes *creatures* conscious, and second, that higher-order representations are partly individuated by their targets, so a targetless one isn't the right sort of thing to do any work. I've tried to steelman both, and I think my case slips between them.

Call it the Mismatch Case. A subject has an ordinary first-order itch state. The higher-order representation that would normally make her states conscious represents her instead as being in a sharp, localized pain. There is a target here — it is just the wrong one. So:

1. If the higher-order representation contributes to what it is like for her, then in the Mismatch Case she is consciously in a pain she is not in. Consciousness has come free of her actual mental life, which is just the absurdity of the no-target case — except that now a target is present, so neither of the standard replies gets a grip.
2. If it contributes nothing to what it is like, then it is a bare switch. Its only role is to flip a state from unconscious to conscious, and everything we wanted explained about phenomenology is being done by the first-order state alone. The higher-order representation is an inert add-on, and it is hard to see why the theory was worth the trouble.
3. If the misrepresentation means that she is not conscious at all, we get the worst result. A subject with an itch and a false belief about it is not a rock, not comatose, not a sleepwalker: she is awake, and something is going on for her. Yet the theory has to say that her mental life has gone dark.

I take the third horn to be where the real damage is. If being conscious just is standing in the higher-order relation, and the higher-order representation is wrong, then there is nothing left over for the subject to be conscious of. So the theory cannot distinguish being conscious of the wrong thing from not being conscious — which strikes me as a reductio of the whole approach.

Three questions, then. First, is this argument new? I have not been able to find it anywhere, though my searching has been mostly blogs and fora rather than journals, and I would much rather be told it is a known objection than reinvent it badly. Second, if it is not new, which horn do higher-order theorists actually take, and how do they live with the costs? Third, I would especially like to know whether there is a reply that does not simply amount to stipulating that the higher-order representation is accurate whenever it is doing its job — because if that is the reply, it seems to me to be doing all the work, and the theory has stopped being an account of what consciousness is and become the claim that we never misrepresent our own conscious states.
