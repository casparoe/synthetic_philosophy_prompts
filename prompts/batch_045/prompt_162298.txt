I help run a first-year seminar called *Borderline Cases*, cross-listed between biology and philosophy, and I'm assembling the reading packet for the week on definitions of life. The standard examples — viruses, prions, mules, fire — have been done to death, and my students can predict where each one is heading before it gets there, so I want something that puts a genuinely undecided case in front of them and leaves them arguing with each other. I've been toying with a test I call the dialysis test: a human on permanent kidney dialysis is not remotely a borderline case of being alive, even though her blood chemistry is being maintained by a machine several times a week, so why should a lineage whose chemistry is maintained by a machine be any different? My colleague's reply, which I cannot shake, is that the test proves too much: a tended fire metabolises, grows, responds to its environment and reproduces, and a fire kept burning continuously in a shrine for centuries would then count as alive for as long as someone keeps feeding it — so, she says, the dialysis test is really just a way of smuggling in an answer about where you draw the boundary of the system. (I also can't tell whether the move "every organism depends on a larger system it did not build; we all breathe oxygen made by the biosphere" is a deep point or a rhetorical trick, and it seems to me to cut both ways.)

Please write me a short story that dramatises this disagreement rather than settling it, using a setting and cast I'll specify. The Annex: a decommissioned bioremediation facility on a cold coast, a few decades out, whose last job was to run a vat of an engineered chemoautotrophic lineage — give it an institutional, dull name like Strain K-7 — that was bred to break down a contaminant in an aquifer. Build the case so that the philosophical work falls exactly where I want it: the strain metabolises, grows and reproduces under its own steam, and the logbook shows roughly nine hundred generations of genuine adaptation to the Annex's cooling water, so it passes the evolution clause of the familiar working definition of life, the one about a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution, with room to spare. But it cannot synthesise one cofactor, a synthetic analogue that the Annex's metering system has always dripped into the vat, and without the drip it dies in about six hours. The funding has ended, a decommissioning inspector has arrived to certify the site closed, and the caretaker — the last person on site, not a philosopher, the one who has been keeping the log — has to decide what to do with the vat. Three characters at most: the caretaker, the inspector, and optionally the retired director on the phone.

Constraints on how it's written: exactly five scenes, numbered 1 to 5, 150–250 words each, with nothing before scene 1 and nothing after scene 5. The philosophy has to be carried by what people do, notice and choose, not by speechifying — no character delivers a lecture longer than two sentences, nobody says 'what is life?' or anything within reach of it, and no character resolves the disagreement or is clearly authorially endorsed in resolving it. The decommissioning checklist should appear, with the familiar working definition printed on it, and someone should notice that the strain satisfies one clause of it and fails another. There must be a fire, either driftwood on the beach or the site's burn barrel, and the campfire parallel has to land as an image and an action rather than as an argument a character makes. At least one scene should shift where the system's boundary is drawn, so that by the end it is genuinely unclear whether the candidate living thing is the strain, the strain plus the metering system, or the Annex as a whole — without any character announcing that this is what is going on. The last scene should be a decision that costs something under every reading, not a verdict; I want a reader who closes the page unable to tell which side you think is right. Finally, because I'm handing this to students, add as the final line of each scene — as part of that item, not as a sixth item — one sentence in brackets saying whether what the scene assumes about life is something nearly everyone who works on this accepts, or is instead one contested reading among others; and where it is contested, name the reading in a phrase, so that a student can tell the settled from the disputed and won't mistake a controversial view for the consensus.
