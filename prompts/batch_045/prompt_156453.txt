I am the registrar of a small university museum, and I am in the middle of renegotiating our loan and transit cover. The policy schedule carries a "fragile objects" category with a much higher premium attached to it, and the insurer's assessor has proposed moving four of our pieces out of that category. I have a fortnight to reply in writing, and I need to know two things: whether her reasoning is sound, and what I should say in reply. I cannot simply dig my heels in — under-declaring could void a claim, and over-declaring on objects we lend regularly would cost us money we do not have.

Her test is disarmingly simple. An object belongs in the fragile category, she says, only if it would break if it were struck; wherever we cannot show that it would break, it should not be rated as fragile. She adds that we have never actually struck any of these objects unprotected, so our claim that they are fragile is nothing more than an inference from what they are made of.

The four pieces, and why each worries me:

1. A first-century glass bowl. For travel it sits in a cast cradle lined with a gel that goes rigid the instant it is deformed sharply. We have twice dropped a packed crate, with a test weight in place of the bowl, a metre onto concrete, and nothing broke. She concludes that the bowl is not fragile. That seems to me to get things backwards: the bowl is as prone to shattering as it ever was, and what prevented the breakage was the crate, not the bowl.

2. A thirteenth-century beaker, part of a conservation trial in which the glass has been given an invisible, reversible coating that hardens the moment the surface is put under sudden stress. Treated beakers of the same glass survive drop tests. The assessor treats this exactly like case 1. I am not sure it is the same: here it is the object itself, not something outside it, that is different at the moment of impact.

3. A bronze bowl, thick and heavy, mounted on a display plinth wired into the security rig: if the bowl is disturbed, a weighted arm swings down and crushes it. So it is true that the bowl would be destroyed if it were struck — yet nobody would call it fragile, since the destruction comes entirely from the rig. Her test seems to give the wrong answer here, but I cannot say precisely what it is missing.

4. A modern studio-glass dish, made from a glass that softens at roughly body temperature. Anyone who picked it up in order to strike it would, by holding it, make it pliable, and it would bend rather than shatter. Is this the same sort of case as 2, or a different one?

I would be grateful for: (i) your view of the assessor's test and where exactly it goes wrong, if it does; (ii) advice, piece by piece, on which of the four I should concede and which I should contest, with the reasoning I could put in a letter; and (iii) a formulation of "fragile" that I could propose as replacement wording for the schedule — one that gives the right answer in all four cases and that an assessor who cannot run destructive tests on our objects could actually apply. If part of the difficulty is that some of these classifications are genuinely unsettled rather than clear, please say which, and say how we should then decide what to declare.
