I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I keep coming back to organised competitive sport as the one place where that distinction has actually been written into binding rules and enforced with real sanctions. So I want to treat the anti-doping code as a philosophical object and ask what it presupposes. Here is the practice as I understand it: a substance or method goes on the Prohibited List if it satisfies two of three criteria — that it can enhance performance, that it carries health risk, and that it offends the spirit of sport. An athlete with a documented diagnosis can get a Therapeutic Use Exemption to take an otherwise banned substance, on the condition that the dose restores normal function rather than exceeding it. Some substances are banned only in competition, some only in particular sports (beta-blockers in precision events like shooting and archery, stimulants generally). Meanwhile a great many interventions that plainly enhance performance are permitted outright: altitude training and hypoxic tents, creatine, caffeine, carbohydrate loading, high-tech running shoes (though certain swimwear technology was eventually banned after a rash of records), corrective eye surgery that can leave an athlete with better-than-normal acuity, and reconstructive surgery after which some pitchers return throwing harder than before. And in the regulations governing some athletes with differences of sex development, competitors have in effect been required to take medication to bring a natural hormone level down in order to be eligible for certain track events.

The pattern puzzles me in several places at once. First, the very same intervention — a corticosteroid injection, testosterone replacement, a stimulant — is treatment when the patient is a teacher and doping when the patient is a cyclist, which suggests the therapy/enhancement line is not a property of the intervention or even of the person's health, but of the role or practice the person is in. Second, hypoxic tents and erythropoietin raise red-cell mass through the same broad physiological route, yet one is a permitted training method and the other a criminalised one; so something other than the physiological effect is doing the work. Third, if therapeutic use is supposed to restore an athlete's own baseline, the code has no clean baseline to restore to: some baselines are themselves partly products of earlier permitted enhancements, some surgical repairs overshoot the pre-injury level, and some natural baselines are treated as unfair advantages requiring suppression rather than as the neutral point of departure. Fourth, the sport-relativity of the beta-blocker ban seems to presuppose that each sport has a specifiable excellence — the specific capacities it is supposed to test — and that the rules protect that excellence rather than health or fairness in the abstract.

A colleague of mine has been pushing an alternative anchor that I can't quite evaluate, and I'd like your help testing it against the code: she suggests forgetting medical normality and anchoring the rules instead to a developmental-luck baseline, i.e. permitting an intervention just in case it leaves the athlete's relevant physiological variable where it would have been had they enjoyed a fair share of developmental goods — nutrition, coaching, freedom from untreated injury — and banning interventions that push them past that counterfactual level, including, in principle, requiring downward corrections for athletes whose natural endowment exceeds it. A second thought I've had is that the code might really be tracking not therapy versus enhancement but whether the adaptation is mediated by effort and training versus delivered directly, so that the deep commitment is to something like the moral significance of effort rather than to health or fairness.

Here is what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory that this practice actually requires: what view of therapy, of enhancement, of natural advantage, of the point of competition does it only make sense under, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Be disciplined about three things I care about. (i) Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the points where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the same rule. (ii) Say where the parts of the practice are pulling in different directions — whether the therapy/enhancement distinction is doing the real work or is a rationalisation for something else, and whether the practice is coherent overall or only locally coherent. (iii) Tell me concretely what would have to change if the anchor were replaced: what would the Prohibited List and the Therapeutic Use Exemption system look like under my colleague's developmental-luck baseline, under a straightforward harm-reduction regime that permits anything safe, and under a pure anti-deception or level-playing-field view — and which items on the current list would collapse, survive, or have to be added. I don't need a map of the literature; I want the analysis.
