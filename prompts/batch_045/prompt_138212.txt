I'm on the animal care committee at our field station and I owe the board a recomendation in writing before Thursday, so please draft the memo.

Two protocols. Protocol A: 24 common octopuses, each trained alone on a three-choice discrimination for about three weeks, then terminal recording under anesthesia at the end — under our permit they can't be released. The PI's case is basically that octopuses are Skinnerian creatures: they shape their own behaviour by reinforcement inside a single lifetime and that's the whole story, so the only thing at stake is what they feel during training, which is brief and mildly aversive.

Protocol B: a field study on New Caledonian crows. In each of six territories the group's tool type differs in ways that don't seem forced by what's locally available, and in each territory there are a few birds whose tools the others appear to match. The team wants to take those birds into aviaries for eight weeks and record whether the remaining birds' tools drift or revert, and whether the original style comes back when they're returned. They pitch this as the first real test of whether a nonhuman lineage is Gregorian in Dennett's sense — a mind partly made of tools handed over by a culture instead of rebuilt by each individual.

The bit the board is stuck on: on that scheme the Gregorian tier isn't a property of an individual at all. You're Gregorian because you were born into a culture that installs thinking tools in you, so whatever Protocol B might damage isn't something any single crow possesses. One member says that makes B worse than A in a way our severity bands don't register — you'd be deliberately dismantling something that exists only at the level of the group and its history. Another says it makes B fine, since nothing is taken from any bird. A third points out we can't even tell whether the local styles are socially transmitted or quietly reinvented by each bird, which would make them Skinnerian or at best Popperian after all — and if we can't tell, maybe we shouldn't be licensing the experiment that settles it by running it.

Write me the memo: recommend what we do with each protocol (approve, approve with conditions, defer for more info, or reject), say whether we should add a "cultural harm" line to the review form and how a reviewer could apply one without just waving through whatever sounds most interesting, and give the strongest version of the argument you're voting against rather than the easy version.
