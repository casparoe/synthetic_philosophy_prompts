A chapter in an old pop-sci book on consciousness — one of those 90s ones, I think the one that introduced the phrase "user illusion", I read it years ago so I'm reconstructing from memory and probably mangaling it — argues roughly this:

Consciousness isn't a window onto what the brain is doing, it's a user interface: a simplified desktop of icons the brain throws up so you can act without being swamped by detail, the way a computer desktop hides the machinery. The bandwidth of consciousness is a tiny fraction of what the senses take in, so what reaches you isn't a report on the real causes of your behaviour but a compressed summary, and the processing that actually moves you is mostly the part that got thrown away. Two consequences are drawn. First, there's no inner someone sitting in front of the screen reading the icons — the "user" is part of the display, manufactured by it, so the sense that there's a self that has the experience is itself produced by the interface, like the cursor arrow is. Second, since the icons are there for action rather than for truth, consciousness does no causal work: it's the exhaust, not the engine. The chapter then says this is why the view can't be tested — any experiment that asks people what they're conscious of just gets more icons, never the machinery — and that it's therefore behaviourism with better PR. It also floats a concrete prediction: if consciousness is an interface, it should have a sampling rate, so experience really comes in discrete frames and the smoothness we report is the interface papering over the gaps, the way a monitor does.

My instinct is that the last two bits don't follow. The whole point of an interface is that poking the icons does something, so "exhaust, not engine" sounds backwards to me. But I'm worried my objection just smuggles a homunculus back in — that I'm quietly saying someone has to be there to read the display, which is the exact thing the view denies. I also can't tell whether "illusion" here means a useful simplification that still tracks something real or a mirage with nothing there at all, and whether the chapter is even consistent about which one it means. And I've seen this kind of position called illusionism somewhere, and I don't know if that's the same claim or a stronger one.

Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a defender of this sort of view would happily accept, what's a normal popularization-level simplification, and what they'd outright reject? Especially: is the epiphenomenalism conclusion genuinely a commitment or just a hostile reading; does the "can't be tested" line depend on conflating introspective report with something the view actually wants to treat as data; is calling it behaviourism fair; and is the discrete-frames prediction something the interface metaphor really supports, or is it an artefact of taking the computer comparison too literally? I'm not writing anything up, I just want to know how much of the chapter to trust.
