I'm in my school's philosophy club and I'm presenting where I've landed on the skeptical argument next week, so I'd like a consistency check on the package before I commit to it in front of people.

(1) Tracking, roughly: I know that p only if, were p false, I wouldn't believe that p, with the counterfactual relativized to the method I actually used. I'm also fine with the other half — if p were true, I'd believe it. I know people complain that relativizing to methods lets you gerrymander the description of the method until almost any belief comes out tracking; I'm not sure how much that worries me.

(2) I don't want to give up closure outright, so I've been leaning on a restricted version I came up with: knowledge is closed under known entailments that I have actually drawn for myself, but not under entailments I merely could have drawn. This is my answer to the skeptic. I know there is a pencil in my hand; I never inferred from that that I am not in the scenario; so closure never gets a grip and the skeptical conclusion never follows.

(3) I do know ordinary things — that I'm sitting in this classroom, that there is a pencil in my hand.

(4) I grant the skeptic his negative premise: I don't know that I'm not in the scenario. By the scenario I mean this: I was hooked up to a simulator overnight that reproduces this classroom in perfect detail, and everything I've experienced since waking up has been fed to me.

(5) Knowledge is fallible: knowing that p doesn't require ruling out every possible way in which you could be wrong about p.

(6) I can know some things on purely statistical grounds — for example, that the ticket I bought in a very large raffle lost, just from the odds, before I check the results.

(7) Whether someone knows something shouldn't depend on what he happened to get around to thinking about, or on whether he's the sort of person who spells out the consequences of what he believes.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look that way? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to be given up and what giving it up costs me. In particular: does (2) actually do the work I need it to do, or does it either collapse into abandoning closure entirely or break the moment someone asks me to draw out the inference? And if the whole set can't be kept, what single revision saves the most of it — is trading sensitivity for safety the cheapest way out, and what do I lose by paying (4)?
