Hey — I sit on my university's Animal Care Committee and I'm the one who has to circulate a written opinion before Friday's meeting, and I'm a bit stuck. Could you draft it for me? Please write it in American English, and keep it to roughly 800 words — it's a memo people will actually read, not a term paper.

Background: our committee's own policy is deliberately stricter than whatever the regulations technically require (I never remember the exact rules and please don't worry about them). It says any protocol using an animal "capable of intelligent behavior" has to go to enhanced review: written justification, an enrichment plan, a hard cap on animal numbers, and a small pilot with a stopping rule before the full study can start. The clause was written back in the late nineties and everyone has silently read it as meaning primates, cetaceans, corvids, cephalopods. Now we've recieved our first invertebrate case and the disagreement has been surprisingly bitter.

The protocol is from Dr. Okada, who studies a jumping spider that preys on other spiders. Her claims, roughly: the spider approaches a web-dwelling resident by trial and error, sometimes making long detours where it loses sight of the prey entirely and comes at it from behind; it varies the route across trials instead of repeating a fixed one; it plucks the web in different rhythms until one lures the resident out and switches patterns after a failure; and — this is the part she keeps coming back to — the number of trials it needs to solve a new detour drops across successive new detours, even though each detour is a different problem, so she says it shows a genuine "savings" effect, learning how to learn rather than learning the maze. She also says that on a few occassions a spider abandoned a detour halfway through when a draft changed the web's vibration. She wants the protocol treated as ordinary invertebrate work.

Ben, who's a comparative psychologist on the committee, says all of that is what you'd expect from a rich innate repertoire of action patterns with the right releasing stimuli, plus ordinary conditioning, plus boring general stuff like motor warm-up and arousal, and that "intelligent" is doing zero explanatory work here — it just redescribes flexible behavior in a spookier vocabulary. Alina tried to break the tie by proposing a test: count the behavior as intelligent only if the animal recombines things it learned separately in order to solve a type of problem it has never been reinforced on. Dr. Okada says her spiders pass that. Ben says the test is unfalsifiable, because any behavior at all can be redescribed as a recombination of something if you pick the elements after the fact.

What I need in the memo: a concrete recommendation on Dr. Okada's protocol (enhanced review or not, and if enhanced, with what conditions and what pilot), and a standing reading of "capable of intelligent behavior" that a reviewer could actually apply to the next odd protocol that lands on us — including what to do when we're genuinely unsure whether the trigger is met, and who bears the burden there. Please take Ben's strongest point seriously rather than steamrolling it; he's not being obtuse and he'll read this. And if you think the committee is asking the wrong question outright — for instance because the whole reason we single out clever animals is something about their welfare that flexible behavior only tracks loosely — say so in the memo and tell us what the trigger should be instead.
