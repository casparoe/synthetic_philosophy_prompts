me and my mate got into a massive argument last night about quantum immortality and I'm still turning it over this morning. He's all in on it. The setup is the usual quantum suicide thing: a device wired to a quantum event, 50/50, bad outcome you die instantly and painlessly, good outcome you're fine and richer. Run it once or run it thirty times, he says it doesn't matter, because you can never observe your own death, so the only futures you'll ever actually experience are ones where you kept surviving. I pushed back on him for like two hours and couldn't put my finger on whats wrong with it, and part of why is that my own gut verdicts on cases don't line up. So here they are, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures them or if one of them has to go.

1. One run, 50% instant death, ten million dollars if you live. I'd do it. Nothing to do with immortality, that's just an ordinary gamble I'd take.

2. Thirty runs of the same device, ten million if you make it through all of them, so one in a billion by ordinary counting. I would NOT do it. And I'd still not do it even granting him the premise that you only ever experience the branches where you survive, which I sort of do grant. I feel like I'm being inconsistent here but I can't locate which bit is the bad bit.

3. Same thirty runs, except on the bad outcome the machine doesn't kill you, it leaves you alive but permanently unconscious, or locked in, unable to move or communicate. I'm even more certain I wouldn't do it. This is the strongest thing I think I have against him: 'there is some continuer of you' isn't enough, the continuer has to be conscious. But I notice this might just collapse into 'most of the branches you survive into are awful'.

4. Measure. He says you should expect to find yourself in the high-measure branches, the ones with more 'amount' of you however exactly that works under many worlds, so a thin sliver of a branch where you're rich shouldn't count for much. My gut says the opposite: if there's any branch where I'm alive, conscious and rich, that's the one I'll find myself in, and how thin it is is irrelevant. But this sits really badly with 3, where the reason I refuse is basically that the fat branches are the horrible ones.

5. Other people. If HE does the thirty runs I'd grab the device out of his hands and I'd expect to be planning his funeral. I don't apply any immortality reasoning to him whatsoever, I just count what fraction of branches contain a living him. But I'm not doing that for me, and I can't say why the asymmetry is OK.

6. The copy version, which I made up in the middle of the argument: on the bad outcome the machine kills you but first builds an exact atom-for-atom replica of you, which wakes up remembering taking the bet and believing it won. Now there's guaranteed to be someone around with my memories and my bank balance. I don't count that as me surviving at all, it's just a new person. So apparently I care about more than 'there exists some future subject who takes himself to be me'.

7. The version where the money doesn't go to me, it goes to my kids, and they exist and get paid in every branch including the ones where I'm dead. I'd do this one without hesitating. It feels completely different from 2 even though the physics and the odds are identical. Which makes me suspect that in 2 I'm secretly not moved by the immortality reasoning at all, and then I want to know what I am moved by.

So: is there a single principle here, about what you should care about when you branch, about how to weight branches, or about what counts as surviving, that gets all seven or nearly all of them? Please state it precisely enough that I can test it, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Also feel free to tell me one of my cases is confused rather than that my verdict is wrong, if that's what you think. And what does whatever principle you land on say about the one I keep going back and forth on: a one-shot medical thing where a drug either kills you instantly (99%) or gives you fifty more healthy years (1%). Is that just case 2 in a white coat, or is it really case 1?
