I work in policy and programme administration for a public research council that funds astronomy, planetary science, and a small, perpetually contested pot of money for what our mandate calls "big question" proposals. After two years of sitting in on review panels, I have become convinced that we apply opposite rules to structurally identical arguments without ever acknowledging the switch. We treat it as entirely respectable to reason from our own position as observers: that our patch of the universe should be assumed unremarkable so that local measurements can be extrapolated; that the apparent fine-tuning of physical constants is partly explained by the fact that only some values permit observers to exist in the first place; that our catalogues are biased toward the objects bright enough and near enough to be detected, and must be corrected accordingly. Nobody blinks at any of this. But when a proposal argues that the number of humans who have lived before us is itself evidence about how many will live after us — on standard demographic estimates, people who have ever lived number in the low hundreds of billions, and if our position in that sequence is an ordinary draw, then the sequence is unlikely to be vastly longer — the same panel reaches for words like "parlour trick," and I do not think a flawless derivation would rescue it. Last month we also voted down, after an uncomfortable hour, a proposal to adopt a written rule for future panels: where two hypotheses fit every observation we could ever make equally well, prefer the one that predicts more observers, on the ground that our own existence is a datum and is more probable under it. Someone observed that this rule would let us settle, without funding a single new instrument, whether the galaxy is teeming with civilizations and whether the universe is far larger than the part we can see. That, I think, was the moment the room decided it must be a bad rule.

What I cannot work out is what our actual practice commits us to. So: reconstruct the implicit principle that separates the observer-selection reasoning we accept from the reasoning we reject. Is it a distinction between spatial and temporal position, between inferences that generate novel testable predictions and inferences that merely reweight what we already knew, between correcting for selection effects on our data and treating the existence of the observer as itself selected — or is it simply a filter that happens to let through the conclusions we were already comfortable with? Then tell me which of the two candidate principles each half of the practice really instantiates: the one that says to reason as if you were a random sample drawn from some class of observers, or the one that says your existence is evidence in favour of hypotheses on which there are very many observers. Does the practice presuppose one of them, is it compatible with both, or does it quietly shift from one to the other depending on the subfield of the proposal? I am also troubled by how much seems to hang on the choice of the class we sample from — all humans, all observers, all observers in something like my epistemic situation, all observers there could possibly be — since the conclusion swings entirely with that choice; does the practice have to presuppose a way of fixing it, and if it cannot, is the practice arbitrary at precisely that point? A colleague tells me philosophers have names for the two principles and a stock cautionary example about a philosopher who infers that the world must be enormous merely from the fact that he finds himself in it; if that rings a bell, by all means use it, but I do not want references or reading suggestions, and I would rather you not lean on details I could not check.

Finally, two practical questions. If we adopted the observer-weighted rule consistently, what would change in what we fund: which proposals would now pass that we currently reject, which would stop passing, and would the rule have any observable consequences at all, or would it only ever reshuffle our confidence while leaving every prediction untouched? And be honest about underdetermination — distinguish the part of our present practice that strictly presupposes an anthropic principle from the part that several rival views would license equally well, so I can tell whether my panel is sitting on a coherent epistemology or on an inconsistency we have simply never had to argue about.
