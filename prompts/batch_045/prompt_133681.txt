I write a column on evolution and human behavior for a science magazine, and Ive just landed one of those year long fellowships: nine months, about six long features, a travel budget. I want to spend most of it on the mess around adaptationism instead of doing yet another nature-nurture-is-over recap. Forty-plus years after Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper, my corner of the science feed still runs on the same engine: a lab finds a correlation, the press release says this is why we do X, and it lands as an evolutionary just-so story with one hedge paragraph bolted on at the bottom. My editor is sympathetic but thinks anything with the word spandrel in the first three paragraphs is inside baseball, so part of what I need is a defense of why this old argument should change how working reporters handle the daily drip, not just how philosophers of biology sort themselves into camps.

So help me build the agenda rather than summarize the debate. Ive been arguing about this with a friend who's a postdoc in philosophy of biology, and we came up with two homebrew tools that I like but genuinely can't tell are insights or just us cosplaying as methodologists. First, call it the base rate rule: anyone claiming trait T is an adaptation has to name the reference class T belongs to and say what fraction of traits in that class turn out to be adaptations. If you can't or won't, then saying adaptation carries about as much information as saying a drug works without saying how often drugs of its kind work. Second, the lineage swap test: take the adaptive story for T and ask whether anyone would still be telling it if T had turned up in a different taxon with a different ecology. If yes, because the story runs entirely off T's shape and nothing off the lineage's actual history, it's a story about usefulness rather than an explanation of origin. There's a third thing I keep half-noticing, function drift: challenged adaptive stories get rescued by quietly redescribing the function, so the trait never quite gets to be falsified. I don't know whether that's a real phenomenon in the literature or just my annoyance at reading press releases.

What I need is a defended ordering, not a survey. Of the open questions in or around this cluster, which three or four should the six pieces be organized around, and in what order, and I care far more about the reasoning than the list. Concretely: which questions are upstream, in that how you settle them settles several others; which are genuinely open versus which only look open because people keep relitigating something that's basically settled and would make for a filler feature; which are tractable for someone who can read papers and interview people but can't run models, do fieldwork, or get a lab to share data; and which ones, if I got a clean answer, would actually change how I write a news piece about a gene for X or a why-we-Y press release, as opposed to being satisfying to philosophers while leaving my day job completely untouched. Be willing to say if one of my three homebrew ideas is confused or already has a name and a literature I should read, and tell me what would move things up or down the list: what kind of interview, or what kind of result landing in the next nine months, should make me reshuffle the series.
