Last weekend at a friend's birthday thing two people got into an argument that I've been turning over since. A third friend of ours — Marcus, who wasn't there — has about 28k set aside as "betting money," seperate from his savings, and he's found what he thinks are fifteen mispriced markets on an upcoming election: a couple resolve in the week before, most on election night, and one or two only after a possible recount. He reckons he has a five-to-eight point edge on each, he's sizing every one with the Kelly formula on its own, and he's come out with a plan to put roughly a third of the bankroll on the table in total. He also says he wants to turn the 28k into 45k for a house deposit within about eighteen months and then stop. Ines and Toby spent an hour on what's wrong with this picture. Roughly, as I remember it:

1. Ines: the whole framework is misapplied. The Kelly fraction comes from maximizing the asymptotic growth rate of wealth over an endless sequence of bets. Marcus has a deadline and a target — 45k by a certain date, then he's out. With a fixed goal and a limited number of rounds, the growth-optimal strategy and the strategy that maximizes the chance of actually hitting the goal aren't the same thing, and with this few rounds and a target that far away, betting small can't get him there at all, so the right stake could easily come out *larger* than Kelly, not smaller.
2. Toby: two replies. First, you don't need infinity to justify Kelly — maximizing expected log wealth is just expected-utility maximization with log utility, and log is a defensible utility for money on its own, since it cares about ratios rather than absolute amounts. Second, on the target: fine, growth-maximizing and goal-hitting can come apart, but for a favorable bet and a target only modestly above where he already is, the goal-hitting strategy ends up looking a lot like a fraction of Kelly, so in practice the recommendation barely moves.
3. Ines: forget the horizon for a second, the bigger problem is correlation. Those aren't fifteen independent coin flips, they're fifteen bets on one election, and a few of them are nearly the same claim wearing different words. The clean formula assumes you settle one bet, re-count your wealth, and start again. Add up fifteen separate Kelly fractions on outcomes that move together and you're massively overbetting. What he should do is write down a joint distribution over outcomes and maximize expected log of final wealth over that, which will hand him a much smaller total all by itself.
4. Toby: agreed, but that isn't a special correlated-bets fix, that *is* Kelly — maximize expected log wealth over whatever joint distribution you actually face, with the fraction formula as the one-bet special case. Where I think you're wrong is the diagnosis. And separately: Marcus's edge estimates are probably overconfident, but the fix for that is to fix the probabilities, not to shrink the stake. Halving or quartering Kelly is a rule of thumb nobody derived from anything.
5. Ines: shrinking isn't just a hack. Even after he's debugged the probabilities there's still error in them, and the growth rate as a function of stake is asymmetric — near the optimum it's flat, past the optimum it falls off fast and eventually goes negative — so errors toward overbetting cost far more than errors toward underbetting, and averaging over his uncertainty about his own edge moves the optimum down. And there's an earlier problem you skipped: the ring-fence. Log utility is supposed to make ruin impossible because it won't let you bet your last dollar, but Marcus has already carved out 28k and decided not to count the rest, so that protection is gone. Do the calculation over everything he actually has, with a real floor where his life stops working, and the stakes come down again.
6. Toby: the ring-fence point is better than the correlation point, I'll give you that, but it cuts both ways — once you admit his utility isn't log, Kelly stops being a recommendation and becomes one input among several. He cares much more about the difference between 20k and 28k than between 45k and 60k, and log says those two ratios are worth exactly the same. And there's a numéraire issue nobody's mentioned: "growth" here is measured against cash sitting still, but his real alternative is an index fund that also grows, and if you measure against that instead, the log-optimal answer isn't maximizing log wealth, it's maximizing log of his wealth relative to the benchmark — which with an edge this modest might mean betting a lot less, or not betting at all. At which point the honest summary is that Kelly is doing very little work here and Marcus is basically just being told not to be greedy.
