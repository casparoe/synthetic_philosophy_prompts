I'm a novelist, and I've agreed to contribute an essay to a collection on revision — revising a manuscript, revising a judgment, revising oneself — which has unfortunately led me away from my desk and into the literature on diachronic rationality. I've come out of it with a position I can't audit on my own, and since at least one unsympathetic philosopher will read the thing in print, I'd like the audit done properly before I commit it to paper. One thing I should say up front about what I don't want: I have no library access where I write and I don't intend to cite anyone, so please argue from the commitments themselves rather than telling me which named positions in the literature these combinations correspond to, or who has defended which of them.

Here is where I've landed, stated as flatly as I can. (1) I'm a Bayesian of a fairly ordinary kind: credences are precise, they obey the probability calculus, and they are updated by conditioning on total evidence; but I don't think Dutch book arguments do any real justificatory work — I take the serious arguments for all of this to come from epistemic utility and accuracy considerations, with the betting set-ups functioning as useful theatre and nothing more. (2) I do accept what I have seen called Reflection: my present credence in a proposition, conditional on my credence in it at some specified later time being r, ought to be r — and I accept this as a substantive requirement on me, not as an identity that already falls out of the calculus. Moreover, I think the strongest consideration in its favour is the diachronic one: someone who knows how I plan to update can offer me a bet now and a second bet later, once my later opinion is in, in such a way that I am guaranteed a loss if I flout it. (3) I also accept a verdict that looks like it contradicts (2): the morning after finishing a draft I am, as reliably as clockwork, virtually certain that the draft is worthless, and I know perfectly well, having lived through this a dozen times, that this is exhaustion rather than evidence — and I hold that the correct response is not to move my present credence toward that verdict but to protect myself against it: I leave myself a note, I don't open the file, I refuse to act on the morning-after judgment. (4) I take that last manoeuvre to be a genuine way of engaging with a normative requirement rather than a way of evading it: arranging matters so that the compromised later self doesn't get to act is, I think, a perfectly respectable response to anticipated irrationality, on a par with not drinking before making a decision. (5) I'm a permissivist about some of my judgments: on the question whether the two-voice structure of the novel I'm currently writing actually works, I think the evidence is messy enough that any credence in a fairly wide band — say 0.35 to 0.75 — is rationally allowable, and that there is no single rationally mandatory value in that range. (6) Finally, a case I'm inclined to feel easy about: I'm at roughly 0.5 on whether readers will find the secret at the centre of the book too obvious, and I know the first batch of reader reports will push me to something near 0.9 or something near 0.1; it seems to me entirely proper to stay at 0.5 now and plan to be moved, and this looks to me like the clearest case where there is no conflict with (2) at all.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: take these six and tell me where the trouble actually is, ordered by severity rather than all flagged at once. For each real tension, say exactly what it forces me to give up and what that costs me — does the verdict in (3) cost me the requirement in (2), or does it cost me something inside (1)? Does the band I claim in (5) survive the requirement in (2), or does (2) quietly collapse it, and if so into what? Is the norm I'm appealing to in (4) really the same norm as the one I state in (2), or have I substituted a requirement on plans and policies for a requirement on credences without noticing? And I want the merely apparent conflicts separated out and explained: I'm tempted to think (6) is one of them, but check me on that, and tell me whether any of the others belong in the same category and I've misdiagnosed them. If a tension you find only gets its grip from some further claim I haven't actually made, say what I would have to add for it to bite. Finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would leave me unable to say in the essay.
