I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas — and I'm stuck on a piece about the effective-altruism / FTX mess that I think is really a problem in moral philosophy rather than in finance, so I'm handing it to you.

Take the version of the story I stipulate below; you don't need to check anything and I don't want you reciting case details back at me. Sam Bankman-Fried spent a couple of years presenting himself as the movement's biggest funder, giving and pledging very large sums to causes that on EA's own accounting do an enormous amount of good per dollar. He also said, repeatedly and in public, that the plan was always to earn a huge fortune and then give it away. Then FTX imploded, it came out that customer money had been used, he was convicted of fraud, and the organisations that had received the money were left arguing among themselves about whether they could keep it. I don't need to know which org did what — I want the structure, not the paperwork.

What I'd like is that episode analyzed through the lens of moral worth and acting for the right reasons: the Kant-flavoured line that an act has moral worth only when it's done from the right motive, versus the pushback — the Huck Finn-style cases, the thought that what you get credit for tracks the reasons you're actually responsive to rather than the reasons you avow — which says worth can survive mixed or even bad motives. Please don't give me a tour of the literature, and definately no reading list or citations; work the case.

Specifically:

1. Who is the agent and what is the act? Is the thing to be assessed a single donation, the whole giving programme, or a life-plan of the "earn to give" sort? I suspect the answer changes which theory even applies, but I can't see how, and that's the first thing I want nailed down.

2. What were the reasons actually operative, as far as we can tell — and does it matter that we can't tell cleanly? Vanity, status inside a movement, genuine concern for recipients, insurance against scrutiny. My hunch is that the interesting fact isn't that his motives were mixed but that the movement supplied a ready-made vocabulary of respectable reasons, so that doing the good thing and doing it for reasons that play well came apart by design. I've been calling this "motive laundering"; tell me whether that's a genuine phenomenon the theory has room for, or just a journalist's word for hypocrisy.

3. The taint question. Does the fact that the money wasn't his to allocate void the worth of the giving, even though the bednets still get distributed and the good is perfectly real? People I talk to split two ways: the act is fine, the person isn't; versus, the wrongness travels downstream and nothing it touches is clean. Which of those is the theory actually committed to, and where does it go silent?

4. Now run it the other direction. What does the episode do to the theories themselves? My sense is that "worth needs the right reasons" looks strong here — it predicts that the giving was never creditworthy — but that it looks strong for a slightly embarrassing reason, since it would say the same thing even if the fraud had never come to light and the good had been delivered. And the motives-don't-matter side looks like it has no way to register the taint at all. If both of those are true, the episode is telling us something about what kind of thing moral worth is — a verdict on the agent, not a ledger of outcomes — and I want you to say whether that's a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly, or a case the theory was never built for. Bonus: moral-worth talk seems built for individual agents with individual motives. Can a movement or an institution have moral worth, or is that a category error — and if it is, has the entire public argument about this been one?

Gimme something disciplined: who the agent is, what the act is, which reasons are operative, what the relevant counterfactual is, and be explicit where you're stretching and where you're just guessing. Around 900 words, plain English, no citations.
