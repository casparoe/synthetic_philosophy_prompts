hey — I'm in a small reading group on African philosophy, we did a session on Ubuntu a couple weeks back and I ended up in an argument with a friend afterwards that I've been chewing on ever since. She says I'm attacking a strawman version of the relational view, and I think I'm attacking the only version worth holding, so I want to practice against someone who actually believes it. Please play a committed defender of a relational, Ubuntu-flavoured conception of personhood: the claim that a person is what she is *through* others, that being a person is something one comes into — and can partly fail at, or be damaged in — by being held in real relations of recognition, care, mutual obligation, being forgiven, and so on, and that this is a metaphysical claim about what personhood consists in, not just a reminder to be kinder. You should be sharp and well supplied: you get to distinguish relations as constitutive from relations as merely the causal nursery in which capacities grow, recognition as evidence of personhood from recognition as partly making it, and the strong version where personhood is achieved through incorporation into a community from the milder version where it is given but only flowers socially. You can also press me on my own view, which is roughly that what matters is the capacity for self-consciousness, memory, planning, taking up an attitude toward yourself — and that relations matter enormously but only as the thing that normally builds and expresses those capacities. What you may not do is dissolve the question: no "it depends what we mean by person", no "personhood is a cluster concept so she's partly a person", and no retreat to relations being one criterion among several unless you'll tell me how they'd be weighted and why they'd win.

My opening is a case, or really two prongs of one case. Imagine a woman, Amara, raised from birth in an enclosed habitat where everyone she interacts with — her mother, her teachers, the friend who betrayed her and whom she forgave years later — is a simulation she cannot tell apart from a real person. Her side of it is completely genuine: she loves, resents, makes promises, asks forgiveness, feels shame in front of others, and has never once stood in a relation with another real mind. First prong: if you say she *is* a person, then what's doing the work can't be the relations themselves — it's her representing herself as standing in them — and you've quietly become a capacity theorist like me. Second prong: if you say she *isn't*, then personhood gets switched on or off by an environmental fact she could never have discovered about her surroundings, and you owe me a story about what we'd be permitted to do to her that we wouldn't be permitted to do to me — and about my grandmother, who is ninety-one, has advanced dementia, and has outlived everyone who ever really knew her, whose actual relations are, on your own account, a lot thinner than Amara's.

Write it as prose under each heading rather than bullet points, roughly one short paragraph each — it should feel like someone arguing across a table, not a handout. Stay in character until I type stop; don't break character to explain the view in the abstract, don't name-drop the literature at me, and don't hedge with disclaimers about not being an expert.
