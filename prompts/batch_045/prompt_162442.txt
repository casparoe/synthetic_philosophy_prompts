I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice, and next week's session is on the cluster of views that have grown up around the Kelly criterion — the position, sometimes advertised under the label "geometric rationality," that a rational agent should maximize the geometric growth rate of her wealth (equivalently, her expected log wealth) rather than the expected value of her wealth. My students have no trouble reciting the standard dialectic: growth-rate maximization versus one-shot expected-value maximization, near-certain ruin for the leveraged bettor, the remark that expected value is dominated by negligible-probability enormous payoffs in something like the way the St. Petersburg payoff is. What they cannot do is say anything useful once the objective stops being "maximize wealth at infinity." So I want to hand out a dialogue that forces the issue, and I'd like it built around a case I invented for the purpose. Suppose a foundation holds $1,000,000 and has committed to building a lighthouse that costs $10,000,000 in full by a fixed date; any shortfall buys nothing, and any surplus is simply returned. Between now and the deadline it can make exactly twenty sequential bets, one per round, each an independent even-money bet with a win probability of 0.6: stake $x and you get $2x back or nothing. Nobody in the dialogue disputes the setup. Some figures I've worked out and would like the characters to be able to use without pausing to derive them: the Kelly fraction here is 0.2, with expected log growth of 0.6·ln(1.2) + 0.4·ln(0.8) ≈ 0.02 per round, a median final fortune near $1.5M, and (on a log-normal approximation to terminal wealth) only about a 1.5–2% chance of ending at or above $10M — although the relevant event is arguably first passage rather than terminal wealth, since hitting the target lets you stop and lock it in, and I'd like that subtlety handled rather than blurred. Staking everything until four straight wins yields $16M with probability 0.6⁴ ≈ 13%; staking everything on all twenty rounds maximizes expected terminal wealth at about $38M but succeeds with probability 0.6²⁰, roughly four in a hundred thousand, its expectation carried almost entirely by a one-in-twenty-seven-thousand path ending near a trillion dollars. And note that making the lighthouse requires a log-growth rate of ln(10)/20 ≈ 0.115 per round, far above the 0.02 that is the most any stake can deliver — so no choice of stake gets you there "in expectation," and you are in a large-deviation regime.

Give me that dialogue between two trustees, equally competent and neither a strawman: call them Nadia, who thinks in terms of growth rates, time averages, and absorbing ruin, and Owen, who thinks in terms of expected dollars and ensemble averages. I want real back-and-forth — each should answer the other's actual moves, not deliver matching monologues. Owen should get his strongest case: that the lighthouse's beneficiaries are numerous and their utility close to linear in dollars, so expected dollars is the right currency; that if the foundation could split its million across many independent bets per round, or pool with a thousand similar foundations, the arithmetic and geometric pictures converge and the log rule reappears as mere risk management — a symptom of the single-agent, full-reinvestment, infinite-horizon framing rather than a fundamental norm; and that nothing in the charter grants that framing. Nadia should get hers: that this foundation lives exactly one trajectory and cannot consume an ensemble average; that ruin is absorbing and Owen's expectation is a St. Petersburg-style tail; and that the classical literature on favorable games (which distinguished a "minimal time to a fixed target" criterion from a "fortune after a fixed number of trials" criterion, and celebrated the log-optimal policy for the former asymptotically) is simply silent at n = 20, a caveat I want her to concede rather than have Owen extract. Please don't let either of them cite papers by name; the point is the argument, and I don't want my students chasing references.

Where I'd like the dialogue to arrive is my own hazy conjecture, which I want tested rather than asserted: that Kelly is not a general norm but a phase boundary, the answer you get when the growth rate demanded by your goal is exactly at or beyond the maximum attainable rate, so that "grow as fast as possible" is all that is left to say. When the demanded rate falls below the maximum — suppose the cliff were at $1.1M instead, requiring only about 0.005 per round — I suspect the probability-maximizing stake is far smaller than Kelly's, and Nadia should be made to concede that her criterion condemns Kelly as recklessly overaggressive in exactly the case her opponent is least likely to think of. When the demanded rate exceeds the maximum, as with the lighthouse, you are pushed into a large-deviation regime where betting more than Kelly is mandatory, and Owen should be made to concede that "maximize expected dollars" is there a disastrous policy rather than a merely incomplete one. If the conjecture is wrong, I'd rather the characters discover how it fails than confirm it politely. If it helps to sharpen the aggregation problem, you may let them introduce a second, smaller project whose value is linear in dollars, so that part of the pot is all-or-nothing and part is not. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,500 words with light stage directions, keep the arithmetic honest — one of my students will check it in a spreadsheet — and close not with a conciliatory handshake but with a precise statement of what the disagreement now reduces to: whether the average that represents this agent is the time-average of one multiplicative path or the probability-weighted average over many, and how much of the original dispute was really about the objective rather than about the norm.
