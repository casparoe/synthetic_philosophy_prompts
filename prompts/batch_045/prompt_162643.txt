I'm in my last year of high school, and for a senior project I've been trying to work out what our licensing rules actually assume about the people who make decisions. The pattern that puzzles me goes like this.

To get a driver's licence you take a test scored against a fixed standard: you compete with the road, not with the other seventeen-year-olds tested that week. The licence you receive is identical whether you scraped through or got everything right. Much the same thing gates entry to law, accounting and engineering: one standard, pass or fail, no ranking, and afterwards nobody asks what you scored. Yet admission to the professional schools that train people for those same exams is a fierce ranking exercise — law and medical schools sort applicants and take the top slice. And once someone is licensed, what we monitor is mostly inputs rather than performance: continuing-education hours, logged flight hours, years of supervised practice. In negligence cases as well, a professional is measured against what ordinary practitioners do, not against what the most able practitioner would have done.

So we treat competence as a threshold when we license, as a ranking when we allocate training places, and as a quantity of effort when we maintain it.

What does that pattern presuppose about competence and about rational choice? I don't mean the slogan version. A teacher told me the relevant words are satisficing and bounded rationality, but the explanations I keep finding say only that real people can't compare every option, which doesn't seem to be the issue here: these rules are written by institutions with staff, time and data, not by someone in a hurry. So I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed properly. Two candidates I've come up with, which I suspect come apart:

One: the exam can reliably separate competent from incompetent but not good from excellent, so a cut score isn't a compromise with limited information at all — ranking at the margin would be invented precision, and a maximising board would be pretending to a resolution its instrument doesn't have.

Two: the threshold is set the way it is because the licensed person will themselves be satisficing later, on a ward or a building site, under time pressure and with incomplete information. What we need from them isn't excellence on the day but that their stopping rule, when they stop looking things up and act, lands somewhere safe. On this reading the exam tests the safety of a stopping rule rather than the peak of a performance.

Which of those, if either, does the practice strictly presuppose, and which rival view does it quietly rule out? Are the three legs consistent with each other, or does ranking at entry contradict satisficing at the gate? And where is the practice simply underdetermined, so that a threshold view and a view that we are really maximising under measurement costs would both generate exactly these rules?

Finally: if a licensing board resolved to be a straightforward maximiser — graded licences, a cap on the number of people licensed, annual re-examination with the bottom tail revoked — what would actually have to change in the rest of the institution? I can see that access and prices move, but I want to know what else is lost, in particular whether there is something about a fixed published standard — that it gives people a target to aim at and a point at which they may stop preparing — that no maximising substitute supplies.

Please commit. Tell me which justification you think is really doing the work in the practice as it exists, and mark clearly the places where you are going beyond what the practice itself settles and where you would change your mind. I'd rather have a definite reconstruction with reasons than a survey of the options.
