I am writing an essay on Ibsen's *The Wild Duck* for a philosophy-and-literature reading group, and I want to run it against Augustine's treatment of lying in the two treatises he devoted to the subject — the earlier one that lays out the graded catalogue of kinds of lie (eight, as I remember the count; correct me if I have it wrong), and the later polemic addressed to Consentius, written against those who defended lying to flush out heretics. Augustine's position, as I read him, has three parts that matter here: that a lie is saying what you take to be false with the will to deceive, and that this is a sin of the speaker whatever the motive; that every lie is a sin, though the catalogue grades them by how far the motive excuses; and that remaining silent, or withholding what is not owed to this questioner, is a different thing from lying and is sometimes permitted. I want to know what that package does to the play, and what the play does to it.

The reason I think the pairing is worth the trouble is that the falsehood the play is famous for is not obviously an utterance at all. Relling's diagnosis is that the average man needs a life-lie, and that taking it away takes his happiness with it. But in the Ekdal household the falsehood is largely built into the furniture: the studio that was bought for Hjalmar to make a marriage respectable, the attic with its makeshift forest of rabbits and pigeons and the wounded duck, the invention that is never invented, the settled routine whereby no one asks about Hedvig's parentage or about how old Ekdal came to be disgraced. I have been calling that an architectural lie — a falsehood held in an arrangement rather than in any sentence, such that the truth is never asserted and never denied, it is simply made unavailable as a question. I have also been calling the household's version a distributed falsehood: no single party seems to hold the proposition to be false *and* intend another to believe it, because each participant is maintaining a different, mutually compatible piece of the arrangement, and each is also, in some measure, not wanting to know.

So there are three things I want worked through. First: does Augustine's definition have any purchase on a lie of that shape, or does it convict only the parasite-sentence that nobody in this play actually gets round to saying? If the answer is that it has no purchase, that is itself a finding about the definition, and I want it said plainly. Second: on Augustine's own terms, who is the offender here? My suspicion is that the rigour lands on Gregers rather than on the Ekdals — he is the one who publishes what was never his to publish, and Augustine's framework, unlike ours, does not treat the true statement as automatically innocent. But I want that checked against the possibility that Augustine's categories have no room for a speaker who is scrupulous about what he says and merely lethal about when he says it. Third: whether the play functions as a reductio of the rigourist position. It is easy to read Hedvig's death and Relling's counter-practice — he does not tell Hjalmar the truth either, he installs a different and more workable falsehood — as an argument that some falsehood is load-bearing. I want to know how much of that argument survives once you notice that Hjalmar collapses into something already hollow, and how much of it depends on our being more sentimental about Hjalmar than the play is.

Please also tell me where the reading fails. I would rather be told that the attic is a symbol and not an argument, and that the ending is a death and not a refutation, than be handed a tidy fit. And I would like, at the end, whatever you think the play adds to the argument that straight ethics-of-speech papers do not supply — if it adds anything.

Practical notes for the answer. Work from the plot as you remember it; the group knows the play well and I do not need or want verbatim quotation from either Ibsen or Augustine, and I would rather you refrained than improvised any. Aim at something like a thousand to twelve hundred words of prose; a compact table mapping the characters onto Augustine's kinds of lie is welcome if you think it earns its space, but do not pad the piece out to make room for it. Do not reduce the play to an illustration, and do not stretch the definition until it stops doing any work. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
