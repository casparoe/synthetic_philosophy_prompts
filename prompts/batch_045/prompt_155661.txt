I am writing a seminar paper on structural realism, and there is one sentence in the literature and in seminar rooms that I think is doing far more work than it can bear. A visiting speaker last week was defending the view against the charge that it is instrumentalism wearing a mathematical costume, and she closed the discussion with: structural realism is the best of both worlds — it keeps whatever makes scientific realism attractive and gives up only what the history of science forces us to give up. Nobody challenged it, but I am fairly sure it was doing different work for different people in the room.

Please take that sentence apart. Set out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured reading, the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, ordered from the thinnest to the most ambitious. For each, say whether it is trivially true or close to platitudinous, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, empty or question-begging — and, which is what I most need, whether it would actually answer the instrumentalism charge, or only restate the position in more flattering vocabulary.

Be explicit about three choice points the sentence leaves open. First, what structure is supposed to be: the mathematical form shared by successive theories; the relations the theory posits among unobservable entities; the pattern of relations among observable phenomena; the causal or nomological structure; or merely whatever survives changes of formulation among empirically equivalent theories. Second, what the two worlds are supposed to be: scientific realism versus instrumentalism, the no-miracles argument versus the pessimistic meta-induction, or something else — and whether best of both means consistent with both, supported by the arguments for both, or simply not yet refuted by the arguments for either. Third, whether the claim is about the limits of what we can know of the world, or about what the world is made of — whether the objects quietly drop out and only the relations remain.

Then test each reading against a case I will stipulate, since that is where the differences bite. Consider an invented episode from the history of a made-up science, the study of telluric currents. The theory of 1880, call it A, holds that space is filled with a medium of interlocking toothed wheels, and predicts a long family of values to extraordinary accuracy. The theory of 1910, call it B, denies any medium: the influence is a state of space itself. B's equations, however, are formally identical to A's, its symmetries are the same, and every number A predicted, B predicts too — only the story about what the equations concern has changed. Now consider three variants: (i) B's equations are formally identical to A's; (ii) they differ, but A's equations reappear when one of B's parameters is set to zero, so A's numbers are recovered as a special case; (iii) A and B share no equations, and only A's observable predictions are preserved. For each reading of the slogan, say which of the three variants the reading is even about, and whether that variant supports or undermines it.

Where a reading turns on a technical worry — for instance, the objection that a claim restricted to structure may end up asserting nothing beyond the world's having enough objects to instantiate it — state the worry in your own words and assess it rather than sourcing it. Please write in British English. I have the bibliography and cannot follow up references, so no citations, no quotations from papers and no suggestions for further reading: I want the distinctions and a verdict on each.
