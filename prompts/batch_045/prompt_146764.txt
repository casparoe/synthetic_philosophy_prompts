I run a biweekly reading group at work for about a dozen ML engineers and quants -- all fluent in Bayes rule, calibration, proper scoring rules, the usual; none with any logic past knowing that undecidability is a thing. In a few weeks I've got 40 minutes and one whiteboard to get them to see why logical uncertainty is a genuinely different problem from ordinary empirical uncertainty, and what the logical induction setup is actually supposed to be doing about it. Don't explain the topic to me; I want a plan for the explanation.

The part I always botch is the ordering. Every time I try, someone jumps in with "isn't this just uncertainty about whether your hardware or your own brain is reliable," and I get dragged into that before we've even established that classical Bayesian credences are deductively closed and so can't represent uncertainty about a theorem at all. So: what has to be true in their heads before the next thing can land, what order, and why that order rather than another.

Give me the one example that carries the whole session. My instinct is a claim about a computation I could actually start running in front of them -- say, whether the 10^9th bit of the output of a specific ten-line program is a 1, where the program is deterministic, decidable in principle, and slow enough that nobodys reaching 10^9 during the hour -- plus a family of such claims indexed by n, so I have something to point at when I talk about learning to reason rather than just grinding. Say what such an example has to have, and say more about what it has to lack: I suspect it can't have any shortcut that settles it without running the thing, or the whole moral collapses into "do math faster," but you tell me. If you'd use something else entirely, argue for it.

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each one gets defused rather than a list at the end. The ones I expect: "just put 0.5 on everything undecided and Bayes-update when you find a proof"; "the traders are hypotheses and the prices are model averaging"; "so it's a better theorem prover"; "it converges in the limit, so that's as good as knowing"; "probability 1 is certainty, so once there's a proof you're back to plain Bayesianism and nothing was lost." And there's one I never answer well: someone always says you should just be uncertain about your own future reasoning -- put a probability on "I'll end up believing X" -- and isn't that the same thing? Tell me whether that's a confusion to squash early or actually the property the whole thing is built to get, and where it belongs.

Finally: one question at the end that shows me they got it rather than just learned the vocabulary, and one thing you'd openly refuse to justify at this level and say so out loud.
