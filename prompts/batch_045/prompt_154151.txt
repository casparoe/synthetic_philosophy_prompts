I'm working on a long piece on the science of cooperation, and I've been reading a magazine feature on the prisoner's dilemma that I'd rather not name, because I may want to cite it and I don't yet know how much of it I can trust. The core passage runs roughly like this: the dilemma itself is the old chestnut — two suspects questioned separately, each does better by defecting whatever the other does, so defection strictly dominates and two rational players land on an outcome both would have avoided. The escape hatch, the piece says, is repetition, and this is where the story gets interesting. If the same pair expects to meet again with a continuation probability high enough, the threat of later punishment outweighs the one-off gain from cheating, and the required discount rate turns out to be surprisingly modest. Game theorists proved the general form of this in the folk theorem: in a repeated game with a long enough or open-ended horizon, cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium, and indeed almost any outcome can be — so, in the author's words, "the longer the shadow of the future, the more cooperation you get." Axelrod's tournaments then settle which strategy actually performs best: theorists submitted programs to play an iterated dilemma against one another, a four-line entry called tit for tat — cooperate first, then copy your partner's last move — won both of them, and the moral drawn was that niceness pays provided you are also provocable, forgiving and clear. The piece then treats this as the explanation for cooperation in nature: reciprocity works, which is why vampire bats share blood meals and cleaner fish refrain from biting their clients, and why, as the author puts it, "the prisoner's dilemma is the one game where being good is not merely nice but optimal." It adds that subsequent work has only strengthened the case — larger, noisier simulations show populations settling into an equilibrium of reciprocity, and once reputation and indirect reciprocity are added, the case for cooperation gets stronger still.

What I'd like is an accuracy audit rather than a verdict, because I need to know which sentences I can quote without embarrassment. Which parts would a game theorist working on repeated games accept as stated, which would they want to qualify, and which are quietly wrong? I'm especially unsure about two moves the argument makes. First, the step from "cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium" to a prediction about how much cooperation you should expect — my instinct is that a result about what is possible is being quietly read as a result about what will happen, but I can't say whether that matters here or how it interacts with the fact that a repeated game with a known last round is supposed to collapse by backward induction. Second, the step from a tournament among submitted entries to a claim about what evolution produces: a friend of mine who does behavioral economics says the tournament result is really a fact about which programs happened to be in the room rather than about which strategies thrive, and that reciprocity has a well-known bootstrap problem, but he couldn't tell me whether that objection is fatal or merely pedantic. I'd also like to know whether the biological examples are carrying more weight than they can bear, and whether the closing line about noise and reputation quietly changes the subject — my sense is that adding noise does not simply reinforce tit for tat, but I don't know the mechanism well enough to write that sentence.

Finally, the feature ends by drawing a lesson I'd quite like to use if it holds: that since cooperation is self-enforcing whenever people expect to meet again, the job of institutions is mainly to lengthen the shadow of the future — more stable neighborhoods, longer tenures, repeat interaction — rather than to police or subsidize good behavior. Does that conclusion follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or does it rest on the parts that don't? Please be explicit about which of my own hunches you think are right and which are wrong, and flag anything I haven't asked about that a careful referee would insist on qualifying.
