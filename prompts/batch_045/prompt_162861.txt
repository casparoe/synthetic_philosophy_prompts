So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond a couple of podcasts, and we have a Slack channel at work where a few of us argue about whatever episode somebody happened to listen to last. Last week it was epiphenomenalism — the view that experiences are caused by brain states but don't cause anything themselves, like the steam whistle that doesn't drive the locomotive. Two people went at it for about forty messages and I definately can't tell who won, so I want a step by step ruling rather than a verdict.

Ines opened by saying the view eats itself: if my experience of pain does no causal work, then my belief that I'm in pain isn't caused by the pain, so it isn't knowledge of the pain, and worse, I can't have any reason for believing epiphenomenalism either, since the conscious reasoning that supposedly led me there did none of the work in producing my conclusion. Sam said that's too quick and gave the version I half understand: the pain and the belief share a common cause. The neural state N causes the pain and also causes the report-belief, so the belief co-varies reliably with the pain even though the pain never touches it. His analogy was a metrics agent: the error event itself never writes the log line, the agent just samples the same register the error flipped, and the log line is still about the error — knowledge doesn't have to be caused by the thing it's about. Ines came back saying correlation isn't tracking. Her gadget: hold every physical and functional fact fixed and swap which quale is attached, or remove it altogether. Since the epiphenomenalist already says the quale makes no difference to anything, nothing downstream moves, so Sam emits the same 'I'm in pain' report. His belief, she said, is insensitive to what he's actually experiencing — it tracks N, not the experience — so at best he knows his brain state. Sam answered that the swap isn't a real possibility: given the actual psychophysical laws N nomically necessitates this quale, and being able to imagine otherwise is just the same modal illusion that drives the zombie stuff; and he added that even if he lost that point, all he'd lose is knowledge of the intrinsic nature — he'd still know that there is experience, roughly what its structure is, and which one he's in under a physical description. Ines said that fallback is the real retreat: if he only gets to 'I'm in pain' by way of 'my C-fibers are firing,' then the subject's access is no better than a neuroscientist reading a scan, first-person authority is an illusion, and anyway if N is nomically glued to the quale he owes us a story about why a necessarily accompanying state is still causally inert. Sam's last message was that the special access she's mourning is exactly what his view denies, and that her standard overreaches: if knowledge of experience required causal contact with the experience, no theory delivers it, because causing a belief doesn't by itself make the belief represent its cause correctly — so the paradox isn't specific to epiphenomenalism at all.

Can you go through that exchange move by move and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short reason each time, instead of just declaring a winner? Where I'm most lost: whether the common-cause reply actually answers the sensitivity objection or just restates the correlation, whether the silent swap case is a legitimate test of tracking or a cheat (Sam's nomic necessity reply felt too convenient to me, but I can't say why), and whether Sam's fallback about intrinsic nature is a real retreat or just the view stated honestly.
