I'm trying to get clear about a structural point in the debate over the problem of old evidence, and I want an explanation of the conceptual mechanics rather than a survey. Please don't spend words on who argued what, or on anything I'd have to look up; I can't check sources and I don't need them.

Set-up, so that we share vocabulary. Take the problem in its standard form: evidence E is already firmly believed by an agent — P(E) = 1 — at the time a theory H is proposed, and the agent then comes to recognise that H, together with accepted auxiliaries A, entails E. Since P(E) = 1, conditioning does nothing: P(H | E) = P(H). Yet the episode strikes us as confirmatory. Call the three familiar families of response:

(A) the counterfactual or "ur-prior" family — the degree of confirmation is measured by what the agent would, or should, have assigned to H conditional on E from a standpoint stripped of E;
(B) the logical-learning family — what the agent genuinely learns is not E but the entailment claim H ∧ A ⊨ E, which need not have had prior probability 1 for an agent who is not logically omniscient, so that conditioning on it can raise P(H);
(C) the bootstrap family — confirmation is not Bayesian conditioning at all but turns on whether the theory's own derivational apparatus uses E as an input in computing some theoretical quantity, so that E can be evidence for H even while already believed.

The case I can't get straight is an interrupted one. An agent first learns E, then loses it, then regains it. In more detail: at t1 the agent learns E and conditions on it. At t2 the agent suffers a cognitive mishap — amnesia, a corrupted archive, a lost data set — as a result of which E is no longer believed; assume for simplicity that credence in E falls back not to its original prior value but to something middling, say 0.4. At t3, while E is unbelieved, the agent formulates H on independent grounds. At t4 the agent reacquires E and also comes to see that H ∧ A ⊨ E. Had the agent never lost E, there would arguably be no confirmation at all; had E never been learned in the first place, there would arguably be full confirmation. So the case puts pressure on whatever feature the accounts take to be doing the work.

Now make the forgetting leaky rather than clean. Suppose the mishap at t2 destroys the belief that E but not every downstream effect of having held it: the agent's prior over the hypothesis space has already been quietly reshaped in favour of E-friendly hypotheses, and all that survives is a vague memory that something in the neighbourhood of E was once established. The counterfactual in (A) now seems to have no clean target. The standpoint stripped of E is not a state the agent was ever in, and the state it occupies at t3 has already metabolised E. So (A) owes us an account of what the ur-prior is a counterfactual to: a merely possible agent, a merely possible evidential history, or the actual agent's prior with certain traces surgically removed. These give different answers.

What I'd like explained:

1. What each of (A), (B) and (C) says about this case, and how sensitive each verdict is to the interruption at t2. My impression is that (B) and (C) treat the interruption as close to irrelevant while (A) makes it decisive. If that is right, explain why — what is each account taking confirmation to be, such that a fact about the agent's memory history matters to one and not to the others?

2. The deeper issue I'm after: whether confirmation is best understood as a transition in an agent's credal state, and hence as essentially diachronic and agent-relative, so that history and memory are constitutive of it; or as an atemporal relation between a hypothesis and a body of evidence, so that how the agent came to be related to E is beside the point. Say how the leaky version of the case bears on that choice — in particular whether the intuition I reported, that the degree of confirmation should depend on what happened at t2, is itself an argument for the diachronic reading or merely a sign that I am asking confirmation-talk to do a job it was never meant to do.

3. If the three families genuinely come apart here, say which one you think handles the case best and why, and say how confident you are.

One last thing: I haven't come across this variant discussed anywhere, which is part of why I'm asking. If you think it collapses into a case the standard accounts already handle, or if some step in my set-up is confused or underspecified in a way that makes the questions unanswerable, say so plainly instead of answering politely, and tell me what would need to be changed. Please also flag the points where you take your own reasoning to be most likely to be wrong.
