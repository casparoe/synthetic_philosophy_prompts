I have to explain Zorn's lemma to a friend in the economics department, and I want a plan rather than the explanation itself. He was in a seminar last week where the speaker used it to extend a partial ranking of policy alternatives into a complete one, said "by Zorn's lemma" and moved on, and he came away convinced it was a bit of a dodge — a way of assuming the conclusion in Latin. He has asked me to explain what it actually says and whether the seminar speaker got anything real out of it. I know the material; what I don't trust myself to get right is the order.

The constraints: one sitting of about forty minutes, over coffee, whiteboard only, no handout. On his side: sets, functions, proof by induction, and a working familiarity with orderings from preference theory; he has met the axiom of choice only in the "pick one item out of each of infinitely many boxes" form. No partial orders as such, no upper bounds, no chains, no ordinals, nothing transfinite. He is also, I should say, mildly hostile: he wants to know why this isn't just induction with extra steps, and why assuming a maximal element is different from helping yourself to the thing you wanted.

Please give me the teaching plan, specifically:

1. The dependency structure. What has to be defined before what, and what actually breaks if I reorder it — is there any step where the usual textbook order can be improved on for someone who already thinks in terms of orderings? Give me a concrete reason for each ordering decision, not just a sequence. In particular, tell me where "maximal" versus "maximum" should get its own moment relative to the statement of the lemma, and where the clause requiring the upper bound to lie *in* the poset rather than somewhere outside it should be isolated and hammered.

2. The one example. I'm inclined to build the whole thing on the finite subsets of the natural numbers ordered by inclusion: chains of them have unions, the unions are usually infinite and so not in the collection, there is no maximal element, and the hypothesis visibly fails for a reason he can see on the board. Is that the right single example to carry the session, or is there a better one — and either way, spell out what the carrying example must contain (a chain whose only natural bound sits outside the set; a maximal element that is plainly not a maximum; a case where the conclusion is true but uninformative) and what it must *not* contain. I'm fairly sure it should not be a vector space or a field extension, because he'll spend the session fighting the linear algebra instead of the lemma; tell me if you disagree.

3. The misconception map, with positions. Here's my guess at what he'll form, in rough order of appearance: that a maximal element is the biggest or best one; that we are choosing elements one at a time forever and so this is ordinary induction dressed up; that since the axiom of choice is obvious for finitely many boxes it should be obvious here too; that a chain's upper bound existing anywhere is good enough; and, at the end, that the lemma hands you *the* answer — one maximal object — when in this application there will typically be many incompatible maximal rankings and the seminar speaker's conclusion is correspondingly weak. For each, tell me the point in the sequence where it should be headed off and what single board picture or one-sentence move does the work, rather than leaving it to a caveat at the end.

4. Where the word "axiom" should first be spoken aloud. He is going to ask whether the lemma is true and whether using it is cheating, and I want to answer that honestly without opening up independence results, the equivalence with the well-ordering theorem, or anything about models of set theory. Tell me what the honest-in-forty-minutes answer is and where it goes.

5. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing: I'm thinking the derivation from the axiom of choice, ordinals, transfinite induction, and the independence facts. Sanity-check that list.

6. The closing check. I want to be able to tell whether the idea landed rather than whether he is nodding. Give me one question or small task that would settle it — ideally one where he is handed a poset he hasn't seen and has to say both whether the lemma applies and what a maximal element there would be — along with what a correct answer would sound like, and what the characteristic near-miss answer would sound like that tells me he has only picked up the slogan.
