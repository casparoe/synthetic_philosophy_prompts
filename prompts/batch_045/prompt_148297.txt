I am writing a paper section on the puzzle of moral testimony, and I also want to use the case in a graduate seminar where at least two people will say, if they can, that I have merely repackaged one of the familiar easy cases. So I need a case built to a specification, not a survey of the dialectic, and I need it to be able to survive being picked at.

The background is the asymmetry I take as my starting point. If a chemist tells me a solvent is neurotoxic, I may reasonably believe her, act on it, and cite her as my reason; nothing is missing from my epistemic or practical situation. If someone holds a moral conviction entirely on another person's say-so — cannot say why it is true, would not recognise a defeater if it came, and would have believed the opposite had the speaker said the opposite — something looks amiss, and it looks amiss in a way that has no obvious counterpart in the chemist case. I work with three accounts of that asymmetry, stated in my own terms and needing no attribution:

1. The understanding account: the defect is epistemic. What matters, in morality, is not true belief but a grasp of the reasons that make it true; testimony transfers a verdict but not a grasp, so the recipient is left with a belief they cannot support, cannot guide action with, and cannot defend.
2. The evidential account: nothing specifically moral is doing the work. The appearance of defect is the ordinary badness of believing someone who is not, and is not known to be, better placed than you are — and on moral questions we usually have no evidence of the speaker's superior reliability and plenty of evidence of disagreement among apparent equals.
3. The own-judgment account: the defect, where there is one, is practical rather than epistemic — a failure to exercise one's own deliberative responsibility, an outsourcing of a judgement that was the agent's to make and for which the agent remains answerable.

The case I need must satisfy all of the following at once.

(a) Deferring is plainly the thing to do. The recipient would be at fault — obtuse, arrogant, a worse moral agent — if they refused to take the speaker's word for it. Any account that predicts a defect here therefore owes an explanation.

(b) The evidential and own-judgment accounts are both silenced, and silenced for the right reasons. The recipient has positive grounds for regarding the speaker as better placed on this particular question, and is not shirking anything: they reflect on what they are told, they act on it, and they remain answerable for acting.

(c) The belief is nevertheless a genuinely testimonial one. The recipient had previously considered the question and arrived at the opposite view; they could not have reached the new view unaided from the non-moral facts available to them together with their prior moral commitments; and the belief stays dependent — if the speaker were to retract, or were shown to be careless in this instance, the recipient would give it up, even though they can now state the reasons for it.

(d) At the same time, the recipient ends up in possession of everything the understanding account says testimony cannot deliver: they can articulate why the claim is true, they are responsive to defeaters bearing on the situation, and the belief reliably guides their conduct afterwards. So the understanding account predicts a defect that the case does not contain, and it is that account which should come out badly.

Constraints, to block the routes that would make this easy. The speaker must possess no non-moral fact the recipient lacks: both are fully informed about what happened, so the case cannot secretly be about who saw what. The recipient must suffer no impairment of the ordinary sort — no panic, grief, exhaustion, intoxication, or general incompetence at moral judgement — but must be a normally competent, normally motivated adult whose judgement is in working order, so the case cannot be a matter of consulting someone who can see straight while you cannot. The speaker must have no credentials, no professional role, no established track record as a moral authority, and no authority over the recipient. The content must be a specific, substantive moral claim rather than etiquette, convention, institutional rule, or taste, and the case must not stipulate that reasonable people antecedently agree with the speaker. One paragraph, ordinary contemporary setting.

After the scenario, give me the demonstration: what each of the three accounts says about the case and why, and where precisely the understanding account's predicted defect goes missing. Then name the single structural feature doing the work; say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed, and which of the easy cases I ruled out it collapses into; and give me the strongest reply available to a defender of the understanding account — in particular, take seriously the reply that once the recipient has the reasons, the belief is no longer held on testimony at all and I have changed the subject, and say how the dependence condition in (c) bears on that reply.
