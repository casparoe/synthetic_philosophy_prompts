I have no academic background in philosophy. I taught myself a working knowledge of probability years ago for reasons that had nothing to do with philosophy, and for the past year or so I have been reading about Bertrand's paradox on my own and writing up what I make of it in a long essay for a general but patient audience.

The thesis I want to defend is that the principle of indifference is not merely hard to apply but, as usually stated, lacks determinate content. The obstacle is the example everyone uses. In the chord case there are three answers to the same question, and the reply I keep encountering is that the question was simply incomplete: say how the chord was produced, and the paradox dissolves. I think that reply is beside the point I care about, because indifference is not only used to describe a chance set-up that someone has to build. It is also used as a way of setting a prior over a real, already-fixed unknown quantity, where there is no mechanism to specify and nothing left to describe. That is where I want my example to live.

So I need a case built to order, and it has to survive a reader who is looking for a reason to dismiss it.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Ordinary life, and a single paragraph: a purchasing office, a workshop, a clinic, a farm, a fleet depot. None of the usual geometric furniture: no circles, chords, straws, needles, spinners, darts, inscribed triangles, or ruled floors.

2. One unknown quantity, which is not itself a probability, a chance, or a frequency. It is bounded: the agent's evidence confines it to a closed interval, so both candidate priors are proper and nobody can pin the result on an improper prior or on an unbounded range.

3. Two scales, both real. The quantity must be expressible in two units that are both in actual professional use, and you should name the units and say who uses each (for example a ratio and its reciprocal, where one trade prints one number on the spec sheet and another trade prints the other). The two must be related by a smooth map that is not affine, and you should state the map. No gerrymandering: no logarithms, square roots, exponentials, or other transformations invented for the occasion. The test is whether a working professional would print both numbers without embarrassment.

4. The evidence must be stated twice, once in each scale, and you must show that the two descriptions denote exactly the same set of possible values. The agent's information fixes a set, not a measure, and neither scale is privileged by the way the information was given.

5. Apply indifference twice, deriving each result explicitly: state the density each application assigns over the evidence set, state the resulting credence for a single named proposition, and show precisely where the two calculations come apart. Do not appeal to a physical randomising mechanism, because on my reading there is none to appeal to: the quantity has the value it has, and the ignorance is the agent's. If you think a mechanism is genuinely required, say so and say what supplying one would do to my case.

6. The two credences must differ by at least fifteen percentage points, so that no reader waves the gap away as rounding.

7. The failure must have a price tag. The agent must face a concrete decision with money, a contract, or a job at stake, where the two credences recommend opposite actions. Give the payoffs and compute both expected values, so the reader sees a real decision flip.

What I want alongside the scenario:

8. Name the feature of the case that is doing the work, and demonstrate it by removal. First, re-run the case with two scales that are affinely rather than nonlinearly related, and show the divergence disappears. Second, if your pair is reciprocal, re-run it with an evidence interval that is unchanged under the reciprocal map, and tell me whether the gap closes, shrinks, or stays put, and why that matters for my thesis.

9. Then the two dodges. The first is that the agent should go and get more evidence; explain why that does not help here, given that updating requires exactly the prior that is in dispute. The second is the defender's: give me the strongest attack a friend of the principle of indifference would make on your case, and then tell me honestly which of those two replies you think survives.

10. Your own verdict, briefly and without hedging into mush: does the case show that indifference has no determinate content, or only that it needs a supplement, and what would that supplement have to be?

One last thing, because it fixes what would count as cheating. The case must not be rigged: if the only way to make the numbers diverge is to choose a scale no one would use, or to stipulate the credences rather than derive them from the two applications of indifference, I would much rather you told me the case cannot be built to these specifications than that you hand me a rigged one.
