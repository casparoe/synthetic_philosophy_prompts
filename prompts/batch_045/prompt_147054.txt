I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background — I work on infrastructure reliability, spend a lot of my time writing postmortems and fault-tree estimates for failures that are individually unlikely and collectively expensive — and for about a year now I've been arguing with a friend from the effective-altruism/rationalist part of the internet about what gets called expected value fanaticism. It started concretely: I argued against spending a whole sprint hardening a storage system against a data-loss bug I estimated at roughly one-in-a-million per year, and he said the arithmetic obviously favored it once you multiply by the size of the loss, and that my resistance was just scope neglect with better PR. It then escalated, as these things do, to donation allocation and to the Pascal's-mugging style case where someone claims a one-in-a-billion chance of affecting an astronomically large number of lives and asks you to act on it. Neither of us has moved. We've now agreed to stop arguing and write up an adversarial collaboration instead, and I'd like you to draft the document we should produce.

The two positions are these. His is the fanatical one: if an action has higher expected value than every alternative, you should take it, full stop — the smallness of the probability is already in the product, so paying attention to it again is double-counting, and any principled rule for ignoring long shots will either collapse back into maximizing or tell you to ignore real risks like a well-anchored one-in-ten-thousand chance of civilizational catastrophe. Mine is a view I'd describe as borrowed from my day job, and I've never seen it stated in this debate, so I want to see whether it survives: probability estimates have a resolution floor. An estimate assembled from a finite body of evidence can't distinguish probabilities below roughly one over the effective size of that evidence, so an expected value computed from a below-resolution probability isn't a small number, it's noise wearing a number's clothes, and it shouldn't drive action however astronomically large the payoff is — whereas a probability that is anchored in a well-validated model (a fault tree with observed component failure rates, an actuarial table) is above the floor and can. He has three replies he keeps making: that this just relocates the arbitrariness into my choice of floor; that it's risk aversion smuggled in as epistemology; and that on any such view there's a large enough payoff that the noise argument should have been applied one step earlier, so I'm only postponing the fanaticism. I don't want you to adjudicate. I want the joint statement.

Please give me: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section — including what we both already accept, namely that EV reasoning is fine for the anchored cases and that the mugging as stated shouldn't move anyone, so the real question is what separates the anchored from the unanchored; (2) a disagreements table with each side's position stated in wording that side would accept for itself, not in the other side's parody of it; (3) the cruxes — I suspect at least these four: whether the dispute is about small probabilities at all or really about the trustworthiness of the probability's source; whether my view is secretly just concavity in the utility of lives saved (which he rejects as treating people non-linearly) or is genuinely a claim about probabilities; whether there is a difference between what you should do, what you should spend money on, and what you should spend your limited deliberation on, so that "fanaticism" might be three different theses that happen to travel together; and whether any stopping rule avoids both fanaticism and the reductio that you must ignore every long-shot risk; (4) for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both of us would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and a note about which outcome should make which side concede; and (5) an honest flag on any part of this that you think is verbal rather than substantive. Please argue from scratch and define your terms rather than pointing at literature — no citations, reading lists, or summaries of what various authors have said; I can't check them and they'd only be name-dropping. Keep the formalism plain-English but exact, and where a claim rests on a dominance or axiom argument, say which axiom is doing the work.
