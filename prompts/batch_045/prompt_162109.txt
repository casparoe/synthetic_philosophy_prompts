I direct the psychometrics group at a national licensure and credentialing program, and what I need from you is a translation rather than a summary. My working vocabulary is that of educational measurement: item response theory and test information functions, differential item functioning, the nested measurement-invariance hierarchy of configural, metric, and scalar equivalence, the Cleary-style regression definition of predictive bias, Messick's unitary conception of validity, generalizability theory, standard setting, and the standing distinction between bias in what a score means and bias in how a score is used. I know the machine-learning fairness literature only second-hand, through reviews, and I want its central impossibility results reconstructed in my terms, one at a time, so that I can see where they actually bite on a problem I have in front of me. Please do not give me citations or a reading list; I cannot act on them.

Here are the results as I understand them, stated in my own words so that you can correct me if I have them wrong. First: when two groups differ in the prevalence of the outcome that a score is used to predict, then requiring the score to be calibrated within groups — so that any given score level carries the same expected outcome in each group — is incompatible with equalizing the rate at which members of each group are mistakenly assigned to the positive class, incompatible with equalizing the opposite error rate, and incompatible with equalizing the proportion of positively classified individuals who in fact have the outcome. All of them hold simultaneously only in degenerate cases. Second: the tension reappears when one distinguishes the space in which the target quantity lives from the space in which it is observed. Two groups may be alike in the quantity of interest and different in the observed variables, or alike in the observed variables and different in the quantity, and which of the two spaces you demand fairness in determines which form of the conflict you get. Third: reassigning decisions by applying group-specific cut points to a fixed score can buy any single one of these properties, but not all of them together with the score's own rank ordering.

My case differs in three ways from the lending and recidivism illustrations these results are usually given. The same score has two distinct uses with different loss structures: a pass-fail licensure decision, and a diagnostic referral to a remedial course for candidates near the cut score, where a false negative costs a career and a false positive costs a semester. The score's precision is not uniform: our test information function is markedly lower for one examinee group across the middle of the ability range, so an error rate equalized between groups may be equalized on a noisier measure for one of them. And the criterion we validate against is observed only for candidates who were licensed and subsequently hired, which in my vocabulary is incidental selection on the criterion, a restriction of range that is itself a product of earlier allocation decisions rather than a fact about the groups.

What I want is this. Take each of the three results in turn and translate it as faithfully as you can into measurement vocabulary: regression of criterion on test, differential validity, differential item functioning among matched examinees, measurement invariance, decision consistency and classification accuracy at a cut score, correction for range restriction. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all.

I have a suspicion I want tested rather than flattered. It is that the two fields condition in opposite directions. The fairness literature fixes the observed score and compares the distribution of the outcome across groups; measurement invariance fixes the latent construct and compares the distribution of the observed responses across groups. If that is right, then calibration, translated, may turn out to be something my field already has a name for, while the demand for equalized error rates may turn out to be a demand about the consequences of a classification that my vocabulary has no way to express as a property of the test at all — and the impossibility may then be less a theorem about the world than a collision between two things that were never both demands on a measurement to begin with. Tell me whether that is right, and whether the residue points to a blind spot in my framework or to the machine-learning debate having run together two demands that do not belong on the same footing. Where you had to smuggle in an assumption of one framework in order to make a claim look translated, say so explicitly.

Finally, I want your confidence calibrated: tell me what would change your mind, which single consideration would lead you to conclude that the impossibility is a substantive discovery about fairness rather than an artifact of defining fairness as a property of rates, and which step in your translation you think is most likely to be wrong.
