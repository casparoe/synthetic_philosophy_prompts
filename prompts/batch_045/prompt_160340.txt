I'm a senior and I help run the philosophy club at my school. Our faculty advisor, Mr. Adeyemi, teaches a short unit he calls "How well do you know your own mind?", and he runs it entirely on cases: he won't let us cite philosophers or psychology studies, and he takes marks off when we try, so I'd rather you didn't either — argue from the cases themselves and from whatever rules they suggest, not from labels. Over the past few weeks he has ruled on the following, in each case calling the person's report about their own mind either trustworthy or not trustworthy: (i) "I have a headache right now" — trustworthy; (ii) a student is shown two posters, picks one, is handed the one she did not pick without noticing, and explains "I picked it because the composition is better" — not trustworthy; (iii) asked in advance whether he would go along with something cruel if the whole group was doing it, a student answers "I'd never" — not trustworthy; (iv) asked whether the new bell schedule shortens lunch, a student says yes — trustworthy, but with his gloss, "not because he peeked inside his own head; he looked at the schedule"; (v) "I'm not upset about what my lab partner said, I'm just tired" — not trustworthy; (vi) "I can see exactly where I left my wallet, I have a clear picture of it on the shelf" — not trustworthy, because on his view a vivid mental picture is not a memory; (vii) asked a minute later whether the room had felt stuffy, someone says "yes, it felt stuffy" — trustworthy.

The case I care about comes from a new school thing. This term we have 50-minute silent "focus blocks", and a few of us in the club built an app that beeps at random intervals during the block and asks "were you on task at that moment?" — the beep, not the answer, is the point, because we compare the answers with what people actually produced. My friend Priya was beeped six times and said "yes" every time, but in fifty minutes she wrote about two paragraphs. Afterwards she said: "I was concentrating the whole time. I'd know — I'd notice if I'd drifted off." She has no stake in this at all: she isn't graded on the block, she happily admits to goofing off in general, and there is no comfortable answer she is reaching for. I have a small bet with another club member about how Mr. Adeyemi will rule.

What I'd like is this, in about 600–700 words. Set out the two or three rules his seven rulings are jointly consistent with — I can think of something like "trustworthy only for what you are consciously feeling right now", something like "trustworthy only when no comfortable answer is tempting you", and something like "trustworthy only when you would have noticed if it had been otherwise", but give me the cleanest versions you can and say explicitly which of his rulings each one handles easily and which it has to strain on. Then say what each rule predicts about Priya's claim, and give me your best guess at his verdict with the reason for preferring it over the others. Tell me also whether the verdict should change if the claim is narrowed from "for the whole fifty minutes" to "at the moment of each beep", since that distinction seems to me to cut between the rules in a way the whole-block version does not. Say which of his existing rulings is the closest analogue to Priya's, and whether it is really close. Finally, name the further case that would best tell the rules apart: my own candidates are "am I nervous or am I excited, right before a debate?" and "do I actually believe he is lying to me?", so tell me which of those does more work, or propose something better. Please end by saying how confident you are in the prediction overall and which single claim in your answer you are least sure of — my own guess is that the least secure point is whether he would treat attention as a present feeling or as a standing, dispositional condition, but tell me if you think it lies somewhere else.
