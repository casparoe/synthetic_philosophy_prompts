I have no formal background in any of this — I work in IT compliance at a hospital — but at a dinner party a few weeks ago a friend who works on AI safety used a phrase I had never heard, the unilateralist's curse, and I have been turning it over since. As I understood his explanation: when some action is available separately to each member of a large group, brings a modest benefit mostly to whoever performs it, and carries a small probability of a harm that falls on everyone, then the more people who are in a position to perform it, the likelier it is that someone does; and because each person weighs only their own rough estimate of the upside against their own rough estimate of the downside, each keeps concluding the action is worth taking even while the risk, aggregated across the group, has become unacceptable. The remedy he described was a kind of principle of conformity — that if your own judgment says proceed but your peers disagree, you should weight theirs very heavily, since your being the outlier is more likely to be your error than their collective one. It sounded plausible in the abstract, so I wanted to test it on a case I could actually describe from memory, and the one I settled on is the human germline editing affair of 2018: a Chinese scientist, He Jiankui, at a university in Shenzhen, used CRISPR to edit embryos in an attempt to disable a gene that HIV uses to enter cells, with the aim of producing children resistant to infection; twin girls were born in October 2018, with a third child the following year; the work was carried out largely in secret and became public only when it was about to be presented at a major international genome-editing summit in Hong Kong in late November 2018; Chinese authorities suspended his research almost immediately, he was later convicted of illegal medical practice and sentenced to three years in prison with a substantial fine, and the aftermath included worldwide condemnation, a tightening of Chinese law on gene editing and cloning, a good deal of new international standard-setting, and a marked chill on a whole field of research. (My dates and details are from memory and I do not care about precision on them — correct me in a clause if I have something badly wrong and move on.) What I want is for you to run that case through that idea, in both directions: give me the disciplined mapping first — who exactly counts as the group of potential unilateral actors here, since it is not obvious whether it is the handful of labs with the capability, the thousands of IVF clinics, or the world's regulators; what counts as acting versus refraining; what the payoff structure actually looked like, given that the hoped-for benefit was concrete and local while the feared costs were diffuse, delayed, and largely reputational or regulatory rather than existential; what the principle of conformity would have told He Jiankui and his two collaborators to do, and whether it would have told them anything different from what the field's own summit statements and review processes already told them, since the norms were arguably already in place and simply failed to bind him.

Then I want the honest accounting of what the idea does and does not explain about how this ended. It seems to me it predicts the shape of the affair very well — one actor, one irreversible step, costs borne by everyone — but I am suspicious of a theory that fits this well after the fact: would it have flagged the risk in advance, or does it only tell you to be cautious in every domain and then look prescient when one of them blows up? I have two particular suspicions I would like you to test rather than humour. The first is that the argument proves far too much: every driver, every coal plant, every construction firm is a unilateral actor imposing a sliver of diffuse risk on everyone for a local benefit, and if the argument counsels deference to the group in all such cases it counsels paralysis rather than caution. So I want you to say what feature actually does the work — the sheer size of the possible downside, its irreversibility, whether the benefit can be had later instead of now, how much the judgments of the various actors are correlated, whether the benefit is genuinely local or is really shared — and to tell me which of those is doing the load-bearing work in this case and which are decoration. The second is a mirror case I invented, which I have been calling the curse of the veto: imagine the same structure except that what each actor can do unilaterally is block a beneficial action, so the catastrophe comes from nobody acting at all. I cannot tell whether the argument genuinely flips into a warning against caution, or whether the symmetry is fake because harms are shared while benefits get captured, and I would like you to say which, and why.

Finally, the reason this is not just armchair: my friend thinks this sort of reasoning supports building an institution, something like a public registry plus a mandatory cooling-off period, in which any signatory laboratory can flag a proposed experiment and thereby force a delay, so that deference to the group stops being a private act of conscience and becomes a procedure with teeth. I want to know whether the argument he is using actually supports that, or whether institutionalizing conformity could make things worse — for instance by pushing the work to labs and countries that never sign, or by raising the prestige payoff for the first one to defect, or by selecting for exactly the least scrupulous actor as the one who ends up doing it.
