Here it is, more or less as i wrote it:

(1) To understand a sentence you need more than knowing which words tend to go with which other words. you need some grip on what the words are about — on what would have to be true in the world for the sentence to be true. call this the hookup condition: the words have to hook onto things, not just onto each other.

(2) The swap test. take a huge parallel corpus — the same documents in english and, say, finnish — and do a systematic substitution: replace every word-type in both languages with its own made-up symbol, keeping every occurrence exactly where it was. Every distributional fact survives untouched: which symbols co-occur, how often, in what order, across which aligned sentences. So a purely statistical learner trained on the swapped corpus ends up in exactly the same internal shape as one trained on the real one, and pairs off sentences just as well.

(3) In the swapped corpus the symbols are just placeholder marks. So the learner isnt hooking onto dogs or corkscrews or anything else: it cant tell the real language from the swapped one, because there's no statistical difference to tell them apart — and if it cant tell them apart, it doesn't represent the difference either.

(4) A statistical translation system is exactly such a learner: everything it gets out of training is distributional facts of that kind. So no such system meets the hookup condition, no matter how much data or how fluent the output looks. call the result hollow fluency — sentences that look right without being about anything.
