Bit of background: I retired from a systems physiology lab a few years back (respiratory control loops, mostly) and I've been working through moral philosophy on my own since, no supervisor, no seminar. I've just got to Strawson on the reactive attitudes, and I think I see the move — stop asking wether responsibility is compatible with determinism and look instead at what holding someone responsible actually consists in, i.e. our being disposed to resentment, gratitude, guilt, and the rest. What I can't tell is whether that's an answer to the old question or a well-mannered refusal to answer it.

So: write me a dialogue, maybe 1200–1500 words, between someone who thinks Strawson has dissolved the problem and someone who thinks he has only relocated it. Both of them sharp, neither a caricature. The skeptic should grant the phenomenology — yes, the reactive attitudes are real, yes, they're woven into ordinary interpersonal life — and still think something central has been dodged; maybe by arguing that the attitudes have their own internal standard (they purport to track the quality of will someone expressed in acting) and so can be assessed by how well they track it, in which case "we cannot help having them" is no more a defence than it would be for a visual illusion. The Strawsonian should get real replies, not just "you can't step outside the practice" on a loop. I want actual back-and-forth, each of them answering the other's last point.

Then have them pushed on three cases I made up, because that's where I got properly stuck. Suppose you could titrate the affective component of the reactive attitudes while leaving the cognitive tracking of quality of will completely intact — a dimmer, if you like. (1) A whole community dials itself to zero and finds it still registers perfectly well who meant well and who did not. (2) People who feel guilt but never resentment toward anyone else. (3) People who feel resentment freely but no guilt at all, and who don't expect guilt from the people they resent. Which of Strawson's claims, if any, does each case break, and which character can absorb it and at what cost? My instinct from control theory is that the reactive attitudes are the error signal in a loop that maintains reciprocity, so zeroing them is like switching the controller to manual — but give the Strawsonian the chance to say whether that analogy helps her or quietly concedes the skeptic's point.

Finally, in your own voice: say which of the two characters' claims you think rests on the shakiest ground, which of my three cases does the most real damage, and what would change your mind — the argument, or the result about the cases, that would flip you the other way.
