For a graduate seminar in metaphysics I am assembling a diagnostic instrument on the sperm-and-egg question: could you, the person considering it, have originated from a numerically different sperm and egg? Please write a decision tree that takes a student who already accepts a coarse-grained origin essentialism — she grants that she could not have been born of two entirely unrelated parents, and not have been a member of a different species — and leads her to a named position on how fine-grained her origin has to be.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names: choose the separating questions first, say which combinations of answers they make available, and only then locate the actual positions among those combinations, marking any combination nobody occupies with a one-line diagnosis of why it is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended). Use six to ten decision nodes, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which view it leads to, with every internal node labeled by the claim that separates its children. The diagnostics must be concrete cases, and all of the following must appear: monozygotic twinning, where one zygote formed from the same gametes later splits into two embryos; a numerically distinct sperm or egg whose genome has exactly the same base sequence as the actual one, which recombination makes conceivable even if it is astronomically unlikely; a gamete whose genome differs from the actual one by a single base pair; the same gametes uniting a day earlier or later than they did; the same nuclear genome developing in a different egg cytoplasm, with different mitochondria and different initial chemical conditions; a corrective edit made to the zygote at the one-cell stage; and a case in which a child of the same two parents, conceived at a different time, would have been raised in her place, next to a variant in which no such child would have existed at all.

Give six to eight leaves. Include the view that the particular gametes are essential, the view that the zygote is essential although the gametes are not, the view that only the genetic sequence matters, a "close-enough origin" view that accepts vagueness and no sharp boundary, an anti-essentialist view that handles modal talk about oneself via a closest candidate, a no-fact-of-the-matter view on which identity across these cases is indeterminate, and a primitivist or haecceitist view on which nothing about matter, genome, or origin settles the question. Label each leaf with the standard name where one exists and "unnamed" where none does, add one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any two leaves your nodes cannot separate. Please write your answer in American English.
