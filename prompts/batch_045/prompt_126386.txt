My flatmate and I have a bet going. I'm writing a literature-review chapter on the epistemology of testimony and I've been tabulating every worked example in one philosopher's papers where she actually delivers a verdict — I won't name her, because the bet is about whether we can pattern-match her, not about who she is. Her record as I have it: (1) a tourist asks a stranger on a station platform which platform the 14:20 leaves from, the stranger says platform four, it's true, no reason for doubt — knowledge. (2) a friend who reliably inflates her own achievements says something self-flattering that happens to be true — no knowledge. (3) an overnight train, a stranger recounts her childhood in a village in a country the hearer knows nothing about, nothing is checkable, there's no track record, all of it is true — knowledge. (4) an otherwise scrupulous colleague is asked something she has no information about, shrugs and guesses, and happens to be right — no knowledge. (5) someone whose entire picture of the world, including everything she "knows" about how trustworthy people generally are, comes from a single informant; the informant is reliable and what she says is true, and the hearer does have ordinary perception and memory of her immediate surroundings — knowledge. (6) a speaker with excellent evidence asserts something true, but the hearer has been told by someone she trusts that this speaker is a compulsive liar, which is false — no knowledge. My supervisor reckons (5) already settles that she's an anti-reductionist of the default-entitlement sort; I think it settles much less than that, since the hearer still has perception and memory to work with.

Here's the case she has never discussed, as far as I can tell. I use one of those answer-engine things — a language model that produces confident prose answers. Over about six months I checked roughly eighty of its answers to obscure factual lookups (who introduced what bill, which firm built which bridge, that kind of thing) against a library database I trust, and it was right seventy-six times. Then I asked it something I have no way of checking and no independent background on: the name of the person who introduced a particular piece of legislation in a state legislature in the early 1930s. It gave a name. A week later I found out from a completely reliable human source that the name was correct. Did I know it, at the moment I read the model's answer? There was no individual human asserting to me, no one in a position to know it at the time apart from whoever compiled the record, and the model has no beliefs, no understanding, and no sincerity or lack of it. My flatmate says she'll say no knowledge, because on her reading of the record the thing that does the work is warrant flowing from a speaker who has it — that's what (4) is really about — and here there's no such speaker, so the model's output is at best evidence I'd have to reason from. I think that's too quick: (3) shows she'll grant knowledge with no positive reasons about the source at all, and in this case I have better evidence than that.

So: what's your verdict on the bet? More usefully, give me the two or three rules that fit all six verdicts equally well — some weak global reductionism where the inductive base has to be somewhere in the hearer's evidence, some default-entitlement-plus-transmission view, some "hearer needs no positive reasons but the source has to be reliable in the relevant domain" hybrid — say what each predicts about the answer-engine case, which one you think she's actually running and why, and what she'd be committing to either way. (If she says knowledge, does she now owe us an account of what makes something a source, given she denied knowledge in (4)? If she says no knowledge, what is my seventy-six-out-of-eighty track record supposed to be doing?) And which further case would actually separate the surviving rules? I've got two candidates and I'm not sure either works: the same system where I've never checked a single output myself and only have the company's marketing and a friend's word for its accuracy; and a human relay — someone who is otherwise a scrupulous speaker, who reads the model's answer out to me without understanding it or endorsing it. Do either of those discriminate, and is there a better tie-breaker I should be feeding her?
