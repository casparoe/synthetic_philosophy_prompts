My younger brother died of a glioblastoma last spring, at 34, with a wife and two kids under seven, and I've been going to the grief group at his church mostly so my sister-in-law doesn't have to sit there alone — I was raised Catholic but I'm pretty lapsed and I don't go in for any of this normally. The group leader keeps saying some version of "God lets it happen because he can bring a greater good out of it", and it's obviously doing several different jobs at once. She used it on me the night I blurted out that no decent person who could have stopped that tumour would have let it happen. She used it again a few weeks later about someone's mother wanting to stop chemo, in the sense that you shouldn't cut short what God might be doing with the suffering. And sometimes she swaps in "it builds character" as if it were the same point. I don't want the standard apologetics package; I want the sentence taken apart.

Here's what I've been turning over on my own, so you can see the axes I care about rather than starting from scratch. One: is the good supposed to be the *point* of the evil — planned upstream, the reason God permitted it — or is it a *repair*, something God brings about downstream in response, in which case it's a lovely thought but doesn't seem to explain the permission at all. Two: how strong is the link supposed to be? The good is logically impossible without that very evil; the good is causally impossible without it; the good just wouldn't in fact have happened; or merely God couldn't have got that good more cheaply. Three: who is the good *for* — my brother, the kids, the rest of us, the universe in aggregate, God? Four: the triviality worry. If God is supposed to be all-powerful, then of course he can bring good out of anything, so on one reading the slogan is true almost by definition and explains nothing; I want that reading put on the table and named as a platitude if that's what it is. Five: the quantity problem. Say the good is courage or the family pulling together. Courage needs *some* danger but why this much, why that particular death, and if the value is really in the response, why couldn't God have got the response to a milder evil, or even to one the person merely believed was happening? Six: the retreat to "we can't see the greater good" — that seems like a different kind of claim entirely, one about our cognitive limits, and I can't tell whether it's compatible with confidently asserting there is one, or whether it ends up saying we can't call anything gratuitous, which I really don't think I want to say about, say, the Holocaust.

So: lay out the menu of precise claims the slogan could be making, the way you'd lay out readings of "ought implies can", and give each a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false — plus a note on which reading the leader probably means and, crucially, which readings would genuinely support her first use (answering why God let it happen) and which, if any, support the second (don't cut the suffering short). I want to know which readings do real theodicy work and which are just consolation dressed up as explanation. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble and no restating my question, then give me the menu; a table would be ideal — reading / what exactly it commits you to / verdict / does it support use one, use two, both, or neither — and then a short paragraph each on the quantity problem and the "we can't see it" retreat, since those are the two I keep getting stuck on.
