I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and the chapter I'm currently writing is about what makes pain bad for the subject. I've got stuck on a distinction that I think keeps getting run together, and I'd like you to map the conceptual space for me rather than adjudicate it. The standard dialectical use of pain asymbolia — subjects who report feeling the pain, locate it, and rate its intensity, but say it no longer bothers them — is to conclude that pain has a separable sensory and an affective dimension, and hence that pain's badness resides in the affective one. I want you to tell me whether the following three dissociations are genuinely distinct, and what each would establish if it obtained: (i) full sensory character retained, affective unpleasantness gone; (ii) sensory character and unpleasantness both retained, but the motivation to make the pain stop gone; (iii) a case I want to put on the table, which as far as I can tell isn't handled separately in the literature — sensory character, unpleasantness, and the desire for the pain to stop all retained, but the subject reports that the pain has stopped mattering to them. They mind it while it lasts, they pull their hand off the stove, they call it unpleasant, but they deny that it counts against their life or gives them any reason to take an analgesic, and they would refuse the analgesic if it were offered — not out of stoicism or a wish to appear tough, but because on reflection there seems to be nothing here worth fixing.

Here is the worry I want explained. If (iii) is genuinely conceivable, then unpleasantness cannot simply be identical with badness-for-the-subject; and if that is right, then establishing (i) would not have located the badness of pain at all, only one of the things that normally travels with it. Walk me through whether that argument actually goes through, and then give me the strongest reply available to someone who wants the badness of pain to consist in its felt unpleasantness. I'm after the conceptual structure, not the empirical or clinical detail, and I don't want citations or suggestions for further reading — just the argument. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would in an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or lists.
