I am in the final year of an economics degree, and alongside my coursework I am taking a philosophy seminar on the ethics of deception. This week's question is whether lying is morally different from merely misleading someone. Assume I have read the seminar material: I do not need the debate explained in its own terms. What I want is for you to restate it in the vocabulary I actually work in, and then to tell me what the translation fails to carry.

On my vocabulary, so you can calibrate: I am comfortable with sender–receiver games over a state space — cheap talk, where messages are costless labels whose content is assigned by the equilibrium rather than by the modeller; costly signalling in the Spence style; verifiable disclosure and the unravelling argument; and information design, where a designer commits ex ante to a signal structure, understood as a stochastic map from states to signals, with the receiver's incentives summarised by an obedience constraint, so that what matters is the induced distribution over posteriors rather than any individual message. I am also used to distinguishing a sender's ex ante choice of signal structure from an ex post deviation within it, and to reasoning about off-path beliefs.

Please do three things.

First, restate as faithfully as you can both sides: the claim that lying is morally distinctive, typically on the ground that assertion is governed by a norm that mere misleading does not violate, and the sceptical claim that the two differ only in technique and not in moral status. Give each as a claim about states, signals, constraints and payoffs, not as a claim about words.

Second, try to find formal homes for two things I suspect are doing the real work. (a) Falsity: in my models a message is just a label, so saying something false is not yet a property of anything. What would have to be added — an exogenously fixed truth-conditional map from messages to events, a verifiability constraint, something else — before a lie is representable at all? And is adding it a translation or an import? (b) A conjecture of my own, which I would like you to test rather than endorse: that the difference is not fundamentally about truth but about records. A lie, on this view, inserts a false entry into something that persists and that others will rely on and act on later; misleading leaves the record true but thin. The wrong lies in corrupting a shared object and in the cost of repairing it, not in the momentary effect on one hearer's beliefs. Is there anything in my vocabulary that a record could be — a public signal surviving across periods, a commitment constraining later equilibria, a belief that third parties treat as given? If the best you can do is identify the record with a receiver's posterior or with a downstream payoff, say so and say what that identification costs.

Third, the residue report. Tell me which moves in the philosophical debate translated without loss; which translated only after you quietly added something to my framework — flag every such addition explicitly, and pay particular attention to places where the translation smuggles in welfarist evaluation, since the non-consequentialist side holds that the wrong of lying is not a matter of outcomes and a payoff-based translation may simply be blind to that claim; and which claims have no counterpart in the vocabulary at all. Then give me your view on what the residue indicates: is it a defect of the economic vocabulary, or evidence that the distinction philosophers defend is thinner or more context-dependent than it looks? One specific request within this: try representing the norm against lying as a restriction on the set of admissible signal structures rather than on payoffs, and tell me whether that restriction and the corresponding restriction against misleading have the same formal shape. I have a suspicion that one is a state-by-state constraint and the other a constraint on the structure taken as a whole, but I would rather you derived the answer than agreed with me.
