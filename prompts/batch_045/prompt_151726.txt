The situation: I'm putting together a grad reading group session on the frame problem and I've hit the wall that everyone seems to hit. Here's the story as I tell it. It comes out of situation-calculus style AI — McCarthy and Hayes defined it in their 1969 paper — and the standard illustration is the robot that has to get its spare battery out of the room where a bomb is ticking; the battery is on a wagon with the bomb, the obvious plan tows the wagon out, and the bomb comes along with it. The formal version is about frame axioms: you specify what an action changes, and you don't get for free that everything else stays put, so you need an axiom for every action against every condition it leaves alone. There are known formal fixes — circumscription and it's descendants — and the Yale shooting scenario is the famous reminder that the first attempts gave the wrong answers. By the end of the eighties a lot of people thought the thing was basically done. Meanwhile the pessimistic reading, the one usually attached to Fodor, takes the same phenomenon as an argument that central, non-modular cognition just isn't computationally tractable — a claim about creatures like us, not about a programming style. And the two camps have been repeating themselves at each other ever since; nothing anybody says seems to move anybody.

My hypothesis, which you should feel free to destroy, is that they aren't arguing about one thing, and that the word solved is doing most of the work. I've been splitting it three ways: (a) the update problem in the narrow sense — which of the indefinitely many things I believe need re-checking when I act; (b) the qualification problem — how many caveats have to be ruled out before I'm allowed to act on a rule at all; and (c) what I've started calling the frame-retirement problem, which I don't see discussed much: not which items inside the frame stay true, but when the whole frame you're working in has become the wrong one. My stock example is a nurse who's been on the same ward for twenty years and gets made ward manager — the frame that made her good at the job is now partly what's in her way, and nothing in (a) or (b) tells you that. I also think solved has at least three senses floating around: good enough that engineers stopped worrying; formally characterised and provably handled inside some formalism; and explained, in the sense that we know how creatures like us pull it off.

So: is this a verbal dispute, two groups answering seperate questions under one label, or a real substantive disagreement about whether there's any tractable notion of relevance at all? Does my three-way split hold up, or does (c) collapse back into (a) under presure? Where does the current situation leave it — does a system like a large language model, which seems to track relevance holistically without representing a frame at all, dissolve the problem, relocate it (say into what you decide to put into a finite context window), or just bury it under brute-force statistics? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like — a formal result, a benchmark, an architecture, a sharper distinction, a different question — or is this one of those debates where no resolution should be expected, and the honest answer is to explain why?
