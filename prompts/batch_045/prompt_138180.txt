I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether treating religion as a natural phenomenon — that is, explaining why human beings in nearly every society form beliefs about invisible agents, perform costly and often painful rituals, and organize communal life around sacred norms, in terms of ordinary cognitive and social machinery — has any bearing on whether the religious beliefs in question are true.

His position, as I managed to reconstruct it, was that the debate is misframed because "religion has a natural explanation" collapses three separable claims, and only the last of them could do any debunking work. The first is that religious belief is produced by ordinary mechanisms: hyperactive agency detection, responsiveness to costly displays as signals of commitment, coalitional and reputational psychology, and so on. The second is that those mechanisms would churn out roughly the religious beliefs we actually observe whether or not those beliefs were true. The third is that once we have the natural story, the truth of the religious claim adds nothing to the explanation — it is screened off rather than contradicted. He argued that popular versions of the debunking argument slide from the first claim to the third without noticing, and that nobody has actually established the third.

I pressed a different objection. If the third claim really is the load-bearing one, the same structure seems available against moral judgment, mathematical intuition, and even ordinary perceptual belief, each of which also has a well-developed natural story about the machinery that produces it. I couldn't tell whether that is a reductio of the argument or merely a fact about how much of what we believe is generated by equipment that was not selected for tracking the truths in question. We got no further than that.

I now have to make a version of this concrete. I sit on the program committee of a small foundation that is spending itself down and will make one final round of awards: roughly four two-year postdoctoral fellowships, in philosophy and religious studies rather than empirical psychology or anthropology, on the topic of what follows from treating religion as a natural phenomenon. I have to propose and defend an ordering, and I would rather be argued with than agreed with.

So: which questions should those four fellowships go to, and in what order? Please lead with your ordering in the first two or three sentences, then defend it. I am not asking for a survey of the field or a reading list, and I would rather you not spend words on citations; I want the reasoning. For each question, say why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable in two years by someone working conceptually with no new empirical data, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what kind of result — a counterexample, a successful formalization, a shift in what people take to be a live option — would move it up or down the list. Please also say plainly which of the supposed implications of this literature are overrated or do not follow: implications for religious practice, for education and public policy, for how someone who accepts the argument should then live, for whether "religion" is even one thing to be explained rather than a family resemblance. Tell me whether my friend is right that the screening-off claim is doing all the work, or whether some fourth thing is. And include at least one question that neither of us thought of, rather than only adjudicating the disagreement I have described.

Attach to each item a rough confidence level in both the claim and its placement in the ordering — the items may be well founded while the ordering is not, and I want to know which. Close by naming the single claim in your answer that you are least confident of and the consideration that would most quickly move you on it. Please keep the whole response under 900 words.
