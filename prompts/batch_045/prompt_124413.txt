I'm assembling a problem set for an advanced undergraduate seminar on population ethics and suffering-focused ethics, and I need a purpose-built case that separates two things my students keep collapsing into each other: they assume that any view that gives extra weight to suffering is basically the same as a view on which suffering has lexical priority. I want a decision problem engineered so that the disagreement is not one of degree. Please work with the following four views, which I am stipulating for the exercise (no need to attribute them to any author, and please do not cite or look for literature):

U: a single cardinal hedonic scale; everyone's pleasures and pains summed at face value, no thresholds, no weights.
NU_w: as U, but each unit of pain is multiplied by a large finite weight w = 100 before summing.
LTNU (lexical threshold): there is a threshold T on the intensity of a single episode of suffering. Below T, pleasures and pains trade off at a finite rate (take it to be 1:1). At or above T, that suffering has lexical priority: no amount of pleasure anywhere in the population, however large, compensates for it. Options are ranked first by whether they involve any above-threshold episode, and only then by the below-threshold arithmetic.
NU_lex: as LTNU, but with T set at zero, so that suffering always lexically dominates pleasure.

The case itself: set it in an ordinary institution that actually makes this kind of call — a hospital ethics committee, a prison healthcare contract, a school district, a regulator — in one paragraph, with no science fiction, and with both options being things a real committee could vote on. Keep the numbers human-scale and motivated: at most a few hundred beneficiaries, each getting a benefit an ordinary person would actually notice (no dust specks, no billions of people, no astronomically large payoffs), and add one sentence on why the magnitudes are credible. Concretely, one option should be a reform that delivers those benefits but leaves one identifiable person undergoing an episode of suffering that is clearly above T while clearly not being the worst thing imaginable (think badly managed post-operative pain rather than torture); the other option keeps that person's worst episode just below T but forgoes the benefits. Everyone who exists in one option exists in the other, so this is not a rerun of the mere addition paradox or the repugnant conclusion.

Required verdict pattern, which is the whole point of the exercise: U and NU_w (w = 100) both choose the reform, while LTNU and NU_lex both block it. Show the arithmetic for each of the four views, and explicitly check that a finite weight of 100 does not flip the weighted theorist, since if it does the case fails to show that lexicality is doing any work. Then give me three variants, each changing exactly one thing: the same total quantity of suffering spread across many people with every episode below T; the same total spread across many episodes in the life of a single person, each below T; and the original concentrated case. Report LTNU's verdict in each, and say which choice about the site of the threshold — momentary experience, person-moment, person at a time, whole life, population total — is generating the answer, and whether that site can be chosen non-arbitrarily.

Finally, three diagnostics. First: name the single feature of the original case that does the work, and show what happens to each verdict if you delete it — cap the number of beneficiaries, push the concentrated suffering below T, or concentrate all the benefits in one person. Second: give the strongest reply available to a defender of LTNU. I expect something in the vicinity of the separability of persons, or the claim that thresholds are not aggregative and that mild experiences do not sum across people the way the case assumes; give the best version you can, and then say honestly whether that reply pushes the view into NU_lex or back down into NU_w, and why that matters. Third: one paragraph on what the case actually establishes about commensurability — whether it gives us any reason to treat pleasure and pain as two dimensions rather than opposite ends of one, or whether it only shows that a lexical-threshold view owes us an account of what counts as one unit of suffering, and what a defender would have to say about the individuation of pains to supply it.
