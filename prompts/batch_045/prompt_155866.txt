Bit of context: about six weeks ago I started as a work coach at a Jobcentre-style benefits office — generic one, don't worry about which country — and next month I've got to give a ten-minute talk to the new intake on "what conditionality is actually for." I also have my first sanction referral sitting on my desk and I havent signed it yet, so this isn't abstract for me.

What I need is a case, not an essay. One paragraph, something that could land on my desk on a Tuesday morning, with real bureaucratic texture — a claimant commitment, a mandatory placement, a "good reason" that I'm the one who has to accept or reject. No trolley problems, no sci-fi, no saintly claimant and no scrounger cartoon. The claimant should be somebody my audience instinctively wants to help, and her refusal has to be a genuine, informed, voluntary refusal: not a letter that never arrived, not undeclared cash-in-hand work, not a mental-health crisis, not a form filled in wrong. No fraud anywhere in the story either, I get enough of that. And the thing she's refusing has to be a condition a decent scheme would actually run — not a punishment dressed up as work, not something degrading, not a placement that obviously displaces a paid job. If your case only works because the state is being monstrous, it's useless to me.

Here's my own crude bit of jargon; use it if it helps. Most conditions seem to me to produce one of two things. Contribution goods: the scheme gets something it can actually use, and the claimant is doing a real share of the cooperative work. Compliance goods: nothing gets produced that anybody wants — the point of the condition is that obeying it is evidence you're still trying and refusing it is evidence you arn't. Real conditions are usually a mix, and I want yours to be a mix too: the refused placement does some real output (sorting donations in a charity warehouse, say), but everyone in the office knows the reason it's in her commitment is mostly the second thing.

Now the part I can't get to work. I want three arguments to come apart into three different answers, and to come apart because of what the arguments actually say, not because I rigged it:

- reciprocity / fair play — the "you take out of a cooperative scheme, so you owe a share back when you're able" argument, the one people reach for when they want to sound tough;
- deterrence — the "conditionality is what stops claims being made when work is available, and what keeps a requirement credible" argument;
- paternalism — the "inactivity is bad for you, the activity is good for you, we're doing this for your sake" argument.

And there are only three things I can actually do: (A) drop the requirement and pay her in full, (B) keep the requirement and tighten it but take no money off her, (C) keep the requirement and withdraw benefit for a fixed period.

I want reciprocity to land on A, deterrence on C, and paternalism on B — paternalism peeling off from both of the others, siding with reciprocity about the money and with deterrence about the requirement. Note the direction I'm asking for: the argument people use to be harsh is the one that has to say pay her, and the argument people use to be kind is the one that has to say make her work. If you can't get all three at once without cheating, say so plainly and tell me which of my specs has to give — I'd much rather know than get a case that smuggles the verdicts in.

Then, after the paragraph: for each of the three arguments, show the reasoning that gets it to its letter and no further. Don't just announce "reciprocity says A because she's already contributed enough" — I want to see where inside the argument the work is being done. After that, tell me which single feature of the case is carrying the load, and what happens to each verdict when you flip it: make the refused thing a genuine offer of paid work instead of a placement; make her twenty-five with no contribution record instead of near the end of a long one; make her refusal invisible to the other claimants instead of something everybody in the waiting room knows about. Then give me the strongest attack a defender of whichever argument comes out looking worst would make on your case — the real version, not a strawman.

Last thing, and it's the bit I actually care about: be committed. At the end tell me flat out which of A, B or C you'd sign off on if you were me on Tuesday morning, and why, and don't hand me the menu and leave me to choose. If you think one of the three arguments just wins here, say which one and say where the other two go wrong. And flag anywhere you've had to lean on a contestable reading of one of the three, because there'll be at least one person in that room who's read the philosophy and will call it out.
