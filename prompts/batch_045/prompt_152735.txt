I'm in a final-year seminar on Wittgenstein and epistemology — we've spent the last three weeks on *On Certainty* — and I have to present a short constructed case next week, so I'm trying to build one rather than just summarize a reading. The argument in class has stalled at a particular point. Our instructor kept saying that hinge propositions are supposed to do real work: they stand fast, they are not supported by evidence, doubt of them would dismantle the practices that make other doubts possible, and Wittgenstein's remark that the bed of the river of thoughts can shift is supposed to show that what plays this role can change. One of the people in the seminar — fairly effectively, I thought — kept replying that every example offered is just an ordinary empirical belief that happens to be extremely well confirmed and structurally useful: the world has existed for a long time, I have two hands, my name is so-and-so. On that view, calling something a hinge adds a description of the belief's role in our practices but no distinct epistemic status, and the interesting-sounding claims about not being answerable to evidence are just dramatizations of very high confidence. So I want a case where hinge status does visible work that the well-confirmed-belief story cannot reproduce, and I want the difference to be isolated rather than merely asserted.

What I need is a two-stage case built to the following specifications. One subject, one proposition P, held across both stages, with the same total body of evidence and the same level of subjective confidence: at stage 1, P functions as a hinge for her — it is not something she has reasons for, it is not something she would treat as open to question, and there is no criticism available to her for not having checked it; at stage 2, the very same P, held by the same person with the same evidence, is an ordinary empirical claim that is genuinely supported or defeated by evidence. The isolating device I want built in is this: there is some single incoming datum — a record, a reading, a discrepancy, an offhand remark — that at stage 1 she correctly treats as noise or as an anomaly to be explained away, and that at stage 2 counts as decisive counter-evidence to P, and nothing new has been learned in between. So the transition has to be driven by a change in what she is doing — in the practice, the procedure, the point of the activity, the rules of the game she is playing — and not by new evidence, not by a change in how confident she feels, and not by a change in the content of P. Further constraints: keep it realistic and present-day, one paragraph for the scenario itself, no skeptical scenarios (no brains in vats, no deceiving demons, no fictitious physics), no amnesia, drugs, or mental illness, and no science fiction; make the hinge local and person-relative rather than a generic platitude on the order of there being an external world — something like the proverbial benchmark not having moved, the scale being calibrated, this being my mother, this being the ward I work on; and make sure stage 1 is not a case of dogmatism, laziness, or negligence, since if the subject is criticizable the example simply concedes the point to the skeptic. My own attempt — a surveyor who has always treated her local benchmark as fixed and constitutive of her mapping practice, and then, after her team switches to monitoring subsidence, treats the same drawer full of old conflicting records as evidence that the benchmark has moved — seemed to fail precisely because the change of practice dragged new data along with it, so the skeptic can say the difference between the stages is just that she acquired evidence. Fix that case or replace it; I don't mind which, as long as the specifications are met.

After the scenario I want the demonstration that it works, and I want the verdicts to fall out of the positions rather than be written into the story. Please: state P explicitly; list what is being held fixed across the two stages and name the one variable; then run three readings on the case at each stage — (i) the reading on which hinges are not beliefs at all but commitments lying outside the game of giving and asking for reasons, so that neither knowledge nor doubt so much as arises for them, (ii) the reading on which they are genuine beliefs we are non-evidentially entitled to take for granted but which do not amount to knowledge, and (iii) the reading on which they are ordinary empirical beliefs that can be known, justified by their role in explaining or organizing the rest of what we take ourselves to know. The pattern I'm after is that all three diverge at stage 1 and converge at stage 2, and that they diverge for reasons internal to each position. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work; show me what happens if you remove it — I expect the case either collapses back into an ordinary case of coming by evidence or degenerates into a case of mere stubbornness, and I want to see which; and give me the strongest objection the skeptic about hinges will raise, ideally the objection that I have quietly changed the proposition, or the evidence, or only described a change in what she would say, together with the best repair you can see and an honest note if there isn't one. No citations or reading lists, and please characterize the three positions by their structure rather than by their authors' names, since the point is the argument and not the scholarship. The whole thing should fit comfortably on two pages, in American English.
