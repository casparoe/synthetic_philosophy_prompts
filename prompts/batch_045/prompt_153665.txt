I am co-writing an essay on whether generational categories — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — are anything more than a journalistic convenience, and my co-author and I have reached a disagreement I do not trust either of us to settle.

Her argument runs like this. Social science already has a perfectly respectable notion of a birth cohort, and demography and epidemiology treat cohorts as real causal kinds. Everyone born in a given window shares an exposure at a formative stage; the stock example is a cohort of children in utero during the famine in the occupied Netherlands at the end of the Second World War, who turn up decades later with a distinctive profile of adult health outcomes. What makes the cohort a genuine kind, on this picture, is not that its members resemble each other at birth but that a shared causal history produces a measurable signature later — the category earns its keep by supporting inferences. Millennials, she says, are the same sort of thing: a birth window, a set of formative exposures (the arrival of the smartphone, the financial crisis, the attacks of 2001), and an adult signature that shows up in survey data. Generational explanation is therefore cohort explanation under a friendlier name, and the critics who dismiss the whole enterprise as astrology with a spreadsheet are rejecting the standard apparatus of social science. She wants the essay to conclude that generations are, in the relevant sense, real kinds.

I am not convinced, and my suspicion is that the argument slides between two different things: a cohort as a demographer uses it — a partition of a population by the date of a shared event — and a generation as the press uses it — a named kind with a character, a set of traits, a date range that polling organizations disagree about by several years, micro-generations and cuspers at the edges, and, in political usage, a share of blame. If the slide is where the work is being done, I want to know which step it happens at.

Please audit the analogy. Specifically:

Give the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — for what plays the role of the defining event, the exposure, the dose, the unexposed or differently exposed comparison group, the follow-up interval, the outcome measurement, the controls, and the mechanism, on the epidemiological side and on the generational side. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion she wants from the ones that are noise.

I expect at least these to be live, but argue where I am wrong: that membership in a cohort is sharp, exhaustive and settled by a date, while generational membership is fuzzy, contested, revised, and partly a matter of self-ascription; that an epidemiological exposure has a dose and a stated mechanism running from insult to outcome, whereas a cohort member encountered the smartphone at four or at twenty-four, in a different country, under a different income, and the mechanism from event to adult attitude is rarely more than a gesture; and that outcomes in cohort studies are measured independently of the exposure hypothesis, while generational traits are largely elicited by asking people to endorse a description of their own generation, which is close to circular, and which sits awkwardly beside the recurring finding that each era's respondents say the same things about the young.

There is a further technical point I have been told and cannot check: that in the age–period–cohort setting, cohort is just period minus age, so cohort effects are formally the residual left after you have modelled being twenty-five and living in 2025, and cannot be separately estimated from the same data without an extra constraint. If that is right, say so plainly and say what it implies here; if it is garbled, correct it. Then add what I take to be the generational version of the same problem: no birth cohort is unexposed to any event, so there is no unexposed comparison group at all, and apparent generational change may reflect cohort replacement rather than anyone changing their mind.

Then the part I care most about. Is real kind even the right thing to ask about, or is the honest target weaker — a kind individuated by shared causal history and good enough to project from, in the way philosophers mean by a homeostatic property cluster, versus a gerrymandered partition that is useful for indexing? Give me a concrete test that would settle it, in the form I could actually run: does knowing someone's generational membership predict their attitudes or behaviour once their age, the calendar year, and their income and education are already in the model, does it predict for people who reject the label, and does it survive a blind version of the identification task? And tell me whether the looping fact — people are told which generation they are, identify with it, and adjust accordingly — counts as making the kind real, or as manufacturing the very data that was supposed to establish it.

Finally: if the epidemiology analogy is the wrong one, what is the right one? A historical period, an artistic movement, a linguistic dialect with fuzzy isoglosses, a wine vintage whose character is partly weather and partly expert reputation, all seem to me to capture something the cohort model misses, and each seems to fail somewhere too. And when you are done, write me the strongest sentence my co-author can actually defend, since I have to hand her something.
