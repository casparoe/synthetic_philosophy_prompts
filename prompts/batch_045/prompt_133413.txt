Question 3 — short answer, 10 marks

We have been using the term intuition pump for a thought experiment that is designed to make you have a particular reaction to a made-up case, so that the reaction can then be used as evidence for or against a general claim. A pump does honest work only if the reaction it produces is a reaction to the feature the general claim is about, rather than to something incidental in the way the case is dressed up. Our course gives one procedure for checking this, called the scrub test. It has two parts.

(i) Scrub. Rewrite the case replacing every evaluatively loaded or merely vivid detail — adverbs, descriptions of character, details of appearance and setting — with a neutral placeholder. Keep the causal and informational structure exactly as it was.

(ii) Swap. Re-run the case with everything about the presentation that is not the variable under test permuted: change which agent is named first, which one is described in the second person, which one you are told about first. The variable under test must stay where it was.

A pump passes the scrub test only if the reaction you are testing survives both operations unchanged. It fails if the reaction changes when only the presentation changes, and it also fails if the reaction survives only because your rewriting quietly removed a second difference that had been doing the real work.

The general claim under test is thesis T: an agent is morally responsible for a harm only if that harm was a foreseeable outcome of what the agent chose.

Case A. Two trainee dispatchers, Ada and Bo, work the same depot, use the same protocol, and handle the same kind of call: one patient, two ambulances. The protocol says: send the nearest available ambulance. On Monday, Ada's screen shows Ambulance 1 four minutes away and Ambulance 2 nine minutes away, and she sends Ambulance 1. On Thursday, Bo's screen shows the same two numbers, and he sends Ambulance 1. Each has done exactly what the protocol requires, with the same information and the same degree of care. On Monday, a bridge cable snaps — a failure that no one at the depot could have anticipated, and that the traffic model put at well under one percent for the decade. Ambulance 1 is stuck for twenty minutes and Ada's patient dies. On Thursday nothing unusual happens, Bo's patient is treated in four minutes and lives. Nothing about the two agents, their information, their instructions or their care differs. Only the outcome differs. Is Ada responsible for her patient's death?

Case B. A hospital intern who has been told three times to check the dispatch log before sending anyone cannot be bothered. He is leaning back in his chair, smirking at his phone, and he jabs the button for the nearest ambulance without looking anything up. Delays of the kind that happened that night were common knowledge on the ward: the city's traffic system had been unreliable for a week. The ambulance he sends sits in traffic for twenty minutes and the patient dies; the farther ambulance would have arrived in nine minutes. Is he responsible for the patient's death?

(a) Which case, if either, does genuine work as a test of T, and why? (3 marks)
(b) Apply both halves of the scrub test to both cases and report what happens. (4 marks)
(c) For each case, state what it varies and what it holds fixed. (3 marks)

Here is the answer I have been asked to mark.

Case A is the one that does the work. Ada and Bo are in identical situations, follow the same protocol, and differ only in how things turned out: an accident that nobody could have anticipated delayed Ada's ambulance, so her patient died, while Bo's patient lived. Because everything the agents themselves contributed is held fixed and only the outcome varies, if we still judge Ada more harshly than Bo then thesis T is false: responsibility tracks what actually happened, and not merely what was foreseeable. Case B is the defective one. The intern is described as smirking and as being unable to be bothered, and that language is there to make us angry at him. So the case is emotional rather than rational; it pumps a reaction out of us instead of arguing for anything.

Applying the scrub test to Case B: once you take out the words like smirking and cannot be bothered, and just say that a hospital employee sent one ambulance when another was available, the sense that he is responsible mostly drains away, so the pump fails. Case A passes both halves of the test. There is no loaded language in it to remove in the first place, and when you swap which trainee gets the bad outcome, our judgment swaps along with it, which shows that the judgment is tracking the outcome and nothing else. So Case A gives us a genuine counterexample to T.

The lesson is that a thought experiment can be trusted only once it has been scrubbed of emotion, because emotion is what makes a case mislead you. Case A has been scrubbed, its verdict is stable, and our ordinary concept of responsibility therefore really does make responsibility hostage to luck. No further argument is needed: once the pump is clean, whatever it pumps is evidence.
