You're a political philosopher who works on the ethics of migration and on associative freedom. Brainstorm a bit before you commit to anything, and think carefully — I want a principle I can argue with, not a list of considerations.

I'm trying to work out what I actually think about a group's right to exclude, going case by case, and I want to know whether one principle fits the pattern or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

- Homeowner won't let a stranger sleep in her spare room during a blizzard: allowed, though unkind.
- Chess club rejects an applicant for being obnoxious at meetings: fine.
- Chess club rejects an applicant for being a woman: not fine, and not fine in a worse and different way, not just "wrong reason."
- A 200-person village that owns all the surrounding land and just won't sell plots to outsiders, so housing stays cheap for villagers: I want to say not fine.
- An indigenous nation with a treaty-recognized territory that limits membership by descent: fine, or much more defensible than the village — and I can't say why.
- A country restricting immigration to protect a generous welfare state: shakily fine, depending on something I can't name.
- Same country restricting immigration to keep the population ethnically homogeneous: not fine at all, even holding the numbers fixed.
- A kibbutz-style commune requiring members to pool income and work, turning away someone who wants in but won't work: fine.
- A city that wants to admit newcomers and grant them local membership even though the national government won't: fine? Which makes me suspect the right belongs to whatever level can actually exercise it.
- Guest-worker arrangement: workers admitted for decades, paying into pensions, no vote, can't bring their kids, deportable at will. I think this is worse than not admitting them — which is weird, since nobody is being excluded.
- Trickier one: a community with completely open entry but no exit. Anyone may join, but members may not leave and can't take out their share of the common assets. My gut says it's wrong in the same family of ways as exclusion, but I don't know how to say it.

My tentative principle: exclusion is OK when it protects some practice or relationship the group actually has, and not OK when it works by ranking people or by defending a merely positional advantage. Test it against the cases and repair it. Which of my verdicts does the repaired version force me to drop, and what does it say about two I haven't decided: (a) a group that never formally excludes anyone but makes applying so degrading that nobody does; (b) a 1950s covenant written by long-dead founders that the current members would vote to repeal but can't, and which is right now keeping people out?
