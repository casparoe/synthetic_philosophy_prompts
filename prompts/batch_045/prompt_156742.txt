I run a fortnightly interview series for the engineering organisation at my company — usually we talk to people about distributed systems, developer tooling, incident response — and next month's guest is a philosopher whose forthcoming book (working title *Coordination All the Way Down*) argues that almost every norm inside a large engineering organisation is a convention in the sense of David Lewis's *Convention* (1969): a regularity in behaviour that persists because nearly everyone conforms to it, expects nearly everyone else to conform, and prefers to conform on the condition that the others do, where there is some alternative regularity that would have served just as well. Her star examples are drawn from our world: the shape of a pull request, who speaks first in an incident call, the fact that nobody merges to main on a Friday, the fact that we all write code as though the linter's opinion were binding. She pairs this with an evolutionary story — conventions get started through precedent and salience, then get locked in because unilateral deviation is costly — and she claims this explains why engineering cultures are so hard to change even when everyone agrees they should change. I read Lewis with two colleagues from work and understood maybe sixty per cent of it, so I want questions I can ask cold, in engineering vocabulary, that don't require me to interrupt with "can you unpack that?".

The problem is that she has one move that shuts down discussion: whenever I raise a case where people comply because they fear blame, or because a rule was handed down, or because the behaviour has moral content rather than being an arbitrary side of the road to drive on, she says "that's a sanction story, not a convention story" and redirects to the coordination core. I want eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, arranged so that this deflection gets progressively harder to use. Please cover the following, in my terms rather than hers: the common-knowledge requirement, given that no engineer at a company of four thousand has common knowledge of anything — does replacing it with ordinary overlapping expectations (or with the dynamics that signalling-game models use) still distinguish a convention from a mass habit, such as everybody indenting with tabs because their editor ships with tabs as the default? The requirement that there be an alternative regularity that would have worked equally well: does that hold for norms with content, like "do not commit credentials" or "do not bypass code review", where the alternative is not something anyone could coordinate on? The free-rider cases, where everyone wants everyone else to write tests and each person would rather skip — can the "prefers to conform given that others conform" clause reach those at all, or is she conceding the whole territory of public goods? The lock-in cases, where everyone privately thinks the status quo is bad (say, the entire industry's tooling assuming a particular timestamp representation, or our own habit of encoding configuration as one enormous nested blob that nobody can review) — what does her account say distinguishes "we are stuck and would all rather switch" from "this is fine", and what, on her account, actually breaks a convention, or is that part imported by hand from outside the analysis? The imposed-standard cases: a staff engineer at my company wrote the style guide, a linter enforces it in CI, and people conform — is that a convention, given that the reason each person conforms is not that others conform but that the build goes red? If she says yes, then is anything backed by sanctions a convention, and what work is left for the notion; if she says no, where exactly is the line between precedent and imposition, and does an imposed rule quietly become a convention once people start coordinating around it? Finally, and this is the one I most want to land, our org has an explicit second-order rule: whenever a decision is genuinely arbitrary, adopt whatever the framework's default is, even when we would have chosen differently. Please build a question around that — whether her account can handle a convention whose content is a procedure for selecting conventions, whether the arbitrariness requirement inherits upward (could there be a different meta-rule that would have worked as well?), and what stops the regress — and if she tries the "sanction story" move on it, I want a follow-up that makes it clear the rule is enforced by nothing at all.

I would like the sequence to close with one question about what evidence could count against her view rather than merely failing to support it: something I can ask her that forces a concrete answer about what observation of a real engineering organisation — a migration that succeeded, a norm that flipped overnight, a team that never converged — would make her abandon or substantially revise the claim that convention-plus-precedent is the right story about all of this. Please keep every question phrased so that a non-philosopher can ask it and follow the answer, and avoid anything that would require her to cite literature I could not check up on afterwards.
