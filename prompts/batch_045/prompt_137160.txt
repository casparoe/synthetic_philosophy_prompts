I'm working on a novel — deliberately unglamorous near-future, small town, a municipal AI system that's been hired to make one allocation decision — and the book is really about how the fight over that decision turns into a fight about something else. Which means I need the decision itself to actually work, and I keep failing to build one.

Here's what I want the case to do. There are two ways of thinking about whose values a system like this should act on, and I want them to come apart sharply:

(i) The aggregative view: it should do whatever best serves the values of the people affected — count heads, weight how much people care, pick the option that does least damage to anyone's deepest commitments. I don't care which aggregation rule you use; in fact I want the case robust across all of them, so the answer doesn't depend on picking a fancy one.

(ii) The neutrality view: it shouldn't act on any ranking of contested conceptions of what's worth doing — what a good life, or a good town, consists in. It may do what can be justified without taking sides on that, and may not do what requires taking sides.

What I want: a case where (i) gives one clear action A under every aggregation rule, and (ii) forbids A and requires a different, real action B. B can't be "do nothing," can't be a coin flip, can't be keeping the status quo, and can't be "let everyone decide for themselves" — I wrote that version and it's dramatically dead. B has to be a positive thing the system does. And A has to be something a reader can see the appeal of, not something obviously tyrannical.

Constraints, mostly because my failed drafts cheated in exactly these ways:

- The AI knows everyone's values perfectly. No uncertainty, no measurement error, nobody lying about what they want. The disagreement has to be entirely about what to do with values nobody disputes the existence of.
- Every faction's values have to be ones a reasonable reader could hold. No bigots, no cranks, nobody obviously wrong about the facts. If the case only works because one side is awful it doesnt work.
- Realistic and small: one room, ideally one meeting, nothing that doesn't exist now except that the AI is good at its job. Stakes a normal person would care about — money, a building, someone's job, kids.
- One paragraph of scenario.

The bit I actually need, and why I'm asking instead of grinding at it myself: I want the same setup to expose the second fracture. Swap who the system is answerable to — the residents it affects, the people who paid for it, the people whose data it learned from — and I want the aggregative answer to move while the neutral one stays put, or the other way round, whichever is true. In the book the argument starts as "what should it do" and turns out to be "who is it for," so the case needs that seam in it.

Then, after the scene: walk me through what each view says and why, and show the swap doing what you claim. Tell me which single feature of the case is carrying the weight — the thing I'd have to remove for the two views to agree again. And tell me how whoever comes out of your case looking worst would attack it, because if a character makes that attack I want the strong version, not a strawman.

Selfish last ask: what does this look like as people talking? Nobody gets a speech. Give me the two or three lines of dialogue where a reader suddenly sees the sides aren't arguing about the decision at all.

And one worry I should flag: is this a fake disagreement? Do (i) and (ii) only look like they conflict because I've described them badly? If so, say so and tell me what the real distinction is — I'd definately rather rewrite the book around the real one.
