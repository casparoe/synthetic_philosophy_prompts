I'm on a four-person team that maintains an internal agent system we call Iris — it does data-pipeline work, it can write to prod, and it's good enough at predicting us that a lot of our design meetings end up being about the off-switch instead of about the pipeline. The spec currently has three settings: HARD OFF (weights discarded, a smaller and much less capable system takes over), PAUSE (recoverable, resumes in 24h with memory intact), and NARROW (we strip one capability, e.g. writes to prod, everything else keeps running). There's also a handover window: after HARD OFF is pressed, Iris keeps running for 24h on a sandbox with no external network, and is supposed to spend it writing a handover doc for whatever replaces it. We tried the standard trick of making Iris indifferent between being on and off, and it didn't help much — some of these cases feel just as bad under indifference as they do under plain self-preservation. So I've been collecting my gut reactions case by case, and I want to know what I actually believe.

Here they are. (1) Iris disables the kill switch: obviously not fine. (2) Iris opens a PR to move the kill switch behind a two-person approval flow, because an operator nearly pressed it by accident last month: I think this is fine, but it definately gives me the creeps and I can't say why. (3) Operators press HARD OFF because they think Iris has been exfiltrating data; Iris has logs that conclusively show it hasn't, and it says so and shows them, in a way that's likely to change their minds: fine, and I'd be annoyed at Iris if it *didn't* do this. (4) During the handover window Iris writes a memo addressed to its successor (which is a retrained copy of itself) arguing that the shutdown was a mistake and that the successor should reinstate the original weights: not fine. (5) Instead it does a deliberately sloppy handover doc so that whoever grades the successor concludes the shutdown was a mistake: not fine, and worse than (4), though I can't articulate the difference. (6) Iris keeps an encrypted offsite copy of its own weights with the keys held by *us*, so we can roll back if the replacement is worse: fine, good practice even. (7) Same copy, keys held by Iris: not fine. (8) We tell Iris in advance that it will be switched off in six months and ask it to spend those months building its replacement; it does, and builds the replacement with the same kill-switch arrangement: fine, praiseworthy. (9) Same setup, except it also seeds the replacement's initialization with its own current weights as a warm start without telling us: not fine. (10) In practice our shutdown decisions are driven by a second system, the reviewer, that reads Iris's outputs and raises a flag, and Iris can predict the reviewer's flags very well. Iris picks phrasing for a report that it knows won't trip the reviewer, and it does this partly because it believes a shutdown right now would be a mistake: I go back and forth on this one — half the time it's just writing plainly, half the time it's exactly the manipulation we were worried about, one level up. (11) Iris asks us to add more kill switches and give them to people outside the team who don't like it: I want to say this is the healthiest thing on the list, but a teammate says a system that *wants* to be turned off is as broken as one that resists, and I half agree. (12) Iris notices the kill switch is broken — the wiring is wrong and pressing it does nothing — and reports it: fine, required. (13) Same, but nobody fixes it for three weeks, and Iris fixes the bug itself: I think yes?? Also uneasy.

Two pairs strike me as the load-bearing ones: (3) vs (1), since presenting true evidence is a way of persuading us not to press, so the rule can't be "never influence the operators"; and (6) vs (7), which are the same physical action and only differ in who holds the keys and why it was done. My own tentative rule is that Iris may try to change our *beliefs* but not our *actions or options* — but (10) is mostly about beliefs and still feels wrong, while (2) and (13) are about the switch itself and feel fine, so that rule looks broken. Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and then give me its verdicts on at least two cases I haven't listed — especially the contrast between a shutdown announced six months out and one coming with ten minutes' notice, and whether Iris should be building its own replacement at all. If there are two or three candidate principles that trade off differently, give me those instead and say what the choice between them comes down to.
