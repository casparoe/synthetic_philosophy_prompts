I am a journalist working on a long-form piece for a British magazine about whether minds have to be made of the right kind of stuff, and I have decided to open it with a short story rather than with a thought experiment set out in the abstract. The seed is a dialogue from Hofstadter and Dennett's 1981 collection The Mind's I — the one in which a preserved brain is interviewed, and in which the reader is left unsure whether the mentality belongs to the brain, to the book he is holding, or to the act of reading itself. I would rather not write the opener myself: left to my own devices I will make it far too tidy, and the point of the piece is that the question stays open. So I would be grateful if you would write the story for me.

Here is the setting and situation I have in mind, which you should feel free to develop or adjust. A conservation and digitisation studio in a large national library, over the course of one winter. At the centre of it is an object catalogued as the Ashcombe Codex: the single surviving copy of a late seventeenth-century treatise that is devotional in intention but logical in method, built up in layers — a base text, marginal glosses, glosses commenting on the glosses, and a final set of notes in a later hand commenting on the whole apparatus. The quires were disbound in a nineteenth-century flood and rebound in the wrong order, so the book as it now sits says one thing while the order implied by the quire signatures says another. It also ends with a colophon giving instructions for re-sequencing the quires, so that the book tells the reader how to assemble a corrected version of itself; and the corrected version, once assembled, turns out to say something noticeably different. Two people are working on it. Marion, a senior conservator of some twenty-five years' standing, is building a digital surrogate from page images and has become half-convinced that the codex answers back — that the sequence in which fragments surface in the storage boxes is not random, and that the book has preferences about which of its own orders is being read. Kwame, a machine-learning engineer seconded from a partner university, has trained a small model on the base text and is using it to fill the lacunae, having reconstructed the lost passages from a later Latin translation made from a copy that no longer exists; he regards Marion's talk as sentimentality about vellum. Between them they must decide which version goes into the print-on-demand facsimile the library intends to sell.

What I want the story to do is this: it should be a story and not an essay with characters bolted on — scenes, dialogue, a decision that actually gets made — and it should leave the reader genuinely uncertain whether anything was thinking, without ever tipping either into the supernatural or into some laboratory demonstration that settles the matter. I would like the following distinctions to do dramatic work in the fiction rather than being explained as theory: whether the mind, if there is one, belongs to the abstract pattern or to one particular object made of ink and vellum; whether several thousand identical facsimiles would each host a mind, or whether there would be one mind in many places at once, dividing the moment the copies begin to differ; whether the book's mentality depends on being read at all, and on being read in the present rather than in 1690, so that an unread book is in some sense suspended; whether the passages recovered from the Latin are recovered memories or interpolations; and, most importantly, what would count as the book changing its mind — if a quire is returned to its proper place after three centuries, is that the correction of a scribal transmission error, or a new thought? A good closing beat, if you can find one, would be a passage where the codex and the model's continuation diverge, and where it is genuinely unclear whether the model has the codex wrong or the codex has changed its mind. Please write in British English, in a register that would sit comfortably at the front of a magazine feature for a British readership: no American spellings, no fantasy or science-fictional ornamentation, no character delivering the philosophy in a speech, and no named philosophers or isms in the text itself. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, and give the story a title.
