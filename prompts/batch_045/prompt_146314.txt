I'm TAing a pretty applied decision-theory course and I'm putting together one problem-set question for next week, and I want a case study rather than a definition-recitation. The theme is Good's theorem about information — the result that an expected-utility maximizer who's offered costless information can't lose in expectation by taking it, since she can always just ignore what she learns and do whatever she'd have done anyway. I want a scenario where that comes out false: an agent who is, as far as priors and preferences go, a completely ordinary vNM expected-utility maximizer, offered information that's free, truthful, and arrives before any choice has to be made, and who is strictly worse off in expectation for having taken it. Constraints: it has to be real-world — a clinic, an insurance office, a hiring committee, whatever, with stakes a normal person could actually face; the signal can be a shrewd human expert or an ordinary test, no perfect predictors or demons; and the loss has to be strict, not a tie. It also has to not be a cheat: no utility attaching to the belief itself (nobody gets sad from the bad news, no "I'd rather not know" preferences), no weakness of will or failure to update or any other irrationality, no cost or inaccuracy in the information, and no one else finding out the content and punishing her for it. My guess is that the only way to make this work is structural — getting the information changes what she's able to do, or when she's forced to choose: it kills an option she'd otherwise keep open, or starts a clock, or otherwise makes the world smaller in a way that isn't just a genie deleting a row from the matrix. If you think I'm wrong about that, or that the constraints can't all be met at once, tell me straight and tell me which one to drop — I'd much rather have that than a polite scenario that quietly cheats.

Format: one paragraph of scenario, no more, then the check. Give me an explicit little decision problem — two or three states, two or three acts, actual numbers and priors — and compute the expected value both ways so the gap is visible on the page. Then: which background assumption of the theorem your case violates, in plain words; what happens to the value if you repair that assumption and make the information purely informational again; what someone defending the theorem would say about your case (I expect something like "that's not a counterexample, that's a case the theorem never covered," but say whether that defense is actually good or whether it makes the theorem less interesting than it sounds); and how confident you are that this is a real structural failure and not just a relabeling of one of the theorem's own conditions. Its going in front of students who've only just met Bayes, so flag anything you had to assume that I'd need to state explicitly in the question itself.
