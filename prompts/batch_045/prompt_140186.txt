I'm writing a novel set in and around a natural history museum, and one of my characters — a retired metaphysician, a friend of my narrator's dead supervisor — writes my narrator a long letter in which he tries to convert her to something Berkeleyan. The scene I keep failing to write is the one where she reads the letter in the fossil drawers and tries to work out whether he is mad or merely wrong. For background I bought a popular paperback on idealism, and its account of Berkeley's master argument — the passage where he dares you to form an idea of trees in a park or books in a locked closet existing with nobody by to perceive them, and then points out that in trying you are yourself thinking of them — reads as follows: "The argument is simple and devastating. Try to imagine a mind-independent object and you will find that the moment you succeed you have made it an object of your own thought; so the supposition destroys itself, and we cannot frame the idea of something existing unperceived. Since an idea can resemble nothing but another idea, no idea could ever represent a material thing existing outside all minds; the conclusion is therefore that matter is not merely unknowable but impossible, and that the belief in it is literally without meaning. What exists are minds and their ideas, and sensible things are nothing but collections of ideas in minds. When no human being is looking, the trees and the books go on existing because God is always perceiving them — a device Berkeley needs to keep the furniture of the world in place when nobody is in the room. The standard objection, that all he has shown is a trivial fact about our own psychology, namely that I cannot picture a closet without being around to picture it, is fatal to the argument; it is in effect what Samuel Johnson meant when he kicked a stone and thought he had refuted him." I want to put a version of this into the letter, but not a version a Berkeley scholar would wince at.

So: how fair is it? I'd like a sentence-by-sentence audit rather than a general verdict — what a sympathetic reader of Berkeley would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, what is a subtle misrepresentation, and what he would reject outright. My own suspicions, for what they're worth: I think the bit about ideas resembling only ideas belongs to a different argument of Berkeley's than the one being summarised, I don't trust the slide from "we cannot frame the idea of it" to "it is meaningless," and I can't tell whether the closing sentence about Johnson is the book being facetious or a genuine claim that the argument is dead. I'd also like to know what is doing the real work in the phrase "frame an idea of": whether Berkeley's notion of conceiving here is imagistic in a way that makes the argument weaker than the summary suggests, and whether the objection the book calls fatal really is, or whether it rests on attributing to Berkeley a conclusion — nothing exists unconceived by *me* — that he didn't need.

The part I most need help with is the extension my character makes, because it isn't in the book. Standing in front of a drawer of ammonites he argues, first, that the same reasoning rules out an unfelt pain just as it rules out an unperceived tree, since bringing a pain before your mind is not the same as there being a pain nobody has, and, second, that the fossil record is therefore not evidence of a mindless prehistory: what geology studies, he says, is the order and steadiness of a set of ideas in the divine mind that have been arranged so as to look like the record of a past no one witnessed, and the distinction between real fossils and merely imagined ones is only the usual Berkeleyan one between ideas that are steady, orderly and independent of my will and ideas that are faint and under my control. Does either extension follow from the argument as it actually is, or does the argument overgeneralise — into unexperienced mental items, into the deep past — in a way that shows what its real scope is? And if you were coaching me: what is the strongest short version of the master argument I could put in this man's mouth without caricaturing it, and what is the strongest reply my narrator, a sceptical conservator who handles very old objects all day, could give that would not itself be a straw man?
