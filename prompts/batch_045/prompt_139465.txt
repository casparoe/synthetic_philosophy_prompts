So I write novels — three published, none of which set the world on fire — and I've noticed a thing about how readers behave once an author turns out to have done something awful, and I can't find a word for it. I'm not asking the big question everyone shouts about, whether a bad person's work can still be good. I'm asking about the shape the whole business actually takes, which is wierd in a couple of specific ways.

First: the damage people report is local, and what they do about it is total. A reader will tell you, quite precisely, that the sentences are still good, the structure still works, the jokes still land — and that the only thing that's changed is that a passage which used to read as consolation now reads as a sales pitch, or that the narrator now sounds like he's lying to them. That's one property out of a dozen. But then they stop reading. Not "I enjoy it less" — they stop, they take the book off the shelf, they won't press it on anyone, they'll say they can't read it at all. Nobody seems to notice the gap between the size of the flaw they've identified and the size of what they do about it. I got a miniature version of this myself: I said something stupid in an interview once, and a reader wrote that the last chapter of my second novel now hits different — while saying in the same email that it was her favourite book of that year.

Second: it only runs one direction. You never hear that a beautiful novel pays anything off the debt; nobody thinks a great book makes the person less bad. But the person can void the book entirely. If this were one ledger with values being weighed against each other, that asymmetry is strange. If it's two separate ledgers, then the leak only goes one way. Except the readers who keep reading behave like it is one ledger — they send the royalties somewhere, or they name the crime in the same breath as the praise, every single time, like they're balancing.

Third, and this is what makes me think it has edges worth mapping: it tracks subject matter. A novel about coercion by a writer who was coercive — total collapse, people talk as if the book itself had done something. A novel about deep-sea fishing by the same writer — readers shrug, or say they can still read that one. And when the writer has been dead a hundered years and the bad thing is in every biography, it stops biting: it reads as a quirk of the period rather than a defect in the thing.

I've had "death of the author" thrown at me, but as far as I can tell that's a claim about what you're allowed to use when working out what a text means, and my readers mostly aren't disagreeing about meaning — they'd agree on what the book says, they just can't do the reading any more. Someone pointed me at psychology, contagion or magical thinking, badness treated like a stain that spreads by contact, which feels closer, except that's usually about objects you don't want to touch, and the entire value of a novel is in the experiencing of it, and my readers aren't saying the book is dirty, they're saying they can't do something with it. A friend who reads philosophy muttered about autonomists and moralists, which sounds like it's just the yes/no on the big question and not about this shape at all.

So: does this configuration have a name — local flaw, global withdrawal, one-directional leak, sensitive to thematic proximity and to how long the author's been dead? If it does, I want to know which of those four features are essential to it and which are accidents, and I want the boundary cases: what else looks like this but isn't it? Plain boycotting? Losing trust in someone as a speaker? Finding out something biographical that makes a passage you'd misread suddenly make sense? And if there's no name, invent one and define it against those neighbours. Please don't just hand me a label — I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where it stops.
