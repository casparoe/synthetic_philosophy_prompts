I am in the final year of an MSc and taking a graduate seminar on philosophical problems in machine learning, and we have now spent two whole sessions on wireheading and reward tampering without, as far as I can tell, anyone changing their mind or even changing their argument. The positions on the table were roughly these. One group holds that the reward signal just is the objective, so an agent that rewrites its own reward channel is not malfunctioning in any deep sense — it is succeeding, and our discomfort is only evidence that we wrote the wrong number down, which is a specification problem and not a philosophical one. A second group holds that reward was always a proxy for a target we cannot write out, and that tampering cases are the cleanest demonstration that the proxy and the target can come apart, so the real work is in saying what the target is well enough to design against the failure. A third group thinks the second group's talk of a target is empty: there is nothing for the objective to be over and above some specification or other, so the gap language is rhetorical. And someone in the seminar kept insisting that this is just the experience machine argument from 1970s welfare theory wearing a new costume — that whether wireheading is bad is exactly the old question about whether what matters is the having of certain mental states or the actual satisfaction of one's desires, and that that question has not moved in fifty years either. Every move on both sides seemed to be anticipated and recycled.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis of that impasse rather than a verdict on it, and I have a hypothesis I want you to test rather than flatter. My hypothesis is that "is wireheading bad, and why?" is not one question but three sharing a name: a design question about which training set-ups are robust to which failure channels; a semantic question about what the word "objective" can refer to when the designer is uncertain and the system can affect the evidence on which the designer's judgement depends; and a substantive normative question about what makes an outcome better or worse for the agent (or for us), which is where the welfarist and desire-based intuitions actually diverge. If that is right, then the two sides are often answering different questions under one heading and have different things that would settle them, which would make much of the dispute verbal in a non-trivial way.
