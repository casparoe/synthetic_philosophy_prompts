hi — I'm a science writer working on a piece on pain asymbolia, you know the thing: after certain strokes / insular damage people report that they still feel the pain — the location, the intensity, the burning or stabbing quality, they can describe it fine — but it just doesn't bother them anymore. They notice it, they don't mind it. I've been going back and forth with two researchers about what that's supposed to show and they keep talking past each other, so I want to try to get them to actually produce something together.

One of them I'd call a separatist: she thinks asymbolia is a real dissociation, which means the badness of pain is a distinct, contingent component that can come apart from the sensory-discriminative one, and the bare sensation isn't what makes pain bad. So the thing we actually have reason to care about is the affect, not the nociception, and pain-without-badness is a coherent possibility. The other one is an inseparabilist: he thinks the reports are misleading in one of three ways — either the patients have lost something conceptual or expressive and are confabulating composure, or the experience itself has been degraded so what's left is mere nociceptive awareness rather than pain, or the sensory dimension of pain is intrinsically valenced so a genuinely pain-like experience that isn't at all bad is just impossible. He definately does not accept that the case shows badness is a detachable add-on.

Please draft the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section — the stuff both can actually sign. Include what they agree on about the phenomenon itself (how stable it is, whether it's all-or-nothing or graded, whether the patients still withdraw / guard / use the pain as information), about what an adequate account of pain would have to do, and about what we owe these patients clinically regardless of who's right about the theory.

2. A disagreements table, where each side's position is stated in wording that side would accept for itself, not in the opponent's framing.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would move at least one of them. For each crux I want a concrete case or measure both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. The ones I have in mind, use them if they're the good ones:

- whether the dissociation is real or a reporting artifact. Test I'd suggest: implicit stuff — does the patient reliably spend effort or accept a cost to turn the stimulus down, does avoidance conditioning still happen, is there facial/autonomic "affective leakage", does the stimulus still prime negative affect on some indirect measure?
- the mirror case: could there be a subject who gets the hurting without the sensory content — it's awful, but ask what it is or where and they can't say in any sensory detail? Separatists should say this is possible if the channels really are separate; inseparabilists presumably say no. Say what each predicts and why.
- the "last time was fine but give me more this time" case: a patient who reports indifference in the moment but later requests heavier analgesia for the same procedure, or shows relief at the prospect of it. Does that retrospective / anticipatory attitude count as evidence about the experience, or is it just belief?
- whether the residual state is pain or mere nociceptive awareness, and whatever neural or behavioural marker both would accept as settling it.

Please also tell me plainly which part of this fight you think is purely verbal — my guess is a lot of it hangs on whether "pain" names the whole syndrome or just the sensory dimension, and on whose report counts when a person's words, face and behaviour disagree — and say why. And if it's cheap, one line at the end on what all this would imply for a system we might build that registers and reports nociceptive states without any distress attached, since my editor will ask. No citations or reading lists, obviously — I just want the structure and the arguments. Format it so it reads like a document two people could put their names to, not like a verdict on who won.
