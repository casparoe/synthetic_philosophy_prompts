I am trying to get clear on what the dispute between platonists and nominalists about mathematics is really about, and I have become stuck at a point where the usual framing — whether abstract objects exist — looks orthogonal to the disagreements that actually do the work. I would like an explanation rather than a survey of positions, and I would be grateful if you could write the answer in British English.

The puzzle I want explained is generated by two features of ordinary mathematical practice that seem, to me, to pull in opposite directions. I am familiar with the standard arguments on both sides, including the familiar point that rival set-theoretic codings of the natural numbers return different answers to membership questions; please do not spend the answer restating those. What I want is an account of how the following two features could both be in good order.

The first is the practice's indifference to which copy. Mathematicians prove results about an arbitrary group, or about the reals, without settling which construction is in play — Dedekind cuts or equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences, von Neumann ordinals or Zermelo ordinals — and the results are then applied wholesale to structures that nobody had specified when the theorem was proved, including structures first defined in a paper published last year. Nothing in the practice depends on picking out a unique intended copy, and indeed the practice seems to get its generality precisely from not doing so. This looks like it should be good news for a view on which existence is cheap: grant that every consistent mathematical theory is true of some realm of objects, and the aboutness of the old theorem is secured for free.

The second is the practice's insistence on privileged identifications, which is what I find harder. Mathematicians write the chain from the naturals up through the integers, rationals, reals and complexes as if the inclusions were literal, and write things like pi belonging to the complex numbers, even though the standard constructions make those claims false or coding-dependent. At the same time they refuse other identifications: a finite-dimensional vector space is kept apart from its dual, though the two are isomorphic, while it is routinely identified with its double dual; the reals and the plane, which are in bijection and indeed isomorphic as vector spaces over the rationals, are never identified. And the same rational is written as sitting inside the reals and inside the p-adic numbers, two canonical but mutually incompatible embeddings, which the practice keeps apart only by appeal to context. So the practice distinguishes canonical from merely existential isomorphism, and it sometimes individuates an object only relative to an ambient structure. That looks like it wants identity to be finer than isomorphism, and hence like it wants objects whose identity is not exhausted by the structure they lie in.

Please explain:

1. What the components of the platonist's position actually are, once we separate the existence of mathematical objects from their mind- and language-independence, from the uniqueness or determinacy of the intended structure, from causal inertness and non-spatiotemporal location, and from claims about what we can know — and which of these components each feature of the practice above requires, as opposed to merely fits comfortably with.

2. Whether a single theory can license both features. If existence is cheap, is uniqueness lost, so that the chain of inclusions becomes a choice among many equally good embeddings rather than a fact? And if the practice needs uniqueness, does it thereby need something the nominalist cannot give?

3. Whether a structuralist can explain the selective identification by appealing to natural or canonical isomorphism, and so recover everything without objects that have identity beyond structure; or whether the canonicity distinction itself is a distinction the structuralist has no room for.

4. How the object-platonist should treat the practice, given that on a literal reading the inclusions are false as written. Is the practice then merely convenient shorthand, and is that a cost or a symptom?

5. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories would license exactly this practice — and what would actually be lost if the mathematical community adopted a strict convention of never writing an inclusion unless it held literally and always naming the embedding explicitly. Would that reform settle anything philosophical, or only conceal it?
