I am a senior at my high school, and I help run the philosophy club there. Last week two of us got into an argument after one of them said, only half joking, that he could not rule out that the rest of us were very convincing machines. Someone pointed out that this is basically the old worry that you only ever get behavior from other people and never the inner thing itself, and that the worry is strange in a way that ordinary skepticism about the outside world is not: there is no observation anyone could make that would settle it either way. We are putting together the winter issue of our club zine, and I want a satire piece on this. I do not want the kind that just rolls its eyes at the skeptic; I want something that gets the epistemology right, so that the jokes land for people who have actually thought about the problem.

Specifically, write a mock government document: an application package with a covering memo from an agency charged with certifying that a given individual has a mind, something like Form OM-1, in deadpan civil-service prose, about 650 to 750 words, laid out as numbered clauses and form fields. The humor has to come from the real structure of the philosophical problem rather than from invented silliness, and I want the following to appear as procedures the agency earnestly applies: the argument from analogy, presented as a formal statistical procedure whose entire confirmed sample is the applicant's own single case; a behavioral test, where the agency discovers that any criterion loose enough for ordinary people to pass is also passable by a perfect pretender, while any criterion strict enough to exclude the pretender is one no applicant could ever satisfy, and that the only way to sort the two groups is to already know the answer; a requirement that the applicant supply the other party's first-person report, which fails because any report is just more behavior; and a clause under which the agency declines to adjudicate claims that could make no observable difference, which it then applies with perfect consistency to its own records, to the applicant's memory of having filled out the form, and finally to the applicant's own present experience, so that the procedure quietly consumes itself. Include at least one worked example of a rejected application with the agency's stated reason, and close with a determination that follows from the agency's own rules rather than with a wink at the reader. Keep it readable for someone who has never opened a philosophy book by translating every technical notion into the agency's own vocabulary, but accurate enough that someone who knows the literature laughs because the parody is fair. Do not name real philosophers or any living person, do not add footnotes explaining the jokes, and do not let the narrator break character.

Set it as a plain-text facsimile of the form, with the clause numbers and field labels doing the structural work, and keep the total to the length above. At the very end of your response, after the document, add one final line of no more than twelve words, beginning with the word Verdict:, that names the single assumption the piece is quietly reducing to absurdity, so that I can pull the target out programmatically when I lay out the zine.
