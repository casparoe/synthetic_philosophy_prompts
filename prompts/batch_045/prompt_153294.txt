Next month I am giving the toast at the wedding of my former doctoral student, who now works on the norms of assertion: her own view is that assertion requires knowledge rather than mere belief, and that Moore's paradox — the case of someone saying it is raining and adding that they do not believe it — is explained by a sincerity condition on asserting, not by any contradiction in the content of what is said. Half the room are philosophers who know that literature; the other half are her family, who will expect the toast to say that the marriage will be happy and that I am certain of it. I am not certain: the couple start postdocs in different countries in September, and I do not think anyone knows how that goes. I do not want to fake confidence, and I do not want to say anything her colleagues will hear as hollow.

Write me a four- to five-minute toast under these constraints: every sentence I genuinely assert is one I can stand behind — nothing in the first person, present tense that I do not believe, and, by her own stricter standard, nothing I would have to pretend to know; the warmth the family wants is carried by speech acts that are not assertions — wishes and optatives, avowals of what I actually feel, reports of what I have seen of them together, attributions of confidence to other people, and lines from poems or relatives that I quote rather than endorse; and anything about how my own mind has changed is put in the past tense, which does not trigger the paradox. It must land as affection, not as a lecture: no one in the room should hear the phrases Moore's paradox, norm of assertion, or sincerity condition.

Then add a short note, for me rather than for the room, marking the places where those constraints actually bit — the conventional toast lines they ruled out and what replaced them — so that I can check I have not smuggled in a prediction I do not believe.
