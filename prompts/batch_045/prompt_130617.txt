I was appointed to the acquisitions and display committee of our regional museum last spring, and our next item is one I have wanted settled for years — so I should say at the outset that I am not a neutral party. The building at issue is the parish church my family has attended for four generations; part of me simply wants it whole again. That is exactly why I would rather argue from our own record than from my wish. Our museum is small: a teaching remit, a regional brief, a collection strong in medieval and Renaissance material and thin everywhere else, and a display budget that cannot fund two versions of anything.

The case before us. In the 2016 earthquake the apse of the village church collapsed, taking with it a fresco cycle of about 1480 by a local workshop. Roughly sixty per cent of the painted surface was recovered in fragments, most with recorded find-spots. There is also a photogrammetric survey from 2011, a set of pre-war colour transparencies, and a restorer's 1958 tracings that record the colour of areas now lost. A consortium of a university imaging lab, the diocese, and a conservation fabricator proposes the following: mount the original fragments on a new honeycomb-panel support in their documented positions, and fill the lacunae with mineral-pigment prints on a lime-based substrate generated from the photographic record, printed at slightly reduced saturation. The effect they are aiming at is that the infill is detectable at arm's length but not in the photographs that will appear on our website, in the guidebook, and in the diocese's own material. The church cannot take the cycle back for at least six years, so it would come to us, and the diocese will lend it only if the wall text describes the cycle as "restored" and avoids the words copy, fake, and reconstruction — which puts us back in the neighbourhood of one of our own precedents, except that this time the condition concerns a word rather than a silence.

Our record, as far as I can assemble it:

1. "The copy in the frame." A copy, by an unknown hand, of a Baroque altarpiece whose original burned in the 1860s; the copy has hung in the surviving carved frame ever since, and since 1971 with us on long-term loan. In 2019 we renewed the loan and kept it on display, labelled as a copy. The minute: "the frame and the wall were made for an image of this size, and this one has been doing that work for a century and a half; removing it would leave a hole that says nothing."

2. Facsimiles of a codex. Since 2011 our light-insensitive... the illuminated codex has been in the vault and facsimile openings in the gallery, with a wall text explaining why. The minute: "the original is preserved, the facsimile lets us show it, and any reader who asks is taken to the original."

3. The declined forgery. In 2015 a private collector offered on long-term loan a painting our curator regarded as a forgery of a well-known 1920s work, on condition that it be shown without any wall text or catalogue entry flagging the doubt. We declined. The minute says only that the condition is not one we can accept; nothing records what we would have done had the same object been offered with a label saying "attributed" or "probably a forgery."

4. The artist-supervised re-creation. In 2018 a mirrored-room installation of 1974 by a living artist, destroyed in a flood, was re-fabricated from the artist's own plans, under her supervision and with her certificate. We accessioned the new fabrication and catalogue it as her work, the label noting the date of fabrication. The minute: "the artist is alive and says that this is the work."

5. The unmarked roof timbers. In 1994 we accepted a restoration of a fifteenth-century roof in which roughly forty per cent of the members were replaced with new oak worked by hand to the same sections. We do not mark which timbers are new. The minute: "it is a roof; it has to carry the building."

The four options on the table are: (A) decline the reconstruction and show the recovered fragments as fragments, in their documented arrangement, with the lacunae left empty and the photographic record beside them; (B) accept and display the reconstruction with the reduced-saturation infill — visible at arm's length, invisible in photographs — in the medieval gallery; (C) accept and display it, but only as a didactic exhibit in our teaching space, not accessioned and not in the medieval gallery; (D) accept and display it with the infill in a single flat neutral tone, so that the losses are legible at any distance and in any photograph.

What I would like from you:

First, set out plainly the two or three rules about what makes an object worth showing — about where the value of an original lies and what a stand-in does or does not inherit — that fit all five of our decisions together. I suspect we have been running something like a history rule (what matters is the object's actual making and its chain of custody), something like an experience rule (what matters is what a visitor standing in front of it gets, once nothing is being concealed), and something like an office rule (what matters is the job the object is here to do for this collection and these publics), but I would rather you named them properly and said how each one has to be stated in order to survive all five precedents at once.

Second, say what each rule implies about A, B, C, and D, and where they come apart.

Third, give me the option you expect us to adopt if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and identify the closest precedent — with an honest account of how close it really is. Each of our five differs from this case in some respect that may matter: in 2 and 5 the original or the functioning whole still exists, in 4 the artist was alive to authorize, in 1 the copy had acquired a century and a half of its own history, and in 3 we never decided the underlying question at all. Tell me which of those differences does the most work.

Fourth, if our record genuinely underdetermines the decision, say what principle each option would commit us to and which of those commitments we would regret most. I am particularly unsure what to make of the reduced-saturation trick: the infill is designed to disappear precisely in the medium through which almost everyone will encounter the cycle, and to be visible only to the person who has made the journey to stand in front of it. Is that a feature of proposal B or the thing that should sink it?

Finally, and most useful to me: name one further case — a variant of this one, or a different sort of object entirely — that would best separate the rules still in play after our precedents, and say what our answer to it would tell us.

Please end with a single machine-readable line, and nothing after it: \answer{A}, \answer{B}, \answer{C}, or \answer{D}.
