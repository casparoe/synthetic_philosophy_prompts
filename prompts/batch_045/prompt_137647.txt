I am a professor of philosophy who works mainly on the ethics of punishment, and I have recently been made chair of my university's Standing Committee on Open Inquiry. The committee has exactly one power: to grant, condition, withdraw, or restore a university platform — an invitation, a named lecture, a keynote, a professional-society meeting hosted on our campus. We cannot hire, fire, or discipline anyone, and because we are a public university our counsel insists that our stated grounds be viewpoint-neutral, which in practice means we must be able to describe what we are responding to as conduct rather than as the content of a scholarly claim. We meet monthly, we have roughly half an hour per case, our members include two students and a university lawyer, and our written reasons are published and are read as precedent at other institutions.

I would like a decision procedure my committee could actually run, in the room, on a demand to withdraw or restore a platform. I do not want a position paper; I want an ordered set of questions, a specification of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. It should include an ordered ladder of available tariffs — from a published statement of censure, through conditioning the event (a respondent, a published rejoinder, a mandatory open Q&A), through a dated postponement, up to withdrawal and to removal of a named honorific — with the conditions that license each rung and an explicit expiry and review date for every rung above censure.

The procedure should also operationalize a set of distinctions I have found myself relying on without being able to state cleanly, and I would like my names for them either disciplined or replaced by better ones:

the authorship condition — that no exclusion is legitimate unless some identifiable body states the charge, the tariff, and its expiry, so that an open-ended exclusion produced by diffuse pressure and merely tolerated by us cannot be ratified or credited as time served;

equivalence in currency — that a sanction which takes an audience, a credential, or an honorific should be denominated in that same good and should not exceed the advantage the person obtained or would have obtained from it;

separability — whether the objectionable matter is separable from what the person was actually invited to do, so that we can tell withdrawing a platform from withdrawing a view;

the counterfactual-audience test — whether this person would have been platformed but for the conduct complained of, which is my rough device for detecting pretext and selective enforcement;

the collateral-bearer test — who in fact bears the cost of the sanction, and in particular whether it falls mainly on people who are not responsible for the conduct and who may even be its victims;

the reply-and-return route — what the person must do to regain ordinary standing, and who certifies that they have done it;

tariff scarcity — the thought, borrowed from the expressive side of the punishment literature, that our condemnations are a signal whose content degrades if we spend the top of the ladder on minor offenses.

Any procedure must also fit, or tell me explicitly which to overrule, our three published rulings: we withdrew an invitation from a visiting scientist who had fabricated data, saying we were refusing to lend her the credential of a platform rather than sanctioning the content of her claims; we declined to withdraw an invitation from a philosopher invited to lecture on his own published argument about a contested social question, and instead required a respondent and a departmental statement of disagreement; and we removed a donor's name from a lecture series after a fraud conviction, but only once replacement funding for an equivalent series was secured, and we set no end date.

Please then run the procedure on three live cases. First: a senior researcher from another institution, invited by a student society to give the annual Darwin Day lecture on her work on avian cognition; the demand to disinvite her cites a thread of posts she wrote three years ago minimising a twentieth-century famine, which she has declined to retract and which is unrelated to her lecture; the inviting society is split, and graduate students in her field have told us her talk is the only one of its kind they will see this year. Second: a member of our own faculty, principal investigator on a federally funded longitudinal health study and sole adviser to four doctoral students, who posted a series of videos asserting a minority group's criminality; the demands are that he be dropped as keynote at the university research showcase and barred indefinitely from university-sponsored public venues; the study recruits its participants from, and provides screening to, that same minority group. Third: a scholar we disinvited four years ago by indefinitely postponing an event, without ever issuing a charge or a tariff, who has since published an apology that the relevant community's own representatives describe as an apology for being misunderstood, and who has now been nominated for an office in a society whose meeting we are hosting.

For each case I want the rulings the procedure yields, the closest precedent, and an honest statement of the principle we would be adopting if we went the other way. At least one of the three should produce a recommendation that a majority of my committee will dislike; tell me which, and why the procedure forces it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle: where it will misfire, what it silently assumes about our standing to sanction people who are not members of our community and about our capacity to establish contested conduct in half an hour without any power to compel evidence, and which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure. For what it is worth, I have argued in print that much of what is called cancellation is simply the ordinary operation of reputational accountability, and I do not want a procedure that flatters that view; I am least confident about whether our own practice of indefinite postponement is anything other than unauthored punishment, and that is the case I most want the procedure to handle rather than duck.
