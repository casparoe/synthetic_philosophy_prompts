I've just come out of a long and unresolved argument with an old friend about conditionality in social assistance systems, and I'd like help working out what the arrangement we were arguing about actually presupposes about obligation, contribution, and desert. Let me set out the practice as the two of us understand it, because I suspect the disagreement turns on it. In most rich countries, working-age support for people judged capable of work is conditional: you must register, document your job search, attend appointments, accept suitable offers, and sometimes perform a placement, on pain of your payment being cut or stopped for a fixed period. The work done on those placements is not treated as work in any other respect: it does not attract a wage, does not count as employment in the statistics, does not accrue pension or employment rights, and leaving it can itself be sanctioned. Entitlement is means-tested against household income and savings, so a partner's earnings or a modest nest egg can extinguish a claim. Claiming while working, or failing to declare capital, is treated as fraud: overpayments are recovered, sometimes with penalties or prosecution, and anti-fraud enforcement is genuinely funded and publicised. Failing to claim money you are entitled to is barely measured, almost never chased, and carries no consequence for anyone. And none of this applies to pensioners or to people assessed as unable to work, who receive support with no activity test at all and are never described as undeserving.

My friend's claim was that this whole apparatus expresses one idea: that you owe something back in exchange for what you receive, and that the currency of the debt is effort or contribution rather than need. He then pushed the idea somewhere I hadn't followed him. If contribution is the currency, he said, then full-time care of small children or of a disabled relative, or sustained unpaid work for a community organisation, is plainly contribution and should discharge the debt; and more sharply, someone who never works because they live off inherited wealth or investment income is the purest case there is of a person living off other people's labour, so a consistent view of this kind should attach obligations to them too, or at least should stop treating the receipt of public money as the only kind of receipt that carries any strings. I replied that conditionality might have nothing to do with desert and be purely instrumental: a crude but cheap way to sort people who could work from people who cannot, to deter marginal claims, to hold down expenditure, and to keep a programme politically survivable. He said that if that were all it was, then the fraud-versus-non-take-up asymmetry, the exemption of pensioners, and the refusal to count placement work as work would all be accidents, which is too convenient; and, he added, an instrumental story would predict roughly comparable effort spent on encouraging take-up as on pursuing fraud, since on that story the problem is mis-targeting in both directions.

I don't trust either of our reconstructions, so here is what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory that this package of practices only makes sense under: what it holds about when one person owes something to another, about what discharges the debt, and about the standing of need. Then, and this is the part I care most about, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined in the sense that several incompatible theories would license exactly these arrangements. Say whether the pieces are consistent with one another: the placement that is compulsory but not employment, the household means test applied to an individual obligation, the zeal about fraud alongside the indifference to non-take-up, the unconditional treatment of pensioners. For each of those, tell me whether it is load-bearing for the underlying theory or a historical residue that could be removed without anything else changing. Finally, take my friend's symmetry proposal seriously and say what a system built on it would concretely have to do differently — who would become subject to requirements who isn't now, what would have to be counted as work, and whether the resulting institution would still be recognisable as the one we were arguing about — and contrast that with what would have to change if the desert framing were dropped entirely and need were treated as the only relevant consideration.
