I teach a third-year seminar on epistemic modality, and I need a case I have not been able to find ready-made: one in which the KK principle holds at the first iteration and fails at the second. I want to use it against a move my students keep making — that once you grant a margin-for-error style argument against the luminosity of knowledge, KK failure is all-or-nothing, so either knowledge is fully self-aware or we are never in a position to know that we know anything. A case where iteration runs out at level three but not level two shows that the failure is graded, and that is the point I want the scenario to carry.

So: construct an ordinary case in which a single subject S knows that p, knows that she knows that p, and does not know that she knows that she knows that p — with the step from the second verdict to the third generated by the account of knowledge you use, not stipulated by fiat.

Constraints:

1. Realistic and one paragraph. A workplace, a clinic, a kitchen, a workshop, a cockpit, a lab bench. No Cartesian demons, brains in vats, lotteries, fake barns, Gettier gadgets, skeptical scenarios, or exotic technology. The proposition p should be the sort of thing the character would actually care about.
2. Everything except the modal margin is held fixed across the three levels. Every belief S forms in the case is true; her first-order method is genuinely reliable; her method for judging her own epistemic position is genuinely reliable too; she is not tired, distracted, impaired, or deceived. If the level-three failure traces to a false belief, an unreliable method, or an environmental trick rather than to margin, the case does not count.
3. State the account you are testing against and justify it as one a defender of KK would grant: a safety condition (S knows that q only if, in nearby worlds in which S believes q by the same method, q is true) or a Williamson-style margin-for-error condition. Then show the arithmetic: what the actual distance to the nearest not-p world is, how much of it each level of iteration consumes, and why level three runs out. Use a quantity where the margin is genuinely tunable and tell me the units.
4. S may not improve her margin from inside the scenario — no second instrument, no expert to consult, no re-measuring — so the level-three failure is not remediable by anything available to her.
5. The feature doing the work must be the coarseness of S's method for self-ascription: her capacity to tell that she knows does not track the remaining margin perfectly, and each act of self-ascription eats some of it. Say explicitly what happens if that feature is removed — if her second-order method discriminated the margin exactly, or if the underlying quantity were sharp rather than graded. I expect the case then either collapses into full KK or fails already at level two; tell me which, and why.
6. Give me the two best replies available to a defender of KK and your estimate of which is stronger. I have in mind, first, the reply that S can know that she knows p by reflecting on her first-order evidence rather than by running a separate and coarser second-order method, so no extra margin gets consumed; and second, the reply that the sphere of nearby worlds relevant to evaluating a higher-order belief is narrower than the first-order sphere, so the level-three belief is safe after all. Address both, and say what I would have to change in the scenario to blunt each of them.

Finally, be explicit about where your own construction is most likely to break. Which parameter is carrying the verdict — the radius of the similarity sphere, the coarseness you assigned to the second-order method, or the choice between safety and margin-for-error? What value of that parameter, or what argument, would flip the result to full KK or to failure at level two? And does the case survive if the test is switched from a safety condition to a sensitivity condition? I would rather have a case with its weak point flagged than one that looks airtight because the load-bearing assumption was left implicit.
