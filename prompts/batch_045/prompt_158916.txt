I'm a retired physical chemist — forty-odd years, mostly Raman and NMR, and far too much of it wrestling with cryostats — and since I retired I've been sitting in on a reading group in the philosophy department. That's how I ran into the identity of indiscernibles and Black's two spheres, and I've come out of it with a pile of gut verdicts that I don't think hang together. I want to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether some of them have to go.

Here are the cases, and how I actually react to each.

1. Black's setup: a universe containing exactly two iron spheres, one mile apart, identical in every qualitative respect — same radius, same composition, same temperature, same everything, nothing else in the universe. Verdict: two spheres, definitely. The principle is false. And I'm unimpressed by the move that says they're discerned by relational properties: each one is a mile from a sphere just like it, so they share those properties too.

2. One electron delocalized across two traps, one quantum state, two places. Verdict: one thing, not two.

3. A Bose–Einstein condensate: a few thousand rubidium atoms, all in the same single-particle state. Verdict: I don't want to say there are really a thousand objects there. The count feels like bookkeeping, not like a number of things. My chemist's instinct kicks hard against this — the mass is a thousand atoms' worth, the thermodynamics depends on the number — but I still think the verdict is right, that those are properties of one state rather than counts of individuals.

4. Two iron atoms at different sites in a crystal lattice at low temperature. Verdict: obviously two. If a permutation-symmetry argument of the kind that moves me in case 3 is going to tell me these aren't two either, then I think the argument has broken, because counting atoms exactly like these did real work in my career.

5. Two electrons in the ground state of a helium atom. Verdict: also two, but I'm much less sure why. And I notice that the people who push the indistinguishability line treat this as a case where "which is which" has no answer at all, not as a case where there's an answer I happen not to know.

6. Two byte-identical copies of the same PDF on two hard drives. Verdict: two files — but somehow less two than the two spheres. The second copy adds nothing, and if someone tells me it's one file with two locations I don't much mind, whereas if they said that about the spheres I'd argue.

7. Two qualitatively identical macroscopic spheres in a world like ours, but described by a properly symmetrized quantum state, so that swapping them makes no empirical difference. Verdict: genuinely torn. Part of me says quantum mechanics is silent here, that at that scale it's trajectories and records that do the individuating; another part says that if the physics doesn't register a difference, the metaphysics shouldn't either.

The principle I've been kicking around is something like: if swapping the labels changes something — a prediction, a trajectory, a record — there are two objects; if swapping changes nothing at all, there is one, and any count above one is a manner of speaking. I think that gets 2, 3 and 6, but I can't see what it does with 1 (swapping the spheres seems to change nothing, since the universe is symmetric, yet there are two) or with 4 and 5 (where swapping seems to change something, so I get two — but then I don't see why the same reasoning doesn't hand case 3 back to the chemists, or why 7 feels different from 1). I've also heard something about weak discernibility — the idea that a symmetric, irreflexive relation like "is a mile from" is enough to make the spheres two — and I half get it, but I want to know whether it's a real answer or a trick with quantifiers, and whether it then forces me to say the condensate atoms are discernible from each other as well, since they bear relations to each other too.

So: is there a principle, or a short list of candidates, that captures this? I'd like each one stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on a case I haven't listed (two qualitatively identical galaxies; or a universe with one sphere versus two, nothing else differing), and an honest account of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which verdict you'd overturn and why. Please write in American English.

And be straight about your confidence: tell me where there's a settled answer I simply don't know versus where the literature is genuinely split, and say what would change your mind — which argument or consideration, if I found it convincing, would flip you on the case you think is closest, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
