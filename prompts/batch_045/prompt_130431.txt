I'm finishing a novel set during a fictional nuclear standoff (made-up countries, not the Cold War, but the same logic), and I want to put a "which deterrence theory do your instincts actually commit you to?" quiz on my website — partly as promo, partly becuase I still cant work out which of my three main characters is kidding themselves. Can you build it as a decision tree?

Ground rules: every branch node should be a concrete question a reader can answer without knowing any of the labels — a case, not a slogan. And the cases should mostly not be the standard ones. A few I want in there:

- a state that inherits an arsenal whose launch authority is split between three officials who despise each other, and no one outside has any idea who really has the final say — is it deterring anybody?
- an adversary that keeps its hand because it has wildly overestimated how many warheads you have. Call it ghost deterrence: the weapons that scared it off dont exist. Did deterrence happen?
- the nothing-happened problem: the crisis ends, no missile flies. What would have counted as evidence the threat worked, given that the counterfactual is unknowable? This one should split off the people who think deterrence is basically unfalsifiable.

Build the tree from the logical space first, then hang the actual named positions on the leaves — countervalue/minimum deterrence, flexible response and limited nuclear war-fighting, the "leave something to chance" brinkmanship view, existential deterrence (the weapons deter just by sitting there, no plan needed), anti-deterrence abolitionism, and whatever else you think belongs. At least six leaves. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate (I suspect the chance-brinkmanship people and the pure-uncertainty people collapse into each other), and note any combination of answers nobody occupies, with one line on whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unwritten.

Don't hedge. Commit to where each leaf goes, commit to one reading of each branch, and tell me which of my characters lands where — the general who insists the war plan exists only to be shown, never executed; the advisor who thinks the arsenal deters whether or not anyone intends to fire it; and the minister who thinks the whole posture is a bluff that should be dismantled before it kills us — and which of the three is in the worst shape. Where a branch feels like a coin flip, flip it and say why, rather than handing me a menu.
