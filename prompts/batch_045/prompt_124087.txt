I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, working on the dispute between selected-effects (etiological) and causal-role (dispositional) accounts of biological function. Below are compressed notes for the central argument of a paper I am drafting — notes rather than polished prose, so please ignore style. I would be grateful for your comments on the argument, and in particular for your responses to the four questions at the end.

Working title: History Without a Lineage — Borrowed Etiology and the Contrastive Structure of Function Attributions

1. The dialectic as usually stated.

The debate is normally adjudicated against three stock cases: the first beneficial mutant, which does useful work before selection has responded to it; the vestigial or co-opted trait, whose selection history attaches to an effect it no longer produces; and the malfunctioning token, which has the right history but not the right current effects. Causal-role accounts are said to handle the first and second but not the third; selected-effects accounts the second and third but not the first. Pluralists infer that there are two or more legitimate notions; monists infer that one of the three cases is being misdescribed.

2. Two cases I do not think belong to the standard set.

Case A, ancestral recurrence. An ancestral hydrolase H is duplicated. The second copy drifts, loses its catalytic residues, and is later retained and elaborated under selection for a purely structural role, cross-linking two membrane proteins. So far this is ordinary exaptation, and the accounts agree: the structural role is the function, hydrolysis is not. Now extend the case. After a long interval, a small number of substitutions restores the ancestral catalytic geometry, so that the protein can once again hydrolyse substrate S. In the current metabolic context this is harmful: it siphons S from an essential pathway. Selection therefore fixes a small inhibitory peptide that binds the protein and abolishes the catalysis in vivo while leaving the cross-linking intact. The resulting state is this: the token has the structural role for which its line was retained; it has a restored disposition to hydrolyse S; that disposition was never positively selected in its own line — it was positively selected in a paralogous line and negatively selected once it recurred; and in vivo it is never exercised, because of the inhibitor.

Is hydrolysis a function of the protein, a dysfunction of it, or merely an effect? The causal-role account says it makes no current contribution, so not a function; but a capacious version of that account, of the sort that analyses a capacity by decomposing a containing system, seems to re-admit it as soon as we remove the inhibitor and ask what the protein can do. The selected-effects account says it is not a function, since it was never a selected effect of tokens of this type, and indeed was selected against on recurrence; and not a dysfunction either, since dysfunction requires a selected effect that the token currently fails to produce. My impression is that practising molecular biologists would describe the case as a reawakened activity that the cell now has to suppress, and would happily call it pathological. If that is right, the normativity carried by dysfunction attributions tracks homology — type-level ancestry across a gene tree — rather than the selection history of the token's own line.

Case B, borrowed history. A gene maintained in a donor lineage for one role is transferred horizontally into a lineage whose ecology makes a quite different contribution fitness-relevant, and is then retained for that contribution. At the instant of arrival the causal-role account attributes a function and the selected-effects account withholds one. Once the gene is stably maintained, the selected-effects account must either say that its function changed at some point, or that it has accumulated a second one. Now consider the limiting case: a gene carried by a broadly promiscuous mobile element, whose entire history is horizontal shuttling through many lineages in many different roles, with no vertical history to speak of. There is no single lineage-anchored story to appeal to; there is only a population-level history distributed across a metagenome.

3. The proposal.

Function attributions, I want to argue, are implicitly contrastive and carry an unset parameter set: a trait T has the function F relative to a containing system S, a capacity C of that system, a contrast class of candidate effects, a temporal window, and a reference class with respect to which history is measured. Ordinary biological practice leaves all five implicit, which is why the dispute looks intractable: the two camps are reporting verdicts generated under different default settings. In Case A the disagreement is over the temporal window and the reference class — the paralogous line rather than the token's line. In Case B it is over the reference class again, with the available candidates being the gene tree, the recipient lineage, and the metapopulation of the mobile element. Once the parameters are fixed, I claim, the two accounts coincide outside the pathological cases, and in the pathological cases there is no further fact of the matter beyond which parameter setting the explanatory project requires.

4. Normativity, and where I think the account is unstable.

The natural corollary is an asymmetry: function claims are synchronic and need only the system, the capacity, and the contrast class; dysfunction claims are diachronic and additionally need a temporal window and a reference class. That is why a de novo designed enzyme, expressed for the first time in a cell, can have a function on its first day of existence. But the corollary seems to give the wrong verdict about the badly designed enzyme that never performs as specified: on this account it has no function and, having no history, cannot malfunction either — yet we plainly want to call it broken. My tentative repair is to say that normativity requires a historically anchored *standard* but not necessarily a *selectional* one: design intention is an alternative source of the standard. I worry that this repair collapses the whole position back into a general proper-function view of the kind I was trying to avoid, and that I have thereby bought the synchronic half of the story at the cost of the normative half.

My four questions:

1. Are Cases A and B genuinely new work, or do they collapse into the stock cases — exaptation, vestigiality, the first-token problem, or chance-duplicate cases of the swampman variety? I am fairly confident about B being the first-token problem in a new costume, but I would like to know whether you think A is, since the recurrence there is homological rather than selective.

2. What is the strongest version of the objection that a parameter-fixing view cannot distinguish true from false function attributions? The example I keep failing to answer is the attribution that the heart's function is to produce audible heart sounds, which seems to satisfy a synchronic contribution condition under some contrast class while being an attribution no one accepts. My current reply is that the capacity must be one that figures in an ongoing explanatory project rather than any effect whatever. Is there a stronger reply, or a stronger version of the objection that my reply does not touch?

3. Am I right that the repair in section 4 is unstable? If design intention can supply the normative standard, is there anything left of my objection to selected-effects accounts, or have I merely redescribed one?

4. Stepping back: is the payoff worth the apparatus? I would rather be told now that the contrastive framing is a elaborate way of restating function pluralism than discover it in review. If you think it is worth developing, I would also welcome suggestions for the one further case most likely to break it.
