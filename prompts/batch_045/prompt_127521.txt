Next month at a one-day workshop on AI sentience funded by an animal welfare foundation I'm giving 25 minutes on why today's deep RL agents aren't even candidates for suffering, and I want to be ready for Q and A. The room: two comparitive psychologists who've spent their careers arguing on behavioural and functional grounds that fish and decapods feel pain, three ML people (one builds RL systems for a living and thinks the whole question is confused), a neuroscientist who works on dopamine and effort-based choice, and a utilitarian philosopher who's maybe 40 percent inclined to think RL welfare might matter. Friendly, but not a crowd that will let a sloppy argument pass.

The argument is still rough, but its shape is this. I don't want to lean on substrate, or on the systems being digital, or on the claim that they lack the right biology. I want a structural point: in standard deep RL the reward is a per-step scalar that is zeroed at every episode reset, that never enters the agents own forward pass (it isn't part of the observation or the state), and that does its causal work only later, by moving weights once the episode is over. So within any episode, what the agent does is fully fixed by weights the rewards it is currently getting cannot touch. But in us, the bad feeling is itself one of the causes of what we do next, right there in the moment. So the reward signal can't be doing the job valence does in us, and a system with no intra-episode causal role for reward has no states that could be suffering. I also want to add a softer backup point: even if you did give reward a role inside the episode, it would look more like wanting than liking, something that pushes rather than something that feels a certain way.

Mark which objections have real force and what I should just concede up front.
