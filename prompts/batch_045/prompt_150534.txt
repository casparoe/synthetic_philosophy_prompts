I am writing a short essay — roughly 2,500 words, for a generally educated reader with some philosophy — on whether generational categories (Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z) name anything real, and I would like your comments on the argument before I take it further. Below is the draft in note form: the spine of the argument, with the transitions still rough and the examples thin. I would rather know now which step is broken than after I have written around it.

The question, as I want to pose it, is not whether people born in one decade differ on average from people born in another. They obviously do, and no one denies it. The question is whether our generational labels cut at a joint, or whether they are a filing system that has been mistaken for an anatomy. To get the question into focus I separate three things that are usually run together. First, a birth cohort: everyone born within a stated range of years, an administrative object with arbitrary edges. Second, an exposure group: people who encountered some event, technology, or economic condition at roughly the same stage of life. Third, what I will call a character-generation: a named figure with a temperament, a reputation, a set of complaints about it, and an implied relation of succession to the figures before and after it. Only the third is philosophically interesting, because only the third is offered as an explanation rather than as a convenience. My strategy is to argue that the third cannot be reduced to the second, that it is not a discovered natural kind either, and that what it actually is — a position in a serial order whose content is filled in by contrast with its neighbours — is both less than realism and more than mere convention.

The firstargument is what I call the stagger problem. Take the roughly fifteen-year span usually assigned to Millennials, early 1980s to mid-1990s. A person born at the start of it was around twenty on 11 September 2001 and around twenty-seven during the financial crisis; a person born at the end of it was around five and around twelve. The same events, encountered at utterly different points in the life course, are supposed to have produced a shared formative signature. But a shared exposure is only formative in virtue of the age at which it is had, so a fifteen-year cohort is not an exposure group at all — it is several exposure groups stacked together and given one name. The standard repair is to concede fuzzy edges and retreat to a core. That concession seems to me fatal, because the core that remains is just the middle of an administrative convenience: nothing marks it except that the boundary cases have been excluded.

The second argument concerns retroactivity. Generational labels stabilise only once a cohort becomes legible as an object of marketing and commentary, and their boundaries are then redrawn backwards: the start of the Millennial range has moved by several years over the last two decades, and micro-generations get invented — Xennials, Zillennials — precisely for the people whom the boundaries mishandle. Discovery does not behave like that. When we discover a real structure, the surprise is in the content and the boundaries get sharper with inquiry; here the surprise is in the boundaries and the content gets rewritten to suit them. I also want to flag a small piece of evidence I find revealing: membership is officially all-or-nothing, a matter of birth year, and yet it is routinely attributed in degrees — someone is very Millennial, or an elder Millennial, in a way that no one is very 1987. If the predicate tracked a birth-year fact, the scalar use would be nonsense; its naturalness suggests the predicate tracks a fit to a portrait.

What I then want to propose positively is that a character-generation is best understood as a slot in a serial order rather than as a kind with an essence. On this view, what is real about generations is the succession: this cohort stands after that one and before the next, and the number of available slots stays roughly constant because the slots are defined by their relations, not by their occupants. The content of each slot is inherited contrastively — the traits assigned to a generation are largely selected to be the negation of those assigned to the generation immediately before it, which is why the reputed traits of a cohort flip when the reputation of its predecessor flips, and why the same cohort can be described as cynical in one decade and as dutiful in the next without any change in the cohort. I call this contrast inheritance, and I want to test it with the observation that the smallest interesting unit of generational discourse is not the cohort but the pair: no one can characterise Gen Z without implicitly characterising Millennials.

The test I propose for the realist is what I am calling the translation test. If generational kinds were discovered rather than devised, we would expect convergence: independent observers carving the same population at the same joints should arrive at the same cuts, and the labels should travel. What we appear to find instead is that each country cuts at years that answer to its own local history, and that a putatively global label like Gen Z is applied to populations whose actual conditions differ enormously. I am unsure how much weight this bears, since a realist can reply that the underlying structure is global while the names are parochial, and I would like your view on whether that reply is a genuine escape or a way of making the view unfalsifiable.

There are cases I know the essay must handle and have not yet handled well. The Greatest Generation looks like a generation defined by an achievement rather than by a birth span, and so may be an honorific that has borrowed the grammar of a cohort. The Silent Generation looks like a slot that was named only because its neighbours were loud, which on my own account should be the normal case rather than the exception. Generation Alpha is being named now, before its members have any public life at all, which is a case of a slot being created in advance of any content — awkward for realism, and oddly supportive of my view. And a hypothetical: if demographic change produced a cohort thirty years long, would we split it, and by what principle other than narrative convenience?

I would like your comments on four things in particular. First, whether the three-way distinction between cohort, exposure group, and character-generation is stable, or whether the third quietly collapses back into the second once the argument is pressed — this is the failure I most fear. Second, which of my three arguments is weakest: the stagger problem, the retroactivity point, or the translation test, and whether the weakest one is repairable or should be cut. Third, whether contrast inheritance is genuinely incompatible with a realist treatment of generations or merely a redescription of it in different vocabulary, because if it is only a redescription the essay has no teeth. And fourth, whether any of this is interesting: is there a claim here that someone who has already thought about the ontology of social kinds would be surprised by, and if not, what single addition would make it so? Apply the translation test yourself to two cases the draft does not handle — say, Gen Z as a global category and the Greatest Generation — and tell me what it returns, since I cannot tell whether my own application is honest. If a section should be cut or moved, say which and why.

Please reply in continuous prose, as you would in a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italicised text, no tables, and no structural signposting of any kind. Work the answers to my four questions into the flow of your response rather than taking them in turn. Around 800 words.
