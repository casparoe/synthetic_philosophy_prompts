I'm giving the toast at my old boss's retirement dinner, and I'd like you to write it.

Miriam Halloran sat on our state's highest court for twenty-two years and is known for one thing: a long run of dissents arguing that the state's capital sentencing scheme could not be administered as the legislature wrote it. She was regularly called an abolitionist and regularly denied being one, and the denial was neither tactics nor modesty. Her position — which she never wrote up anywhere but in her opinions, and which she dislikes hearing paraphrased at dinners — was roughly this. Some murders genuinely merit death, so retribution is not by itself an argument against capital punishment; on her view a state that never inflicts a punishment that is actually deserved has failed the victim and the offender both. But a punishment is only the state's to inflict if the state has the standing to inflict it, and a state that will sometimes kill someone who did not do the killing, and that can never afterwards undo it, has no such standing. She insisted this was a retributive defect and not a utilitarian one: nothing in it turned on whether the risk of error outweighs the gains from deterrence or from closure for victims' families, and she thought the familiar death-is-different framing and the evolving-standards analysis put the argument in the wrong place altogether. What mattered to her was that desert is something a state must be entitled to exact, and an irreversible mistake is not a misjudged desert but the exercise of a power that nobody has. She also refused the consolations that usually travel with this view: she would not use the phrase the system worked about any grant of post-conviction relief, and she thought calling an exonerated person lucky was close to obscene.

The room will be about sixty people. A third of them are men she and her clerks got off death row, together with their families. Another third are career prosecutors and people from a victim-advocacy organization in the county she grew up in, who think she spent her career finding reasons to spare murderers and who came anyway because they love her. The rest are former clerks and colleagues who have been arguing retribution and deterrence at one another for two decades and will catch every allusion. Her brother Ray, a retired police officer who is convinced the deterrence evidence is settled and that she simply ignored it, will be at the next table.

Last week she asked me for one thing: that the toast not be about that. She has spent twenty-two years being introduced as the capital punishment judge, she wants one evening about something else, and she means it. So: a warm toast of roughly five minutes, seven hundred words or so, that never uses the words death penalty, capital punishment, deterrence, retribution, desert or innocent, and that never claims or implies that she won, since she mostly lost and her dissents are still dissents. It has to land for all three groups at once — the exonerees must not be turned into exhibits, the prosecutors must not be quietly informed that they were on the wrong side, and the clerks should recognize the actual structure of her argument without anyone being lectured. The one thing she would want, and the test I'll apply, is that nobody in that room was spoken past.
