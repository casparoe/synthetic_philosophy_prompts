I'm in the second year of a doctorate and I'm trying to convert a reading-group handout on dispositions into the opening section of a paper, and I've reached the stage where I can no longer tell whether what I've assembled is a position or just a list of things I would like to be true. So let me state it flatly and have you stress it. (1) Dispositions are real and irreducible to their categorical bases: the glass's fragility is not identical with, and is not entailed by, any purely microstructural description of the glass; you need irreducibly modal vocabulary to say what that structure does. (2) But the causal work in a manifestation is done by the basis together with the stimulus; the disposition is not a further cause running alongside them, and I don't want to say that every shattering is systematically overdetermined by a microstructure and a disposition. (3) Dispositions are intrinsic: whether a thing has a given disposition supervenes on its intrinsic properties. This is what lets me say that the carefully packed glass is still fragile, and that a wire guarded by a finkish cut-off device is still live. (4) Despite (1), the right account of x is disposed to M in response to S is a reformed conditional analysis: x has some intrinsic basis B such that, were x to undergo S and retain B long enough, S together with B would bring about M — and I add a second, separate repair clause for masks, to the effect that the manifestation occurs only if nothing extrinsic blocks it. I want the persistence clause and the mask clause to stay distinct: finks and masks are, on my view, two different failures of the naive conditional, needing two different fixes. (5) Dispositions are individuated by their stimulus and manifestation conditions and by nothing else. (6) Dispositions are metaphysically prior to the laws: laws hold because of what the things involved are disposed to do, not conversely, so an account of dispositions should not help itself to a stock of contingent laws in order to say what the dispositions are.

Two cases of my own do a lot of work in the handout. Call a case an autological mask when the blocker is intrinsic to the object: a ceramic whose own lattice, under impact above a threshold, undergoes a phase change that dissipates the energy, so that the thing never breaks however hard it is struck and however long it retains its microstructure. I want to say that this object is fragile. Call a case a chaperone when the manifestation needs an extrinsic enabler: a salt that dissolves in water only when a trace ion is present in the water, and does nothing at all in ultrapure water. I want to say that this salt is soluble, that the ion is a background enabler rather than part of the stimulus, and that redescribing the disposition as solubility-in-ionised-water is gerrymandering rather than a discovery.

What I want is an audit, not a survey, and I should say that I'm away from the library for a fortnight, so I need you to argue from the cases and from what each of my commitments commits me to, rather than pointing me at papers. Tell me which of (1) to (6) actually pull against each other, and rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal; for each real tension say precisely what would have to give, what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set, and whether the cheapest repair is to drop a commitment, qualify it, or split it into two claims only one of which I need. Also tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection — a friend in the group insists that (1) and (2) are straightforwardly incompatible, and I think she is wrong but I can't say why in a sentence — and, if my verdicts on the autological mask and the chaperone are themselves the weak points rather than the principles behind them, say so explicitly. I would rather not surrender (1) or (3), but if that is where the pressure really lands, tell me. End by naming the single revision that would clean up the most.
