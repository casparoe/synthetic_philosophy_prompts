I'm in the first year of a law-and-psychology PhD program and I've finally talked my advisor into letting me write my own second-year paper instead of being an RA on somebody else's study. I want to spend it on something that could actually move somebody's view rather than on something that just adds one more citation to an already enormous pile.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. Everyone in the area cites the Libet-style setup — the slow negative drift over motor cortex before a spontaneous self-paced movement, the time people report for when they first felt the urge or intention to move, and the claim that there's still a short window near the end in which the movement can be consciously called off. Then everyone uses that package to argue either that conscious willing is an illusion or that the veto window rescues it, and both of those moves feel like they assume way to much. I've also been reading the pushback that the pre-movement ramp may not contain any decision at all — that it's just slow stochastic accumulation drifting toward a threshold, so that what looks like early preparation is really just noise occasionally crossing a boundary, and the reported time of intention is a reconstruction rather than a reading. So: stipulate, for the sake of the prompt, that the accumulator-ish story is basically right for arbitrary self-paced finger movements. But also stipulate that a big pre-registered multi-lab follow-up finds something that does not fit that story for a different kind of case: when people make a deliberate, reasons-responsive choice between two specified options, the pre-movement signal, starting somewhere around a couple hundred milliseconds out, reliably encodes which of the two options the person is going to pick, before the time at which they say they decided. Call it the decoded-deliberation result. Assume it replicates.

Given that world, what are the two or three open questions in the neuroscience-of-free-will area that you'd actually prioritize, and why? I want the reasoning more than the list: what makes each question upstream of the others (so that answering it changes what the others even are), what's genuinely tractable for someone who can't collect data and isn't a neurophysiologist, and what result over the next couple of years would move it up or down your ranking. And the part I care about most: which of the usual alleged implications actually follow from a world like that — criminal sentencing, blame in ordinary interpersonal life, the insanity defense, the idea that if people internalized the neuroscience they'd stop holding each other responsible, the "the law will dissolve once everyone knows this" thing my roommate keeps insisting on — which of these are real, which are overrated, and which just don't follow at all? Please rank them and say what you're ranking them by, and tell me which two I should build the paper around.
