I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory working on the normative side of AI governance, and next term I'm leading a first-year graduate reading group on risk, uncertainty, and political decision-making. I want to end the handout with a classification instrument for the precautionary principle, and I want it built the way you would build it if the names didn't exist yet: start from the logical space carved out by a handful of branching questions, put the actual views in the cells afterwards, and then tell me which cells are empty and why nobody occupies them. The reason I want a tree rather than a list is that in the literature as my students encounter it the labels do all the work — everyone agrees on the words and disagrees about which cases they cover — and I have found, trying to draft this myself, that my tree keeps collapsing into a single axis of how bad the worst case is, which cannot be right, because people who agree completely about the worst case still disagree about whether precaution applies.

So here is what I need. Seven or eight branch questions, at most twelve leaves. Every node has to be a question about a concrete case that a student can answer without knowing what position it leads to — no node may be anything like do you accept the precautionary principle, and ideally no node should mention the principle at all. Draw the cases from my field: a statutory pause on training models above a compute threshold; a duty to file evaluation results with a regulator before deployment; a ban on publishing the weights of a model that lowers the skill barrier for a class of biological work; a small outdoor field trial of a climate intervention; a gene-drive release for malaria control; and a requirement that developers carry liability insurance. The distinctions I want load-bearing are these: whether the uncertainty is better described as an inability to assign any probability or as an inability to assign a precise one; whether what is irreversible is the outcome, the option set, or the institutional settlement, since in my cases a pause that expires after six months and a liability rule that shapes industry structure for a decade are both called precautionary and seem to me to be doing different things; whether the status quo itself carries catastrophic risk of comparable size, so that precaution appears to forbid both acting and not acting; whether abstaining destroys the information that would reduce risk later, which several of my cases turn on; whether the burdens of abstaining fall on identifiable people now or on merely possible people later; and whether the case is genuinely one-shot or one episode in a long sequence of similar choices. Leaves that have to appear somewhere: the strict worst-case or maximin family, including the versions that put weight on less-than-worst cases; the proportionality-and-comparison style of view associated with the 1992 Rio Declaration; the view that shifts the burden of proof procedurally without saying what the right outcome is; the view that the principle is entirely reducible to expected-utility reasoning plus aversion to ambiguity; the view that it is paralyzing and so contentless because it condemns every option including inaction; the symmetric view that precaution must be applied to precaution itself; and, if the space allows it, the view that precaution is really a claim about legitimacy, consent, or who gets to decide rather than about outcomes. For each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two more things, and they matter more to me than the shape of the tree. First, I want the empty cells handled honestly: name at least two combinations of answers my questions permit that no one defends, and say for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered. Second, flag the pairs your tree cannot separate — I suspect the proportionality view and the ambiguity-aversion reductionism come apart on almost nothing my questions can see, and I would rather have that admitted than have an extra node invented to fake the separation. Please also write in your own epistemic position: mark which of your claim-to-leaf mappings you hold confidently and which you would give up, name the argument or case that would make you redraw the tree, and tell me where you think the instrument is most likely to misclassify a real person — where someone's answers to your cases will sort them into a view they would reject. I am not asking you to defend my own sympathies, but I should disclose that I lean toward the procedural versions, so please do not let the tree beg the question in their favour.
