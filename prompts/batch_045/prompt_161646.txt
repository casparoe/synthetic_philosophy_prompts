I am about to write the opening chapter of a thesis on radical interpretation, and before I commit myself I want an audit of the package of views I find I am holding, because I cannot tell which parts of it are genuinely pulling against the others and which merely look as though they are. The commitments, in the order I arrived at them, are these.

1. Interpretivism. There is nothing more to what a speaker's words mean than what a suitably placed interpreter could in principle work out from evidence of the speaker's overt responses — assent and dissent — in circumstances the interpreter can independently identify. Semantic facts are constitutively tied to interpretability; they are not hidden facts that interpretation merely tracks.

2. Charity, as a constitutive constraint. An interpreter must assign truth conditions in such a way that the speaker comes out by and large right about the world, with intelligible desires and a largely coherent pattern of belief. This is not a heuristic for discovery but a condition on there being any content to discover.

3. No semantic difference without an observable difference. The evidence base is as restricted as in 1. Two speakers who cannot be told apart by any overt disposition — who would assent and dissent in exactly the same circumstances, and would go on doing so — cannot differ in what they mean.

4. The duplicate. A molecule-for-molecule copy of one of us, thrown together by pure chance a moment ago and interacting with its surroundings in all the ways we do, has no thoughts at all. Nothing it produces means anything, and nothing it produces is true or false.

5. Indeterminacy. Two assignments of truth conditions can both fit every possible piece of evidence, and there is no further fact that settles which of them is correct.

6. Occasion-sensitivity. What a speaker means on an occasion is not fixed by shared conventions or by a prior theory common to speaker and interpreter; if it is fixed by anything, it is fixed by the passing theory the interpreter improvises as she goes along. Conventions are neither necessary nor sufficient for communication.

7. Compositionality nonetheless. The right form for a semantic theory is still a finitely stated, compositional theory of truth conditions for a language, deriving theorems for whole sentences from axioms for their parts.

The pair I keep circling back to is 2 and 5. It looks to me as though charity is meant to do real work in selecting among candidate theories, and as though indeterminacy then leaves it with no work to do, so that one of the two is idle. I have been told both that this is the real problem and that it is no problem at all, and I no longer trust my own judgement about it.

What I want from you is this. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal; for each one say exactly where the pressure comes from and precisely what would have to be given up, together with what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; and tell me whether there is a single revision, or a single distinction I have failed to draw, that would tidy up more than one of them at once. Please be concrete about the mechanisms, and do not pad the audit with a general exposition of radical interpretation.
