I write about science and ideas, and I am midway through a long piece on institutional reform — specifically, on how organizations decide which inherited procedures to keep. It began with a state agency instructed to strike every administrative rule whose written rationale no longer appears anywhere in the record, and it has since grown to include a hospital system that wants to replace mandatory in-person bedside handoffs with a line in the chart, and a university that wants to abolish the oral dissertation defense on the grounds that nobody can state what it is for. Two rough distinctions from the reporting keep doing work for me, and I am not sure they are sound. I call a practice an orphan when its originating reason is documented and known to be dead, and cryptic when no one now can say what work it is doing, including the people who defend it; and I sort practices by reversibility, since some can simply be reinstated if their removal goes badly (a meeting, a form) while others are ratchets — the thing that made them function is exactly what dissipates while they are gone, whether that is a trust network, an apprenticeship lineage, or a body of local knowledge that was never written down.

A political theorist I interviewed walked me into the following argument, which I have reconstructed as cleanly as I can and in which any errors of formulation are mine. (1) Chesterton's fence: the reformer who cannot say what an inherited practice is for may not remove it — in Chesterton's parable, the second reformer answers, "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it." (2) The force of the fence is epistemic rather than sentimental: long-standing practices carry functions nobody can see, encoding local knowledge, doing coordinating or compensatory work, holding equilibria in place that an outside reformer will misread as waste — which is also why Burke thought each person's private stock of reason is small next to the stock accumulated by many generations. (3) So the fence does real work only for cryptic practices; for orphans it is trivially satisfied, since you can say what the practice was put up for and you know that reason is dead. (4) But for cryptic practices the function is invisible by hypothesis, and the only procedure with any reliability for making it visible is removal under observation: stop doing the thing, watch what breaks, restore it if you can. Archival and historical inquiry tells you why a practice was introduced, not whether it is still load-bearing. Call this the probe requirement. (5) The fence therefore forbids, in exactly the cases where it has force, the only procedure that can satisfy it: you may not remove until you can say what the practice is for, and you cannot say what it is for until you remove it and look. (6) So the fence is either idle — already satisfied, in the orphan cases where nothing turns on it — or self-blocking, in the cryptic cases where it forbids the one route to the knowledge it demands. (7) A norm that is satisfied only where it is not needed and needed only where it cannot be satisfied guides nothing; so the appeal to inherited wisdom, dressed up as an epistemic principle, is really only a bias toward whatever happens to exist.

I do not want that conclusion, and my reporting keeps turning up reforms that went badly precisely because nobody asked what the old procedure was holding up — so if I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (7), and I would sooner give up (4) or (6). But I would like to be told I am wrong if I am. Which step should I reject, why did that step look fine when I wrote it down, and what does each available rejection cost me — what do I have to give up elsewhere in the picture, and does the reversibility distinction I have been carrying around actually help, or is it decoration? One more thing, since some of this may end up in print: please say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of. I would much rather run a hedge than a clean sentence that turns out to be false.
