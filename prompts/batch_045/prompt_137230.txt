I direct a graduate seminar in decision theory, and after last week's session two colleagues stayed behind and argued over lunch; they have since asked me to referee, and I would rather not answer off the cuff. One of them had presented Good's theorem in roughly this form: for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer whose utility function is fixed and takes only acts and states as arguments, the ex ante value of costless information is never negative, since the expectation of a maximum is at least the maximum of an expectation — you can always act as though you had not looked. Their exchange, as I reconstruct it:

(1) Miriam: Then the well-documented reluctance to take free predictive genetic tests is simply irrational. If the result could affect your plans, you should look; if it could not, looking costs you nothing.

(2) Ted: The theorem is fine, but its antecedent is exactly what is in dispute. It assumes utility attaches to acts and states, not to the agent's own credences. Add a self-directed term — a disutility that is a function of your own subjective probability of a bad outcome, the disutility of dreading it — and the proof no longer goes through, because information then moves utility directly and not merely through your choice. The refusers may be expected-utility maximizers with a richer utility function, not violators of the theory.

(3) Miriam: That concedes too little. Re-run the proof with a self-directed term B: the Jensen step only requires that B be convex in the credence. The natural model of dread, with disutility proportional to subjective probability, is linear and hence convex — the expected dread after learning equals the dread before learning — so the theorem survives untouched. A self-directed term is not enough; you need B to be non-convex.

(4) Ted: Then take the case that is arguably the phenomenologically accurate one, where the cost of finding out is lumpy rather than proportional. Call it threshold dread: a discrete penalty that switches on once your credence in the bad outcome passes some threshold, so that a person can live with a one-in-three chance of a late-onset disease and still be unwilling to convert that chance into certainty. That B is not convex; the expected penalty after a conclusive test exceeds the penalty at the prior; the test's expected value really is negative.

(5) Miriam: Grant that the proof breaks there — but that only relocates the argument. Good's theorem is conditional and says nothing about which utility functions are rationally admissible. The real question is whether lumpy, threshold-shaped dread is rationally permissible: if it is not, the refusers are irrational for a reason the theorem never stated, and if it is, the theorem simply does not apply to them, which the theorem never denied.

(6) Ted: Then the theorem is normatively close to empty. Its antecedent fails precisely in the cases where people actually decline information, so citing it to convict them of irrationality assumes the answer to the only question at issue; the appearance of a clean, purely formal argument against information-avoidance is an illusion.

Please referee this rather than settling the topic from scratch: I want a verdict on the arguments as they were actually made, not a fresh treatise on the value of information. Structure your reply in four named sections, in this order: "Verdict up front" (two to three sentences); "Move-by-move" (one short paragraph per numbered move, each opening with a rating from clearly mistaken, through partly right but incomplete, to clearly right); "The technical crux" (roughly 200 words: is Miriam's convexity point in (3) correct, does Ted's threshold case in (4) genuinely defeat it, and what exactly is the condition on the self-directed term); and "What both are missing" (roughly 200 words on anything the exchange leaves out — the ex ante/ex post contrast, strategic or observer effects on the value of a test, or whatever you take to matter more than either of these).
