So I'm in this cross-listed poli-sci/philosophy class on collective decision making, and we spent a week on herding: the thing where a line of perfectly rational people, each able to see what the people before them did, can end up doing something dumb even though every individual step was reasonable. My professor tossed off something like "which is why elections are secret" and moved on, and I haven't been able to let it go since, because it seems like at most half the story.

Here's what bugs me. In every election I've voted in, the whole apparatus is built around me not seeing anyone else's choice and nobody ever seeing mine: I go into a booth alone, there's a curtain, my name gets ticked off and the ballot is separated from it, and in a lot of places even photographing your own filled-out ballot is against the rules. And yet the same election is wrapped in herding machinery — months of public polling, endorsements everyone openly justifies in terms of "momentum", primaries where states vote one after another and the entire narrative is about who's surging after the first couple of contests, news outlets that hold off calling a state until the polls there have shut. And there are still small assemblies — the Swiss kind, some New England town meetings — where people raise their hands in front of their neighbours and it doesnt seem to cause a crisis. So the practice seems to say: my neighbour's revealed vote is dangerous information when it reaches me one at a time in real time, harmless or at least unavoidable in the aggregate, and dangerous again if it reaches me the day before I vote.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. What view of other people's choices as evidence does the secret ballot presuppose? I can think of four candidates and I can't figure out how they fit together: (1) other people's votes are not pure reports of their private information, they're already partly social, so pooling them destroys whatever independence you needed for the crowd to be wise in the first place; (2) people are bad at discounting — once you've seen the tally you can no longer tell apart what you actually knew from what you inferred from the tally, so the tally contaminates instead of informing; (3) it isn't epistemic at all, it's about coercion and vote buying — if you can't prove how you voted, nobody can buy or threaten your vote — and the anti-cascade story is something we tell afterwards; (4) something about legitimacy, that a collective decision is only really ours if it's built out of seperate judgements rather than imitation. Which of these does the practice strictly need, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and are any of them in tension? Note especially the weird bit: if (3) were the whole story, then publishing everyone's vote after the fact, or having a ballot you could look up later, should be unproblematic, and I'm not at all sure it is.

Then the part I actually want your opinion on. My friend has this scheme he won't shut up about, he calls it a "shadow tally": everyone votes secretly exactly as now, but a randomly drawn 10% of voters get a message an hour before polls close showing them the current tally of the other 90%, and they get to recast their vote, with only the second one counted. His pitch is that you keep the independence of the secret ballot for most of the electorate and then let a small, explicitly-designated sample inject the social information in a controlled dose. I think it's either brilliant or insane. Tell me what someone designing this would have to believe — about how much and what kind of information is in ordinary voters' private signals, about whether herding is a failure of aggregation or a failure of incentives, and about what makes an outcome legitimate — for the scheme to make sense. And then tell me whether, on the secret ballot's own terms, we should have replaced it with something like this ages ago. If you think the whole anti-cascade reading is backwards and the secret ballot is really only about coercion, say that too, and tell me what would actually change in how we design elections if we stopped believing the cascade story.
