I'm drafting a paper on supererogation, and I've built an argument whose conclusion I don't accept and whose steps I can no longer separately evaluate. I'd like you to find the exit.

Take "A is supererogatory for S" to mean at least this: S's doing A is morally praiseworthy, and S is morally permitted to omit A — not required to do it, and not blameworthy if she doesn't.

(1) Praiseworthiness is reason-responsive: S is morally praiseworthy for doing A only if she did A because of considerations that favor it, and those considerations are genuine moral reasons for her to do A. Where no moral reason speaks for an act, performing it is at most lucky or agreeable, not praiseworthy.

(2) If A is supererogatory for S, no moral reason speaks against S's doing A. The act is morally good and its omission is permitted, so nothing on the moral scorecard is against it.

(3) So for any supererogatory act: there is a moral reason for S to do A, and no moral reason against.

(4) Reasons settle what one ought to do: if there is a moral reason for S to do A and none against, then S has most moral reason to do A, and S morally ought to do A.

(5) If S morally ought to do A and omits A without excuse, S is blameworthy for the omission.

(6) So every supererogatory act is one whose omission is blameworthy — contradicting the definition. Nothing is supererogatory; every morally good act is required.

The argument also refuses to stop where I want it to. If (6) is right, then gratitude is never fitting, since thanks are owed only to someone who wasn't merely doing what she ought — you don't thank the driver for not running you over. There are no favors, no "above and beyond," no admiration, no saints: the whole family of positive reactive attitudes that answer to optional goods loses its objects. And the form doesn't seem moral-specific. Substitute "prudential" throughout (1)–(5) and every optional-but-worthwhile improvement becomes a requirement whose omission is a rational failing; substitute "epistemic" and every belief the evidence slightly supports becomes rationally mandatory. That is far more than I set out to prove.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the alternatives priced, not just catalogued — for each step, what a theorist who gives it up is thereby committed to, and whether that commitment is one the friend of supererogation can live with. I'd least like to lose (1) and (5), and I keep going back and forth between (2), (3) and (4); but if you think (5) is where to cut, say so and tell me what I'd be giving up. A referee has told me the trouble is really (2), on the grounds that the very features that make an act supererogatory — its cost, its risk, its claim on the agent's own projects — are reasons against performing it. I don't know whether to count those as moral reasons: if they are, (3) is false and the argument dies early; if they aren't, I can't see how the omission is morally permitted rather than merely prudentially tempting. Say which horn you'd take and why, and say what the defender of supererogation should concede counts against the category as opposed to against the argument.

Then, the part I most want your diagnosis to earn its keep on, because I can't find it handled anywhere and it's what I'd press in a seminar. Call it the problem of self-imposed cost. Three cases. First: two people can pull a child out of a canal; one is a passerby, the other has, earlier in the day and in order to be admired for it, strapped heavy weights to herself so that the same rescue becomes arduous and dangerous. Same benefit, same risk to the child, far greater cost to the agent — so, on any account that grades supererogation by cost, the second act goes further beyond the call. My reaction is that she has gamed something and the extra cost shouldn't count. Second: a person who registers as an anonymous kidney donor, or binds herself by a public pledge to give away half her income, takes on costs the rest of us don't have — and here the self-imposed cost seems to be what makes her admirable rather than something that undermines the admiration. Third: a person whose own carelessness created the danger now rescues at real cost to herself; I'm inclined to say this isn't supererogatory at all but something closer to a duty of repair. Give me a principle that sorts these three, tell me what it predicts about the firefighter who chose her job years earlier and foresaw the risks, and tell me whether your principle is independent of which step of my argument you rejected — that is, whether the problem of self-imposed cost is a genuine extra problem for accounts of supererogation or only looks like one from inside the collapsed argument.

Be committed. Open with a single sentence naming the step you'd reject, then defend it; price the alternatives honestly; and tell me how confident you are, what would move you off your diagnosis, and whether more than one step has to go.
