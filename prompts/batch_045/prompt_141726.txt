hey, I need a piece of satire for a small collection I'm putting together — the kind where the joke only lands if the institutional logic is actually, technically right.

Form: the orientation packet handed to new members of the Sortition Chamber, the upper house of a country that has replaced elections with selection by lot. Numbered clauses, plus a short FAQ at the end and one sidebar. Around 700–800 words. Voice is deadpan government-plain: the kind of booklet that says Congratulations and then takes something away from you two paragraphs later. American English please.

Everything institutional is invented, so don't go looking anything up — just make it hang together internally. Things I want it built on:

- Members are drawn from the National Register of Eligible Persons and serve a single non-renewable term; no one may serve twice, ever. The packet should be extremely proud of this and mention it more than once.
- But the people who run the draw, write the briefing materials, and facilitate the deliberation are permanent civil servants, and the packet keeps noting, without ever noticing, that several of them have held the same post for twenty-odd years. The career politician hasn't been abolished, they've just moved down a corridor and stopped having to stand for anything.
- Declining to serve is an offence, with a fine scaled to income, which the packet presents as a fairness measure rather than a compulsion measure.
- The bit I most want played straight: every seated chamber is paired with a shadow panel drawn from the same register at the same moment, which never convenes and is only surveyed by mail. If the seated chamber's votes drift too far from the shadow panel's survey answers, that is logged as evidence of deliberative distortion, and the chamber can be dissolved and re-drawn. Nobody in the packet should find this odd — the reformers genuinely cannot distinguish changing your mind from breaking the sample.

The target is the descriptive-representativeness ideal and the way term limits relocate careerism onto unelected staff — not any named person, theorist, or real country, so keep it fictional. Don't let the booklet wink at the reader or tell them what to think; stay deadpan, but make it legible enough that someone who's read about citizens' assemblies can point at the sentence where the real jab is. Bureaucratic cheer beats grimness here. Close on whatever input the lottery still can't randomize.
