You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory, the foundations of probability, and the methodology of assigning credence to untestable hypotheses. Please think about this carefully and at some length: before writing your answer, spend a while brainstorming candidate questions — including ones I have not listed — and then prune them against explicit criteria rather than presenting me with a survey.

Some context. I am seventy-one, retired after a career in observational astrophysics (large-scale structure, survey calibration, a good deal of error budgeting), and over the past three years I have been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy and running a small reading group with two retired colleagues, one a chemist and one a former statistician. I have also ended up with a modest donor-advised fund — enough for perhaps two or three postdoctoral fellowships for two years, or the equivalent in released time for junior faculty — which I have decided to spend on conceptual work rather than on another instrument. I would rather point it at one narrow cluster of questions than scatter it, and I have maybe five productive years of my own left to think alongside whoever takes it up.

The cluster I keep returning to is Pascal's mugging and its neighbourhood: an interlocutor claims, by way of some hand-wave about magic or other dimensions, that unless you hand over some trivial sum he will bring about an astronomically large number of deaths, and the naive expected-value computation seems to favour paying, while almost no one thinks one should pay. The familiar families of reply — bounding the utility function, putting complexity-based floors on priors, refusing to rank acts by expectation at all, decision-theoretic replies that turn on what a policy of paying would incentivise, probability thresholds, variance penalties — each seem to me to carry real costs, and I want to know which of the open questions in this area actually deserve someone's years. Here are the candidates I have scribbled down, offered only as raw material: (i) whether the puzzle is really about the unboundedness of the utility function or about the structure of the hypothesis space, given that for any complexity budget there are exponentially many distinct hypotheses of that complexity, so the total probability mass available to "some scenario with stakes on the order of the inverse of its prior" need not collapse the way any single prior does — the packing or multiplicity problem, as I have been calling it; (ii) whether the content of the improbable story matters over and above its description length — compare a mugger who invokes outright magic with one who cites a specific speculative mechanism that our best physics does not actually exclude, such as vacuum metastability or a future civilisation running an enormous number of mind-bearing simulations, holding the stated stakes and the claimed probability fixed, and ask whether giving the second story more credence is a mistake or whether mechanistic fit is doing work that complexity bounds miss; (iii) what changes when the demand is made simultaneously of very many people, so that the aggregate stakes are enormous while any individual payment is causally inert, and what changes when such offers recur across a life, so that what is chosen is a policy rather than an act; (iv) what changes when the demanded item is not fungible money but something like a small breach of a professional norm, an hour of attention, or a year off a research programme, so that the cost side is not a scalar; (v) the inverse case, where the tiny probability attaches to an enormous benefit purchased by a large sacrifice, and whether the helping/harming asymmetry should matter here as it seems to elsewhere; and (vi) the meta-question of whether this cluster belongs to decision theory at all, or is better funded as a question in the philosophy of probability and of scientific reasoning.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a bibliography: given roughly two or three researchers for two years and one interested amateur with five years, which two or three of these questions should be prioritised, in what order, and why? Please be explicit about the criteria you are using — how much other work depends on the answer, tractability in a couple of years, how neglected the question is, whether a positive or negative answer would actually change anyone's practice — and about the trade-offs between them. Tell me, too, what evidence or argument over the next two years would move a question up or down your list, and which candidates you would deliberately defund because they are nearer to settled than they look or because their resolution would not propagate. Finally, be candid about which of these a retired physicist with a working knowledge of probability could make genuine progress on and which genuinely require philosophical training I do not have. One practical note: I am writing this from a place with no library access and no wish to chase references, so please argue from the structure of the problems themselves rather than from who has said what about them.
