Design a classroom game for an undergraduate philosophy of physics seminar — 18 students, one 80-minute session, no prerequisites beyond propositional logic, and nothing needed except index cards, envelopes, and a whiteboard — that makes participants feel the force of Bell's theorem rather than being told it.

Use a three-player parity game: three players are placed in separated corners; a referee gives each a setting, 0 or 1, subject to the constraint that the three settings sum to an even number; each player then outputs a single bit. The team wins the round if the XOR of the three outputs is 0 when the settings are all 0, and is 1 for each of the other three admissible setting-triples. Players may agree on any strategy and share any written instructions in advance, but once separated they may not communicate, and an inspector enforces this. Structure it in three rounds: first let them play with shared randomness and watch them converge on a 75 percent ceiling; then require the strategy to be fully deterministic and fixed in advance, so that each player fills in a personal two-entry lookup table before separation and the group can see directly why no assignment of entries satisfies all four admissible setting-triples at once; then run a final round in which the referee's settings, instead of being drawn at random, are computed from the players' own strategies and from whatever they share — the group should discover that they can now win every round without communicating, and should have to argue about whether this is cheating or whether it exposes an assumption the earlier rounds were making silently.

Include a scoring scheme that needs no money (points, a team standings board, a forfeit), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each round onto one premise of the argument — locality, settings independence, and the step from a single-shot inconsistency to a bound on success rates over many rounds — and that is explicit about the two things the game cannot show: that shared randomness cannot improve on the ceiling, and that nothing achievable in the room actually beats it, since the quantum prediction is something the facilitator must assert rather than demonstrate. Close with what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run: players signalling by coughs or timing, someone trying to win by guessing the others' outputs, the group concluding that the absence of communication was the whole content of the theorem, and someone objecting that the final round is an excuse rather than a discovery.
