I spent rather more than thirty years as a field biologist — mostly a long-term study of a marked population of a small colonial seabird on one island in the north-east Atlantic, with a parallel record, kept almost by accident at first, of the soil chemistry and vegetation of the nesting terraces the birds build up and foul — and I have only come to philosophy since retiring, by way of a reading group at the nearest university. The talk I have rashly agreed to give there in a fortnight is my attempt to say what I think is wrong with the units-of-selection literature as I encountered it. Roughly: I want to argue that the question 'what is the unit of selection?' is really three questions run together — a bookkeeping question about which entities actually change in frequency across generations, a causal question about which differences among organisms explain the differences in reproductive success we measure, and a design or 'what is it for' question about which beneficiary the trait appears to serve — and that these normally have the same answer, which is why nobody noticed they were separable. The cases I care about are what I have started calling lagged niche construction: the terraces my birds modify persist far longer than a bird generation, so the environment doing the selecting in one decade was built by birds from the two or three decades before, whose allele frequencies were not the same as the current ones, and the entity whose properties explain the fitness differences is not the entity whose frequencies are changing. My conclusion, which I expect to be the controversial part, is that the gene's-eye and multi-level-selection camps are each answering a different one of my three questions and treating their answer as the answer, and that the way forward is to index every claim about units to a question rather than to keep hunting for the unit. I am not wedded to the jargon; if there is a cleaner way to make the tripartition, or if the lag case can be subsumed under something like the extended phenotype without remainder, I would rather find out now than from the floor.

What I would like from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defence of the view. The room will be about fifteen people: a committed multi-level selectionist who talks about groups as organisms and leans heavily on the covariance term of the Price equation; a gene's-eye partisan of the older school who thinks the whole matter was settled decades ago and that niche construction is the extended phenotype rediscovered; two doctoral students who have just learned the Price equation and think it dissolves the problem rather than restating it; a philosopher of science who works on causal modelling and will want to know whether my 'the terrace did the selecting' is a causal claim or merely a variance decomposition with a causal gloss; a historian of science who suspects the entire debate is verbal and will ask why anyone should care once we say everyone is right about something; and a whole-organism biologist who thinks both camps have erased the animal and its development. Please give me the eight or so objections I am most likely to get from that mixture — not the objections I would get from a purely philosophical audience — ordered by how much damage they would do me, each phrased the way that particular person would actually put it rather than in neutral philosophical prose, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where my reply is soft or question-begging. Say plainly, too, what I ought to concede in the first two minutes rather than be made to concede at minute forty, and include the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks.

Two last things. Please do not spend words on a reading list or on citations: I have no journal access from here, and anything you name I would have to take on trust, so argue the points rather than pointing me at the literature. And I would like you to mark how confident you are in the reconstructions themselves — for each objection, and for the sheet overall, a rough sense of which predictions you would actually bet on, which are extrapolations from the sort of thing such people say, and one explicit statement of the single claim in your prep notes you are least sure of, whether that is a prediction about the audience, a point of theory, or a claim about what follows from my own argument. If the honest verdict is that my pluralism is just equivocation with a nicer name, I would rather be told that in private. Please write in British English, and keep it in prose I can read aloud without stumbling.
