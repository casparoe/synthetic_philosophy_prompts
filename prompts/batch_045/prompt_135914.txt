You are a decision theorist and population ethicist who has spent years on the problem of comparing outcomes of infinite value. Please think this through carefully: before you name any position, lay out the logical space — the questions, the answer-patterns they permit, and which combinations are jointly satisfiable — and only then place the actual views inside it.

I run a reading group on longtermist ethics and want a "which view are you actually committed to?" quiz: eight questions, each a concrete case with three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer-patterns onto positions. It has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they prefer, so no question may name a view or ask anything as bare as "do you think actual infinities are possible?"

At least four questions must be built on these cases. (i) Heaven and Hell: infinitely many blissful and infinitely many miserable people — is adding one more miserable person a worsening, and is enlarging the heaven an improvement? (ii) A sparse amendment: in an infinite world, only the people whose index is a perfect square — a set of asymptotic density zero — are made better off. Is that an improvement at all? (iii) The rearrangement case: a world whose welfare stream is only conditionally convergent, so that two enumerations of the very same people yield different totals. What does the view say here, and what must it give up in order to say it? (iv) The escalator: steady, unbounded improvement forever, against a world fixed at a high finite level.

Add one case about an astronomically small probability of affecting an infinite quantity, and tag every leaf as surviving or collapsing under (iii), since that is where my group stalls.

Leaves that must appear: aggregative consequentialism with extended, non-Archimedean summation; expected-value fanaticism; infinitarian paralysis or indifference; dominance- and overtaking-style orderings that rank by finite windows or eventual catch-up and never sum; density- or average-based comparisons; person-affecting, threshold, and satisficing views; views that buy coherence by surrendering completeness or transitivity; and a deflationary leaf for "no determinate answer." Where a combination of answers is empty, say whether that is because it is provably incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied — and flag any two leaves the quiz cannot separate rather than pretending it can.
