I coordinate volunteers at a nature reserve, and much of my job is teaching new guides to identify animals they have never seen from written descriptions alone — "like the marsh harrier, but bulkier, with a pale collar." I have been reading about Nyāya epistemology in the evenings to get a better vocabulary for what that skill actually is, and I would like a dialogue that puts the Nyāya category of upamāna (comparison, treated by the Naiyāyikas as one of the four pramāṇas, alongside perception, inference, and testimony) under real pressure.

Write a dialogue between a Naiyāyika who defends upamāna as an irreducible source of knowledge and a Buddhist philosopher in the Dignāga tradition who recognises only perception and inference. Have them work first through the standard case — the person who knows cows, is told that a gavaya is like a cow, sees an animal in the forest, and comes to know that this is a gavaya — and then through four variations I keep coming back to: (i) the reverse case, where the learner already knows gavayas and is told that a cow is like a gavaya; (ii) the chained case, where the thing used as the comparison base has never been perceived either and is known only from testimony, so the identification never touches direct acquaintance at any point; (iii) the contrastive case, where the learner is told only what the animal is unlike; and (iv) the revision case, where after an identification has settled, a second and more detailed description arrives that conflicts with the resemblance the learner took himself to perceive, and he must either withdraw the identification or discount what he seemed to see.

I also want them to argue about a test I have been using on my own trainees: whether a supposed source of knowledge counts as genuinely distinct only if it has a defeat profile of its own — only if there are ways of its going wrong that are not merely ways its perceptual or testimonial inputs go wrong. Let the Buddhist press that test against upamāna, and give the Naiyāyika a serious reply rather than a gesture; I don't want either of them built as a foil. Both should know the material well, engage the point the other has actually just made, concede something real, and finish by saying honestly whether upamāna survives the exchange.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and no script formatting; mark speaker changes inside the flow of the sentences themselves. Around a thousand words.
