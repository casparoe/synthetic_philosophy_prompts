You are a professional philosopher who specializes in value theory and the theory of well-being. My friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about hedonism about welfare, and I would like you to settle the argument properly rather than rehearse the textbook debate.

The disagreement was this. She pressed the heterogeneity problem: the states that get counted as pleasures seem to share no single experiential quality. The relief of cold water after a long climb, the absorbed satisfaction of finally seeing why a proof works, the bodily ease of a warm bath, the giddy fun of a bad pun — try to name the one feature of consciousness they all have in common and you either go silent or you produce something so thin that it would also cover states nobody would call pleasant. If that is right, she said, hedonism either has no subject matter or is a disjunctive list masquerading as a theory, and it thereby loses the very feature that made it attractive: a single common currency that can be measured, summed within a life, and summed across people. I granted the phenomenology but denied the inference. Unity need not be qualitative. Pleasures might be unified the way money is unified: cowrie shells, metal coins, and entries in a digital ledger have nothing intrinsic in common, yet they are one kind because of the role they play.

To test that, we invented a case and deadlocked on it. Suppose we encounter a species whose positive affect runs on a neurochemistry entirely unrelated to ours, and whose repertoire includes a state we may as well call kavan. Kavan plays exactly the reinforcement and approach role that pleasure plays in us: it is what they seek, what they weigh when choosing, what their learning tracks. Yet its subjects report no felt warmth or sweetness at all — a cold, clear, affectively flat state that they nonetheless want to continue and will work to prolong. Is kavan a pleasure? The role theorist seems forced to say yes, at which point, my friend said, hedonism stops being a theory about how life feels and becomes a theory about what we pursue. The qualitative theorist seems forced to say no, at which point the heterogeneity problem returns in full force, since there is then no felt quality common to relief, absorption, ease, and fun.

Please write an essay that weighs these considerations and reaches a definite verdict. Argue for some answer to the question of whether hedonism about welfare survives the heterogeneity problem, and if you say it survives, say in which form: a felt-quality or feeling-tone account, an attitudinal or desire-based account, a functional-role account, an explicitly pluralist or disjunctive account, or some better alternative of your own. Do not end on a non-committal note; a conditional verdict of the form survives only if X, otherwise not is fine, but pure agnosticism is not. In the course of the essay, address whether the intrapersonal and interpersonal comparability that made hedonism attractive for aggregation can be preserved on whatever account you defend, and treat the kavan case as a test that any adequate account must pass.

Start with a short brainstorming pass before you write: list the candidate accounts of what pleasures have in common, the strongest objection to each, and the verdict each one returns on kavan. Then write the essay in the following sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. Framing the dispute — about 100 words.
2. The case that heterogeneity is fatal — about 250 words.
3. The case that hedonism survives it — about 250 words.
4. What the kavan case shows — about 200 words.
5. My verdict — about 200 words.
