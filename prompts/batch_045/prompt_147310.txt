I want a number from you, together with the decomposition that produces it, on a question about the Pasadena game. The game is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads falls on the nth toss the payoff is (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n dollars, so the payoffs run +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5 and onward, with the nth outcome carrying probability 2^{−n}. Each outcome therefore contributes (−1)^{n−1}/n to the expected-value sum, which converges to ln 2, about sixty-nine cents. But the positive payoffs and the negative payoffs each have infinite expectation taken on their own, the series converges only conditionally, and by reordering the outcomes one can make the same payoff schedule sum to any real number at all, or diverge.

The proposition I want priced is this: the Pasadena game has a determinate fair price, and that price is ln 2 dollars. Count the proposition as true only if there is exactly one number that a fully informed agent could take to be the price at which a single play is neither advantageous nor disadvantageous, and that number is ln 2. Count it as false if the correct verdict is that the game has no fair price, that any of a wide range of prices is rationally permissible, or that the fair price is some number other than ln 2. My own credence is about 0.55, and I want yours.

Please decompose along roughly these lines, adding terms I have missed and subtracting any you think are idle. First: the long-run-average question. It is my understanding, and I would rather be corrected than agreed with, that the payoff's tail is thin enough for the classical weak law of large numbers to apply to repeated independent plays, so that the average payoff per play converges in probability to ln 2, but at a rate on the order of 1/log n, which means that for any number of plays a person could actually run the average is dominated by a handful of enormous outcomes; if that is wrong, tell me what the right result is before you use it. Second: whether the availability of such a limit settles the normative question even when the expectation itself is undefined, or whether the fair price of a single play is conceptually tied to the sum rather than to the long run, so that convergence of sample means is simply beside the point. Third: whether the indexing of outcomes by the toss on which the first heads appears is privileged in a way that makes summation in that order non-arbitrary, or whether the ordering is an artifact of how we happen to have described the chance setup, in which case rearrangement arguments bite. Fourth, and this is the term I care most about: whether the value is continuous under approximation. Clamp the payoff symmetrically at plus or minus M and let M grow and the expected value of the clamped game returns ln 2, since the clamped tails shrink like 1/log M. But cap only what the house can be made to pay, at some fixed maximum M however large, while leaving the player's liability uncapped, and the expected value is minus infinity for every M, and remains minus infinity as M grows; a sequence of games that plainly converges on the original Pasadena game thus carries a value that does not converge on ln 2, and mirror-imaging the cap gives plus infinity. How much does that discontinuity move you, and is there a principled reason to prefer the symmetric regularization to the one-sided one, given that no house that could actually exist has an unlimited bankroll? Fifth: whether it is ever acceptable for a decision theory to return no verdict at all, so that the absence of a fair price would be a tolerable outcome rather than a failure.
