I work as a network and systems engineer, and I would like a philosopher's reconstruction of something my field mostly takes for granted.

The practice first. For decades the dominant design norm for the Internet has been to keep the network itself simple and to push functions out to the endpoints. Reliable delivery, ordering, duplicate suppression, encryption, and anything involving what the data means are supposed to live in the hosts; the network is supposed to move packets and little else. The standard argument runs like this: a function can be completely and correctly implemented only with the knowledge and help of the application standing at the endpoints; a version of the function placed lower in the system will be incomplete, because the lower layer cannot tell whether the whole job succeeded, and redundant, because the endpoints must implement it anyway; and placing it in the network taxes every application that did not want it.

I recently came across a vocabulary from philosophy of biology that seems to bear on this, and I want to know whether it really does. Skyhooks: top-down sources of order that do their explanatory work without themselves being built out of machinery below them. Cranes: things that do real lifting but are constructed out of simpler parts. And greedy reductionism: the error of explaining a phenomenon by jumping straight to the smallest parts and skipping the intermediate levels. So:

1. What would someone have to believe about levels of explanation for the end-to-end norm to be more than a rule of thumb? Is it committed to anti-greedy-reductionism, to the claim that whether a file arrived intact is a real feature of the world that cannot be stated, predicted, or explained in the vocabulary of packets, windows, and voltages? Or is it fully compatible with a thoroughgoing reductionism, on the grounds that the transfer is nothing over and above the physical events and that our preference for endpoint implementation is purely a matter of tractability, cost, and where the relevant knowledge happens to sit? Please distinguish what the norm strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where several rival philosophies of explanation would license exactly the same engineering.

2. The skyhook question. When the argument says that only the application knows what it needs, the requirements that shape the lower layers arrive from outside the network, from the purposes of users. Viewed from inside the system, is that a skyhook, order imposed from above by something that does no mechanical work, or merely a crane standing outside the boundary I happened to draw? Does the answer depend entirely on where one draws the system boundary, and if it does, what should I conclude about the usefulness of the crane/skyhook distinction?

3. Something I cannot classify, and which I suspect is the usual case. Most of the interesting machinery in a real system is specified teleologically but implemented mechanically. A congestion controller is defined by what it is for, sharing capacity and keeping queues short, and is built out of counters, timers, and window arithmetic; its specification refers to a good it does not itself contain, while its operation refers to nothing of the kind. Call these teleologically specified, mechanically implemented components. Are they cranes, skyhooks in crane's clothing, or does the distinction classify not components but the relation between a specification and its implementation, and so simply fail to apply here?

4. The practice is not consistent, and I want to know how much that matters. The Internet we actually run is full of layer violations: address translators and middleboxes in the core, content distribution networks that terminate encryption on users' behalf, routers that signal congestion to endpoints, and now a transport protocol that has absorbed encryption and moved the whole apparatus into application space. Engineers defend these, and defend the end-to-end principle in the same breath. Is the best reading that the norm was never a metaphysical claim about levels but a claim about where costs and knowledge fall? Or should we say it was always a claim about which cranes are load-bearing and which are scaffolding that can be kicked away once the higher-level function is up and running?

What I want back: the implicit theory of explanation and of levels, reconstructed; a verdict on which parts of the norm actually require that theory; and a statement of what would have to change in design practice if we adopted greedy reductionism outright, and what would change if we allowed ourselves skyhooks.
