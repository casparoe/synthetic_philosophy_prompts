I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the epistemology of ignorance — in particular on the idea that ignorance can be an achievement rather than a mere gap — and I keep running into a legal practice that seems to lean on it. In many criminal jurisdictions a jury may be instructed that a defendant who suspected some fact to be highly probable and deliberately took steps to avoid confirming it can be treated as having known that fact: cultivated ignorance is allowed to satisfy a knowledge element. What I want from you is the implicit theory of belief and agency this practice requires, given that the same body of law elsewhere treats an honestly held false belief as exculpatory and generally treats what a person believes as not something they can settle at will.

Two things in particular. First: does the doctrine actually presuppose that self-deception is possible — that an agent can intentionally bring it about that she does not know something — or does its two-part structure let it reach its verdict while presupposing only that inquiry, and not belief, is under voluntary control? If the latter, the defendant it describes looks simply like a knowing risk-taker, and it is unclear to me what would be lost by convicting him under a recklessness standard instead; so I would want to know whether the practice strictly requires the stronger reading or is merely compatible with it. Second: the asymmetry between native ignorance, which excuses, and cultivated ignorance, which does not, suggests that what is doing the work is the etiology of a belief-state rather than its content at the time of action. What would have to change, doctrinally and evidentially, if we gave that up and judged defendants only by their doxastic state when they acted? Is the practice as it stands coherent, or does it secretly inherit the paradoxes that make self-deception look impossible?
