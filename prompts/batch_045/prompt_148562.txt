I’ve just had a long and inconclusive argument with a friend about whether an output from a non-human system can ever count as a performative utterance, and I do not want to have it again. Our disagreement started from a scripted customer-service chatbot that sends a fixed message saying I apologize for the outage whenever an outage is detected, where the company’s policy says customers should treat that message as an official apology; my friend thought this was enough for a real apology, while I thought something essential was missing. We then moved to a harder case: a blockchain-based organization whose code automatically publishes a statement that a member is expelled as soon as a vote passes, and whose governing rules say that publication is the expulsion. We realized we were each assuming a different theory of what a performative utterance has to be, so before I read any more speech-act theory I want a specification of what I should be demanding of one.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of performative utterance must meet in order to settle disputes of exactly this kind. Fix the purpose narrowly: the theory must be good enough to adjudicate whether outputs of non-human or semi-automated systems—scripted chatbot messages, automatic institutional publications, smart-contract declarations, and similar cases—can count as promises, orders, apologies, declarations, or other illocutionary acts, and if so under what conditions.

The deliverable should be the requirements themselves, with the case or consideration behind each. Cover at least: the paradigm human cases any theory must get right (explicit performatives such as the words used in a marriage ceremony, the naming of a ship, a bequest in a will, and a bet about tomorrow’s rain; and implicit cases such as a one-word order to leave a room); the main varieties of infelicity (an unauthorized person attempting a christening, a promise made without any intention of keeping it, a joke or theatrical use, and a case in which the hearer entirely fails to understand what is being done); and the new algorithmic cases above. Say which distinctions the theory must respect—constative versus performative, explicit versus implicit performative, locutionary versus illocutionary versus perlocutionary, happiness or unhappiness versus truth or falsity, illocutionary force versus propositional content, speaker intention versus conventional procedure, and assertion versus declaration—and which of those distinctions are genuinely load-bearing rather than optional preferences of one school. For each requirement, give the concrete case that motivates it.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for this purpose and which are merely preferences of particular camps; for example, say whether it is a genuine desideratum that no performative be truth-evaluable, or only a preference inherited from an early Austin-style contrast with constatives, and whether a theory must require a speaker with intentions and beliefs or may treat conventional uptake and institutional rule-following as sufficient. Most importantly, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the conflict: for instance, whether a theory can both require genuine speaker intention for every illocutionary act and also allow a mindless algorithmic publication to be a full declaration, or whether it can both demand that illocutionary categories be mutually exclusive and exhaustive and still accommodate utterances that seem to have more than one force at once.

If it helps, you may close with at most a short paragraph on how Austin’s early performative/constative account and Searle’s later treatment of declarations each fare against the list; do not write a full scorecard or bibliography, and keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring.
