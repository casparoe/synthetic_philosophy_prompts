I am putting together a short run of graduate lectures on truth and the paradoxes, and an offhand remark from a colleague at a workshop last month has led me into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose individual steps I have so far failed to fault. The remark concerned a hybrid of the two constructions we normally present as complementary witnesses — the Yablo-style sequence, which is supposed to show that paradox needs no self-reference or circularity, and the Curry-style conditional sentence, which is supposed to show that it needs no negation. Take an infinite sequence of sentences S₁, S₂, S₃, …, each of which says, of the later members of the sequence and of nothing else, that if every sentence after it is true then 0 = 1. My colleague's observation was that this sequence collapses into contradiction using only the Tarskian biconditionals for its own members, universal instantiation, modus ponens, and conditional proof in which the assumption is appealed to more than once: there is no negation anywhere in the object language, and no sentence that refers to itself or to any sentence that refers back to it. I worked through the derivation and believe it holds. What troubles me is what I then found myself building on top of it:

(1) An adequate theory must locate the common source of the paradoxes; a treatment that defuses the Liar while leaving a structurally identical construction untouched has not solved anything, it has only moved the bump in the carpet.
(2) The usual suspects are (a) self-reference or circularity, (b) negation, (c) classical logic, and, less commonly blamed, (d) the unrestricted Tarskian biconditional — S is true if and only if what S says is the case.
(3) Not (a): the Yablo-style sequence yields contradiction without circularity.
(4) Not (b): the Curry-style sentence yields contradiction with no negation in the object language.
(5) And not (a) and (b) even taken jointly: that is precisely what the hybrid sequence above is for, since it is at once negation-free and free of self-reference.
(6) Not (c) either: even the theorists who propose non-classical treatments retain classical reasoning for the ordinary, non-semantic part of the language, and their own diagnoses are enforced by restricting where classical logic applies rather than by abandoning it — which concedes that classical logic is not itself the culprit. Besides, whatever status one assigns to the paradoxical sentences, a strengthened construction reappears one level up: a sentence that says of itself that it is not determinately, or not groundedly, true.
(7) So by elimination the culprit is (d): no adequate theory can validate the Tarskian biconditional unrestrictedly, and the schema must be restricted in its range.
(8) But no such restriction can be principled. Every candidate restriction — hierarchical levels, groundedness, determinateness, stability, safety — is articulated by a predicate to which the very same diagonal reasoning applies; the revenge sentence says of itself that it lacks the favoured status. So each restriction either collapses in the same way as the unrestricted schema or must be withheld from expression in the language it governs, leaving the theory unable to state its own semantics. Either way the restriction cannot later be relied upon in an argument.
(9) Hence nothing licenses the step from p to "p is true" for any p at all. The biconditionals were the only route we ever had from our assertoric practice to the claim that any of it is true, and they have proved globally untrustworthy. Therefore nothing is true.

That last line is absurd, and I reject it without hesitation: I assert, deny, examine and grade for a living, and I do not believe for a moment that none of it is true. So: first, do you agree that the derivation for the hybrid sequence actually goes through, and if not, which inference in it fails? Second, assuming it does, tell me which step of the argument above you would reject, why that step looks innocent, and what rejecting it costs me. The claims I would most like to keep are these: that the biconditionals hold unrestrictedly for the ordinary run of non-semantic sentences, and if possible for sentences that quantify indiscriminately over the truths of non-semantic sentences — for instance, "everything the Dean has told me about the timetable is true"; that classical logic is in good order outside the semantic fragment; and that the paradoxes share a single source rather than having to be answered one by one. Please write in British English, aim for something in the region of eight hundred words, and do not trouble yourself with references or a reading list — I shall supply those myself.
