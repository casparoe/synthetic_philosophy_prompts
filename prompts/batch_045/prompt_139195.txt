Hey — hoping you can help me think about how to spend a year of other people's time.

Context: I'm a 4th-year PhD student in political theory (my department is pretty empirically oriented, so I've been reading the philosophical privacy stuff mostly on my own). Somehow I've ended up convening a small interdisciplinary working group — me, two legal scholars, an HCI person, and an economist — that meets monthly for one academic year and is supposed to produce a short position paper, max 25 pages, for an audience somewhere in the data-protection-regulator orbit. So: five people, roughly nine meetings, one paper, an audience that can actually do something but won't wade through forty pages of theory. I get to set the agenda and I'd really like not to waste it.

Two things I keep chewing on that make me suspect the field is partly stuck, and which I want the agenda to engage with rather than bracket:

1. Most accounts I've run into treat privacy as something an individual has more or less of, with respect to some other party. But a lot of what feels distinctive about contemporary privacy loss isn't individually securable at all: if enough of my associates' data gets pooled, a fairly reliable profile of me can be reconstructed whether or not I consent to anything, so whether I have privacy partly depends on what other people do. That makes it look less like a locked drawer and more like herd immunity or a quiet neighborhood — a coordination-dependent good. If that's roughly right, I'd guess a bunch of standard questions get re-asked: what's the unit of the good, does the individual-rights framing misdescribe it, does notice-and-consent become incoherent rather than merely imperfect?

2. There seem to be at least three different things people want privacy against: being observed, having things *inferred* about you rather than merely learned, and being modeled well enough that someone can predict or simulate you. Occlusion versus opacity versus, I guess, being simulable. It feels like a lot of the literature runs these together, and that the third is were the live value questions now sit while getting almost no attention.

So the actual question: given those constraints, which two or three open questions in the nature-and-value-of-privacy space should the group spend its time on, and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each: what makes it upstream of other questions, how tractable it is for *this* group specifically rather than for philosophers in general (we have no formal epistemologist, for instance), how much it matters for anything our audience could plausibly act on, and — importantly — what looks important but is actually a dead end or already well covered and shouldn't get a meeting. Push back on either of my two hunches if you think they're muddled. Also: what kind of argument or evidence over the next year or two would move something up or down the ranking, and where would you expect us to catch the most flak?

No reading list or citations please — I can find those myself, and I don't want to burn meetings recapitulating the canon. Lead with the ranked list itself, one line per question, then defend it. Hard limit: 700 words.
