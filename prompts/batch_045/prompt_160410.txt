hey — so in my effective giving group chat everyone keeps describing moral trade as basically just market trade with a different commodity: you each have comparative advantage in some bit of the world, you specialise, you swap, both sides come out ahead, so it's a clean win and there's nothing morally interesting left to argue about. And they ride that pretty hard to get three conclusions: that refusing to trade on principle is just leaving value on the table, that it doesn't matter what your counterparty actually cares about (even if you think their cause is worthless, or worse than worthless), and that we should all be doing far more of it — someone even floated building an actual exchange for it.

I've been doing a bit of this myself, which is why I care. My flatmate is a vet who cares about farmed animal welfare, I care about global health, and every month we each put £150 into the other one's charity instead of our own. Three wrinkles have come up. She wants to stagger it — I pay in month one, she pays in month two — and calls that extending credit. A bloke at my climbing gym offered me a trade where his side is his church's mission trip, which I think is somewhere between useless and actively harmful; he thinks bednets are a waste of money; his pitch was still that it's just trade, so we both win. And I've been mucking about with two extensions nobody in the chat has mentioned: trading with someone who has no moral views at all and just wants cash for their own consumption, and "trading" against my own future giving — pledging now to give in five years' time, in exchange for someone else giving now.

Can you audit the analogy properly? Take ordinary market trade as the source and moral trade as the target, and tell me what maps onto what, which bits of the source the analogy quietly drags in that the target hasn't got, and whether the three conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table. I'd like:

1. Summary — 60 to 80 words, whether the market picture survives as an argument and what it can and can't carry.
2. The mapping — a table of correspondences (the goods, the budget, comparative advantage, gains from trade, the exchange rate or price, enforcement and trust, whoever ends up consuming the benefit, the numeraire), 200 to 250 words including the table.
3. Where it holds — the structural parallels that are real, 150 to 200 words.
4. Where it breaks — split it into breaks that matter for those three conclusions and breaks that are cosmetic; call out explicitly which features of actual markets the analogy imports that moral trade lacks (money as a common measuring rod, contract law, the trader also being the consumer, no third-party beneficiaries, no externalities), 350 to 400 words.
5. My three cases — the staggered credit idea, the mission trip trade, and my hired-gun and borrow-against-future-giving extensions, 250 to 300 words.
6. A better analogy — if there is one, 100 to 150 words.
7. What survives — which of the three conclusions the market framing can still support, and how much weaker each one comes out, 100 words.

No need to name-drop anyone or point me at anything to read, I just want the argument. British English please.
