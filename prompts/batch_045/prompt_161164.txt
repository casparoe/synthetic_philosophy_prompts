I help run a small short-fiction-and-philosophy evening at a public library — a story is read aloud, then someone with a philosophy background leads a discussion — and our next session is on whether a mental state could be made out of something very different from what makes it up in us. The stories we've used so far have leaned on aliens and mind uploading, and they've all turned on one big reveal; I'd rather do the opposite. I want the difficulty to come from a case where realization is partial, gradual, and tangled up in paperwork, so that the question is not "is it really conscious?" but "by what standard do we decide, and what happens when every standard we can state is either too generous or too strict?"

So: write a short story set in a neurology-and-ethics consultation service at a large hospital, some decades ahead, where a statute — call it the Substrate Independence Act — requires that anything which has the same causal organization as a human being, in the sense of being caused by the same sorts of inputs, producing the same sorts of outputs, and disposing the system to respond the same way to the same hypothetical situations, be treated exactly as a human being for purposes of consent, pain management, and legal testimony, regardless of what it is built from. The protagonist is the staff "realization assessor," the person whose signature makes that determination binding, and I'd like two of her cases to run side by side. The first is a patient she has followed for nearly twenty years: a slow-growing condition has been managed by a long series of resections, and most of the tissue on one side of the patient's brain has been replaced by implanted devices tuned to restore her behaviour, her speech, and everything she reports about herself — but the devices work nothing like the tissue did, being continuous, distributed, and without anything that maps part-for-part onto what they replaced. The patient is scheduled for another resection and must consent to it, and if the assessor now rules that the devices do not after all realize her mental states, then the consent the patient gave for the last operation may not have been hers to give, which is the kind of thing that reaches a court. The second case, due the same week, is a colonial reef organism: sessile, without neurons, a million clonal units coordinating through slow chemical gradients over hours, which learns to anticipate and avoid a repeated damaging stimulus; a conservation agency wants it certified as a subject of protections. Over the course of the story I want the assessor to find that the Act's criterion pulls apart at both ends. Read permissively, it seems to certify systems nobody believes are subjects — a colleague keeps raising the hospital's climate-control system, and I want that to land as a genuine problem rather than as a joke or a paradox. Read demandingly, so that the causal organization has to be specified finely enough to include how the realizer does what it does, the patient's devices stop qualifying altogether and the reef's slow gradient looks like a different state rather than the same one. A third colleague should press a way out: that what matters is not the organization alone but causal-historical continuity with what was there before, which would rescue the patient and sink the reef.

Please let the bind be carried by scenes, characters, and an institutional deadline rather than by anyone explaining the philosophy in general terms — no narrator's summaries, no character who seems to be lecturing the reader, and no tidy resolution, since I want the discussion afterwards to have something left to do. Give the assessor something personal at stake in the patient's case, and end somewhere I couldn't predict from this description. Third person or first, your choice; roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words.
