I help run a volunteer river-monitoring group — about thirty of us, mostly retirees plus a few bio undergrads — and once a week we test a stretch of the river for nitrate with a handheld colorimeter. There's a written protocol, and we calibrate against a standard solution at the start of every session. One of our volunteers, Frank, has read a bit of epistemology and has started making trouble: he says every calibration step just pushes the question back a level. How do we know the standard solution is really the concentration on the label? How do we know the instrument maps absorbance onto concentration the way the manual says? How do we know the vial we picked up is the one we think it is? He isn't a cartoon skeptic — he doesn't go home and doubt that he has hands, and he doesn't actually want us to stop testing — his point is just that our chain of reasons never lands on anything that isn't itself a claim somebody could ask for a reason for, and he wants to know why that's fine for us but would be a bad sign in, say, astrology or dowsing.

I've been reading up on hinge epistemology — Wittgenstein's On Certainty, the hinge and riverbed imagery, the idea that doubt and justification only work against a background of things that aren't in the doubt-game at all, and the dispute between the reading where hinges are objects of some kind of non-evidential entitlement and the reading where our "certainty" about them isn't a cognitive or epistemic state at all but something shown in what we do rather than said. What are the most important implications of all that for how I should actually handle Frank and the protocol?

Two things I'd like you to take seriously rather than skate over. First: I've been playing with a distinction and I want to know whether the view can support it or whether it collapses. Some of the things we never doubt seem load-bearing — if they gave way, the whole practice would come apart. "The instrument behaves today roughly like it did yesterday" is like that; so is "nobody in the group is quietly faking their readings." Other things nobody has ever doubted, but nothing in the protocol actually turns on them: whether this week's sample point is numerically the same spot as last week's, or whether the river is even the same river we named on the paperwork. Call the first kind load-bearing hinges and the second vestigial hinges. Does hinge epistemology give me any non-arbitrary way of sorting our assumptions into those two boxes — and if it does, is the sorting really tracking how much depends on them, or just the fact that nobody has got round to questioning them yet? Those feel like they could come apart, and I can't tell which one is doing the work.

Second, a practical wrinkle: our numbers go to a state agency that uses them when deciding whether to fine a couple of upstream farms, so the protocol isn't just an internal document. Frank has suggested we print the assumptions right into it — a short list of "we take it for granted that the instrument is stable, that the labels mean what they say, etc." That feels to me like it might make things worse, not better, but I can't say exactly why. Does dragging the hinges into the space of explicit statable claims stabilise a practice or destabilise it, on these views? And on the back of that: what should I actually say to Frank, and how much of any of this belongs in the training we give new volunteers?
