Someone from my department left this on the whiteboard after a works-in-progress session and I've been chewing on it for a week. Its supposed to show that expected-value fanaticism refutes itself.

(1) Rank outcomes by total welfare, welfare is unbounded above, and the probabilities are objective and known.
(2) Rational choice is maximising expected value.
(3) Then for any finite cost c and any probability p > 0 there is a finite payoff G with pG > c, so the long shot beats the sure modest good.
(4) Over a life you get offered a long series of independent long shots of that shape; (2) and (3) apply to each one severally, so rationality requires accepting all of them. Call it the sweep.
(5) Each shot is overwhelmingly likely to miss, and there are very many of them, so the sweep leaves you, as near to certainly as you like, with nothing: you paid every time and the good never arrived. That is much worse than the sure goods you turned down at each step.
(6) A rule for living is unacceptable if following it makes it as near to certain as you like that you end up far worse off than under an available alternative, where the residual chance of coming out ahead is too small to be distinguishable by any evidence you could have at the time of choosing.
(7) So (2) is false, even granting (1).

What bothers me most is that the same (5)–(6) machinery run in reverse seems to prove far too much. Over a long life you also face a great many tiny risks of catastrophe, and if near-certainty is what matters then ordinary life — driving, surgery, having kids, whatever — comes out forbidden, since some catastrophe or other becomes as near to certain as you like. So either the machinery is broken or it condemns everything, and I cant tell which.

I also tried a reply that got waved off. If the shots on offer jointly exhaust the outcome space — if I can buy every member of the partition — then the sweep pays off with certainty and the ruin story never gets started. She said that's an artefact of a toy case and the argument only needs the everyday situation where the shots overlap and you cant cover everything. Maybe so, but it felt like it should tell us something.

I'm teaching a decision theory seminar this term and don't want to bring this in until I know where it breaks. I'd rather keep (1) than (2), and (3) is just arithmetic. Which step do you reject, what does rejecting it cost, and is there a repaired version that still bites?
