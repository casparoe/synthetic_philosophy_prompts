I have to give a ~10 minute response to it next week, and I dont want a formal theorem or a proof sketch — I want to actually get *why* the objection is or isn't scary, gut-level.

Here's how I've been trying to sort it out in my head. Tell me where this breaks.

Call the *refutation distance* of a bad argument the number of steps the judge has to be walked through before they reach the step where it goes wrong. Then it feels like there are three different things that all get lumped together as "obfuscation", and I suspect cross-examination only kills the first one:

1. Plain length. The liar gives 1000 steps and the error is at step 612. But the honest debater can binary-search: make the liar commit to the intermediate claim at step 500, then 750, then 625, etc. So refutation distance is logarithmic, not linear. This case seems totally fine, and I think this is the one people are secretly picturing when they say debate handles obfuscation.

2. Local plausibility. Every single step looks fine in isolation; the mistake only shows up in how the steps combine. E.g. a quantity that gets tracked under two different labels and is quietly swapped halfway through, or a budget constraint that each step individually respects but the whole chain violates. Here "check step 500" doesn't help at all, because step 500 is genuinely fine.

3. Nested. To check whether step 500 is correct you have to redo a sub-argument which itself is long and has the same problem, so the refutation distance multiplies instead of adding.

My questions, roughly:

- Is 1 vs 2 vs 3 the right way to carve this up, or am I missing the distinction that actually matters? In particular I can't decide whether 3 is genuinely different from 2 or just 2 with extra steps dressed up.
- For 2: I have this feeling the honest debater should be able to win by *renaming* things — force every intermediate quantity to be defined once, given a name, and then only ever referred to by that name, so a swap becomes a local and checkable error again. Is that a real move, and does it generalise? Or does the liar always have some way to reintroduce the same ambiguity one level down?
- I made up a condition I've been calling a "one-way argument": a family of arguments is one-way if producing a false argument of length n costs the liar about n, but refuting it costs the honest side something superlinear, or just more attention than the judge has. If one-way arguments exist then debate seems to just inherit whatever limits the judge has, and if they don't then obfuscation always seems answerable in principle. So: any principled reason to think they do or don't show up in realistic domains like law, wet-lab science, or code review? And I explicitly do NOT want the P vs NP answer — I mean, is the relevant asymmetry about *computation* at all, or is it really about the judge's attention and patience?
- Last one. My gut says the thing that actually saves debate is that the liar has to commit to a full, consistent story before they know which part will get probed, so they can't adaptively patch it. But if the liar can produce something with enough internal redundancy that lots of different probes are all consistent with it, I lose the thread. Can you state what the honest debater's advantage is even *supposed* to be, in one sentence, and then say which of 1/2/3 it survives?
