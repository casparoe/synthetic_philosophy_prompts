Since I retired a few years back I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the libary on Thursday mornings. I was a research chemist for most of my working life, so I'm used to arguments where the terms get nailed down first, and I keep being surprised how slippery everything is in this stuff. Last week we did the bit in Aristotle where he asks whether it's already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, and two of the group, Marisol and Doug, got properly stuck into each other. I want to tidy up my notes over the weekend but I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

(1) Marisol opened with the fatalist squeeze: if "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is true today, then it was true yesterday, and the day before that, and nobody can make it stop having been true that things were heading this way — the past is fixed. So the battle can't not happen, and the admiral sitting there deliberating whether to attack was always a charade. She thinks the only way out is to say the sentence is neither true nor false yet.

(2) Doug said she's sliding from "it will happen" to "it must happen". It's necessarily the case that if it's true the battle will happen then it happens, but that's necessity of the whole conditional, not of the battle — the battle still could have been avoided, it just won't be. Truth, he said, is one thing and being knowable, or caused, or within anyone's power, is another, and running them together is the only reason the argument looks scary.

(3) Marisol came back with a complaint about grounding rather than knowledge. Grant that the battle does happen: what is it about the world right now in virtue of which the sentence is true today? Every present fact — where the fleets are, the weather, what the admiral had for breakfast — is compatible with there being no battle, so on Doug's picture the truth of the prediction is a free-floating extra fact bolted onto nothing. And she pressed him on what work that truth value does: he talks one way when he deliberates and another way when he describes the world, and if its never shows up in anything he can actually do with it, why insist on it?

(4) Doug's answer was that it does show up, in the logic. If neither "there will be a battle" nor "there won't be a battle" is true, then "either there will be a battle or there won't be" is true without having a true disjunct, which is a funny kind of truth. He also said truthmakers are optional equipment: the world includes the future as well as the present, and the sentence is made true by the fleets, the laws and how things actually go, not by any snapshot of now.

(5) So Marisol offered a positive picture instead of just objecting. A future-tensed sentence, she said, picks up a truth value at the moment its outcome becomes causally unavoidable — not merely predictable, but such that nothing later could prevent it — and she called the moving boundary the "settlement front". Before it arrives the sentence is genuinely open, not merely unknown to us. She added that this copes fine with conditional planning: "if the wind holds, the admiral attacks" can be straightforwardly true now while its consequent is still open.

(6) Doug then brought in relativity, and this is where I started loosing the thread. He said that if settlement is a real feature of the world rather than a fact about our information, it has to have a time, and there's no privileged "now" — so two people strolling past each other in the street disagree about whether some distant event is already settled, and truth turns out to be frame-relative. And worse, he said, she has a dilemma: if the world is deterministic then everything was unavoidable from the start, her settlement front collapses back to the beginning of time and we're straight back to the fatalism she began by worrying about; if it's indeterministic, then settlement only creeps in as each event is actually produced, and nearly every sentence about tomorrow — including dull ones like "the tide will come in this evening" — comes out neither true nor false, which is an awful lot of ordinary talk to throw away.

They went round a few more times and neither of them moved. Could you adjudicate it move by move: for each of (1) through (6), say whether the person making it is basically right, basically wrong, or half right and half wrong, and briefly why. Then tell me who has the better of it overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I'm especially curious whether the physics in (6) is doing real work or is just a distraction, and whether Marisol can answer the bit about ordinary future talk without giving up the whole view.
