I'm applying for a small independent-scholar grant — nine months at half time, a modest stipend, no institutional affiliation — and I need a two-page proposal. I should say up front that I have no academic background: I work in insurance pricing, and I've been reading about decision theory in the evenings with two friends (one is a statistician, the other teaches high school mathematics), so I know enough to set up the Allais paradox but not enough to know whether my idea about it is new, naive, or already refuted.

Here is the half-formed idea. In the usual textbook version, you choose between a sure million and a gamble that is slightly better in expectation but carries a small chance of nothing; then you choose again between two gambles that are the same thing with the certain outcome removed, and most people's two answers can't both be fit by expected utility, because the second pair is just the first pair mixed with the same extra chunk of probability. My friends and I got stuck on what exactly people are reacting to in the first pair. I want to split the usual explanation into two: an attraction to certainty as such, and something I'd call the settlement structure of the gamble — the fact that a risky option leaves you in a stretch of not knowing, during which you can't commit to plans, can't borrow against the outcome, and will eventually learn something about yourself, whereas the certain option settles immediately. My suspicion is that a good deal of the Allais pattern is about settlement rather than about certainty: if the bad branch of the risky option were resolved right away and only the good branch left pending, or if both options resolved at the same future date, the pattern should shift in ways the certainty story doesn't predict. If that's right, then the interesting philosophical question isn't whether independence fails but whether the standard way of describing a lottery — as a probability distribution over final monetary states — is a complete description of what's being chosen, and when re-describing outcomes is a genuine improvement in the theory rather than a trick for making any preference come out rational. I know the obvious objection: once you allow outcomes to include the feeling of suspense or the thought that I nearly won, you can rescue any violation whatsoever, so please make the proposal say how to tell a principled re-description from gerrymandering.
