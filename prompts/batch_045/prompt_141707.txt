I'm writing a short chapter for a handbook on professional ethics — the topic is the line between persuasion and manipulation in doctor–patient and teacher–pupil relationships — and before I commit myself to any argument I want to find out what I actually think, so I have been working through cases and going with my gut. Please don't give me a map of the literature or a list of names; I have read enough of it to know the usual moves, and what I need is a principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself. Here is where my verdicts have landed.

- A GP who wants a reluctant patient to start statins says that most people of his age with that cholesterol level take them and are fine. This is true, but she has chosen that comparison class because it flatters the risk, and she would not have used it with a patient who had asked for the absolute figures. My verdict: persuasion, entirely permissible, and this is what I would want my own GP to do.
- A friend invites me to a dinner party and adds that she knows how swamped I am and I really must not feel I have to come. My verdict: manipulative — it raises the cost of declining while pretending to lower it — but only mildly, and I don't resent her for it.
- A charity appeal shows one named, photographed child and pre-fills the standing order at ten pounds a month, and everyone involved knows it works by way of the identifiable-victim effect rather than by any thought about what the charity achieves per pound. My verdict: manipulative, and I dislike the technique, but I don't think it is wrong, and I cannot say why.
- A union organiser, securing a strike vote, dwells on one genuinely nasty but quite unrepresentative thing the manager said. My verdict: persuasion, permissible. The anger is about a real wrong even if the anecdote is not representative of anything.
- A manager offering me a stretch role dwells on its prestige and does not mention that the last two people who held it were passed over for promotion — a fact he knows I would weigh heavily. My verdict: manipulation, not permissible, and clearly worse than the dinner invitation although nothing he says is false.
- The hospital canteen moves the fruit to eye level and puts the pastries behind the till. It works partly on people who never notice anything, and the aim is their own health. My verdict: not manipulation, and I am comfortable with it, though it nags at me that the mechanism is invisible to the people it moves.
- At a seminar a colleague introduces the one step I am known to doubt with the words "as we all know", and I let it go past. My verdict: a mild manipulation of my beliefs — and I do not want a principle that makes ordinary seminar rhetoric impermissible.
- My partner, before ever asking whether we might move to another city, spends a month arranging viewings and mentioning school catchments, so that by the time the question is asked there is already a default. My verdict: manipulation, not permissible, and the case I feel most strongly about — except that I cannot say what distinguishes it from the GP.
- A teacher tells a struggling eleven-year-old that he is the sort of person who is good at this. She does not yet believe it, she says it for its effect, and the effect is that he improves and comes to believe it. My verdict: I go back and forth.
- I take a beta blocker before a lecture and delete an app from my phone before a deadline. Obviously fine.

The principle I keep reaching for has four clauses: an influence is manipulative only if (1) someone intends it, (2) it does its work by a route other than the target's own appreciation of the reasons there are — it operates behind the back of deliberation rather than as a consideration offered to it, (3) it would be significantly less effective if the target were told in advance what route is being used and why, and (4) the target has not authorised that route. But this principle seems to me to mispredict the canteen, which satisfies the transparency clause and so comes out as manipulation when I want it clean; it fails to separate the GP from my partner, since both are selective presentations of true information; and it convicts the teacher, which may be right but leaves me unable to say why a manipulation can be the right thing to do.

So, three things. First: give me a principle, or two or three candidates, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon — I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that saves all ten by becoming a list. Second: tell me what your principle says about cases I have not listed, in particular advance consent of the "you have my permission to push me into going" kind, self-manipulation, and influence designed by an institution or a recommendation system where no single person has the relevant intention. Third: say whether on your account manipulation is pro tanto wrong at all, or only one defect among others that a good action can have, and whether manipulating what someone believes and manipulating what they choose need to be handled separately.
