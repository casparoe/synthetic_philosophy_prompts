hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background (I did one "ethics in tech" module at uni and hated it), and I've been building a little group-decision service as a side project. It's for shared houses and small housing co-ops: everyone submits a ranking over the options, the service spits out a group ranking, done. No interpersonally comparable utility, nothing fancy — I'm basically doing ranked pairs with a tiebreak.

I keep hitting the same wall and it's making me feel stupid. So: here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know (a) whether there's one rule that gets them all, (b) which of them I have to give up, and (c) what the rule says about cases I haven't thought of.

The rule of thumb I've been using (call it R):
- If two options differ only in what happens to one person — their own body, their own stuff, their own private experience — then that person's ranking decides that pair. I call these personal pairs.
- For every other pair, majority over everyone who's affected.
- Then topo-sort. If there's a cycle, keep the status quo.

Cases (numbered so you can refer to them):

C1. Two-person flat, one shared streaming account, one film with an explicit scene. Options: N = nobody watches it; P = Priya watches it alone on her laptop with headphones; L = Leo watches it alone the same way.
Priya: N > P > L. Ideally nobody watches it; but if someone has to, she'd rather watch it herself than have Leo watch it — she thinks it's corrupting and she'll take the hit herself. Weird, but it's what she submits.
Leo: P > L > N. He thinks it'd be hilarious if Priya watched it, would settle for watching it himself, and hates the idea of it going unwatched.
My verdicts, and each one feels obvious separately:
  C1a, N vs P: Priya's business → N.
  C1b, N vs L: Leo's business → L.
  C1c, P vs L: both prefer P → P.
That's L > N > P > L. I stared at it for an hour. All three feel right individually and they can't all hold.

C2. Dana's therapy notes. Option A: they go in the house knowledge base where the other five can read them. Option B: they don't. Dana says no. The other five are curious and would genuinely find it moving. My verdict: Dana's call, the five don't get a vote, curiosity isn't a stake. R gets this one.

C3. Thermostat: everyone prefers 21 to 19. Verdict: 21. Nobody's personal sphere involved. R gets this.

C4. Rae cooks durian in her own room, her own groceries, her own stove, door shut. Three housemates say the smell makes the shared kitchen unusable for a day and they'd honestly rather she didn't. Verdict: Rae's call. Cook what you want, the rest of you can deal.

C5. Rae vapes something very strong in her own room, door shut. The same three say it drifts into their rooms and they can taste it. Verdict: not Rae's call, the three win.

C4 and C5 are the pair I can't reconcile. Both are "her own room, her own body, other people's discomfort, nobody's health at risk in either case," and I come out opposite ways. I don't think the difference is physical vs. mental — the smell is physical too — and I don't think it's how many people are bothered, it's the same three. If your rule says I'm wrong about one of them, tell me which and why; I'd rather have a rule than two lucky guesses.

C6. Runtime nomination. In my implementation people nominate their own personal pairs when they submit, so the set of personal pairs isn't fixed by me in advance. Two consequences I don't know what to do with. (i) If nominating a pair always wins, everyone will nominate everything, so I need a definition of "differs only in what happens to you" that people can't game, and I don't have one. (ii) Sometimes two people nominate the same pair: Mina and Jon both nominate "which of us gets the one parking spot" as personal to them. Gut: no idea. Whatever the app does there should at least not be arbitrary.

C7. Status-quo fallback. When the pairwise stuff cycles I currently return "no decision" and leave things as they are, and I feel fine about that — I'd rather the app do nothing than do something dumb. But in C1 the cycle walks away from the status quo (L beats N), so the fallback doesn't rescue C1, which is annoying and makes me think "keep the status quo" isn't really the principle I think it is.

Also: a friend who did a PPE degree said this is a known thing — "Sen's liberal paradox"? "the impossibility of a Paretian liberal"? — and laughed when I said I'd fix it with a better sort algorithm. If that's right, tell me what the actual result is and what the serious proposed ways out are, and whether any of them is something I could actually implement as a decision procedure. "Restrict the domain of admissible preferences" doesn't help me — I can't tell my users what preferences they're allowed to have.

Format: give me a JSON object, and no more than two sentences of prose outside it. Schema:
