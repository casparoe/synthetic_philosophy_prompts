I'm putting together a seminar for second-year undergraduates on the neuroscience of free will, and I want to puncture a habit they acquire within about a week of meeting the material: they read the Libet-style findings — a slow cortical readiness potential beginning several hundred milliseconds before the reported onset of the conscious urge to move, the report of that urge arriving roughly a fifth of a second before the movement itself, and the fact that subjects can still abort an action very late in the sequence — and conclude that the data alone settles whether conscious intentions cause actions. A colleague in the psychology department has bet me that no clean classroom case can separate the claim that the brain has already settled *which* movement will occur from the claim that it has merely got *ready* to move while the content of the action is settled later. I should like to win that bet, and to win it honestly rather than by stipulation.

So here is what I want: a matched pair of cases, call them A and B, set in an ordinary psychophysics laboratory — EEG cap, EMG leads on the forearm, the clock-face dot, instructions to flex a finger whenever the urge arrives, nothing implanted, no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no quantum dice doing any work, and no more than a paragraph for each scenario — in which every observable is identical: onset latency, slope and scalp distribution of the readiness potential; the reported time of the conscious intention; the time and magnitude of the EMG burst; and the success rate of late vetoes. The two cases must nevertheless differ in the causal question: in A the conscious intention is causally inert with respect to which movement occurs, in B it is causally necessary for which movement occurs. The difference between them has to be a difference in what the readiness signal is a preparation for — whether it already encodes the specific movement, or only that one of a prepared set is imminent — not a difference in physics, not a difference in the neural time course, and not something obtained by writing into the story that in B the intention causes the movement.

Then please do the design work rather than leave it implicit. For each case state the counterfactual test explicitly — had there been no conscious intention at the relevant moment, would that movement have occurred at that time? — and show that the two cases return opposite answers while the observation record stays fixed. Run the three readings I give my students across both cases and say what each one must say: (i) the hard reading, on which unconscious cerebral initiation is the cause and the conscious intention is a report or a by-product rather than a cause, with only the abort left standing; (ii) the veto or "free won't" compatibilist reading, on which the capacity to withhold is where freedom lives; (iii) the reading on which the readiness potential is not a decision signal at all, but a non-specific preparation or an accumulation toward a threshold that carries no content about the action. Tell me which of the three comes out worst — my suspicion is (ii), precisely because it cannot tell my two cases apart, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is right. Close with an honest note on which single feature of the construction is doing the work, on what happens to the pair if that feature is removed — for instance if the experimenter is permitted a decoder fine-grained enough to read which finger off the scalp signal, or if the readiness-potential onsets are allowed to differ — and on how a defender of the worst-off reading would attack the cases rather than accept the verdict they generate.
