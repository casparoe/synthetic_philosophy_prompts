I am assembling a one-page handout for my upper-division seminar on moral responsibility and historical judgment, and I want its centerpiece to be a decision tree rather than a survey of views. The tree's job is to take a student from a single neutral case description — a historical agent who did something we now count as seriously wrong, knowingly and without coercion, and about which opinion in his own time was genuinely divided — to a named position on two separate questions: whether he is to be blamed, and whether he is to be honored. I want blame and commemoration kept as distinct targets throughout, since the man-of-his-time move does quite different work in each, and most of my students run the two together.

The distinctions I want doing the work are these, though you may rephrase, reorder, or add to them: (i) whether anyone in the agent's own time condemned the practice at all, and separately whether such condemnation was available to someone in his particular position and with his information; (ii) whether the practice was merely conventional, legally required, or positively admired where he stood; (iii) whether his own avowed principles already condemned it, so that condemning it needed no new moral framework, or whether it took a framework he did not have; (iv) whether the wrong was causally necessary for the achievement we honor or separable from it; (v) whether he lived long enough to see the moral change against him begin to form, or died before any such consensus existed; (vi) whether we, who are doing the judging, are beneficiaries or institutional successors of the practice — I suspect an acquittal handed down by beneficiaries is a different speech act from one handed down by outsiders, and I want that scruple placed in the tree rather than in a footnote; (vii) whether honoring him now does present work, such as ratifying or wounding, that is independent of what he deserved; and (viii) whether we would accept the same excuse for a living person in a society where the practice remains conventional, since my students readily excuse the dead on grounds they refuse to apply to contemporaries.

Please build the tree subject to the following constraints. Use six to eight branch questions, each phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to; no node may be phrased as a request for a label, and none may be phrased as whether he was a man of his time. Produce eight to ten leaves, each carrying the name of the position, or unnamed if you think nobody holds it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want represented at least: full presentist condemnation; an excuse that concedes the wrong and removes blame; the variant that does not excuse but exempts, that is, denies he was a candidate for the blame question while leaving the honor question fully open; a view on which commemoration is a choice about what we want to be shaped by rather than a verdict on the man; a view on which our inheritance disqualifies us from acquitting; and some form of suspension on grounds of our own poor access to his situation.

Flag at least two combinations of answers that no named view occupies, and say briefly whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and name the extra question that would separate them if you had room for it. Finally, run Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus through the finished tree, tell me where each lands on both questions, and tell me which of the three sortings you take to be the most doubtful, and why.
