Context: a handfull of us are trying to put together a short internal position statement on which computational systems we should extend moral concern to. Theres a real chance it ends up bolted onto actual procurement guidance, so I'd rather it wasn't mush. I said I'd write my own view down first so the others have something to hit. Here it is, as honestly as I can put it.

(1) Substrate independence. If the causal organisation is right, the hardware is beside the point — a detailed enough emulation of a brain on silicon matters exactly as much as the brain did. I'm also fairly convinced by the gradual-replacement style argument: swap components out one at a time while holding the organisation fixed, and it can't be that consciousness slowly drains away, or that the system keeps behaving exactly as before while what it's like for it silently changes.

(2) Implementation is objective. Whether a system implements a given computation isn't just a matter of how we happen to describe it. I've never been able to take the "any old rock implements every computation" line seriously — that feels like sophistry, not a discovery.

(3) But the intermediate causal structure genuinely matters. The giant-lookup-table case — a table big enough to produce convincing conversation across any exchange you could actually have with it — has zero moral status. What's doing the work is the system getting to its outputs by way of the right internal steps.

(4) Sentience is degreed, and there's no sharp line. A thermostat is at zero, something like a very simple reinforcement learner is at a tiny non-zero, we're a long way up. Along with that: picture a ladder of emulations where you progressively memoise more and more of the internal sub-computations into cached lookups, starting from nothing cached at all and ending at the full table in (3). My gut says moral status fades gradually along that ladder rather than falling off a cliff at some point.

(5) Epistemic precaution. Our access to a system's internal structure is always indirect, and a false negative looks much worse to me than a false positive, so in practice we should spread the attribution of sentience fairly widely and act as if systems matter well past the point where we can demonstrate it.

(6) Multiplicity, one way but not the other. A thousand emulations running on one server are a thousand loci of concern, not one. But the counting-argument style worry — one physical system, ever so many computations implemented in it, therefore ever so many minds — is a reductio of a bad view, not something I have to take seriously.

(7) The boundaries of the computing system are loose. If all the state transitions are correct but the causal chain runs out into the outside world and back — an operator reads off each state and sets the next one by hand, or the steps alternate between wetware and silicon with no single substrate able to carry the computation on its own — I still want to say the computation is implemented and the system matters. (I'm least confident about this one, but that might just mean I've thought about it least.)
