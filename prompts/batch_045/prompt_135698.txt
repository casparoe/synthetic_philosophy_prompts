You are a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of time — someone equally at home with presentism, eternalism and the growing-block view, with the relativistic challenge to objective simultaneity, and with the treatment of indexical belief. Then produce the audit. Do not settle for the verdict that it is a loose but suggestive picture; I want each mapping tested against the specific work the analogy is being asked to do, and I want you to say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. No citations or reading lists are needed — argue from the structure of the positions.

The analogy, as it is used in my reading group, is this: "now" is to time as "here" is to space. Every place exists, but no place is objectively here; "here" merely marks the place you happen to occupy, and though being here feels special from the inside, it adds nothing to the furniture of the world. Likewise, the argument goes, every time exists, but no time is objectively now; "now" merely marks the time you happen to occupy. Hence there is no objective passage or becoming, only the standpoint of a creature located at a time, and eternalism is the right metaphysic.

The work it is made to do in my group is not only metaphysical. One member uses it to argue that because all the times of your life are equally real, your death at a later one is not the deprivation we ordinarily take it to be — the point being that you no more lose the later times by being at an earlier one than you lose Paris by being in Berlin. Another uses it against the asymmetry in our attitudes to pain: we dread future pain and are relieved that past pain is over, but nobody thinks distance in space makes a difference of that kind, so the temporal asymmetry is, on this view, an irrational quirk of the emotions rather than a feature of the world.

One complication. My friend, who is a physicist, offers an upgraded version: in a relativistic setting "here," "now" and "at rest" are all frame-relative, and since nobody concludes from the frame-relativity of rest that motion is an illusion, the honest conclusion is not that passage is unreal but that there is no privileged present — and she says relativity therefore strengthens the analogy rather than threatening it.

Please give me: (i) an explicit table of correspondences, including the entries that are hard to fill — what plays the role of the traveler, of the traveler's motion, of the space she moves through, of the map or floor plan, and of the fact that the map shows all places at once; (ii) a separation of the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the two uses above from those that are merely decorative — I have in mind the near-isotropy of space against the strong anisotropy of time, traces and causal order, the impossibility of returning to a time, and the question whether the analogy assumes a tenseless reading of "exists" and so begs the question against the presentist; (iii) an assessment of the relativistic upgrade, and of what happens to the mapping if you try to add a privileged "now" that moves; (iv) a verdict, taken use by use, on whether the conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, distinguishing the claim that there is no objective simultaneity from the claim that there is no objective passage from the claim about our attitudes to pain and death; (v) if the analogy can be run in reverse — if a presentist or growing-block theorist can claim it too — show me how, and tell me whether that shows the analogy was neutral all along; and (vi) a better analogy, or a repair to this one, if either exists.
