hey — I need a piece of satire for a lecture on the "was the agricultural revolution a mistake?" literature (hunter-gatherer affluence, surplus and the origins of inequality). Could you write me a deadpan mock goverment consultation document — memo header, an executive summary, and a short FAQ, about 600 words, American English — issued by something like the Intergenerational Subsistence Reversion Commission, a body charged with evaluating a practical proposal to reverse the Neolithic transition and restore forager-level leisure, equality and affluence.

The catch that drives the whole joke: its charter contains a no-depopulation condition — it has to keep today's population fed while reverting to a mode of production that famously can't feed them — so the straight-faced, bureaucratic conclusion it edges toward is that the condition has to be relaxed, in euphemisms like demographic rightsizing, voluntary non-provisioning, and Phase II eligibility. Let it arrive there without ever raising its voice.

I want the comedy to come from the actual content rather than from made-up silliness, so please get these right: the "original affluent society" point that affluence is wants met rather than goods accumulated, and that the relevant comparison is leisure and not calories; the demographic ratchet, where surplus creates people and the people then block the exit; the nobody-ever-chose-it problem, since the transition was gradual, local, piecemeal and often reversed, so there is no single decision to undo; the messy bioarchaeological evidence on stature, enamel hypoplasia and anemia, and whether it really shows decline or just who ended up buried where; and the average-versus-total welfare wrinkle, that farming may have made nearly every individual worse off while making far more individuals. It should also quietly undercut the romantic version — forager egalitarianism was not a utopia, and the violence and infanticide rates belong in the picture — but never by stepping out of character to say so.

Keep it legible as satire, and keep it fair: an archaeologist or a philosopher who likes the "mistake" framing should feel got rather than misrepresented, and my students should come away seeing why the framing is harder than the pop-prehistory version of it. No bibliography and no citations — assume you have no web access, and just give me the document itself.
