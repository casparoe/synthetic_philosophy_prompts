A friend of mine -- a physicist who now runs a small systematic trading book -- has talked me into the following. Consider a gamble on a fair coin: whatever you stake is multiplied by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails.

(1) If your wealth evolves multiplicatively and you face the same kind of bet repeatedly, the growth rate you actually experience along your single trajectory converges almost surely to the expected log return per round.
(2) You live one life in time; you do not receive the average over the parallel selves in the ensemble. So the object of your choice should be the time-average growth rate, not expected wealth.
(3) For this coin, expected wealth grows at about 5% per round while the time-average decays at about 5% per round, because the ensemble average is propped up by a vanishing set of lucky paths. Expected-value maximization is therefore a mistake, and the log rule -- a stake of one quarter of wealth here -- is the correct policy.
(4) Nothing in (1)-(3) turns on the stakes being money. It applies to anything that compounds: an endowment, a lineage's gene copies, a population, a firm's capital.
(5) Nothing in it turns on the horizon either. The log rule is myopic: it is the same at every step, so it is what you should do on the last bet as well as on the first.
(6) So: whenever what you care about compounds, maximize expected log -- including for a foundation making many small independent grants, for an individual with a fixed retirement target and a deadline, and for a fund that is liquidated if it falls below a capital floor.

I think (6) is false, and I suspect the argument proves too much, but every step looks fine to me. Three cases bother me most. (a) A foundation whose payouts are many small, nearly independent bets looks as though it should maximize the expected value of the aggregate; the log rule would make it decline every positive-expected-value, negative-expected-log grant, which seems to rule out insurance, diversification, and grantmaking in principle. (b) An agent with an all-or-nothing target and a deadline: maximizing the probability of hitting the target is not the same as maximizing the growth rate, and if the target is out of reach at the log-optimal growth rate, the log rule assigns it a probability near zero. (c) With a capital floor the process can be absorbed, so the almost-sure convergence in (1) is conditional on surviving, while the log rule is willing to accept very deep drawdowns along the way.
