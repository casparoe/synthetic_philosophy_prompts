I do interpretability work and I keep flip-flopping between "obviously these models represent things, look at this feature" and "we're drawing lines through a point cloud and then naming the lines". So I want to try it bottom-up instead: below are my gut verdicts on whether a component really represents the thing we'd be tempted to say it does. What I want from you is the principle (or small set of candidate principles) that I'm implicitly using, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself.

To make the cases comparable, they're all directions in a residual stream, or a single neuron / dictionary feature. Three labels: a direction is *readable* for P if a linear probe on it predicts P well out of distribution; *load-bearing* for P if clamping it at different magnitudes moves the model's behaviour the way you'd expect if it tracked P, and moves nothing else; *inert* if nothing downstream reads it in normal operation, so you can delete the direction at no cost in loss.

My verdicts:

1. Gabor-ish edge and curve detectors in the first layer of a conv net trained on photos. Yes, trivially. If anything isn't a representation, this is.
2. A "French vs English" direction in a small LM: readable, load-bearing (clamp it and the output turns French). Yes.
3. The board-state directions in the Othello models people trained on nothing but move lists: linearly decodable, and flipping a direction flips which squares the model treats as its own and which moves it says are legal. This is my strongest yes of anything on the list, and I don't fully know why it's stronger than the others.
4. A dictionary feature that fires on pictures of bridges, the phrase "my dad", and screenshots of Excel spreadsheets. No. That's a gerrymander that happens to be a real direction in activation space, and I say no even though it's load-bearing: clamping it makes the model start producing all three, sort of.
5. A direction in a fine-tuned model with no counterpart in the base model — you only find it by diffing the two — that, when clamped, reproduces the fine-tune's tic (say, putting an emoji at the end of every reply). Yes, but grudgingly: my instinct is that it represents "the fine-tune's output distribution" rather than "emojis", and I can't say what turns on that difference.
6. In a multimodal model, one direction that fires for a photo of a dog, the written word "dog", and a bark. Yes, and weirdly a *better* case than #2, even though nothing about it is load-bearing in any clean way.
7. A "is this sentence true" direction: readable, transfers to a differently-trained model, and clamping it nudges which of two continuations wins — but the nudge is tiny and only inside a couple of prompt templates. I genuinely go back and forth here, sometimes within the same conversation.
8. And the pair I can't get straight. (a) A probe that reads "is this code buggy" off activations very accurately and robustly, with zero causal effect — ablate the direction, nothing changes. My verdict: no, that's a readout, not a representation. (b) A direction in a Wikipedia-trained model for "the capital of France" — readable, and clamping it makes the model say the city. My verdict: also no, that's memorised lookup in a fancy costume. But then #3 is *also* a model that only ever saw token sequences and has a direction you can clamp, and there I said the strongest possible yes. I don't know what the difference is supposed to be and I suspect one of these three verdicts has to go.
9. A readable, load-bearing, but inert direction: steering with it works fine, yet no head or MLP reads it during normal forward passes, and deleting it costs nothing. My gut says yes, it still represents something — but that verdict sits badly with everything I said about causal role in #8.

So: is there one account here — some mix of causal role, decodability, robustness, cross-model or cross-modal stability, and something about what training actually selected for — or do I have to choose between a baroque principle that saves all nine verdicts and a clean one that overturns two or three? Tell me which clause is doing the work in which case, and which of my verdicts your best candidate simply can't accommodate. And give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, especially: a direction that decodes "what the user wants to hear" as opposed to "what is true" in a chat model; a direction that decodes a property of the model's own upcoming output ("I am about to write a poem"); and two models whose features match up one-to-one so you can patch activations between them.
