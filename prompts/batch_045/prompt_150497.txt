I am a professor of philosophy — my own research is in decision theory and collective action — who, for reasons that looked better a year ago than they do now, agreed to chair my university's Committee on Research with Irreversible Downside. We meet monthly for ninety minutes: a structural biologist, a computer scientist, a grants administrator, the university's counsel, and myself. We see roughly twenty proposals a year — dual-use protocols, large training runs, small outdoor field trials, open releases of data or code — and we have no dedicated staff, no budget for outside review, and a standing rule that every decision be written up in under two pages in language that could survive both the faculty senate and, if a reporter ever asks for it, the front page. Most proposals we can wave through on ordinary research-ethics grounds. The ones that stop us are those where a single group can, by acting alone, impose a large downside on people who had no say in the matter, and where the case for acting rests on that group's own confident estimate of an upside that other competent parties dispute. I have been reading about the unilateralist's curse — roughly, the observation that when several parties can each trigger an action unilaterally, the action gets taken whenever any one of them judges it worthwhile, so that the multiplicity of decision points selects for the most optimistic error instead of averaging errors out — and I am fairly convinced it describes our situation. What I cannot do is hand four non-specialists a paragraph of decision theory and ask them to apply it before lunch.

So I want the curse turned into a checklist my committee can actually run in about thirty minutes per proposal: questions in a fixed order, a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer to each, an explicit stopping rule that returns a no, and an explicit handoff to a named person at the points where the question is one of values rather than facts. I have half-formed distinctions of my own that I would like the procedure either to use or to refute, and I would rather you argue with them than flatter them. First: I suspect the curse bites hardest when the downside is what I have been calling redundant — a step function, so that once anyone triggers it the harm is essentially total and further triggers add nothing — and much more weakly when the downside is roughly additive in the number of actors, since in the additive case the real problem is total volume and the remedy looks like a cap or a quota rather than a veto or a quorum. Second, I want the mirror-image case handled: where any one party can block rather than trigger, outcomes are selected by the most pessimistic estimate instead of the most optimistic one, and the remedies come apart — quorum versus unanimity, and different pathologies in each. If the procedure can tell us which regime we are in before it prescribes, say how; if it cannot, say that plainly. Third, I want substitutability treated carefully: whether declining really hands the action to someone else, and whether merely anticipated substitution licenses acting, as opposed to substitution we have evidence for. Fourth, I think some of our hardest items are irreversible without being physical at all — publishing a protocol, or releasing an anonymized log of near-miss incidents, permanently changes what other parties can choose — and I want to know whether the screen treats those as actions of the same kind.

Two further constraints, and then the cases. The procedure must be honest about the places where it cannot eliminate judgment: where no defensible number can be produced in thirty minutes by people who are not specialists, what it says to do instead; which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; and where it will systematically misfire — over-blocking, or laundering a hunch as a finding. And it must be able to be turned on itself: our own attempt to convene other institutions around a coordinated pause on a class of experiments is itself something we can do alone, and it carries its own hazards, including the possibility that a leaked agenda or a rumored moratorium triggers a rush by others to act before the window closes. Please apply the finished procedure to three items we are likely to face: a group's proposal to publish a protocol that makes a hard-to-culture pathogen substantially easier to work with; a request to run a physically negligible outdoor measurement campaign as a precursor to stratospheric aerosol work, where the objection is normalization and precedent rather than the experiment itself; and the committee's own plan to organize a cross-institutional concert and to publish an anonymized near-miss incident log. I would like at least one of those to come back with a no I will not enjoy defending to my colleagues — I would rather find that out from your worked examples than from the faculty senate.
