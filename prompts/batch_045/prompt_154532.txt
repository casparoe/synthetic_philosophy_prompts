I spent a career in experimental physical chemistry and have only in the last two years, since retiring, been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at a nearby college, where I am usually the person who checks the arithmetic rather than the one who settles the argument. The group has been working through the chapters of Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas on cooperation and superrationality, and at our last meeting two of the other members, Miriam, who taught statistics for many years, and Gerald, who reads a good deal of decision theory, had an argument that ran well past the end of the session. I can follow their calculations but not their disagreement, and I would be grateful for an adjudication.

The case they were discussing is the Platonia dilemma: twenty people are told that each may send at most one telegram before a deadline, that they cannot communicate with one another, and that if exactly one telegram is received its sender gets a billion dollars, while if none arrive or more than one arrives nobody gets anything. Both of them accept, for the sake of the argument, the usual superrational prescription in the original problem: each sends with probability one twentieth, which makes the chance that exactly one telegram goes out as large as it can be, a little under thirty-eight percent.

The argument began when Miriam proposed a variant. Suppose, she said, that the telegraph line is unreliable and that every telegram, independently of every other, has a one-half chance of being lost in transit. Then each player should send with probability one tenth rather than one twentieth: the losses merely dilute the stream of telegrams, and one compensates by sending twice as often, so as to keep the expected number arriving what it was before; the group's chance of success is unchanged, still a little under thirty-eight percent. The general rule, she said, is to send with probability one twentieth divided by the delivery rate, unless the line is so bad that this would require sending more often than always, in which case one simply always sends.

Gerald replied that this calculation smuggles in the assumption that the twenty randomizations are independent physical events, which is exactly what superrationality denies. Superrational thinkers, he said, arrive at the same answer; if Miriam's die comes up favorable, so does everyone else's, not through any physical influence but because it is the same reasoning. So the outcomes are all or nothing: either all twenty telegrams go out or none do. In the variant, if all twenty go out, the chance that exactly one arrives is twenty times one half raised to the twentieth power, which is roughly one in fifty thousand; if none go out, it is zero. The superrational answer in the variant is therefore to send with certainty, and Miriam's tidy one tenth is an artifact. Worse, he said, the variant exposes that the one twentieth answer was never available even in the original problem, since it required the very independence of randomizations that superrationality rules out; strict superrationality in Platonia says that everyone sends and nobody wins.

Miriam answered that the picture of twenty dice landing together is not superrationality but the magical thinking that Hofstadter's critics have always accused him of. Nothing in the definition requires randomizers to be shared. What must be the same is the strategy, the same rule and the same probability, not the outcome of a coin toss. Otherwise, she said, the very notion of a mixed superrational strategy would be incoherent, and the one twentieth prescription would yield zero rather than thirty-eight percent, which cannot be what Hofstadter had in mind. One must distinguish the answer, which is a rule, from its realization, which is a private event.

Gerald conceded that he was claiming no physical influence, and said his point was epistemic rather than causal. Two superrational thinkers facing the same problem converge on the same answer, and an answer that still contains an unsettled coin flip is not yet an answer until the coin has landed. Once each player must wait for a private coin, all any of them knows is that the others are following the same rule, which is just the ordinary game-theoretic picture of independent mixed strategies, and Miriam's superrationality has evaporated into it. He added that this picture is not even stable: if the other nineteen telegrams each arrive with probability one in twenty, a player who abandons the die and sends for sure does about twenty times better than a compliant player, and if all twenty act on that, everyone sends and nobody wins anyway.

Miriam replied that this objection mistakes superrationality for an equilibrium concept. It was never offered as a refinement of Nash equilibrium; it is the injunction to maximize on the assumption that everyone follows the same strategy, and of course a player who defects from that reasoning at the last moment does better if the others comply, that being the entire lesson of the prisoner's dilemma rather than a new difficulty introduced by the telegraph variant. The coincidence reading, she said, cannot give any nontrivial answer to Platonia at all: it says to send with certainty, so everyone sends, so the billion dollars goes unclaimed by construction. A reading that guarantees failure in the case it was designed for has not scored a point against one that delivers a thirty-eight percent chance.

Gerald said he would concede the arithmetic but not the moral. What Platonia really shows, he said, is that superrational reasoning without a shared randomizer cannot do better than about thirty-eight percent, whereas twenty players who could all see one public die roll, a number read out over the radio, could designate a single sender and succeed every time. So the value lies in correlated randomness, which is a piece of physics rather than of logic, and private dice are a substitute that Miriam has smuggled in under the word superrationality. He went on to say that this is why, when Hofstadter tried the thing on his readers with the Luring Lottery, the response was, as he recalled it, a rush of entries rather than a display of restraint, though he allowed that he might be misremembering, and that what the experiment measured was appetite rather than reason.

Miriam said briefly that whatever his readers did bears on psychology rather than on what they had reason to do, and that a public signal does not solve Platonia but deletes it.

Then we had to give the room back.

Would you go through the exchange point by point and tell me who is right about what: which moves are correct, which are correct only given a premise that is itself in dispute, and which are simply wrong, including any arithmetic that does not hold up, correcting my figures quietly if I have garbled them? I would also like to know what both of them are missing, the consideration that would have moved the argument forward rather than around in circles. I have no stake in which of them wins. Please write in continuous prose, as one would in a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface or italic emphasis and no tables, and in American English.
