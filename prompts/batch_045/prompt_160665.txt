You are a professional philosopher who works on formal social choice, fairness, and bargaining theory. Take your time with this: before writing anything, sketch out two or three candidate readings of the practice I describe, note what would tell against each of them, and only then commit to the one you think is best supported.

When a debtor's estate is too small to pay everyone, insolvency law in most jurisdictions distributes it among creditors of equal priority pro rata, in proportion to the size of their claims: if there is only enough to pay forty cents on the dollar, every creditor receives forty per cent of what they are owed, whatever the size of the debt. Notice what this does when claims are wildly unequal. Take an estate of £200,000, one creditor owed £1,000,000 and another owed £100,000. Pro rata gives roughly £182,000 and £18,000. But no possible distribution could have paid the large creditor more than £200,000, so the two creditors' realistic ceilings stand in a ratio of two to one, not ten to one, and a rule of the kind defended in the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution — which gives each side the same fraction of the most it could possibly have got, rather than the same fraction of what it asked for — would divide the same estate roughly £133,000 and £67,000. Maximizing the product of the two recoveries, in the spirit of Nash, would pay the small creditor in full and hand the remainder to the large one.

So: what view of fairness, or of what it is to make a comparable sacrifice, does pro rata sharing actually presuppose — that each creditor should bear a burden proportional to what was at stake for them, or merely proportional to what they claimed? Is the choice of the claim as the denominator essential to the practice, or an accident that happens to coincide with the equal-relative-concession reading whenever no single claim exceeds the whole estate? Is the practice underdetermined, in the sense that several quite different theories of fair division would license it and it therefore gives no evidence for any one of them? Does it sit comfortably alongside the law's own insistence that nobody may recover more than the full amount of their claim, which is precisely what makes a ceiling diverge from a claim? And what would have to change in insolvency practice if we took the Kalai–Smorodinsky idea seriously, and is there anything about the institution of bankruptcy that would resist the change?
