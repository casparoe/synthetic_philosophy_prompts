Had an argument at a dinner thing last night with a friend who works in structured finance — I claimed the subprime blowup is basically a Rosenthal centipede with the wheels falling off, she said that's a cute metaphor that doesn't survive contact with what actually happened, and we left it unresolved. So please do the mapping properly, both directions.

Here's the episode as I'll stipulate it, so don't worry about getting the history exactly right, just take it as given: a subprime mortgage gets originated, sold to an arranger, bundled into an MBS, tranched, the mezzanine slices get repackaged into a CDO, pieces of those get referenced again by synthetic CDOs so the same underlying loan is counted several times over, the AAA tranches get credit-default-swap insurance from a monoline or from AIG, and end up on the books of pension funds, money market funds and a couple of Landesbanken. Every link takes a fee and passes the risk along; the pot is fees, issuance volume and the apparent yield on AAA paper; the game ends when the music stops and whoever is holding it eats the loss. Roughly: 2007 is when the subprime indices start sliding and two Bear Stearns credit funds blow up, 2008 is the terminal node. A handful of players took early — the funds that bought CDS protection, the desks pushing the indices down — and for a long while taking looked like the expensive move, because you pay carry and eat mark-to-market losses while the chain keeps passing.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a vibe: who counts as a player (borrower? broker? rating agency? CDO manager? insurer? or is the whole thing really one player against itself at successive nodes), what exactly a node is, what pass and take are, where the terminal node is, and whether the horizon is in any sense known — Rosenthal's original ran a hundred rounds, so what is the hundred here? Then say what backward induction actually predicts: presumably that nobody should ever have written the first loan, i.e. the chain never gets off node one, and pin down which link of the induction is the one that fails in the real case.

Then two ideas of my own, which I'd rather you stress-test than be polite about. Call the first the looping centipede: in the textbook game a pass consumes a round and moves you one step nearer a known end, but re-securitisation and synthetic referencing meant a pass could re-use exposure that had already been passed — the same mortgage sitting inside several different CDOs — so the pot appeared to grow without any genuinely new node being created. Does that make sense as a formal variant, and if it does, does it break backward induction in a different way than the usual unknown-horizon fix? Call the second the opaque endgame: the induction has to start from the payoffs at the last node, but here nobody could check what the last node was worth, since the terminal payoff depended on default correlations nobody could observe, so arguably the players couldn't even write down the game they were in. If the end node is unverifiable, does the induction have anything to bite on, or is that just the common-knowledge objection in a costume?

And the reverse direction, which is what I actually care about: take the three usual responses to the paradox — (a) bite the bullet, backward induction plus common knowledge of rationality really does say defect at node one, and the fact that lab subjects cooperate just shows people aren't like that; (b) add a small probability of an altruistic or muddled type and let reputation do the work; (c) the game as modelled isn't the game being played — and say which one the 2008 chain is evidence for, and whether it's evidence for any of them at all. My hunch, which you should knock down if its wrong, is that (b) has the wrong shape here because most of these were one-shot arm's-length deals between strangers with no relationship to build, and (a) is close to unfalsifiable in a case like this because you can always rewrite the payoffs after the fact. Then say what the episode shows about the paradox itself: is the centipede the wrong toy for financial chains, or is it the right toy and the interesting cases are precisely the variants with a manufactured pot and an invisible end — and would a lab centipede with a looping or an opaque endgame produce the familiar people-cooperate-for-a-while-and-do-better result for the same reason, or a different one?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one-sentence verdict on whether the centipede explains anything real about 2008 and what, if anything, 2008 does to the paradox — then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me. Where the model has nothing to say about some feature of the case, say so plainly instead of forcing it to speak.
