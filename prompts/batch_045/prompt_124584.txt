hey, I need help putting together a final exam. Act as a political philosopher / ancient philosophy specialist who has taught Republic II more times than you can count, and who has also marked way too many papers where everyone writes the same paragraph about justice being a harmony of the soul and the just person being happier. Please think carefully about this one, and before you write anything do a bit of quiet brainstorming about where the actual crux in Glaucon's challenge lies — what a student who genuinely understands the dialectic sounds like, versus one who has just memorised the labels. Dont hand me a safe generic essay question.

The setup: upper-level undergrad seminar on ethics / the Republic. Students have read book II, they know the city-soul analogy and the function argument, and they know the standard moves. One question, 90 minutes, expected answer about 1000 words, marked out of 20.

What the question has to do: it should be unanswerable by reciting the summary (Glaucon issues the challenge, Socrates replies via the city-soul analogy, justice is a virtue, the just person is better off). The difficulty should come from the students noticing something about the structure of the challenge itself. Two things I care about in particular:

- Glaucon demands that justice be shown worth choosing for its own sake and not merely for what comes of it, but the ring test is framed entirely in terms of what the agent gains or loses by using it. So there is a real question whether for its own sake and for its payoffs can be pulled apart in the way the challenge needs them to be, or whether anything Socrates can say will quietly smuggle a payoff back in and thereby change the subject.
- Whether the standard Socratic reply — injustice disorders the soul, guilt, corrupted character, loss of any coherent relation to yourself — is a real answer to Glaucon or a dodge that leans on contingent facts about how human psychology happens to work.

To force that, I want the question built around a small family of thought-experiment variants I made up, so there is nothing to recite:

- the classic ring of Gyges: invisibility on demand, used over a whole life
- the Open Ledger ring: everything you do while invisible becomes public knowledge the instant you take it off — people learn exactly what was done and to whom — but nobody ever learns who did it
- the Clean Slate ring: invisibility plus a guarantee of zero residue in the agent. No guilt, no shame, no self-contempt, no habituation of appetite, no shift in your tastes or your future desires, and no accidental slip-ups. Your character the day you take it off is identical to your character the day you put it on, whatever you did in between.

The question should make them use these to work out what exactly the ring is supposed to isolate. I want them to feel the squeeze from both sides: the classic ring may be too weak to isolate justice at all, because it leaves internal sanctions in place, while the Clean Slate ring may be too strong to be fair — it might be a piece of dialectical cheating that settles the argument by stipulating away the only thing a virtue theorist says is at stake. And if the virtue theorist's best reply does survive even the Clean Slate ring, they should have to say what shape that reply would have to take.

Deliverables:

1. The question itself, phrased the way you would actually put it on a paper — no hand-holding, no bullet points inside the question, no and then discuss whether Socrates succeeds filler.
2. A model answer of 700–900 words, written at the level a genuinely strong student could actually produce in 90 minutes, not a polished journal article.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points: marks for each, one line on what a top answer must do to earn them, and a one-line note on how people typically get it wrong. Be explicit about what separates an A from a B — name the specific moves, not adjectives like sophisticated or nuanced.

Two more constraints. No secondary literature: the question and the model answer should not require any citation or footnote, and nothing should hinge on who said what in the scholarship. And please flag anything a marker should explicitly NOT reward, so I don't accidentally hand out marks for a well-written recap of the function argument that never engages the ring at all.
