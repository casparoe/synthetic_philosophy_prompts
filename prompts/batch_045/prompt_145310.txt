The setup: I run a small seminar on transformative AI, and every week someone reaches for one of the three canonical analogies — the printing press, the Industrial Revolution, nuclear weapons — and every week the discussion dies in the same place: someone points out a similarity, someone else points out a disanalogy, and nobody has any way of saying which of these is supposed to matter more. I want to fix that by writing the specification first. So: what does a historical analogy for transformative AI have to get right in order to be worth invoking at all?

Please write that specification. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — the concrete historical episode, or the way analogies misfire in practice, that makes it a requirement rather than a taste. Among other things, I want you to sort out the following, using your own judgment about how they should be framed:

- The distinction between analogies of causation (how the technology arrives and spreads) and analogies of consequence (what it does to labor, states, war, knowledge). Most people conflate these and then argue past each other. Is competence in both genuinely required, or can a good analogy be one-sided?
- A near-miss requirement. My own proposal: no analogy should be accepted unless its advocate can also name a historical episode that superficially resembles the target about as closely, along the same dimensions, but in which the expected transformation did not arrive — a negative control. Say whether this is a real desideratum or an unreasonable burden, and if it is real, what it would take to satisfy it.
- A give-up condition: the observable, stated in advance, that would show the analogy has stopped being informative. An analogy that can accommodate every outcome is not doing any work. Where should the burden of stating this fall, and how specific does it have to be to count?
- The rhetorical economy of analogies: who benefits from a given analogy being found persuasive, and does that contaminate it? I want your view on whether the interests served by an analogy are a legitimate part of assessing it or an ad hominem distraction.

I have my own candidate that I keep pushing and want tested rather than humored: the mid-century containerization of ocean shipping. The technology itself was almost trivial; what took decades was the reorganization around it — port infrastructure, cranes, ships, labor agreements, insurance and liability practice, standard-setting bodies — and measured productivity gains lagged the invention badly, in the way computers were once said to be visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics. My claim is that this is a better structural match for AI than any of the canonical three, because the binding constraint is complementary assets and institutional rearrangement rather than the capability itself. Tell me what a specification that vindicates my candidate would look like, but equally what a specification that rules it out would look like, and don't flatter me.

Two further things I care about most.
