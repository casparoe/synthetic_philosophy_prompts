Its the chapter where I argue that van Fraassen's Reflection principle, in the usual form (my credence in p now, conditional on the hypothesis that my later credence in p will be x, should equal x, plus the corollary where you take the expectation over the values you're unsure between), is straightforwardly false, and that the right fix is a replacement I'm calling Interventional Reflection.

The rough shape of the argument, so you know what the objections have to bite on. The counterexamples I build the chapter around are what I call loop cases: propositions whose truth is causally downstream of your own future credence about them. Central bank publishes a forecast of high inflation; the forecast itself moves expectations and the bank then tightens, so conditional on my future credence in high inflation being 0.9, inflation is likely to be low. Or the smaller ones — if I become convinced halfway through a lecture that its going badly I speed up and it does go badly. In those cases Reflection demands a fixed point that knowledge of my own causal footprint on the world destabilises: I set credence x, and setting it to x is itself a reason to expect not-p, so my conditional credence comes out below x and no x is stable. So Reflection fails, not because the future self is unreliable or brainwashed or forgetful, but because the future self is in the causal story.

The repair: don't defer to the credence you will in fact have, defer to the credence you would have under an intervention that sets your later credence to x while blocking every causal path from that credence to the subject matter of p, leaving the evidential paths running into the credence intact. When there are no such paths it collapses back into plain Reflection, which is my main selling point, and I claim the Dutch strategy argument can be rerun on the intervened-on credences so I keep the pragmatic motivation.
