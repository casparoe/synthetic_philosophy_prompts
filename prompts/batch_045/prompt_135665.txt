You are a professional philosopher with research expertise in philosophy of mind (panpsychism and the combination problem), population ethics, and decision theory under severe uncertainty, and you have advised research institutions before. Before you begin drafting, work through the case carefully: set out the strongest version of the argument for taking the addendum seriously and the strongest version of the argument for setting it aside, mark where each one turns on a substantive philosophical claim rather than on tone or institutional credibility, and only then settle on a recommendation. The deliverable is a memo, not an essay: it must end in a bottom line our board can actually vote on.

I chair the Research Ethics Advisory Board at a national particle physics laboratory. Our remit is to review proposed experimental runs for risks to any being capable of suffering and to report a recommendation to the director. We cannot veto a run, but no run has yet proceeded over a negative recommendation.

Before us is a proposed eighteen-month high-luminosity run of our fixed-target hadron beamline, which will produce on the order of 10^19 inelastic collision events. A postdoctoral theorist at the lab has submitted an ethical risk addendum. Its core claims are: first, there is a real but small credence — she proposes somewhere between 0.1 and 1 percent — in some micropsychist or broadly Russellian view on which at least some fundamental physical events have experiential properties; second, on such views we have no principled way to rule out that the states associated with some classes of collision event are states of suffering, and nothing in our current taxonomy of subjects even poses that question; third, even at one-in-a-thousand credence and one-in-a-million event types being associated with suffering, 10^19 events swamps every other item on our agenda in expected terms, and so the run should not be approved until the risk is assessed.

The addendum has split us. Our senior experimentalist offers what she calls the ubiquity reply: if micro-experiences accompany ordinary physical events, then comparable events occur in vastly larger numbers in the sun, in the upper atmosphere and in every cooling cup of coffee, entirely independently of anything we do, so the run's marginal contribution is approximately zero and the expected-value framing is a red herring. The author answers that this shows the problem is not of our making, not that we are permitted to add to it — and that the reply's force differs depending on whether the view in question says that complex integrated arrangements merely rearrange existing subjects or bring genuinely new subjects into existence.

Other members invoke the fanaticism worry: an institution with a fixed review budget and limited public credibility cannot govern itself by astronomical headcounts multiplied by speculative credences, and the review capacity spent here is capacity not spent on risks we actually understand. Our charter also requires that recommendations be defensible to a reasonable non-specialist.

Draft the memo I will circulate before our vote in three weeks. It should do the following: recommend one of four dispositions — approve the run outright; approve it subject to a narrowly scoped, time-limited follow-up with defined triggers; defer pending review by a body with a different remit; or find the addendum out of remit — and say which; state the single strongest consideration on the side you are rejecting, in a form its advocates would recognize as their own; specify what kind of evidence or argument would change the recommendation; and treat the addendum neither as frivolous nor as settling anything. Aim for 700 to 900 words. The laboratory, the beamline and the addendum are particular to us, so please reason from the description above rather than from anything published.
