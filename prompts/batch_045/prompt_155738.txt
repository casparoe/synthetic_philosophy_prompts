Last night over dinner a friend of mine — who does decision theory professionally and is otherwise a very sensible person — argued that acausal trade is not trade at all, but a club whose members pay dues to members who may not exist, on the theory that the other members are paying too. I pushed back: the one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of yourself does seem to me to have a determinate answer, and if cooperation can be got there without any communication, I don't see why adding spatial separation, or a different utility function on the other side, should make the whole thing evaporate. Three hours later we had moved nothing, and I suspect that is roughly where the debate actually stands. So I want to stop arguing and write it instead.

What I need is a piece of sustained deadpan, and the form I have settled on is the annual letter to limited partners from a hedge fund whose entire book is acausal trades. Around 700 to 900 words, laid out like a real investor letter: a performance summary at the top, then a section on 'our edge', then a longer risk-factors section, then a short closing paragraph on the year ahead. Finance-speak throughout, confident and slightly bland.

The comedy has to come from the mechanics, not from decoration, so please get the following right rather than gesturing at them:

One, the fund never trades with a counterparty; it trades with its model of a counterparty, and the counterparty trades with its model of the fund, so both books can show a profit indefinitely and no discrepancy can ever surface. I want the performance numbers to depend on this and to be quietly uncheckable.

Two, the agents best placed to model the fund are precisely the ones whose aims the fund's investors would find alien — superintelligent, indifferent to everything humans care about, abundant. The letter should treat this not as a problem but as a selling point, under some name like 'aims diversification' or an 'orthogonality premium'.

Three, the legibility or commitment-race side of it: to be an attractive counterparty you must make your own decision procedure cheap to simulate, which is itself expensive and constraining. The fund should be buying, selling, or auditing this as a service.

Four, the Pascal's-mugging shape of the whole enterprise. Somewhere in the letter there should be a single trade where an enormous expected payoff rests on a model so thin that any probability you assign would be arbitrary, and the fund's response to this should be a risk-management euphemism rather than a wince.

The one invented detail I want the piece committed to: the fund has launched a retail product that pays ordinary people to make themselves legible and predictable to hypothetical counterparties — that is, to sell off the option value of ever acting unpredictably, in exchange for returns denominated in a utility function nobody at the fund has ever observed. That is where the reader should feel the actual cost of the theory. It must never be stated as a moral claim; it should appear as client service, with maybe a testimonial.

Constraints: nobody in the document ever says the enterprise is absurd, and there is no winking at the reader. Every failure is reframed in the house vocabulary ('unrealized counterparty variance', 'model drift', 'counterparty confirmation lag'). Bury one sentence in a footnote that a specialist would recognize as the strongest real objection to acausal trade, and have the letter dispose of it as a minor accounting matter. My friend should be able to read the thing and find his own objection in it without the piece ever naming it; someone who has never heard of the topic should just read a smug letter from a fund that is obviously up to something.
