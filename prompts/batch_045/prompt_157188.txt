I'm retired — forty-odd years in chemical physics, mostly spectroscopy — and I've been going to a philosophy reading group down the library for about a year now, so I'm picking up arguments decades after everyone else had them. Lately we've been stuck on the probability problem in the many-worlds picture: if the wavefunction never collapses and every outcome happens somewhere, what on earth does it mean to say the 30% outcome is *unlikely*, and where does the squared-amplitude rule come from rather than just "count the branches and give them all the same weight".

So: I want a number. Your credence that this actually works out — that the Born rule can be got out of an Everettian picture (branching, decoherence, no collapse) without quietly helping yourself to it somewhere along the way, so that the squared-amplitude measure is derived rather than assumed. And tell me what you'd count as that being settled either way, because I'm not sure I'd recognise a solution if I saw one.

But the number alone is useless to me, so decompose it. Roughly, I want:

- that the decision-theoretic route (get rational credence out of axioms about how a rational agent should choose when she knows she'll branch) avoids circularity rather than just re-describing the Born rule in the language of betting;

- whether the long-run-frequency style of argument is hopeless, given that you seem to need the Born rule already in order to say which branches are the typical ones;
- how much of the whole disagreement is really just a fight about what "probability" means, i.e. verbal;
- and my other hobby-horse, which I'm very fond of and slightly suspicious of: in statistical mechanics you also need a measure before you can say anything about typicality, and nobody thinks you get it by counting — the dynamics hands you phase-space volume. Is the Everettian's job just to find the analogue of that, and is amplitude-squared a candidate measure or the very thing in dispute?
