ok so I need help finding the broken step in an argument my flatmate keeps running on me. He claims the Church-Turing thesis isn't really a thesis at all — that it's been established, and that people who keep calling it a "thesis" are just being historically polite. Here it is more or less as he gives it.

(1) Call a function on the natural numbers "effective" if there's a finite description of a procedure that a human clerk could follow with pencil and paper, unlimited scratch and unlimited time, with no insight, guessing or luck, and which for each input eventually halts and prints the right answer. The Church-Turing thesis (CTT) says: every effective function is Turing-machine computable.

(2) There's a general trick for pinning down the extension of a vague informal notion without having to define it. Find a formal notion F and show (a) everything falling under F falls under the informal notion, and (b) everything falling under the informal notion falls under F. Then the two have the same extension, vagueness notwithstanding. This is how people argue that first-order derivability captures informal logical consequence: soundness gives (a) using only the boring, uncontroversial part of "follows from", and the completeness theorem gives (b) as a piece of pure mathematics about the formal side, with the vague informal notion swapped out for a precise stand-in. Nobody had to sharpen "follows from" by hand.

(3) So run the same trick on computability. Direction (a) is easy: hand the clerk a machine table and he can grind through it step by step, so every Turing-computable function is effective.

(4) Direction (b). Suppose for reductio that some effective function f is not Turing computable. Then there's a mechanical procedure P for f. Being mechanical, P advances in discrete steps; at each step the clerk reads and writes only finitely many symbols, and what he does next is fixed by finitely much information available right there. But that is exactly what a Turing machine is: finitely many states, a finite alphabet, and a next-step rule depending only on the current state and the symbol under the head. So a Turing machine can shadow P step for step and print whatever P prints. Contradiction.

(5) So (a) and (b) both hold and, by the trick in (2), "effective" and "Turing computable" have the same extension. CTT is settled — and settled without anyone having to say what "mechanical" really means.

(6) Here's what makes me suspicious: the same reasoning seems to settle a question I'd have thought was about physics, not concepts. Take the doubling clerk. He runs a simulation of machine M on input x, but he performs the nth step in 2^-n seconds; if the simulation halts he writes down its output, and if he gets to the end of the first second without it halting he writes 0. Finitely described, no insight needed, every step is an ordinary local step, he halts after one second — and he decides the halting problem. If my flatmate's argument is sound, that thing is ruled out in advance by step (4), and the whole discussion of whether supertask-ish devices could hypercompute is a conceptual confusion rather than an open question about what the universe permits. I don't believe that. I also don't believe CTT is as easy as (5) makes it sound, but that part is more of a gut feeling.

So: which step do I reject? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (2) than keep my sense that hypercomputation is an open question, since I've seen (2) do real work elsewhere — but I want to know what keeping it costs me, and whether there's a version of the argument that survives and just concludes something weaker (like: CTT is a priori but tells us nothing about physics). And if the problem is in (4), can you say exactly what it assumes that (1) doesn't hand it? I keep wanting to say that "finite" is doing two different jobs there and I can't make it sharp.

Also: is it relevant that for logical consequence one half of the squeeze is a theorem of pure mathematics about the formal side, while for computability both halves seem to lean on the informal notion? Or is that a red herring?
