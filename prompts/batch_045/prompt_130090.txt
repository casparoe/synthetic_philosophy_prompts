I have no academic background — I work in logistics and have been reading about induction and simplicity on my own for about a year, mostly from lecture notes and blog posts I can still get at offline — and I've become convinced of a picture I now want to stress-test rather than have explained to me again. The picture: Occam's razor isn't a separate methodological rule but a byproduct of a theory of prediction. Weight every computable hypothesis by two-to-the-minus-its-shortest-description, average their predictions, and you get something no computable predictor can beat by more than a fixed overhead, with the overhead absorbed into a constant that doesn't depend on how much data you've seen. Assume I grant all of that. My question is about where the picture stops doing any work for someone who actually has to choose between two hypotheses on a Tuesday afternoon.

Please take the role of a formal epistemologist who is unusually good at finding the single small, hand-checkable example that carries the whole weight of a conceptual distinction — the kind of example that gets reused in seminars because nothing shorter makes the point. Before writing anything final, brainstorm four or five candidate cases and throw all but one away; I want your best example, not a menu, and I want you to say in a sentence why it beat the others. Take your time with this.

What I want is one worked example that pries apart three things I have started to suspect I am conflating:

(A) the shortest program that produces the data — the raw simplicity measure;
(B) the two-part cost: the length of a hypothesis plus the length of the data encoded given that hypothesis;
(C) what a working scientist would call the simpler hypothesis — fewer moving parts, smooth, mechanistic, the kind of thing you'd sketch on a whiteboard.

Constraints on the example:

1. It must be small enough for me to verify by hand. A sequence of ten to thirty symbols, or a dozen or so (x, y) pairs. If you use pseudocode, keep it to a few lines and count lengths in some crude unit you state up front — bits, tokens, whatever — and be upfront that the crudeness matters.

2. It should have (A) and (C) picking different winners, and the reason must not collapse into the choice of reference machine. I already know the point that any two universal machines differ only by a fixed translation program, and that at small scales this constant dwarfs everything — I want an example that survives that objection, not one that illustrates it. If you think no example can survive it, say so directly and then give the case that gets closest.

3. It should engage what I've started calling "the memorizer's discount." For small data the shortest program is often just print followed by the data itself, so the formalism seems unable to tell a hypothesis apart from a lookup table, and the difference only appears once you specify what you're being scored on out of sample. But I don't want the obvious moral, which is just "collect more data." I want an example where the gap between copying and generalizing shows up for some other reason: perhaps because the generating process is compressible in a way the hypothesis isn't, or because predicting the next symbol and predicting the next symbol after someone intervenes come apart, or because the hypothesis that compresses best is one no one could ever run. Land on one of these sharper versions rather than the size-of-sample one.

4. It should make vivid why the convergence guarantee and the practical advice pull in different directions — why a theorem that is, as I've granted, true, still leaves me with no guidance about which of two hypotheses to act on this week.

Do not cite anything, name papers, or point me at a reading list: I have no library access and I'd much rather you reason from the definitions I've sketched than try to recall a literature you can't check. If you need a formal notion, define it inline in plain words.

Be committed rather than even-handed. Choose one example, state in a single sentence the conceptual claim it is supposed to establish, defend it, and then add a short closing paragraph on how the example could misfire — where someone who thinks the razor just is short-description-length would push back, and whether that pushback is aimed at your example or at my framing. And if you think my three-way distinction between (A), (B) and (C) is itself muddled or collapses on inspection, say so plainly and redraw it before you give me the example, rather than politely working with a bad distinction.
