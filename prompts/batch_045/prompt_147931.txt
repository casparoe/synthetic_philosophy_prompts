I sit on the board of a small land trust, and I have been trying to make sense of the position of a wild-animal-welfare researcher who reviewed our management plan last year and who we may invite to speak at our annual meeting. Her verdicts on the cases that have come up so far, as I recorded them, are these:

She was strongly in favour of eradicating invasive rats from an offshore island to protect ground-nesting seabirds, and her argument, as I recall it, was as much about there being fewer rats alive afterwards as about the chicks being eaten. She was against a housing development that would have cleared a stand of coastal scrub, but the substance of her objection was almost entirely about how the animals already living on the site would die — killed or injured during clearing, or displaced into occupied territories — rather than about the loss of the habitat as such. She was cautiously in favour of immunocontraception for overabundant deer in a county park. And she supported a programme to vaccinate wild rabbits against myxomatosis, but with a caveat I could not follow, along the lines that keeping more rabbits alive is only clearly a gain if a rabbit's life is on the whole a good one, and that nobody is in a position to say whether it is.

The case I actually need a prediction about is our own. We hold roughly four hundred hectares along a creek, most of it retired cattle pasture that is still drained by old tile lines. Our ecologist has proposed breaching the tiles and re-flooding about three hundred hectares as marsh and wet prairie. If the comparable sites are any guide, the plausible ecological outcome is a very large increase in the abundance of frogs and salamanders, aquatic insects and dragonflies, small rodents, and the birds and mesopredators that eat them — that is, mostly short-lived, high-fecundity animals, with most juveniles dying before they reproduce — along with better shelter and food for the individuals that are there. The drawdown and reflooding would be phased and timed outside the main breeding season, so the construction phase itself should kill far fewer animals than a bulldozer clearing scrub would. My own guess is that she will oppose the project, and I cannot tell whether that is because she is running a defensible view I should take seriously or because she simply dislikes anything that increases the number of wild animals.

So: what rule or family of rules fits her four verdicts, and what does each of them predict about the marsh? I am particularly unsure whether she is committed to something about the *number* of wild animals — that an additional short life of the sort a wetland produces is itself a welfare cost, so that reducing carrying capacity is a benefit — or only to something about how well animals live and how they die, in which case better habitat should count in our favour. There is a third possibility I cannot rule out, that she is not running an axiological view at all but an epistemic one, and abstains from any intervention whose effects on welfare cannot be modelled with confidence. Tell me which of these the verdicts pin down and which they leave open, whether the pair of verdicts on the scrub and on the rats are jointly consistent, and whether her position even coheres if she declines to say whether the lives in question are on balance worth living.

There is also a second proposal in the county that I keep coming back to: the same kind of retired, drained farmland on an adjacent parcel, to be converted into a solar array — graded, graveled, and fenced, so a permanent reduction in the number of animals the land supports, carried out in a way that displaces rather than kills most of them. Would she welcome that? Does that case separate the candidate rules better than the marsh does, and what verdict on it would tell me which rule she is really running? Finally, if we do invite her: what would we have to show her about the marsh — population trajectories, causes of death, which taxa are actually affected, whether the invertebrates rather than the birds dominate the arithmetic — for the question to be settled rather than merely argued?
