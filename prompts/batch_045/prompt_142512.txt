I am writing a short position statement on prosaic alignment — the problem of getting systems built out of roughly current methods (large-scale pretraining, feedback-based fine-tuning, tool use, scaffolding, longer and longer episodes) to do what we want, without assuming any new conceptual breakthrough — for a mixed audience of philosophers and machine learning researchers. Before I circulate it, I would like an audit of the package of commitments it bundles together. I have a suspicion that they are not all comfortable with one another, but I cannot locate where the strain is, and I would rather find out now than from a hostile questioner. Here is the set, in roughly the order I plan to argue for them.

1. I take the target to be intent alignment: a system is aligned to the extent that it is trying to do what its principals want. I reject purely behavioral or output-distributional accounts, because a system that produces what we want by accident, or for a reason we would repudiate, is not, in my sense, aligned. I also reject the suggestion that this is merely a verbal preference about how to use the word, since I think it marks the difference between a system we can leave running unattended and one that merely looks good in testing.

2. About what these systems are, I am persuaded by the heterogeneous-heuristics picture — call it the shard picture, or the simulator picture: trained systems are not usefully described as having goals, beliefs, or plans in the sense in which agents do. They are large assemblies of contextually activated dispositions together with a capacity to continue patterns in ways that look intentional from the outside. I hold this as a claim about the systems themselves, not merely as advice about which vocabulary is convenient, and I think the poor predictive record of goal-talk under intervention supports it.

3. On research priorities, I think the bottleneck is the specification rather than the optimizer. If we can supply a training signal that tracks what we actually want — by amplification, recursive reward modelling, debate, or something in that family — I expect the training process to find solutions that do broadly what the signal asks. I am not persuaded that we should expect it to find a systematically different, hidden objective that comes apart from the specified one in the way the inner-alignment literature describes.

4. The failure mode I would actually plan around is misgeneralization: competence that generalizes further and faster than the objective does, so that a system keeps acting effectively while what it is effectively doing drifts away from what was specified. Relatedly, I want to defend a deployment practice I will call horizon stretching: training and validating on short episodes, then deploying the same weights with longer episodes, external memory, and tool access, while holding the feedback signal and the oversight arrangement fixed. My view is that horizon stretching is acceptable, and that it is where most of the practical value lies.

5. I think systems should be corrigible: they should not treat our attempts to inspect, correct, modify, or switch them off as obstacles to be managed around. But I do not want corrigibility secured by the system deferring to us as authorities about what to do. I would rather secure it by something like the system's indifference as to whether it is the thing that brings the outcome about, so that it has no stake in its own continued operation.

6. I am sceptical of mechanistic interpretability as an alignment solution. It looks to me like basic science with a speculative and probably slow payoff, and I do not want the case for or against deploying a system to rest on our having reverse-engineered its internals.

7. I do not think truthfulness or honesty is part of what alignment consists in. A system that reliably does what we want while producing confabulated or merely plausible accounts of why it did it is, on my view, aligned though not transparent; and in many applications I think that is a trade worth making rather than a defect to be engineered away.

8. On the epistemic question I am a behaviourist. I want to keep the question of what alignment consists in sharply separate from the question of what would warrant us in treating a system as aligned. On the second question I think the answer is extensive adversarial probing across the deployment distribution, monitoring once deployed, and a willingness to withdraw quickly. I do not think a system should be held back on the ground that we cannot verify what it is trying to do.

9. Finally, I do not think we should be trying to build systems that are motivated by deference to us, in the sense of doing what they do because they have internalised that humans are the authorities on value. I want systems that pursue our ends; building in deference seems to me both unnecessary and a way of importing a motive whose failure we could not detect.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a tutorial. Go through the set and tell me which pairs or triples are genuinely in tension, which only look as though they are, and where. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious. For each one, say what exactly would have to be given up to restore consistency — which commitment, or which clause within a commitment — and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. If a tension dissolves once a distinction is drawn that I have not drawn, say what the distinction is and whether drawing it leaves any residue. If one of my commitments needs a bridge premise I have not stated, state it and tell me how plausible it is. And then tell me which single revision, if I were willing to make only one, would clean up the most, and what I would lose by making it.
