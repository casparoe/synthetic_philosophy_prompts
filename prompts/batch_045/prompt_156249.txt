I've been arguing with two friends from a reading group I'm in — we're all broadly consequentialist-leaning, none of us are philosophers by training — and they finally talked me into writing down where I've landed on cluelessness so they can have a proper go at it. The case where this actually bites for me is a donation decision: whether to give to something longtermist and existential-risk-flavoured, or to something boring and near-term where I have decent evidence, or to just spend it on a house deposit. Here's my profile: (1) I think the right action is roughly the one that maximizing expected value. (2) I take cluelessness seriously — for most real-world choices with long-run effects, my best estimates of what happens downstream are close to noise, and in some cases I suspect the expected value isn't even well-defined because the sums over all the possible outcomes don't settle down. (3) Because of (2), my default response is a hedging one: don't maximize under your best-guess model, pick the option that does acceptably well across a wide spread of empirical hypotheses and across a range of plausible moral views. (4) I'm also longtermist-flavoured: the far future plausibly contains astronomically many lives, so the stakes of what we do now are astronomical, which is why long-run effects should dominate the near-term stuff in my deliberations. (5) But I reject fanaticism — I don't think a one-in-a-billion-billion shot at an astronomically huge payoff automatically swamps everything else on the table; at some point you're allowed to just set the long shot aside. (6) And when the cluelessness is really radical, when I genuinely can't tell which way an option pushes, I think nothing much is rationally required of me beyond not doing things that look clearly bad, so it's fine to spend money and time on near-term projects I actually understand the effects of. (7) Separately, I've made up a rule I've started leaning on, call it sign-robust satisficing: only take account of effects whose sign is stable across most of my hypothesis space, treat everything else as tied, and don't let how big an effect might be do any work in the decision.

What I want is an audit of this package, not a lecture on cluelessness generally. Go through the pairs and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which ones only look like they do — my friends have each picked a different thing to object to and I can't tell who's right, so I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything as suspicious. For the real tensions, rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one say what specifically would have to give, what it would cost me to give it up, and whether there's a cheaper patch that keeps more of the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most mess at once. Oh, and please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
