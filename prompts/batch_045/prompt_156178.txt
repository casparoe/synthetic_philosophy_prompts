Hey — I need something unserious for once. Our college is doing another one of its periodic "efficiency" reviews, and the working group that was set up to review the review (I got drafted onto it, don't ask) has voted to shut down our Center for Truth and Verification. Their stated reason: truth is not a substantive property, so there is nothing for a center like that to investigate or audit. I teach a seminar on truth, and I've been arguing with a friend in the linguistics department about whether anything remotely like that conclusion follows from the deflationary picture — it doesn't, obviously, but the bad inference is funny, and I want to hand the document to my students as a diagnostic exercise: find the three places where the premise is quietly doing work it can't do. So: write me the memo.

Form: an internal administrative document — a memo header (From, To, Re, Date; invent the units and the names, nothing real anywhere), followed by a numbered set of FAQ-ish clauses, about 700 words total. Voice is deadpan bureacrat-speak: stakeholders, going forward, the Center regrets any inconvenience. The setup: the Center is dissolving itself and replacing itself with a Disquotation Desk whose entire remit is to delete every occurrence of "true" from university communications, on the grounds that calling a claim true adds nothing to just making the claim. What I care about is that the comedy comes from getting the position right rather than from a cartoon of it, so the Desk should fail in the ways it would actually fail. Specifically, I want it to run into (a) the generalization problem — some handbook clause has to say that everything in some earlier document is true, and the Desk can't eliminate the truth talk without writing out an unending list of sentences; (b) blind endorsement — somebody has to endorse whatever some official said without being able to repeat, or even know, what it was, and "is true" is doing all the work there; and (c) the normative bit — a staff rep or a faculty member objects that inquiry is supposed to aim at truth and that assertion has some sort of correctness standard, and the memo answers with the characteristic deflationary shrug (truth is no more than..., nothing here says inquiry isn't worth doing) in a form a specialist would recognize as the real move rather than a strawman. Then the reductio I actually want underscored: because truth isn't a property with a nature to be investigated, the memo closes the fact-checking and evidence-audit unit and reallocates its budget. That inference should be made straight-faced, with the reasoning spelled out, and it should be the thing a reader recoils at.

Two more things if you can fit them in. First, a small aside where the Office of Existence and the Office of Reference send anxious notes, since the same style of argument would seem to deflate them too — existence is just the quantifier, reference is just disquotation again — and the memo brushes them off with exactly the kind of unprincipled "don't be ridiculous" that the view owes us an answer to. Second, the compliance self-audit at the end should turn up a self-referential sentence about its own truth, which the memo has to admit it has no tools for — not because it hasn't thought about it, but because on its own view there is no property there to analyze. Keep it deadpan throughout: no winking at the reader, nobody saying "ironic, isn't it," no real philosophers named, no citations, no reading list. Invented quotes from invented assistant deans are very welcome. It should make someone who knows the literature laugh rather than wince, and still be readable, and still obviously a joke, to a first-year.
