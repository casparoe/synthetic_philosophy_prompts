I sit on a small committee that has to sign off on a permanent museum exhibit about a massacre in a district town in 1931, when a colonial police force fired on a crowd of striking railway workers and their families. The documentary base is thin and lopsided: police firing reports, a medical officer's admission tally for the colonial hospital, some missionary correspondence, and a set of oral histories recorded in the 1970s from survivors' children. Nothing from the strikers, and nothing at all about the women killed in the fields beyond the town, since they were not taxpayers and appear in no register. Two historians on the committee, working from exactly the same box of material and neither of them careless, have written accounts that cannot both be right as framed: one describes a mutiny suppressed at some cost, the other a market-day massacre. At our last meeting a third member, a retired judge, ended the discussion by saying that the facts speak for themselves, that our job is to present what happened and let visitors draw their own conclusions, and that if two historians disagree the remedy is more archive, not more interpretation. Nobody wanted to be the person arguing against the facts, so we adjourned.

I want to know what that sentence could actually be asserting. Please give me a menu of the distinct precise claims it might be expressing, ordered from the platitudinous to the wildly strong, and for each one tell me what kind of claim it is (metaphysical, epistemic, methodological, or normative advice about how historians should write), whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, substantive and doubtful, or straightforwardly false, and whether it would actually deliver the judge's two conclusions: first, that our historians' disagreement must be curable by further evidence rather than by argument about framing, and second, that an exhibit stripped of interpretive framing is neutral rather than simply framed in a way nobody notices.

Let me add the crude apparatus I have been using, in case it is useful and in case it is confused. I have been separating the historian's work into three jobs: deciding which surviving traces are evidence of anything at all; deciding which larger episode a given trace belongs to, so that a shot is placed inside a mutiny, a market-day gathering, or a botched training exercise; and deciding which of the available descriptions of that episode is apt, so that the event gets called a riot, an insurrection, an atrocity, or a failure of crowd control. Are these really three jobs rather than one, and which of them could in principle be settled by the world alone, with no residually interpretive choice left over? Where exactly do the familiar sceptical pressures bite: the point that not every fact about the past becomes a fact of history, and the point that the same sequence of events can be emplotted as tragedy, farce, or bureaucratic accident without any single statement in the account becoming false?

Two further things I would like handled rather than gestured at. First, the archive's silences: the women in the fields leave no trace, and I cannot decide whether their absence is itself among the facts the slogan is talking about, or whether a view on which the facts are only what the records contain has quietly built a political verdict into the metaphysics. Second, a test I have been toying with and do not trust: imagine our two historians exchanging their political sympathies and their narrative preferences while the box of material stays fixed, and ask whether they would converge. Which of the readings you list predict convergence under that counterfactual, which are compatible with continued divergence, and is the test well designed — does it isolate anything, or does swapping sympathies also swap what counts as a relevant trace, and so rig the result?

Finally: the Rankean motto about showing the past as it actually was is often treated as the same slogan in a more dignified register. Is it? If it is a different claim, say which of the readings on your menu it shares and which it avoids, and whether it escapes the difficulties that sink the stronger readings of the judge's line.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no enumerated lists. I would rather read eight hundred or so words of connected argument than anything set out schematically, so carry the verdicts along in the sentences themselves.
