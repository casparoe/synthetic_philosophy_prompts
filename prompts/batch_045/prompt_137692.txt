Two stipulations, so we are not talking past each other. First, by an acausal threat I mean a threat made by an agent with no causal channel to its target, who can nonetheless predict the target's decision procedure and condition its own behavior on the predicted output — for instance, an agent that says it will construct and torment an enormous number of high-fidelity simulations of beings the target cares about unless the target behaves in a specified way, where the two never meet, no enforcement mechanism is needed, and the only cost of carrying out the threat falls on the threatener. Second, assume that a model fine-grained enough to predict what a person would endorse or say under reflection is, on ordinary functionalist assumptions, fine-grained enough to instantiate something that matters if the original person does.

Here is my opening move. I claim that the surrogate-goal strategy and this vulnerability are not two separate problems that happen to co-occur but are linked by a direct trade-off, which I will call the fidelity–leverage dilemma. A surrogate earns its keep only by being rich: a thin proxy — smiles, clicks, survey responses — is exactly what decouples under optimization pressure, and the standard remedy is to move the target to something like ideal reflective endorsement, which is just a demand for high-fidelity models of human judgment. But any such model is a hostage population, and its size is under the threatener's control, not ours: the threatener may name a number of simulated subjects large enough to swamp any finite stake we asked the system to protect, and the usual defenses against coercion are unavailable, since there is no channel to close, no enforcer to deter, and no causal contact to prevent.
