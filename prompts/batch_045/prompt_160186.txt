a friend has been pushing this on me for a fortnight and I've been throught it about a dozen times without finding the hole, but I'm fairly sure the conclusion is false.

Setup: assume the only thing that can make an individual act wrong is its making a causal difference to something that matters. Assume also what everyone in the meat case seems to grant — that my buying one pack of chicken makes no such difference: the birds are already dead, the pack is sitting in the cabinet, and the supermarket reorders on a rolling average in batches of a hundred or so, so no single shopper's fillet moves an order. Then:

(1) There's a batch to reorder at all only because demand is high; had demand been slightly lower, one fewer batch would have been ordered.
(2) Demand's being high isn't some further thing over and above people's individual purchases — there isn't a collective shopper doing any of the buying.
(3) So if demand makes a difference to how many birds get bred, and demand is nothing over and above the individual purchases, then the individual purchases make a difference.
(4) Which contradicts the starting point, that no individual purchase makes one.
(5) And by symmetry it gets worse: every buyer is in exactly the same position, so if the move in (3) works for my purchase it works for everyone's — meaning all ten thousand purchases are each pivotal for a batch, i.e. a million birds, a hundred times more than anyone ever ordered.
(6) So apparently: either individual purchases do make a difference and the inefficacy objection is dead, or aggregate demand causes nothing at all, or — the bit I really can't accept — whether eating meat is wrong just depends on how the ordering software happens to be set up, so that if the government slapped a hard national cap on animals slaughtered at the current level, my eating exactly the same chicken would become causally inert and therefore fine.

Where does it break? I'd rather hold on to (1) and (2) than to the inefficacy claim, but I'd rather not be told to drop a premise merely because I dislike where it leads. Please don't just hand me the standard expected-difference reply (tiny chance of being pivotal times big harm) — this argument never mentions chances, and I want to know what's wrong with it as it stands, and what rejecting the step you pick commits me to about the other cases, voting and emissions included. Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and flag the point you're least sure of.
