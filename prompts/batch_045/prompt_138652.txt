Hey, I need a favour for our reading group — we're doing a few weeks on practical reason and rational choice, and this week's topic was indifference: what you're supposed to do when you genuinely have no preference between two options, the old Buridan's ass setup (the animal sits exactly equidistant between two identical piles of hay and, having no reason to go to one rather than the other, allegedly starves). We spent most of the session going in circles about whether "just pick one, it doesn't matter" is even advice an agent can follow — like, is arbitrarily picking a real action, or do you need some reason however tiny, and if you do need a reason, are you still choosing or just reacting to whatever stray consideration happens to show up first? Anyway someone said the best test of whether a position has legs is to try to write fiction around it, so here I am.

Could you write me a short story (800–1200 words is plenty, it doesn't need to be epic) set in a near-future government office I'm calling the Division of Indifferent Petitions. It only receives cases that have already been certified as exact ties: two applicants for the last place in some programme, identical on every criterion the statute lists, no further information obtainable even in principle. The office decides them with a physical lottery machine called the Casting Die, and the official doctrine behind it is something called the Bundling Rule, which says that when A and B are exactly equally choiceworthy there aren't really two options at all, there's just the bundle {A, B}, so choosing either member is choosing the same thing and nothing of any moral significance happens at the moment of choice — which is also why the Die doesn't need to be clever or fair, it just needs to stop the file.

The protagonist should be a caseworker in her last week before quitting — Mira, or whatever you want to call her — and I'd like the story to turn on two things. First: she's noticed that a lot of petitioners, once they're formally told their case is an exact tie, become strangely unable to act at all; they stall, miss deadlines, won't sign. But the very same people will decide instantly and calmly the moment you add a third option that's clearly worse — a decoy, say: you can have the place, but with a two-year deferral attached. Nobody in the office agrees on what this means. Some staff treat it as a symptom, a kind of deficit in the petitioners; others think it shows the petitioners are the only rational ones in the building and that the Bundling Rule is the incoherent thing, because a bundle apparently isn't the kind of object anyone can actually choose. Second: Mira's final case is a tie on paper, but she has a hunch the file has missed some small real asymmetry — the sort of detail that would be definately irrelevant if the case really is a tie, and decisive if it isn't — and she has to decide whether to go looking for it, whether manufacturing a difference is a way of respecting the petitioners as agents or just a way of lying to herself about what the Die is for. Please let the story dramatise the disagreement rather than settling it: no narrator stepping in at the end to sum up the philosophical moral, keep the Die itself a bit unexplained, and don't give Mira a big moment of clarity where she dramatically refuses to use it — something small and slightly anticlimactic would honestly be better.
