Me and a friend spent about three hours arguing about Arrival last night and got nowhere, so I'm outsourcing it. Her claim is that the film is basically a worked illustration of Hofstadter's frame message / outer message / inner message distinction from Godel Escher Bach, and I think that's a stretch. Her mapping: the twelve shells showing up is the frame message (something is being said, decode me), the whole business of building a shared vocabulary with the heptapods is the outer message, and whatever the aliens actually want is the inner message. My objection is that Hofstadter's story puts the fragility in the inner message — you know there's a message, the trouble is getting at the content — whereas in this film everyone already agrees something is being communicated and the crisis sits in the middle layer, in a fight about which human practice the disputed glyph belongs to: trade, gift, weapon, tool, time. I kept calling that a frame-level disagreement wearing a translation problem's clothes. She says that's just the outer message doing its normal job.

I don't want you to pick a winner between us so much as actually do the reading. Take the film — Villeneuve's Arrival, Louise the linguist and Ian the physicist, the circular logograms, the twelve sites each getting only a slice of the message so no one nation has the whole thing, the blowup over the ambiguous glyph, the ending where learning the language rewrites how Louise experiences time — and read it through that distinction. Since you can't look anything up, take those as given and don't spend words recapping the plot beyond what you need.

Concretely, what I want:

1. A mapping I can argue with. Where exactly do the frame / outer / inner boundaries fall here, and who is the intended decoder — Louise, humanity as a whole, the heptapods, or somebody in the future?
2. Whether my point in the argument is coherent in Hofstadter's terms or whether it collapses back into the outer message. If it doesn't collapse, does it earn a name, and does it do any work on cases outside this film?
3. Something I don't think the framework was built for: in Arrival the outer message rebuilds the receiver. Hofstadter's picture assumes you can separate the message from the machine that plays it, but here learning to read the script changes who is doing the reading, and the content turns out to be addressed to the person Louise becomes. I've been calling that an auto-initiating message — a message whose outer message includes the specs for the receiver that can understand its inner message. Say whether that's a real category or whether Hofstadter already has room for it via his own self-referential examples (the record that encodes its own player, the genotype that's read by machinery it specifies), and whether those are really the same move.
4. The time loop, which seems worse than point 3. If the effect the message produces — Louise's book, humanity's help three thousand years on — is part of the reason it gets sent at all, then meaning isn't located in sender, signal or receiver but in a causal loop. Can the frame/outer/inner apparatus say anything disciplined about that, or does it just go quiet? Be willing to say it goes quiet.
5. Where the reading resists. The film runs on a very strong linguistic determinism that almost nobody in philosophy or linguistics defends, and Hofstadter's view is that meaning isn't intrinsic but also isn't up for grabs in that way. If the premise is false, does the reading survive as a reading of the film, or does it collapse into reading a thesis the film doesn't hold?

Write in British English please. And don't just explain the distinction and then summarise the movie — the thing I actually want is an argument about whether the lens fits, where it illuminates a scene, and where it distorts either the film or Hofstadter. If you think one of us was more right, say which and why.
