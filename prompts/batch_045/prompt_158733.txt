I teach an upper-level seminar called Moral Psychology and I'm putting together a handout for our session on the guilt/shame debate two weeks from now. I want it to be a decision tree rather than a reading summary, because my students are quite good at memorizing which author holds which view and terrible at figuring out which view they themselves hold. So: build me a tree that takes a student from their own reactions to concrete cases and lands them on a named position among roughly six to eight leaves — guilt-primacy, shame-primacy, some kind of division-of-labor or both-are-needed view, a rehabilitation-of-shame view, a deflationary view on which the two aren't really distinct at the level of phenomenology or are just one emotion with different eliciting conditions, and a rejectionist view that says neither is the emotion we should be cultivating and we'd do better with something like regret, remorse, or agent-regret without the self-directed sting. Every branch node should be a question a student can answer without knowing where it leads.

For the diagnostic questions I'd rather you not lean on the standard act-versus-self contrast alone, since everyone has heard that one and it doesn't sort people very finely. A few distinctions I've been mulling that I think do more work: what the emotion is *addressed to* (the victim as creditor, the community as witness, an internalized observer, or nobody in particular); whether it's backward-looking and debt-like or forward-looking and regulative, or instead status-altering in a way that no amount of repair reverses; whether it can take omissions as its object — guilt about what you failed to notice, shame about a trait you didn't choose, and whether either of those is really within the voluntary; and what exit the emotion makes available to the agent, since repair, disclosure, self-punishment, withdrawal or self-exile, and moral bookkeeping of the I've-already-been-good-so-I'm-licensed sort are, I think, where the views really diverge. If the logical space admits combinations nobody occupies, mark them and say whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I'd rather hand students an honest note about that than a tidy diagram.

Then the second half, which is what I actually care about: the question of whether a culture can *choose*. Same format, smaller — maybe five or six branch nodes and three or four leaves — routing from what a person thinks about a handful of institutional levers (how public sanctions are, whether they're administered by peers or specialists, whether wrongdoing is framed as a debt or as a stain, whether the community doing the disapproving is one the agent identifies with, whether there's any exit available) through to positions on whether guilt-dominant or shame-dominant moral ecologies are something a society could deliberately move toward, or whether the mid-century anthropological guilt-culture/shame-culture contrast was always a description of side-effects nobody could steer. Please build in the leakage worry: institutions that try to induce guilt may produce shame anyway, and ones that rely on shame misfire when the agent doesn't care about the audience's regard. Keep the whole thing readable for undergrads — no jargon terms they'd have to look up, and where a position needs a label invent a plain one. I have a suspicion the guilt/shame contrast is doing less work than the literature assumes, but I don't want to bake that into the handout, so make the tree neutral on where it lands.
