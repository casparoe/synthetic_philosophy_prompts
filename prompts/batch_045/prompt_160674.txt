i'm four drafts into a novel set on a planet that isn't Earth and there's one scene I keep flubbing, and I think it's because I don't actually know what the argument is about, so I end up making one character a strawman.

The setup: my field biologist, Dass, has done two seasons on a colonial glider called a harrin. The whole clade is soft-bodied, so there are no fossils, none, and Dass can't do lab manipulations out there. Harrin grow a big keel on the underside — the "rasp" — on only one of the colony's three morphs. It's expensive to grow. It's rigid because it's mineralised, and the hollow inside is there because of how it's built: the thing is inflated with gas and then hardened, so the chamber is an architectural leftover of the building method. In the aquatic juvenile stage that same chamber works as a float. In the adult, the keel resonates when the morph glides and makes a low sound, and Dass has decent data that bigger-keeled colonies fledge more young — partly because the sound seems to coordinate hunting, partly because it startles prey into the net-glides. Related species have keels of every size and Dass can find no clean match between keel size and anything ecological she can measure. Her colleague Fenn is a Gould–Lewontin style sceptic and thinks "it's an adaptation for X" is a just-so story with a number taped to it.

Here's the thing: I get to stipulate the actual history, because I invented the world. What I want from you first is the spec, not the verdict.

What does a theory have to deliver before a claim like "the rasp is an adaptation for X" is in good standing? Give me the requirement list, with the case or consideration behind each one — the distinctions it has to be able to draw and not collapse (adaptation, exaptation, architectural by-product of a developmental constraint, trait currently maintained by selection but not originally selected for, trait drifted up and later co-opted, trait good for the colony rather than its bearer), whether "adaptation" is a historical claim about a token lineage or a forward-looking claim about current fitness contribution and whether one trait can be an adaptation under one of those and not the other, what kinds of evidence are admissible and which ones become non-negotiable when the usual channels are closed (no fossils, no clean comparative spread, no experimental manipulation), what separates a testable adaptationist hypothesis from a story, how much optimality you're allowed to demand and what happens to costly or locally suboptimal traits, how finely the trait itself gets individuated and what you do when it has more than one current function, and whose fitness is even in question when the bearer is one morph of a colony.

Then the parts I actually care about: which of those requirements are real desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp; whether some subset can't be satisfied together (if the list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly, that's the most useful thing you could tell me); which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidate theories; and which requirements are negotiable given my purpose, which is narrower than a journal's — I need the standard that makes both Dass and Fenn reasonable people arguing well from the evidence available in a field camp, not the standard for publishing. A short scorecard at the end for the reverse-engineering/optimism style, the Gould–Lewontin style, and whatever third option you think belongs on it, but keep the weight on the criteria, the scoring is the part I can fake.

Last thing, the novelist's question: if I want the disagreement to be a genuine stalemate rather than Fenn being pig-headed, which bits of the rasp's real history should I stipulate?
