i TA the intro philosophy-of-mind course and we finish on the extended mind next week — last session, about 20 minutes left, and I want to close by reading a poem out loud instead of wrapping up on the board. Could you write me a sestina (six stanzas of six, plus the three-line envoi, standard rotating end-words) where the form actually does the argument instead of decorating it.

The case I've been using all term instead of the well-known notebook one, becuase I wanted a resource that's genuinely load-bearing rather than a lookup table: a paintings conservator who has worked with the same hand-made swatch book for thirty years. She mixed and laid down every chip herself, it isn't indexed or alphabatized, she navigates it by muscle memory, and when a colour she needs isn't in it she experiences that as a gap in her own thinking rather than as missing information. Then, around the middle of the poem, the book gets scanned: the digital version is colour-calibrated, backed up, searchable by wavelength, strictly more reliable than the paper one in every external sense — and she still checks it against the paper one, doesn't take it at face value, and it doesn't become part of her.

So the six end-words should be the things whose position keeps shifting — I was thinking something like paper, hand, scan, gap, trust, grain, but swap any of them if a different set makes the rotation work harder. The whole point of the sestina, for me, is that the same words keep coming back in a new slot each stanza, so I want the reader to feel the inside/outside line being redrawn over and over and to notice that what makes a resource count isn't that it's causally hooked up to you (the lightbulb and the bus timetable are causally hooked up to you too) but that it is there when you need it, reliable, and endorsed without deliberation — and that the scan fails on the last of those while winning on the others, so reliability on its own doesn't get you constitution.

The envoi should use all six words and should be where the poem stops teasing and says the one thing. I'd like it to land on the claim that the dispute isn't really about how big the mind is, but about what a bit of the world has to be like in order to be a proper part of a thinking system, and to leave open whether settling that is a discovery about minds or a decision about how to count them.

Some constraints so it doesn't come out generic: it should never lean on "it causally affects what I do, so it's part of my mind" — that's exactly the inference I've spent two weeks training them to be suspicious of. No cyborg or Borg or phone-as-limb imagery, and no triumphant "the mind leaks out into the world" ending; the conservator has to come through as a person with a trade, not as an illustration. If you can work in some version of the worry that this lets far too much in, great, but it has to be in the poem's own terms — no stanza that's just a footnote with line breaks. Lines roughly even in length, readable aloud in about two minutes, and don't mangle the syntax to hit the end-words. Someone who knows the literature should be able to tell which move the poem is making at each turn; someone who doesn't should just get a good poem about a woman and her book.
