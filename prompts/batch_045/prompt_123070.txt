I have to hand something to our hospital's policy committee by the end of the month, and I would rather have the specification before I go back to the candidate accounts. I'm a philosophy professor, drafted — I suspect as the token humanist — onto the committee rewriting our determination-of-death policy; the document has to do three jobs at once, saying when life-sustaining treatment may be withdrawn, saying when organs may be procured, and giving bereaved families one sentence about what happened to the person they brought in. I have taught the material twice and it goes badly each time: half the students arrive convinced that brain death is a legal fiction invented to make procurement easier, the other half that a ventilated body with a destroyed brain is simply and obviously a corpse, and neither half can say what an account of death would have to get right for the dispute to be settled. The uniform act our statute copies offers two alternative prongs, irreversible loss of circulatory and respiratory function or irreversible loss of all functions of the entire brain including the brain stem, and the attempt to revise it was quietly abandoned a couple of years ago, which suggests to me that some of what we want cannot be had together.

Four cases keep doing the work in my notes. First, a patient with catastrophic brain injury who is not brain dead: support is withdrawn, the heart stops, and after a short interval the team declares death on the circulatory prong and procures organs, even though that circulation is not irreversible in any absolute sense — with enough exertion it might be restarted — but permanent, because a decision has been made not to restart it; if permanence is enough, the time of death seems to depend on a decision. Second, a body with a destroyed brain, on a ventilator, with hormones replaced: circulation, temperature regulation, wound healing, even gestation continue for months, so if death is the loss of the organism as an integrated whole this body still looks integrated, and the brain criterion starts to look like a proxy for the thing rather than the thing. Third, suppose a perfusion technology becomes clinically available that can re-establish cerebral microcirculation and recover some cellular and network activity hours after circulatory arrest: irreversible then comes to mean irreversible with the equipment we happen to own, and the moment of death becomes a function of what is on the ward that day. Fourth, the two prongs can simply disagree, with the same patient dead under one and not dead under the other, and our policy will need a rule for which governs.

So what I want is the desiderata. What must an account of death be able to deliver to be adequate for a document like ours — which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it must respect (death as an event or as a process, irreversibility or mere permanence, the phenomenon itself or a proxy for it, one concept or several role-relative ones), which requirements are genuine and which are the preferences of a camp, whether that be neurologists, transplant surgeons, or philosophers with a taste for tidy metaphysics? Assume I know the usual candidates — the whole-brain criterion, the brainstem criterion, the higher-brain or neocortical criterion, the circulatory criterion read as irreversible or as permanent, and the organism-as-a-whole view — so name them rather than explain them, and give me no reading list and no citations; I have the literature and I do not want it handed back to me. Answer as exactly seven numbered items, with no introduction and no closing summary: items one through five the requirements themselves, each in two sentences at most, each tagged HARD or SOFT, each with the case that earns it a place on the list; item six the subset of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and which one has to give; item seven the single requirement that excludes the most candidates, and which candidates it excludes.
