I sit on the governance committee of a learned society whose major decisions are made at an open all-member business meeting held once a year: a few hundred members attend, votes are by show of hands, and decisions take effect immediately. Three episodes forced this review. Two years ago the meeting narrowly voted, late in the day and after a long and heated session, to expel a member for remarks the majority found offensive. Last year it voted, on the strength of one eloquent speech and against a written warning from the treasurer, to launch an independent online journal that is now projected to run a large deficit. This year a motion to cut a small but well-regarded graduate programme failed badly in an emotional session and would plainly have passed had the vote been retaken; the chair refused to reopen it.

We are split between two packages. The first keeps the open meeting but requires (i) a second, separately noticed vote, with the strongest contrary case circulated in writing beforehand, before any decision takes effect that imposes a personal sanction or commits us to net spending above a threshold, and (ii) a mandatory written note from the relevant officer, distributed with the agenda and read aloud before the vote, on any motion with fiscal or legal consequences. The second, urged by two of my colleagues, would replace the open meeting with a hundred-member assembly chosen by lot for one-year terms, meeting twice a year, on the grounds that our attendees self-select for time, travel money, and strong feeling. A further suggestion, which one member says mirrors Athenian practice, would make whoever moves a motion liable to censure or to a share of the costs if the motion is later judged to have harmed the society.

Draft the memo I will circulate: recommend one package or a specified hybrid, with scope, thresholds, dates for adoption and review, and a clear disposition of the proposer-liability suggestion. Read our three episodes against the three Athenian failures — the Mytilene decision and its reversal the next day, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates — and say which reform each case actually supports and which it does not. Give the strongest version of the case against whatever you recommend; I expect the direct-participation objection to be the one that hurts. I want no citations, dates, or figures I would have to look up. Tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and what would change your mind, and if the whole project of treating a fifth-century assembly as our cautionary tale is misleading us — or if one of my three episodes is a bad analogue and I am seeing Athens in it because I went looking — say so plainly rather than handing me a tidy memo that uses Athens as decoration.
