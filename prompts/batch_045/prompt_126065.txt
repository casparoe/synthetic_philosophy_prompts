so a city council I'm advising is toying with the idea of running one real decision through a decision market instead of a normal vote, and they've asked me for a one-pager on what such a mechanism would have to get right before I'd sign off on it. I want the spec, not the pitch.

The setup to write it against: the decision is whether to bring in congestion charging for the downtown core. The council picks a welfare metric up front (say some composite of median commute time, downtown air quality and small-business revenue, measured 18 months after launch). Traders buy and sell contracts that pay out conditional on the measure being adopted, and the market's recommendation is binding unless a supermajority overrides it. Real money, real losers, and it all has to survive an audit and a local newspaper.

Give me the desiderata: what any mechanism like this has to deliver to count as good enough for that job — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results from social choice or market design it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp.

I already know I'm going to get "markets can be manipulated" and "the metric is gameable". Fine, put them on the list, but the bits I'm actually stuck on and want handled properly:

- the adoption/do gap. What gets priced is how the metric looks in worlds where the policy is adopted, but what the council needs is what happens if it does the thing. Adoption is itself caused by stuff — lobbying, who's mayor, the state of the budget — that also causes the outcome, so a contract can trade high without the policy doing any of the work. Say what a design would have to add for the price to mean what the council wants it to mean, and whether that's even available with one city and no control.
- abstention. What should the mechanism do when nobody shows up to trade, or the spread is wider than the decision is worth? A thing that always outputs a confident recommendation even on no information is worse than one that can shut up. I want a requirement about this.
- metric drift. The metric is frozen before the debate, but the policy may change what residents care about, and the residents get a vote later.
- the scoreboard problem. It's a one-off decision with a payout 18 months out, so we may never find out whether the market was actually well calibrated — which makes it hard to judge the mechanism itself. Is "you can tell after the fact whether it worked" a requirement, and what's the cheapest way to get it?
- the people implementing the policy are also allowed to trade on it.

Also: flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied (my hunch is that "legible enough for the public to trust it" pulls against "robust to adversarial traders", but you tell me), and tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate designs. If it's quick, a small table scoring binding futarchy, an advisory-only market, and a citizens' assembly that just gets the prices as one input is fine — but the criteria are the part I care about, so keep the weight there.
