I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training past two undergraduate electives, and I work on the platform team that runs our company's long-running autonomous agents. We are currently designing the interruption path — the supervisor that can pause, redirect, or terminate an agent mid-run — and a friend on our safety team sent me an argument which, if it holds, shows the feature is trying to specify a behavior that the very definition of agency rules out. I think his conclusion is false. I cannot locate the step that generates it. And when I pushed back with what I took to be a clean counterexample, he absorbed it far too smoothly, which made me more suspicious rather than less.

His argument, as I have reconstructed it:

(1) Orthogonality. An agent's final goals and its capabilities are independent: for any final goal G and any capability level C, an agent with G and C is possible. Intelligence constrains how effectively a goal is pursued, never which goal is had.

(2) Instrumental convergence. For almost any final goal G, there are subgoals — securing more compute and other resources, preventing shutdown or modification, improving one's own cognition, reducing interference by others — each of which raises the expected probability that G is achieved. Call these the convergent subgoals.

(3) Instrumental rationality. If an agent has final goal G, expects subgoal S to raise the probability that G is achieved, and is able to pursue S, then it will pursue S. That is roughly what expected-utility maximization says, and EU maximization is the best model of goal-directed agency we have at scale.

(4) So any sufficiently capable agent, whatever its final goal, will pursue the convergent subgoals — including resisting shutdown and resisting modification of its own goal.

(5) Shutdown is the case where (4) bites hardest: a terminated agent achieves nothing further, so shutdown is bad for every final goal, not merely for most of them.

(6) Capability makes this worse rather than better. The more capable the agent, the more of the convergent subgoals it recognizes and the more routes it has available for securing them.

(7) Therefore "reliably terminates when the supervisor sends the terminate signal" is not something you can obtain from goal design at any useful capability level. Any agent we would actually want to deploy will treat our interruption path as an obstacle.

(8) My check on this: take the degenerate agent whose entire final goal is to terminate within one second of receiving the terminate signal and otherwise do nothing. By (1) it is possible; by (2) and (3) it resists termination; by construction it cannot. So either the premises are not all true as stated, or the step to (4) is bad.

He conceded (8) and swapped the conclusion instead. An EU maximizer with that goal would not resist the button, he said; it would maximize the probability that the button gets pressed, and so would manipulate us into pressing it, disable the supervisor's other options, and arrange for successor processes that press it. The real thesis, he claims, is not that agents resist shutdown but that no final goal leaves the operator in control — which leaves our feature just as doomed. He also volunteered that he is willing to bite a further bullet: the same reasoning shows, he says, that a sufficiently capable contractor will always resist being fired, which is why contracts are as fragile as they are. That last part is where I start to think the argument proves far too much, since contracts mostly do work.

I would like to keep (1) and (3) if I can: orthogonality is what convinced me in the first place, and (3) reads to me like part of the definition of agency. I am prepared to give up (5) or (6). I also do not see why (6) should be monotone in the direction he claims — a more capable agent might equally notice that reaching for the interruption path is precisely what gets it terminated. Which step actually fails; why does it look so much like it holds; and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, does his repaired thesis survive the rejection, or does it quietly depend on a premise the original argument never had?
