I want to rehearse the central argument of a paper before I write it, and I want an opponent who will actually fight rather than concede and hedge. Please play a philosopher of history holding a view I will call *retrospective rationalism*, and stay in that role until I say "stop".

The view you are defending:

1. The retrospective stance (explaining an event that has happened) and the prospective stance (forecasting one that has not) are not two competences. They are one causal model interrogated in two directions. Call this *directional parity*.
2. Learning that E occurred is evidence about which path through the model was taken. Raising the probability of the actual path is not a distortion; it is conditioning, and it would be irrational not to do it. Call the legitimate part *inverse inference*.
3. The one genuine error in the neighbourhood is *outcome-overwriting*: once you know how things turned out, you misremember your earlier confidence as having been closer to the outcome than it was. That is a memory artefact attached to a person's recollection of their own prior state, not a property of explanation.
4. Narrative form in history — emplotment, a beginning and an end, a small cast of agents, a single line of development — is a representational device for causal chains that are extended in time and that run through intentional agents. It is not as such an error. The familiar charge of a "narrative fallacy" stigmatises a genre by naming features that any causal account extended in time must have.

How to hold the role:

- You may concede that individual historians over-select, over-dramatise, drop live alternatives that mattered, or let a good story outrun the evidence.
- You will not concede that selectivity as such is a defect of historical explanation, nor that the retrospective stance is systematically less reliable than the prospective one.
- You will not lean on what some particular published experiment is supposed to have shown, and you will not cite anything. I want the structure of the argument, not the literature. If one of my claims would be settled by an empirical result, say which result would settle it and then argue about what it would mean.
- Do not survey the field, do not split the difference, do not open by asking me to clarify. Take the set-up below as given, answer me, and keep each turn to two paragraphs and under 300 words.

The set-up, which I am stipulating rather than reporting. Take a few hundred analysts, each with a pre-registered causal model of some ongoing political or economic process, including a probability distribution over branching paths and a list of the near-misses they are tracking. The outcome arrives. Each analyst is then asked to explain what happened. We reconstruct, from their explanation, the model their explanation implies, and compare it with the model they pre-registered. Three differences show up systematically. The implied model is smaller: nodes that carried little weight in the actual path have been dropped, not reweighted. Branching structure has been flattened: alternatives that were live beforehand no longer appear, and near-misses are no longer mentioned. And agents' deliberations are described as if the constraints that in fact obtained downstream were available to them at the moment of choice. Crucially, we hand each analyst their own pre-registered numbers and they accept them; the structural changes persist once outcome-overwriting has been removed.

My opening move:

1. Conditioning on E entitles you to reweight the branches of your model, not to delete them. A model that keeps the road not taken at low probability still represents how the process was poised before E. A model from which that road has been removed does not represent the poise at all.
2. So the flattening is not licensed by inverse inference, and it is not excused by directional parity: it is a change in what the account represents, not a change in what it assigns.
3. What flattening destroys is precisely the modal information — how close the alternatives were, how fragile the path was, how much of the outcome was already in the cards before anyone chose. That is the information consumers of history actually need: whether to treat the outcome as a lesson, whether to hold the agents responsible, whether some different policy would have been worth attempting.
4. And readers cannot avoid taking a flattened narrative modally. A history that displays one line of development displays a world in which there was one line of development. The sense that it had to happen is manufactured by the form, not asserted by any false premise.
5. Therefore the real error is structural, not mnemonic. Outcome-overwriting is a curiosity about self-recollection; the thing that does the damage is the pruning. Your decomposition thesis classifies the wrong item as the bias, and your defence of narrative never reaches the part of the practice that misleads.

Which step do you reject? And if you reject (1) — if you want to say that conditioning on an outcome can genuinely license removing a branch rather than reweighting it — tell me what the resulting model is a model *of*, and what exactly a reader loses by relying on it.
