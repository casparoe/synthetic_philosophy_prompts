I convene a philosophy reading group that meets for ninety minutes on Sunday evenings: about fourteen people, mostly working or retired engineers, mathematicians and software developers, no formal philosophy training, and a low tolerance for anything that sounds like verbal cleverness. Next month's session is mine, and I want to spend it on Benacerraf's dilemma: the tension between the requirement that a semantics for mathematical sentences treat them the way it treats ordinary sentences, and the requirement that an account of mathematical knowledge not lean on our standing in any information-carrying relation to abstract objects.

I don't need the dilemma explained to me; I know it and I know the usual menu of responses. I need a plan for explaining it to this group, and I need the plan to be driven by what depends on what. Three things I have built myself and want you to work into the plan, or tell me to drop.

First, a distinction I intend to hand them: shallow uniformity — a mathematical sentence can be negated, embedded in conditionals, and attributed to other people's beliefs exactly as an ordinary sentence can — versus deep uniformity — its truth condition is generated compositionally out of the referents of its parts in exactly the way the truth condition of a sentence about a cat on a mat is. My hope is that the distinction lets me say what the paraphrase-style responses buy and what they quietly give up. Tell me whether it carves the dialectic where it needs to be carved, or whether it distorts something, and give me the one sentence I should say when I introduce it.

Second, the carrying example I have been planning to build the hour around: a structural engineer's report on a footbridge, stating that two girders will never fall into a damaging resonance because the ratio of their natural frequencies is irrational. I like it because it puts a mathematical vocabulary item in the middle of an empirical, causal, professionally load-bearing claim, so that no one in the room is tempted to treat it as a sentence about notation. My worry is the opposite one: that the causal story about the bridge will absorb all the attention and the semantic constraint will never be felt. Say whether this is the right carrier, what such an example has to have and what it has to lack, and at which minute it should enter.

Third, my candidate check for whether the idea has landed: I write the bridge sentence on the board and ask them to produce the nominalist-friendly paraphrase, then to embed that paraphrase inside the inspector did not realise that ___. My thought is that the embedding is where shallow uniformity bites and where a paraphrase that looked fine in isolation starts to misbehave. Confirm it, repair it, or replace it — but I want a check that distinguishes genuine grip from someone who has merely learned to say that platonism has an epistemology problem.

Please structure your reply in the following sections, at roughly these lengths:

1. Opening frame — the single sentence or move I should start with, and why it must come before anything else (~80 words).
2. Dependency map — the components in the order they have to arrive, with the dependency noted for each and rough minute markers across the ninety minutes (~300 words).
3. The girders — verdict on the carrying example, what it must have and lack, and where in the sequence it goes (~250 words).
4. Shallow and deep uniformity — whether the distinction earns its place, how to state it in one line, and where it misleads (~150 words).
5. Misconception map — the misconceptions this audience will bring or form, in the order they will surface, each with the point in the sequence where I should defuse it and the single sentence I should say there. Include at least these, and add the ones I have not thought of: that this is simply the question whether numbers exist; that the culprit is an optional causal theory of knowledge and dropping it dissolves everything; that the paraphrase move costs nothing; that this is a sceptical paradox which threatens mathematical knowledge the way a brain-in-a-vat threatens ordinary knowledge; and that there is a solution to be found rather than a price to be paid (~400 words).
6. What I will refuse to settle — the nearby topics I should name as out of bounds, and the line I should use when someone asks about them (~120 words).
7. The check — the question or task I end on, what an answer that shows the idea has landed sounds like, and what a fluent answer that misses it sounds like (~200 words).

No reading suggestions, no bibliographies, no history of the literature beyond what the plan itself needs; and please keep the plan about the sequence rather than turning into the explanation I am going to give.
