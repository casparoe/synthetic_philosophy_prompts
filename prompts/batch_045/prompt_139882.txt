I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy who is loosely attached to a cognitive neuroscience lab, and in three weeks I'm defending my thesis prospectus: a thirty-minute talk followed by about twenty minutes of questions from the lab's Friday meeting plus my committee. The room will be my two philosophers — one a first-order representationalist who thinks higher-order theories get the explanatory order backwards, one an agnostic who mainly wants to know whether any of this could ever be tested — plus the lab: a PI and a postdoc who are both committed to global neuronal workspace theory and who hear every higher-order view as workspace broadcasting with extra vocabulary, two graduate students who run psychophysics on confidence and metacognitive sensitivity and who think in terms of type-1 and type-2 performance, and a neurologist who works on disorders of consciousness in the ICU and who has seen a lot of patients whose behavior and whose reports come apart. My thesis is that the standard versions fail because they model the higher-order state as a judgment or a belief-like representation, roughly on the order of I am now in state S, and that we should instead model it as what I call an indexical registration: a non-conceptual, de re, demonstrative-like state produced by a dedicated self-monitoring mechanism that selects and points at a currently active first-order state without describing it, and whose identity is partly fixed by the state it points at, so that it cannot misidentify or mistarget and is not a representation that might fail to refer. Against the classic belief-like version associated with Rosenthal I argue that the conceptual version makes consciousness too intellectual and leaves the misrepresentation problem insoluble; against the dispositionalist variant I argue that reducing the higher-order element to a disposition to judge destroys the constitutive claim and leaves you with a first-order theory in disguise; and I claim the indexical version handles the Block-style overflow and meat machine worries, the rock objection, and the obvious induction from self-monitoring to consciousness in infants and non-human animals, while remaining something a lab could in principle test.

The empirical spine of the prospectus is a dissociation I construct hypothetically — I want to be clear that this is my own invented probe, not a published study — involving a patient whose first-order sensory processing is intact and whose conceptual metacognition is intact and well calibrated, but whose self-monitoring mechanism has been degraded, so that she discriminates at a high rate, knows perfectly well that she discriminated well, and reports no visual experience at all; and its mirror image, a patient in whom the monitoring mechanism runs on a degraded or empty first-order state and who reports a bare, contentless something happening, an experience with no phenomenal character beyond its bare presence. I claim the first profile is predicted by no view except mine, since workspace theorists should predict preserved experience with preserved first-order processing and the metacognition people should predict that well-calibrated confidence is itself the mark of consciousness; and I claim the second profile is one that neither first-order representationalism, for which consciousness just is a matter of having content of a certain kind, nor any assertoric higher-order view, for which the higher-order state inherits its content from the target, can accommodate. My own sense is that the first case is my strongest card and the second is my weakest, since I have to say something coherent about what a contentless experience is and about why the notion isn't simply empty.
