I record a long-form interview series on the mechanics of creative work. My next guest is a cognitive scientist — call her Dr. A. — who has just published a book-length formalization of the variation-on-a-theme picture of creativity: the view, in the lineage of Hofstadter's knob-twiddling image, that a creative act is best modeled as a point in a space of stylistic parameters, or knobs, that a creator has implicitly defined by her previous work, so that creating is movement through that space rather than invention of the space. Three claims in the book are load-bearing. First, the factorization claim: any mature creator's corpus is well modeled by a space of roughly seven to twelve continuous and largely independent dimensions — things like density, harmonic or tonal distance, degree of figuration, scale of gesture — recoverable from the corpus itself, with novelty consisting in occupying a point not previously occupied. Second, what I'll call the reachability claim: uptake is a non-monotonic function of distance from the creator's own prior region, so that too near reads as competent but empty and too far reads as noise; she offers a rough quantitative version, a band lying outside the convex hull of the previous work but still recognizable to a substantial fraction of the relevant audience. Third, the outsourced-filter claim: the model needs no internal critic, because selection is done after the fact by a field or audience; the theory is one of generation, not of value. Her reliable escape hatch, when pressed, is that whatever you point to is just a traversal in a bigger space.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Nothing generic: no question about what drew her to the topic, or about influences. Press where the view is actually thin. I want at least one question each on: (i) changes of the space itself — a creator who introduces a new parameter or a new idiom rather than a new setting — together with the worry that if any novelty can be retro-described as a traversal in an enlarged space, the theory predicts nothing; (ii) the filter, and specifically the cases with no audience: abandoned drafts, work heard by nobody, the private sense that something is wrong before anyone else has heard it; (iii) the two ends of a career, the first works produced when there is no corpus to define any knobs from, and the late works whose settings share no dimension with the early ones, so that it is unclear in what sense it is still the same style or the same creator that the space belongs to; (iv) the gap between the model's narration and the phenomenology, the fact that creators report accident, discovery, and being led by the material rather than choosing a parameter value; (v) mechanization, since if the space is estimable from a corpus then sampling it should produce candidate variations, and I want her to say whether she treats that as a prediction of the theory or as a reductio of it; and (vi) whether a model of generation can say anything about why originality is valued, or whether value is left entirely to reception. Sequence the questions so that her escape hatch becomes progressively harder to reach, and end with one question about what evidence could count against the view and one about which of her three claims she would surrender first and what would be left of the theory if she did.

For context on my own sympathies: I find the model convincing as an account of working variation inside a settled idiom and unconvincing as an account of changes of idiom. I want the questions to be fair to her rather than to smuggle in my skepticism, but I do want the worry genuinely pressed rather than flattered.
