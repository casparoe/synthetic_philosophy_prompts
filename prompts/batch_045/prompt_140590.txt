I need a consistency note for my university's Committee on Nonhuman Subjects, and I would rather it were drafted by someone with no stake in the outcome, because I have a large one: the application now before the committee is mine.

Our chair, who wrote or shaped every decision the committee has issued, retired in June. She described herself as a Nozickian and summed her position up as follows: animals belong in the felicific calculus and nothing more, whereas persons are protected by moral side constraints and may not be used as mere means however good the consequences. Her precedents are these:

1. 2019/14 — routine culling (maceration) of day-old male layer chicks: approved. The birds' pain must be minimised, but no constraint bars the practice.
2. 2020/07 — a terminal hypoxia study on six chimpanzees, anaesthetised throughout, expected to yield a therapy benefiting thousands of children: approved, 4–1. The apes' suffering weighs in the balance; nothing vetoes it.
3. 2021/03 — cerebral organoids grown from donated human cells, showing organised electrophysiological responses to noxious stimuli, to be subjected to a painful stimulation protocol and then destroyed: approved.
4. 2022/11 — inclusion of adults with advanced dementia (no language, no prospective memory, no capacity to refuse) in a phase I study involving repeated painful procedures and no prospect of benefit, with proxy consent given by family: rejected, on the ground that no balance of burdens and benefits could license it.

The new case, 2024/09, is my application to study language acquisition longitudinally in eight chimpanzees from an enhanced line — a germline construct introduced before birth, as a result of which they develop within the human range on executive function, episodic future thinking and symbolic communication; unedited, they would develop as ordinary chimpanzees. The study involves fourteen months of repeated sedation, cerebrospinal fluid sampling, and one scheduled six-week period of social isolation. There is no prospect of benefit to the subjects and a large expected epistemic gain. They can be asked, in the relevant sense, and can refuse; the protocol does not ask them. The line was created at a partner institute abroad, so we have never ruled on whether such animals should exist — only now on how they may be used.

My instinct is that we must refuse, but I drafted the protocol, so I am the worst judge of it.

What I want from you is a short memo, in 550 words or fewer and in British English: (1) the rules that fit our four precedents equally well — I can think of three, one of which protects these animals and two of which do not, but there may be a better one, perhaps turning on whether a being could in principle be asked and could refuse; (2) the decision you recommend as the one our own precedents commit us to, with your reasons and the closest analog among the four; (3) the principle we would be adopting whichever way we went; and (4) the single further case we should force ourselves to decide, in the written reasons, in order to separate the surviving rules.
