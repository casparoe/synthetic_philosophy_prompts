You are grading a sit exam for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar. The seminar used two labels of its own for positions in the debate about standing to blame: the Fairness View, on which a blamer lacks standing when she is unwilling to accept the application, to her own conduct, of the norm she invokes against her target; and the Relationship View, on which standing depends on the relationship between blamer and target rather than on the blamer's own record.

Do not restate the student's claims approvingly without checking them: in particular, say whether each quoted conclusion actually follows from the considerations offered, and note any place where two different questions are being run together. Conclude with a one-sentence summary of the answer's overall standing. Please write your assessment in British English.

Question

A blames B for breaking a promise. B replies that A broke a similar promise last month and said nothing about it, and therefore has no standing to blame him.

(a) Distinguish two complaints B might be making: first, that A's blame is unfair or disrespectful toward B; second, that A's blame is a defective or insincere expression of the judgment it purports to express.
(b) Explain why it matters that standing is a matter of the blamer's normative situation rather than of whether the blamed act was wrong. What, if anything, does B's objection show about whether B's promise-breaking was wrong?
(c) Now consider the Confessor: she freely admits her own similar promise-breaking, offers no excuse for it, and judges it exactly as harshly as she judges B's. Does she have standing? Which, if either, of the two complaints distinguished in (a) is available to B against her? Explain your answer with reference to both views.

Student's answer

The two complaints in (a) differ in their object. The unfairness or disrespect complaint concerns what the blame does: blame is a demand addressed to the target, and it is unfair to make that demand while exempting oneself from the norm that is supposed to ground it. The second complaint concerns whether the blame really expresses the judgment it appears to express: the hypocrite says that promise-breaking is wrong, but her own conduct suggests she does not really believe this, so her blame is defective as an expression of judgment.

This matters for (b) because standing and wrongness are independent. A blamer can lack standing even though what she says is perfectly true: she is not saying something false about her target's conduct, she is simply not in a position to say it. So B's objection, even if it succeeds, shows nothing at all about whether his promise-breaking was wrong; it shows only something about A.

The Confessor has standing. She does believe that promise-breaking is wrong, and she applies that norm to herself as strictly as she applies it to B, so her blame is sincere in the second sense above; and sincerity is what standing requires. What B is left with is only the tu quoque charge, that A did the same thing herself. That charge is a fallacy: the fact that someone else has done the same thing is logically irrelevant to whether B's own act was wrong. B's objection to the Confessor therefore fails entirely.

The ordinary hypocrite is different. She is unwilling to accept the application of the norm to herself, so on the Fairness View she lacks standing: she is not in a position to make the demand she is making. And because she cannot properly address that demand to B at all, the blame carries no normative force for B whatsoever; B is released from any obligation to answer it and may simply set it aside, as he would set aside a demand made by someone with no authority over him. This is why failures of standing are sometimes described as leaving the blamer without a voice in the matter.

The Relationship View reaches the same verdict by a different route. Standing depends on the relationship between blamer and target, and a blamer who has recently broken a similar promise has damaged that relationship, so the relationship no longer supports the demand either.
