Write an essay of about 1,000 words on whether Molinism's middle knowledge can survive being translated out of theology and into the setting I actually spend time arguing about: an ideal predictor.

The setup, so we are talking about the same thing. Middle knowledge is knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: what any possible agent would freely choose in any possible circumstance, known prior to and independently of any decision about which circumstances will obtain. The standard objection is the grounding objection: nothing seems available to make such counterfactuals true. Not God's will, on pain of collapsing middle knowledge into free knowledge and destroying the freedom it was meant to protect; not the agent's nature or the circumstances alone, on pain of their being either brute or straightforwardly false.

The secular analogue: an agent P with unbounded computation and a complete model of agent A's decision procedure, which never intervenes and never interacts with A, but reliably reports what A would do in whatever circumstance you hand it. Call what P exploits counterfactuals of agentive freedom. The first question for the essay: are these any better or worse grounded than the Molinist's?

The second question is the one I want the essay to earn its keep on. Some agents are anti-predictive: their policy is to read off any prediction of their choice that they have reason to regard as reliable, and do otherwise. For such an agent the relevant counterfactual appears to have no stable truth value at all, since whatever P settles on would be falsified, and no fixed point need exist. So consider whether the Molinist can and should restrict middle knowledge to the well-founded counterfactuals of freedom, and what that restriction costs. Two costs to weigh. First, if anti-predictive agents are possible, providential control over any world containing them is limited in a way Molinism advertises itself as avoiding. Second, and worse: if it is God's own omniscience that makes the prediction reliable, and an agent's policy is to do otherwise than what is reliably predicted, then middle knowledge may generate its own pathological case, and the restriction may not be available to the Molinist even in principle.

Weigh both sides, but defend a verdict: either the grounding objection, sharpened by the anti-predictive case, sinks Molinism and the secular analogue together, or both turn out to be in the same boat as ordinary counterfactual claims about agents and the objection is overwrought. Say which, and say what you take the strongest consideration on the other side to be. Argue from the structure of the problem; I do not want citations, and nothing should hang on who said what first.
