I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a section of a chapter on off-path reasoning in the backward induction paradox, and I've been toying with a variant of the centipede game as a foil. The four-node centipede I use has Player 1 moving at nodes 1 and 3, Player 2 at nodes 2 and 4, and these payoffs: node 1, Take (2, 0) or Pass to node 2; node 2, Take (1, 6) or Pass to node 3; node 3, Take (12, 5) or Pass to node 4; node 4, Take (11, 30) or Pass (21, 21). Backward induction delivers Take at node 1 and an outcome of (2, 0), against (21, 21) if both players pass throughout. My variant, which I've been calling the amnesiac centipede, leaves the tree and the payoffs alone but puts Player 1's two decision points in a single information set: P1 is not told which node she is at and retains no memory of having moved, so she must name a single action that is applied at both node 1 and node 3, while Player 2 observes the node perfectly and knows that P1 is in this situation. Two people in my reading group argued about whether the variant changes anything, and I genuinely could not referee it, so I'd like your help.

Ines, who works on epistemic game theory, opened by saying backward induction has nothing to do with recall: in the ordinary game the only way to resist the argument is the familiar move that being at node 3 would be evidence that Player 2 is not an expected-utility maximizer, and amnesia removes exactly that inference, since P1 cannot localize herself and so cannot condition on a surprise; her single action therefore gets evaluated at node 1, where Take pays 2 against the 1 she would get if P2 took at node 2, so the outcome is (2, 0) again. (2) Rahul replied that this treats P2's off-path behaviour as fixed by backward induction, when the point is that P2 gets to move at all only if P1 chose Pass — and because P1's action is the same at both nodes, P2's conditional inference is total rather than merely probabilistic: conditional on being at node 2, P2 knows with certainty that P1 will also Pass at node 3, so P2's best response is Pass (30 against 6) and P1's single action pays 11 rather than 2; no trembles or reputation machinery needed, just deduction from the fact that P2 is moving. (3) Ines answered that this is the same conditioning-on-an-unreached-node trick that generates the paradoxes, and that it is specifically illegitimate here because it treats P1's two visits as one decision: without perfect recall the equivalence between mixed and behavioral strategies fails (Kuhn's theorem needs perfect recall), so a deviation by P1 can mean either one random draw governing both visits or a fresh choice at each visit; only on the first reading does P2's inference pin the later action, while on the second P1's node-3 action is uncorrelated with her node-1 action, P2 falls back on the equilibrium prediction of Take at node 3, takes at node 2, and we are back to (2, 0) — so Rahul's conclusion rests on a modeling choice about the self that the variant itself does not settle. (4) Rahul said the choice is not free: on the second reading the two choosers have the same information, the same options and the same preferences, so any non-arbitrary decision rule returns the same action at both visits, and independence would have to be stipulated rather than derived; and on the first reading the ex ante and interim perspectives coincide in a way they do not in the original game — even if P1 at the information set splits her self-locating belief fifty-fifty between the two visits she makes under Pass, Take pays .5·2 + .5·12 = 7 against Pass's 11, so Pass wins both at the planning stage (11 against 2) and at the action stage, whereas in the ordinary centipede you want to pass ex ante and take at node 3. (5) Ines said the fifty-fifty is doing all the work and is not innocent, because the number of visits depends on the action being evaluated: under Take there is exactly one visit, the self-locating probability of being at node 3 is 0, and Take's 2 beats Pass's 1 — a self-confirming fixed point, just as Pass-with-1/2 is another; with imperfect recall there is no Bayes rule to anchor self-locating belief, so the variant does not dissolve the paradox but exchanges which node am I at for which fixed point. (6) Rahul replied that the two fixed points are not symmetric, because Take survives only if P2 credibly threatens to take at node 2, and that threat collapses once P1 plays Pass with any probability ε: conditional on being at node 2 P2 knows P1 will Pass again, P2 passes, and P1's Pass pays 11 against Take's 2, so Take is eliminated for every ε > 0 while Pass survives the same test; the reason the test bites here and not in the original game is structural — in the original game P1's nodes are separate information sets with separate randomizations, so a tremble at node 1 carries no information about node 3, which is exactly why reputation arguments there have to posit an exogenous irrational type with a substantial prior. (7) Ines conceded that the ε-test shows Take fails a trembling-hand-style test but denied it shows Pass is the solution, since the standard refinement machinery — sequential equilibrium, trembling-hand perfection as usually defined — is stated for games with perfect recall and does not straightforwardly apply; the honest conclusion, she said, is that the variant has no answer until one fixes a convention about what an agent is under imperfect recall, and its real value is diagnostic: what sustains backward induction in the ordinary centipede is the independence of the decision points, not the length of the game or the size of the gains.

Could you adjudicate this move by move — for each of the seven moves say whether it is clearly right, right with an important qualification, or mistaken, and sketch the reasoning in each case, including checking the payoff arithmetic where a move depends on it — and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd also like one suspicion of mine checked: I have a feeling the whole exchange presupposes something about what P2 knows about P1's condition, or about whether P1's rationality is common knowledge between them, that neither party makes explicit; tell me whether that is a real gap in the dialectic or a red herring. Finally, please be explicit about the epistemic status of what you claim: which claims are settled and uncontroversial among people who actually work on this material (I would guess at least the backward induction result in the perfect-recall game, the strictness of the relevant best responses, and the failure of the mixed/behavioral equivalence and of the usual sequential-equilibrium apparatus under imperfect recall), which are contested, and which turn on a convention about how to model an agent who cannot tell her own decision points apart; I would much rather be told that something is a live methodological dispute with two candidate answers than be handed one answer dressed up as the consensus.
