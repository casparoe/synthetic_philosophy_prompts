I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long magazine feature about whether the built and digital world carries politics inside it — the old claim that the design of a thing can settle a question of power as durably as a statute can, and that some artifacts are, as it were, frozen legislation. I've spent a few months collecting cases and noticing my own gut reactions, and the pattern is much less tidy than the slogan suggests, so I'd like help articulating the principle, if there is one, before I try to write around it.

Here are the verdicts I keep returning to. The low overpasses over the parkways on Long Island, built low enough that buses could not pass beneath them: yes, genuinely political, and political in the thing itself rather than merely in the intentions of the official who ordered them. The mechanical tomato harvester that required a firmer tomato and in practice helped push small growers off the land: no, or at least not in the same way — that reads to me as economics running through a machine rather than politics in it. The face-recognition lock on a new apartment building, shipped with a default confidence threshold that fails more often for some residents than for others: yes, political, and more clearly than the harvester, even though the vendor will describe the number as an engineering parameter. A park bench with armrests down the middle so that nobody can lie down: yes. A roundabout that replaces a four-way stop in a residential neighborhood — it cuts severe injuries but speeds through-traffic and quietly reassigns whom the street is for: my gut says this has political effects without being political, and I cannot make that distinction precise. A two-page form teachers must file to send a student to a restorative-justice program instead of suspending them, where the length alone means almost nobody files it and a progressive policy is quietly nullified: yes, political, which troubles me because the designers' intentions were the opposite. A video-call app that mutes everyone on joining: political, but only weakly, since anyone can unmute; whereas an elevator that requires a key fob above the third floor feels strongly political even though the fob rule is just as easy to revoke. A subway stairwell the transit authority has left unrepaired for years in a station used mostly by night-shift workers: yes, I want to say political, although nothing there was designed. And a pothole-reporting app that tenants' organizers repurposed to coordinate a rent strike: I do not think that shows the app has politics, but I do think it is the most politically important item on my list.

An editor offered me a distinction I half-like — that some artifacts impose a politics while others merely permit one — and I want to know whether that, or something better, is doing the real work. So: please state a principle, or a small set of competing principles, precise enough that each clause is traceable to one of my verdicts, and that yields verdicts on cases I have not settled. The two I keep going back and forth on are the preauthorization hold a pay-at-the-pump terminal places on a debit card before you fuel up, which effectively prices out people whose balances are thin, and a city's use of a learned model to rank 311 complaints for repair, which allocates a scarce public good by a rule nobody wrote down and nobody intended. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts a good principle would force me to abandon: I suspect the harvester and the roundabout cannot both stand, that the unrepaired stairwell breaks any account requiring design or intention, and that the repurposed app means the interesting question is not whether artifacts have politics but which ones can be argued with.
