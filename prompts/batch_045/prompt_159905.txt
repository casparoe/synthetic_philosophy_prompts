You are a philosopher of mind and epistemology who works on perception, and who has also done applied work on evidence assessment and research-integrity review. Please think carefully and work in two stages. First, brainstorm: lay out the distinctions from the literature on the cognitive penetration of perception that could matter here — penetration of experience versus penetration of judgment, reporting or response; whether a reported effect is better explained by task demands, imagery, or memory than by a change in what was seen; encapsulation and modularity, and the reply that apparent penetration shows up mainly under degraded, brief or ambiguous stimulus conditions; the difference between penetration by long-term perceptual learning and expertise and penetration by an occurrent belief, hope or desire about the very hypothesis at hand; the worry that an experience shaped by the belief it is supposed to confirm cannot give non-circular support; and the 2016 Behavioral and Brain Sciences review by Firestone and Scholl, which argued that the published reports of top-down effects on perception do not establish what they are taken to establish. Say explicitly which of these distinctions must survive being turned into a five-minute checklist for someone with no training in the field, and which should be dropped as unusable at the front line.

Then write the procedure. I am the verification lead for a citizen-science biodiversity recording platform whose records feed a national species atlas, and that atlas is used for Red List assessments, for designating protected sites, and as evidence in planning decisions that are legally binding on landowners. My reviewers are volunteers with no psychology or philosophy background; they handle roughly three to four hundred verification decisions a week at about five minutes each, and any downgrade has to be defensible in a two-sentence note to the volunteer who submitted the record, many of whom are the same people we ask to go out again next week. I can also change what we collect at submission time, if that is where the real fix lies, so tell me which fields the form should capture. What I need is an ordered set of questions to run on a record, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops and says no or downgrades the record to anecdotal or corroboration-only status, and the points where it hands the decision to a named human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts — the asymmetric costs matter here, since a false positive record can freeze a housing development while a false negative can hide the collapse of a species that was already declining. Some of my reviewers think the answer is simply to require photographs; I do not think that settles it, because the photographer chooses what to frame and a photo is still classified by someone with expectations of their own.

Please then run the procedure on three cases and show your work, not just the verdicts. First: an experienced volunteer with a long county listing history reports a rare vagrant warbler at a well-known migrant trap, eighteen hours after a rare-bird alert naming that species goes out to the local email list; a twelve-second view, no photograph, detailed written field notes, three further observers reporting the same bird later that day after the same alert; it would be a first county record and my communications team has already drafted the press release. Second: a novice submits a blurred photograph of one member of a well-known lookalike species pair, during a week in which the platform targeted her postcode with a prompt to look for that species. Third, at the level of the whole dataset rather than a single record: during weeks when we run those targeted prompts, reports of the target species rise by around forty per cent, and the prompt is my team's most-used engagement feature and appears in the deck we show funders. If the procedure says something about that third case that costs me a feature I built, I would rather hear it now than in the annual review. Finally, flag the cases the procedure cannot settle at all, be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say plainly what this procedure must not be used for — including, I suspect, whether it may be used to tell a volunteer what they really saw rather than what their record is worth as evidence.
