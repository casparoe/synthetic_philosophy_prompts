I have been working through the dialectic over Thomson's lamp and I no longer trust my grip on what the disagreement is actually about, so I want the conceptual geography rather than a summary of positions. Here is the case as I am stipulating it: a lamp is off at t = 0 and is toggled at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and in general at 1 − 2^(−n); there are infinitely many toggles before t = 1 and none at or after it. Thomson's conclusion is that such a task cannot be performed, on the ground that the description supplies no answer to the question of the lamp's state at t = 1 while the lamp must be on or off. The standard reply is that this is a non sequitur: the description constrains the state at every time strictly before t = 1 and simply says nothing about t = 1, and a description's silence is not a contradiction in the thing described.

My difficulty is the inference from *not specified* to *not had*, and then from *not had* to *not performable*. Please separate the candidate conclusions — that there is no fact of the matter about the state at t = 1, that the state is determinate but unknowable from the description, that the task is metaphysically impossible, that it is merely physically impossible given how switches and lamps work, that the description is semantically incomplete, and that the case shows something about the scope of classical logic or bivalence — and say for each what additional premise about states, causation, or the semantics of task-descriptions would be needed to reach it, and which of those premises is doing the real work in Thomson's argument.

Two complications I want handled, because they are what made me doubt the tidy story. First, the convergent analogues: a runner whose positions converge on a limit point, or a dimmer whose brightness is halved at each step, where continuity seems to fix the value at t = 1. Is the contrast with the lamp merely that one sequence converges and the other does not, which would make the whole puzzle a trivial remark about divergent series and leave Thomson's insistence that the impossibility of a supertask has nothing to do with the convergence or divergence of an associated sequence unexplained? Or does the convergent case also rest on a substantive stipulation that the description itself does not provide, in which case the two cases differ in degree of determinacy rather than in kind? Second, my own variant: take two lamps wired through an interlock that guarantees they are never both on, and toggle them alternately on the same schedule, so that at every time before t = 1 they are in opposite states while the interlock rules out the pair (on, on) at t = 1 and permits the other three combinations. The joint description is silent about t = 1 yet nontrivially constrains it. Does that show that indeterminacy here admits of degrees, such that the flat verdict of no fact of the matter is too coarse? And does the presence of a real constraint at the limit in the two-lamp case give us any reason to think the single-lamp description is less silent than it appears, or is the constraint inert with respect to the original argument?
