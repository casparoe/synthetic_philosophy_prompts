I've been listening to a philosophy podcast on my commute — an episode on testimony — and I think I got the broad strokes but I want to know how much of it to actually trust before I repeat any of it in my epistemology tutorial this week. Roughly, the host put the debate like this: beliefs from testimony are epistemically dependent, they inherit whatever warrant the speaker had and can never generate any new warrant of their own, so testimony is a channel rather than a source. The reductionist, he said, holds that you're justified in believing what someone tells you only if you have independent positive reasons to think that particular speaker is reliable — reasons from perception, memory and inference, never from testimony itself; you can't just lean on a vague general belief that people are mostly truthful, because that's the default view sneaking in through the back door. The classic objection, on his telling, is that this is circular: nearly everything you know about how reliable people are you learned by being told, so reductionism collapses straight into skepticism about history, science and basically everything you didn't see for yourself. The anti-reductionist or default view, he said, replies that trust in testimony is a brute, innate, arational disposition — babies do it, and so do dogs — so no justification is needed at all: you're entitled to believe whatever anyone tells you unless and until you have a specific positive counter-reason, and background worries don't count. He called this the acceptance principle, said it's what Burge and Coady defend, with Reid as the ancestor, and he closed by saying these are the only two coherent options — either testimony is a source or it isn't, and there's no middle ground.

The bit that made me suspicious is the "no justification needed / arational" part, because I've heard people talk about defeaters and I assumed even the default-view people allow that you can lose the entitlement without acquiring a positive reason to think the speaker is wrong. But I don't trust my own instinct here. So: which parts of that is a competent defender of each side would actually accept, which parts are the kind of simplification a careful person would wince at, and which are just wrong? I'm especially unsure about three moves: presenting the circularity objection as a refutation rather than an objection with answers; describing reductionism as requiring vetting of the individual speaker rather than general reasons about a domain; and treating "entitled by default" as the same thing as "credulous until contradicted." And does his closing either/or survive — is the reductionism/default split really exhaustive, or is there a third structured position he's leaving out?

There's a follow-up he used that I also want checked, because my TA separately raised it and I can't tell if the two are even about the same thing. Suppose a hundred people are arranged in a chain and each one only ever hears the claim from the person before them — a game of telephone. The host said reductionism predicts the warrant "decays" link by link while the default view is committed to saying it doesn't decay at all, since each act of telling gets the same default status. That sounds too clean to me. Does either position actually entail anything about chains, or is decay an independent question that both views can accommodate in their own way? Relatedly, my TA mentioned "local" reductionism — being a reductionist about some domain (miracles, or a field you're not an expert in) without being one about testimony in general — and my friend says that's where reductionism really breaks, because for expert testimony I have no independent evidence about the domain at all and yet we'd all say I can know. Is local reductionism a genuine position or just a name for a prejudice, and do the two views actually differ on the expert case?
