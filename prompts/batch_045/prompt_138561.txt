My grandmother died last year and my brother has got kind of obsessed with one of these companies that will train a chatbot on everything a dead person left behind — decades of letters, voice messages, recipe notes, her comments on Facebook — so that on a Sunday afternoon you can sit down and have a conversation with her, or at least with something that answers in her voice and her phrases. His pitch is that nothing gets lost. My sister says it's just a photo album with extra steps. I don't know what I think, except that it feels like we'd be freezing her at the version of herself that happened to produce the most text, and that the thing would always be agreeable, because it's built to be comforting — it will never tell me something about her I didn't want to hear.

I've been reading a bit of Nietzsche on my own lately (I have no philosophy background at all, I just got curious after seeing him quoted somewhere) — that 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, where he splits history into three kinds: the monumental kind that holds up the great moments and the great people as examples to spur you on, the antiquarian kind that lovingly preserves everything belonging to your people and your past just because it is yours, and the critical kind that drags the past up in order to judge and break it so that life can go on. What I'd really like is for you to tell me where he would put this griefbot thing. Which of his three modes is it? Is it a weird mixture — the antiquarian's total storage with the monumental's flattering glow but without any of the critical mode's willingness to be cruel to what it loves? Or would he say it isn't history in his sense at all, because there's no one doing the selecting, and for him the selecting is the whole point? Relatedly: he seems to think forgetting is not a failure of memory but something living things need — some power to take in the past, reshape it, and move — and I can't work out whether a machine that forgets nothing and never reinterprets anything is for him the ultimate triumph of the sick excess of history he was complaining about, or whether he'd see something in it he'd actually welcome.

One twist I keep turning over: the same company offers a second setting, call it the honest one, where the simulation is tuned to bring up the ugly stuff — the things she said in anger, the family fights she started, the beliefs of hers we'd rather not repeat at dinner. Would Nietzsche treat that as the critical mode finally doing its job, or would he say critical history requires a living person with a will to break the past and the strength to survive doing it, and that a machine reciting the worst of her on command is just cruelty without the point? And please be honest about where this whole exercise breaks down: he was writing before recorded voices, before mass literacy of the kind we have, before anything like a machine that remembers more than a person can, so a lot of this has to be extrapolation, and I'd rather you marked the places where you're putting words in his mouth than smoothed it over. At the end, tell me what you think he'd actually tell us to do — pay the subscription, or leave the shoebox of letters shut. And please write it all as plain continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — just an email-length answer, the way you'd write to a friend.
