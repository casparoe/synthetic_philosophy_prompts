Here's my opening, which is that student's point cleaned up. I've been calling it the retrofit gap: everything in your toolkit — a selective-sweep signature in the genome, a comparative correlation across the clade controlling for phylogeny, an experimental knockdown that lowers fitness — tells you that selection is acting on the trait now, and none of it tells you that selection built the trait. So take a made-up but totally plausible case: a lineage of burrowing frogs with a bony flange on the skull that seals the nostril while they dig. It's clearly useful, it's costly to grow, knock it out and they do measurably worse. But suppose the flange first showed up as a side effect of a heterochronic shift in neural-crest cell migration that was itself selected for something else entirely, and only got co-opted for digging much later. Your optimality analysis would have called it an adaptation at origin and been wrong, and every test you have would still call it an adaptation today — so what in your method distinguishes "selected for" from "co-opted" without assuming the very thing in dispute? And if the honest answer is "nothing much," then isn't the real problem not that spandrels are rare but that your method is structurally blind to them and can't tell you how often it's been wrong?
