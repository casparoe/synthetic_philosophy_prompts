For most of my career I was a quantitative geneticist in animal breeding — some thirty years estimating variance components in closed nucleus herds — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science, at first out of idle curiosity about what the numbers I used to publish actually meant, and latterly with more seriousness. In six weeks I am giving my first philosophy paper: a forty-five-minute talk with thirty minutes of questions, in a philosophy department's weekly colloquium series. I would be grateful for your help in preparing for the question period.

The thesis I am defending is that a heritability coefficient is not a measure of how genetic a trait is, nor a measure of how refractory it is to intervention, but something more like a local sensitivity coefficient: a property of a trait–population–environment triple, defined only over the distribution of environments actually realized in the population sampled, and good for extrapolation only within the support of that distribution. My argument has four steps. First, both the numerator and the denominator of the ratio are population-relative quantities: additive genetic variance depends on allele frequencies, and through genotype–environment interaction it depends on which environments are realised, while phenotypic variance depends on the spread of those environments; so two populations can differ in heritability while differing in no fact at all about how genes act in individuals. Second, and this is the point I care most about, hold the genotype distribution and the individual reaction norms fixed and shrink the environmental variance, and the ratio rises, tending towards one as the environment is homogenised — so a strikingly high heritability can be evidence of an unusually uniform environment rather than of strong genetic determination, and my own old experimental herds were, I now think, close to a limiting case of this. I illustrate with a deliberately stylised example with invented numbers: a hypothetical national cohort in which a school reform equalises inputs, the mean of a measured attainment score rises by four tenths of a standard deviation, and the heritability of that score rises from 0.42 to 0.61 over the same period, a pattern I read as the reform's signature rather than as any strengthening of genetic influence. Third, the number licenses short-run prediction of response to mass selection inside the sampled support — the breeder's equation is not an illusion — and says nothing whatsoever about the effect of an intervention that moves individuals outside that support; from which it follows that the hereditarian inference to the causes of a between-group difference and the progressive inference that a highly heritable trait is therefore fully malleable are both invalid, though for quite different reasons, and that neither side in that dispute has earned the rhetorical use it makes of the number. Fourth, my practical prescription: that estimates should be published with an explicit statement of the environmental support over which they hold, and that anyone proposing to use one to forecast a policy effect should first apply a uniform-intervention test, asking whether the policy relocates individuals to environments already represented in the sample. I also note in passing, without resolving it, that genomic-relatedness estimates capture only tagged additive variance and typically come out lower than the classical designs — a complication I expect to be pressed on.

The room will be about fifteen people. The host is an analytic philosopher of science who works on causal explanation and is a committed interventionist about causation. There is a philosopher of biology in the department who insists on the distinction between the causes of an individual's having a trait and the explanations available for variation within a population. Two quantitative and behavioural geneticists are coming over from the medical school, one of whom works with twin and adoption designs and one with genomic methods; both, I suspect, regard my talk as an outsider's belated discovery of things the field settled decades ago. Several graduate students from a science-and-technology-studies programme will attend, broadly sympathetic to the social-justice critique of behavioural genetics and suspicious of any legitimate use of the number. And two or three people will have come because they believe the talk is about genes and intelligence, and will be either hoping or fearing that it is.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, rather than by their abstract importance. For each: who raises it, and how that person would put it in their own vocabulary — and since I am new to this literature, please gloss any term of art in a sentence, and tell me how to say the same thing back in mine. Then a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and one line on where that reply is soft. Flag the objections I should simply concede rather than answer, and tell me what to concede in my first two minutes to take the heat out of the room. Finally: name the objection that has real force against my own position and that I should prepare to meet rather than hope everyone misses, and separately the one question I am privately hoping nobody asks. If the thesis as I have stated it is muddled, or if my second step is straightforwardly wrong as a claim about what variance-component estimation does, say so plainly before you begin — I would much rather hear that from you than from the medical school in front of an audience.
