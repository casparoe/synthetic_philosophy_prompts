Our library runs a philosophy and film night once a month — mostly retirees, a few undergrads from the community college, nobody with any modal metaphysics at all — and I somehow let myself get talked into leading the discussion after we screen Everything Everywhere All at Once in a couple of weeks. Relevant fact about me: I emigrated eighteen months ago, left a marriage and a job and a whole city behind, and I have this completely un-philosophical feeling that there is a version of me still back there who didn't leave, that I owe her something, and that she would definately be ashamed of me. So I am not coming at this neutrally, and I'd rather you knew that up front.

Here's the ask. I've been reading about counterpart theory versus transworld identity — the Lewis-style view that nothing is literally in more than one world, so a claim like her being a singer is made true by some similar enough counterpart elsewhere, versus the view that the very same person turns up in several worlds and modal claims are claims about that person. Fix me in a sentence or two at the start if I've garbled that, because I will be repeating it out loud to forty people.

But I don't want the film used as decoration for the debate. I want it used as a test case, and I've got a criterion I made up that I want you to try to break. Call a counterpart answerable if what it did is something you can coherently feel guilty about or proud of, as opposed to merely similar to you. On the Lewis picture resemblance does the work and guilt is just a feeling layered on top; on transworld identity there is one person, so guilt is unproblematic. My sense is that the film runs on answerability and not on resemblance at all: most of the other Evelyns are not similar to her in the respects a counterpart theorist could appeal to — a singer, a movie star — and yet the film treats every one of their lives as evidence about her, and treats her as answerable for all of them. If that's right the film isn't illustrating either theory, it's proposing a third answer to what makes a modal claim about a person true, namely responsibility rather than similarity. I want to know whether that survives contact with the reasons anyone wanted counterpart theory in the first place — the problem of one thing having incompatible properties across worlds, the fact that similarity is hopelessly context relative, all of that.

So: is my answerability thing coherent, and what would it get wrong? Which three scenes should I put in front of the group, and what does each show about which view is actually doing the work — I was going to use the rocks, the bagel, and the laundromat at the end, but you pick. Where does the film cheat, needing real identity in one scene and mere resemblance in another to get its emotional payoff, and is that a defect or just how fiction works? And finally, does the movie know something about modality that the arguments don't — my worry is that the reading collapses into be kind, be present, which is true and useless, and that I'll stand up there and say something a fourteen year old could have said without the metaphysics. Give me language I can actually say to a room where maybe two people have heard the word modal. Skip any reading list; I'm not going to follow it up.
