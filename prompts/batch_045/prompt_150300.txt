I have been asked to give a talk next month and I would rather find out now what I have not thought through than discover it in the question period.

Some background on me: I have no training in political philosophy. I worked as an actuary until I retired, I got interested in selection by lot after a long and peculiar jury service, and for the past few years I have been reading and arguing about it on my own, mostly with friends. The invitation came from a network of people who organise citizens' assemblies, and I have agreed to twenty-five minutes with about twenty minutes of questions afterwards.

The room will be roughly sixty people, and it is not a seminar. Around two thirds of them have actually staffed deliberative mini-publics: professional facilitators, the contractors who run the recruitment lotteries, and civil servants from the participation teams of city councils. There will also be three academic evaluators who study these assemblies empirically, two elected politicians who sit on a parliamentary committee on democratic modernisation, and a journalist who writes about democratic reform and is sympathetic but allergic to enthusiasm. Nearly everyone there already likes citizens' assemblies in a modest form. What they do not like is the suggestion that such a body should have binding power rather than advisory power. They are practical, cost-conscious, attentive to what actually happens in a room, and suspicious of any argument that sortition is a cure for democracy in general.

The thesis I want to defend is narrow, and I would rather it be attacked on its own terms than mistaken for the usual case:

First, I do not want to rest the argument on descriptive resemblance or on freedom from donors and campaign money. Those are real advantages and other people have made that case well. Second, the distinctive thing random selection buys is a decision-maker with no electoral horizon. Elected officials discount whatever falls outside the next contest. The usual remedies for this — constitutional entrenchment, supermajority requirements, delegation to independent agencies — all work by binding successors, and every one of them can be undone by a later majority. Third, appointed experts are weaker on this dimension than they appear: they are drawn from a narrow slice of the population and their effective time horizon is set by the prestige and career cycles of their own field. Fourth, if that is the argument, then the standard assembly design throws the advantage away. Short terms with constant rotation mean members spend their whole tenure learning and leave exactly when they become competent, so the real continuity sits with the permanent civil service. I want to propose long non-renewable terms — about eight years, with staggered overlapping cohorts — and no rotation. Fifth, scope: this justifies binding authority over a narrow class of decisions whose main consequences fall twenty years or more out, such as pension parameters, long-lived infrastructure, statutory emissions targets, and constitutional questions, and it justifies nothing at all over emergencies or short-run distributive fights. Sixth, the part I am most attached to: a mandatory implementation lag. A decision takes effect only after a fixed delay, chosen so that most of the members who voted for it will still be alive and still living in the jurisdiction when it bites. If the point is to get people to weigh the long term, make them partly bear it.

What I need from you is a preparation sheet, not a defence. Specifically:

The eight or so objections I will actually get from this particular room, ordered by how likely they are multiplied by how much damage they would do, not by their philosophical depth. I want each one phrased the way that person would phrase it — the practitioner worrying about employers releasing someone for eight years and about attrition, the civil servant worrying about what happens to an amateur body that meets for most of a decade, the evaluator asking how any of this could be measured, the politician asking who elected these people. For each objection, a reply I could give in about ninety seconds without notes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft, where it merely changes the subject, and where I should simply concede the point. Then: what I should concede myself in the first two minutes, before anyone raises it, because I would rather look like I know the weaknesses than be caught out. Which single reply has to land for the proposal to be worth anyone's time at all. And, finally, the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, along with what I should say if they do.

Two requests about form. Please do not give me a balanced survey of the pros and cons of sortition, and do not give me references or a reading list; I have no library access and I cannot chase citations. Assume the audience grants that consultative assemblies work reasonably well and is asking only whether my version should be given power.
