I'm assembling a set of short case studies for a graduate seminar on explanation in evolutionary biology, and the next one I need is the 2012 ENCODE controversy over junk DNA, read through the lens of the adaptationist programme and the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique. I don't want a reminder that ENCODE was criticized; I want a disciplined mapping that runs in both directions: what the critique says each side in that dispute was doing and should have done, which features of the episode it explains cleanly, which it misdescribes or cannot reach at all, and what the episode in turn shows about the critique.

The facts are well enough known, but I'll stipulate them so the analysis doesn't turn on a detail either of us might misremember. In 2012 the ENCODE consortium published a large set of papers reporting that roughly 80 percent of the human genome shows reproducible biochemical activity — transcription, transcription-factor binding, open chromatin, and similar marks — and the accompanying press coverage presented this as the end of junk DNA. Critics replied that the project had used a causal-role or activity notion of function rather than a selected-effect one, and pressed three arguments: the C-value paradox, i.e. that genome size varies by orders of magnitude across eukaryotes with no relation to organismal complexity, so that onions, garlic and some salamanders carry far more DNA per cell than humans do; mutational load, i.e. that if most of the genome were functional the burden of deleterious mutation would be unsustainable; and the transposon point, i.e. that much nonconserved DNA looks like decaying mobile-element copies. The rough picture that has since settled out is that only a smallish fraction of the genome — on the order of one tenth — shows signs of constraint or purifying selection, while a much larger fraction is transcribed or bound at some time in some cell type. Take all of that as given.

Here is what I want the case study to do.

First, the mapping, done strictly. What plays the role of the trait here, and what plays the role of the adaptive story? Gould and Lewontin's target was a method — atomize the organism into traits, tell a story about what each is for, treat failure to think of an alternative as confirmation — not a claim about proportions. So ask whether either side in 2012 was an adaptationist in that sense. There is an inversion worth taking seriously: the position that sounds Panglossian is the one claiming function everywhere, yet the junk-DNA position also rests on a substantive assumption about natural selection, namely that it is too weak in a population of our effective size to remove slightly deleterious DNA. Say clearly which side the critique actually indicts, if either, and whether the indictment is about their conclusions or only about their procedure.

Second, apply a partition I've been using in my teaching, and tell me whether it holds up. Sort the disputed sequence into four categories: elements maintained by selection for what they do for the organism; architectural byproducts, in the strict spandrel sense of necessary consequences of how the system is built; elements that originated as one of the first two and were later co-opted for a current use; and selfish or intragenomically favoured sequences, which are functional but functional for themselves rather than for the organism. Which of these categories can biochemical assays such as ENCODE's not discriminate between, and what evidence would discriminate them? Then the question I actually care about: is that fourth category an alternative to adaptation that Gould and Lewontin's list of alternatives omits, or is it only a spandrel under another name? If it is a genuine omission, does it matter that the framework's notion of what a trait is for quietly presupposes that the organism is the only relevant beneficiary, and does the genome undermine that presupposition in a way that organismal morphology does not?

Third, burden of proof. The critique is often read as denying that adaptedness can be the default hypothesis, so that the adaptationist owes an argument rather than the skeptic owing one. At genome scale, is the claim that a stretch of DNA is junk a default in the same sense, or is it itself a substantive claim that needs the same kind of comparative and population-genetic support the adaptationist is being asked for? Describe what a test satisfying Gould and Lewontin's own positive proposals would have looked like in 2012, and whether either side had it.

Fourth, the verdict in the direction that interests me more: case testing theory. Does the episode vindicate the spandrels critique as a diagnosis of how a research programme overreaches, or does it expose the critique as having no resources for the dispute — given that whatever resolution there has been came from conservation data and from population genetics, and not from the architectural-constraint and exaptation alternatives the critique recommended? And take up the question that Gould's own insistence on separating historical origin from current utility raises here: does that distinction dissolve part of the 2012 dispute as verbal, with both sides right about different questions, or does it deepen it?

Finally, be explicit about silence. Name at least two features of the episode that the adaptationism-versus-spandrels frame cannot reach, and say what would have to be imported — a theory of function, near-neutral theory, a levels-of-selection framework, something else — to reach them.

Assume an audience of graduate students who know the critique but have not thought about it below the level of organismal form. Write about 1,200 to 1,500 words. I will add the citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to papers and authors; and if at any point you need a fact I have not stipulated, say so and mark the inference as conditional rather than quietly filling it in.
