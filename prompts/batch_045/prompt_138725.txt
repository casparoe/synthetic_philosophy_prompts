Please take the role of a professional philosopher who has also done applied work inside institutions — someone who has helped rewrite an actual policy handbook and therefore knows the difference between a procedure that survives contact with the people who have to run it and one that gets quietly rewritten on the third case. Before you answer, I'd like you to think out loud for a while about what a relational conception of personhood is actually committing anyone to in a setting like mine, and only then produce the operational version. The translation step is where I think the real difficulty lies, so please don't jump straight to a tidy checklist; I want to see the distinctions you considered keeping and the ones you decided to throw away.

Some context. I direct the conduct and conflict-resolution office at a mid-sized residential university. After a genuinely ugly stretch of cases last year, the university senate voted to replace our adversarial hearing model with what the student-government report called a restorative response pathway, and the report justified it explicitly in terms of a relational conception of personhood of the kind discussed in African philosophy under the heading of Ubuntu: the idea that a human being is not a self-contained bearer of rights who subsequently enters into relationships, but is partly made the person they are by standing in the right kind of relationship to others, and that a wrong is therefore primarily damage to a network of relationships rather than an offense by one isolated will against another. I remember the slogan-ish version from an undergraduate course — roughly, that a person becomes a person through other people — but we now have to turn that into something my staff can run, and several of my colleagues privately think the vocabulary was borrowed to dress up what is really a budget-cutting exercise.

Here are my constraints, which I think are the ones that bite. The pathway will in the first instance be run by two student-affairs staff with no philosophy training, who have ten working days from intake to written outcome; the outcome has to be recorded on a standardized form because it is appealable to a university tribunal; legal counsel has to sign off on anything that touches our contracts with students; and a separate compliance office holds a veto wherever external rules require a formal process regardless of what we would prefer. So I need, in words those staff can follow: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each rather than a pious one; the points at which the pathway should stop with a no and hand the case back to a hearing or to the police; and the points at which it should hand the decision to a named person because the question has stopped being a question of fact or of procedure and has become a question of values. I also want you to be blunt about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. Two worries I keep circling: first, that the community will get reified, so that whoever is loudest or most senior gets to speak for it and override the least powerful person in the room; second, that some respondents will simply deny that they are in any relationship with the people they harmed or with the university at all, and I don't know whether the framework has anything to say to them or whether it just presupposes the very thing in dispute. If the honest answer is that the relational framing collapses into autonomy-plus-consequences once it is operationalized, or that what we are doing is restorative-justice boilerplate with an African word attached, I would rather be told that than handed a usable-looking procedure.

Please then run the procedure on three cases. One: a first-year who fabricated data in a lab report, where the faculty supervisor wants only a mark penalty and the student's research group says the breach was against them too. Two: a graduating senior with a documented pattern of harassment toward one person, where that person wants no contact, no process, and no record, and my office would normally respect that completely. Three: a long stretch of low-level anonymous harassment in a residence hall where no individual complainant is identifiable but the hall's common life has more or less collapsed. For each, tell me where the procedure terminates and what it hands back. I suspect at least one of these will come out against my office's current instincts — particularly the second, where my staff's view is that the complainant's wishes settle it — and if so I want the reasoning rather than a softening of the conclusion. Finally, say plainly what this procedure cannot do: which cases it should refuse to take, what it will systematically get wrong, and what my office should stop pretending it is achieving.
