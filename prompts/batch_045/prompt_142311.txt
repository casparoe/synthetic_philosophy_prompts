Construct a case in which accuracy-first epistemology equipped with the quadratic (Brier) scoring rule and accuracy-first epistemology equipped with the logarithmic scoring rule tell the same agent, with the same evidence, to adopt different credences, and do so in a specified direction and by enough to change what the agent does.

The case is for a paper I am writing on whether accuracy-first epistemology can deliver determinate rational requirements without an independent argument for a privileged scoring rule. So the case has to be one in which a realistic agent, holding a fixed body of evidence, is genuinely pulled two ways by the choice of rule, and in which the pull shows up in conduct rather than in a fourth decimal place.

Specifications the case must meet.

1. One agent, one partition of three mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive hypotheses H1, H2, H3, where H3 is the rare but high-stakes cell: the diagnosis that must not be missed, the failure mode that is catastrophic if it occurs, the low-probability event whose occurrence triggers a costly protocol.

2. The evidence is ambiguous in a way a reader can see. It does not single out one chance distribution over the partition; it narrows things to a set C of distributions all compatible with the evidence. Motivate C from the story: two defensible reference classes, a test whose operating characteristics are known only within a range, two guidelines that disagree about the base rate, and so on. Present C as the convex hull of two explicitly specified distributions, so that it is a one-parameter family and every step can be checked by hand.

3. The agent cannot escape by being imprecise. The setting requires a single number on a form: a risk score, a probability that feeds a protocol. The agent must adopt one precise credence.

4. Both theories handle the ambiguity by the same rule: the agent should adopt the credence that minimises worst-case expected inaccuracy over C, that is, maximise minimum expected accuracy. Hold this fixed across the two theories. The scoring rule is the only variable. If you think a different selection rule for action under ambiguity is more defensible, fix that rule and use it for both theories, but say in one sentence which you use and why.

The verdicts I need.

Let b be the credence the Brier rule recommends and l the credence the logarithmic rule recommends. I need l(H3) greater than b(H3), by a margin that flips a decision. Build into the scenario a threshold on the credence in H3 at which the costly protocol is triggered, a rule-out threshold, a monitoring trigger, a recall decision, whatever fits the setting. Require b(H3) to fall at least 1.5 percentage points below that threshold and l(H3) at least 1.5 percentage points above it, and require l(H3) to be at least 1.5 times b(H3). The two theories must disagree about whether to act.

My hunch is that the logarithmic rule is the one that keeps the rare hypothesis above the threshold, since its penalty is unbounded as a credence goes to zero and it should therefore be more reluctant to let the credence in H3 get small. But the point of the exercise is to derive this rather than assume it. If in the case you build the divergence runs the other way, say so, explain why, and rebuild. And if your derivation keeps pushing one of the two recommendations to an endpoint of C, treat that as a sign the set is badly shaped for the purpose and reshape it.

Constraints.

Both rules are strictly proper, and the disagreement must not arise from one of them failing to be proper or from your having used an improper variant. Everything but the scoring rule is held fixed: same agent, same partition, same evidence set C, same rule for acting under ambiguity, same threshold. The divergence must fall out of the mathematics of expected inaccuracy rather than being imported: no costs of error inside the accuracy calculation, no decision-theoretic stakes, no penalty for imprecision, no value-of-inquiry considerations. Stakes enter only through the threshold, after the credences are fixed. Neither recommended credence may assign 0 or 1 to any cell. The numbers should be ones a real protocol could use, so no one-in-a-million events. The ranking of the three hypotheses by credence should be the same at b and at l: the disagreement is about magnitude, not about which hypothesis is most likely. The scenario itself is one or two paragraphs, realistic, set somewhere like a clinic, a laboratory, an engineering failure review, or a forensic unit, and contains no demons, no urns, and no prediction markets.

Deliverables, in this order.

(a) The scenario, with the numbers in it motivated by the story rather than reverse-engineered from the answer. A reader should be able to see why the evidence pins down C and not some other set, and why the threshold sits where it does.

(b) The derivation. Write expected inaccuracy under each rule as a function of the agent's credence c and of the chance hypothesis Q in C. State the maximin condition for each rule. Solve for b and l on your one-parameter family, showing the first-order condition and checking second-order or boundary behaviour. Report both credences as explicit numbers to three decimal places.

(c) A cross-check that does not presuppose your solutions are optimal. Evaluate worst-case expected Brier inaccuracy at b and at l, and worst-case expected logarithmic inaccuracy at l and at b, and confirm that the ordering is what it should be.

(d) An account of which feature of the case is doing the work. Characterise, in one sentence each, which member of C each rule selects and what is distinctive about that member. I take it the relevant difference between the rules concerns how they treat credences that are small but not zero, but I want that to fall out of the arithmetic rather than be asserted.

(e) Remove the feature and show the divergence collapses. At a minimum, collapse H2 and H3 into a single catch-all cell and rerun the derivation on the resulting two-cell set, showing whether the two rules now select the same credence and explaining why either way. Optionally, say what happens when C is a singleton, or when the logarithmic rule is replaced by a bounded strictly proper rule.

(f) The attack. How would someone who holds that accuracy-first epistemology does yield determinate requirements respond to your case? Address at least three replies: that one of the two rules is the correct one and the choice is therefore not arbitrary; that ambiguous evidence should issue in imprecise credence rather than in a maximin point; and that a divergence of this size is an artefact of an unrealistic evidence set. Say which reply you find strongest and why.

(g) One paragraph on what would have counted as cheating in this exercise, and on why your case is not cheating.
