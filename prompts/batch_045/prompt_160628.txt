I run a philosophy club at my school — roughly thirteen students between sixteen and eighteen, one fifty-minute lunch period, a classroom with a projector and a pile of index cards, and no way to use money or grades as stakes. A while ago I read a short piece arguing that no interesting condition of your own mind is *luminous*: that is, there is no condition C, apart from the trivial ones that always or never hold, such that whenever C holds for you, you are in a position to know that it holds. Applied to knowledge itself, this is supposed to show that the KK principle fails — that one can know that p without being in a position to know that one knows that p. What convinced me on the page was not the conclusion but the machinery behind it: the way a demand for a margin of error, applied across a long series of cases in which neighbouring cases are indiscriminable to the subject at the time, propagates backwards and eats away at every case in the series, so that no case can be both a case of knowledge and a case in which one is in a position to know that one knows. I have not been able to get anyone in the club to feel the force of that, because when I explain it, it just collapses into the platitude that people are sometimes unsure — which nobody has ever denied.

My own attempt failed in an instructive way. I played a series of tones at very slowly increasing volume and asked students to raise a hand the moment they could hear one; then I ran the same series again and asked them to raise a hand only when they were *sure* they could hear one. The two rounds came out nearly identical, everyone started watching the person next to them and copying, and the student with the best hearing simply became the group's oracle. So I need a properly designed activity instead: a game or structured exercise with explicit rules, materials, a scoring scheme worth caring about without money, and a minute-by-minute timeline, in which the students generate the regress themselves. My hope is that the rules force them to demand some margin of safety before committing — say, by making a confident-but-wrong answer much worse than an abstention, and an abstention somewhat worse than a correct answer — that this demand visibly propagates through a chain or sequence of cases, and that they end up discovering on their own that any chain of confident judgement has to have a first link, and that the first link is exactly where the difficulty lives. I would also like a phase in which they try to repair the scheme by adding a second-order requirement (only commit when you can tell that you can tell) and find that the repair makes things worse rather than better, since that seems to me to be the part that separates this view from ordinary caution about our own fallibility.

Please also give me the debrief: how to map particular moments in the activity onto particular pieces of the argument — the role of indiscriminable neighbouring cases, the reliability or safety requirement, the step from "I know" to "I am in a position to know that I know," and the difference between that step and mere modesty or ordinary doubt about the external world — without lecturing beforehand or naming the principle until afterwards. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including the copying problem I already hit, the students who abstain out of boredom rather than caution, and the one participant whose perceptual acuity distorts the whole series. Finally, be frank about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to state myself — I suspect it is the distinction between knowing something and being in a position to know it, and also the fact that in the real argument the safety requirement is a claim about knowledge rather than a scoring rule I have imposed, so I would like that limitation spelled out rather than papered over.
