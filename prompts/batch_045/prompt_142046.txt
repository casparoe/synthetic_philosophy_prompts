I'm giving a 40-minute work-in-progress talk next thursday to a joint philosophy-and-sleep-lab group — it's a philosophy of mind seminar that's been opened up to the sleep researchers upstairs. The audience is roughly: one senior Wittgensteinian who is basically a Malcolm loyalist (thinks the whole "are dreams experiences" question is a pseudo-question that dissolves once you see how dream-talk is learned), two sleep-lab people who run dream-report studies and think REM and its physiology settle things, a clinical psychologist who treats nightmare disorder in PTSD patients, and a handful of grad students who've read the cassette-theory paper and are pretty sure Dennett already ended this debate.

My thesis: dreams are real experiences, but an experience doesn't have to be fully determinate to be there. I'm arguing for a "sketch model" — the phenomenal character of a dream can be determinable without being determinate, so the report you give when you wake up doesn't discover a finished dream and doesn't fabricate one either; it fills in something that was already there and was genuinely open. My two moves: (1) an analogy with perception where you see that something moved without seeing exactly how it moved, and (2) the charge that both Malcolm and Dennett are running a hidden determinacy requirement — Malcolm because he thinks there being something it was like needs a criterion, Dennett because his "in principle no experiment could tell" argument only bites if a real experience has to already have determinate content. I also use a made-up case I call the bilingual dream: same sleeper, same sleep stage, woken by an English-speaking experimenter reports a dream containing English, woken by a Japanese-speaking one reports Japanese, and I want to say both reports are true of the same open experience.
