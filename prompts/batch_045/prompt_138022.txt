I'm in the third year of a PhD in psycholinguistics, and my lab group has been having the same argument about linguistic relativity for two years without anyone visibly moving. Nobody in the room defends the crude version — that the grammar or lexicon of your language fixes what you are capable of thinking — and nobody denies the equally boring opposite, that language is part of the mind and so of course it shows up somewhere in cognition. Everything is fought over the middle: a monolingual sample of speakers of language A differs from a matched sample of speakers of language B on some task that is not itself linguistic — say, noticing a boundary in a continuum that English labels with one basic term and the other language splits into two basic terms, or the speed and direction of an inference about how an event's causal role was assigned after reading a description in which the agent was backgrounded. My colleague says these effects are either strategy artifacts (the task lets you silently name or categorize to yourself, so you are measuring what is easy to encode, not what is represented), or they are real but merely performance-level (attention, salience, memory, response bias) rather than differences in conceptual structure, or they are real and interesting but not specifically *linguistic*, because every such sample also differs in subsistence practices, schooling, literacy habits, and visual environment, and no design in the literature that I can reconstruct actually pulls the two apart. I keep switching sides depending on the week, and I've started to suspect that the argument isn't going anywhere because of what kind of argument it is rather than because of the evidence.

My working hypothesis is that there are three debates under one name, and that the field changed the subject without noticing. The mid-century formulation looks to me like a claim about conceptual schemes: whole ways of carving up experience, norms of what counts as a fact or an explanation, the sense in which speakers of different languages might be said to inhabit different worlds. That claim is the one people find electrifying, and it is also the one that is hardest to make answerable to a reaction-time or forced-choice design. The current empirical literature mostly tests something much more modest: whether a habit of encoding that a language forces on you, several thousand times a year, tunes attention, categorization, or recall in ways that spill into tasks with no words in them. Those look like different questions, and I suspect each side is silently reading the other's results as evidence about its own question — the enthusiast takes a 40ms facilitation as a fragment of the big claim, the skeptic rejects the big claim and therefore treats the 40ms as noise or artifact. On top of that I think there is a genuinely hidden methodological question doing most of the work: whether any task can be built that is forced to be non-verbal in the way the skeptic needs. I've been sketching one and calling it the encoding-decoupling test — the discrimination or inference task is run under articulatory suppression, with a response format that requires no categorization the language lexicalizes and no same/different judgment along the disputed dimension, plus a transfer condition where the stimulus set is one that no language in the sample has a compact label for; if the effect survives, the strategy-artifact story is gone, and if it disappears, the enthusiast can still say the effect was real but only in the ecology where language is actually available. I genuinely can't tell whether this test would settle anything or whether it just relocates the same fight one level down, and I also can't tell whether my colleague's "it isn't specifically linguistic" objection is an empirical claim about confounds, a demand that no study could meet, or a philosophical view about what "linguistic" has to mean.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey — please don't give me a reading list or a tour of the classic studies, I've read them and the tour is what keeps restarting the argument. Using what each side actually says, tell me what kind of disagreement this is: verbal (a fight over "thought", "concept", "perception"), a disagreement about which tasks count as data, a hidden empirical or design question in disguise, bedrock starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, or two questions under one name as I've guessed. If my three-debates hypothesis is right, say which of the three is live and what the status of the other two is; if it's wrong, say where it misdescribes the literature. Then tell me what would count as progress given the diagnosis you land on — a distinction, a design, a formal or computational result, a change of question — including an honest assessment of whether my encoding-decoupling test is the right experiment, the wrong experiment, or merely a new place to have the same argument, and whether there is any outcome that would make my colleague and me stop. And if you think no progress should be expected, I'd rather hear that with the reason than be given a conciliatory summary.
