I teach a final-year option in historiography and the philosophy of history. My students have read Ranke's slogan about showing the past as it really happened (wie es eigentlich gewesen), Carr's What is History? (1961) with its distinction between the "facts of the past" and the "historical facts", and Hayden White on emplotment and the historical text as a literary artefact. I need one ninety-minute exam essay question on historical objectivity that separates students who have genuinely worked through the problem from students who have learned the labels — objectivist, reconstructionist, constructivist, narrativist — and can narrate the dialectic: Ranke says the past is recovered by critical method, Carr replies that the historian selects and that facts of the past become historical facts only when they are put to work, White radicalises this into the claim that the past has no narrative form and that emplotment is imposed, and then someone proposes a middle way. A question that can be answered by telling that story is useless to me.

Build the question around the following invented case, which you may rename but whose structure you should preserve:

In 2148 the "Meridian Trace" is opened: a complete and authenticated record of the mismanaged withdrawal of a garrison and its civilian dependants from the city of Meridian in 2031 — every message, draft, deleted file, financial entry, biometric and satellite log, with metadata and unbroken chains of custody. Nothing is lost, nothing forged, no particular fact disputed. Call this trace-completeness. Two historians use it. One writes The Meridian Decision, a tragedy: a commander destroyed by obligations that could not all be honoured. The other writes Meridian: A Farce of Logistics: improvisation, vanity and bungling. They agree on every particular fact and deny nothing the other asserts at the level of particular fact. A third historian, impatient with both, publishes the paratactic chronology: everything in the trace, in date order, no selection, no causal claims, no theme. Nobody regards it as history — but it is the only work that adds nothing to the trace. A fourth observes that the two rivals share a framework nobody argues about: both take a nation-state's decision as the unit of agency and a single week in March 2031 as the frame, so neither considers an account whose subject is the supply network or whose period is the preceding decade.

The question should force candidates to locate the disagreement rather than label it, and to confront the asymmetry that evidence excludes some emplotments while determining none — which raises whether a residue that constrains without determining is enough to distinguish history from fiction, and, if it is, in what the remaining objectivity consists.

Please supply:

1. The question as students will see it, in about 200 words, worded so that no candidate can infer which answer the examiner favours, and so that it is answerable from the concepts alone — closed book, so no marks anywhere for editions, page numbers, quotation of the German, or further reading. If you want the ambiguity of eigentlich raised, give it in English paraphrase as a parenthetical aside, not as a marked requirement.

2. A model answer of 800–900 words that takes and defends a position rather than surveying options. It must at least: distinguish three or more candidate construals of "objective" (correspondence to the trace; impartiality of the historian; intersubjective reproducibility of procedure; Carr's relational objectivity across past, present and future) and show that the verdict on the Meridian case differs by construal; say whether the tragedy/farce disagreement is truth-apt and, if so, what would settle it; and name one thing a defender of historical objectivity must give up and one thing a White-style sceptic must give up for the dispute to be more than verbal.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 built from five must-hit moves at four marks each. For each move give the specific thing a first-class answer does, and the characteristic way a competent but mediocre answer fails it — the failure mode, not just a lower mark.
