I have spent twenty-five years making a living from invented people who are trusted as if they were real, which I assume is why the chair of our Committee on Research with Human Participants asked me, the one non-scientist on it, to draft this month's decision memo. She wants prose the investigators can actually answer, and something the committee could publish, since we post short public rationales for our determinations.

The protocol is called Project Overhear and comes from a team of five in political communication. They want to know whether sustained exposure to counter-attitudinal evidence, delivered by a trusted insider, changes what members of a closed online community believe. The target population is parents of children with a rare pediatric neuroimmune condition. Two matched, members-only groups on a large social platform were selected. In each, a substantial subgroup holds that the condition was triggered by routine childhood vaccination, and treats hospital, journal, and public-health sources as captured by industry. The unit of intervention is eight fabricated persona accounts per community: composite photographs, biographies, posting histories, and, crucially, time. The personas will be cultivated over seven months as ordinary members, answering questions, exchanging private messages, being present through hospitalizations. Only then do they begin to introduce contrary evidence in the first person. A second arm posts identical content from a visibly new, outsider account. Outcomes are measured by coded analysis of scraped posts and messages; there is no direct contact with subjects and no individual debriefing. The endgame is a single disclosure post to each group at the conclusion, a plain-language report to the moderators, and deletion of the personas.

Two things about these communities matter and are not in dispute among us. First, much of their hostility to institutional medicine is warranted: the condition is poorly characterised, and a large share of members spent years being told their child's illness was psychological before anyone believed them. Second, the groups are not only epistemic units. They are where parents go at three in the morning, and membership carries real social goods.

The committee has already cleared the purely observational arm as minimal risk. The dispute is the persona arm and the disclosure plan, and we have split three ways: approve as proposed, since closed online groups are quasi-public and this is continuous with decades of unobtrusive field research; decline outright, since the subjects are vulnerable, the relationships are fabricated over months, consent is impossible, and the disclosure will be read by many members as fresh confirmation that they are being surveilled; or approve a reduced design that drops the persona arm or relocates it to a non-clinical community, perhaps with moderator gatekeeping consent.

The philosophical distinction I want the memo to work with, in my own words, is the one in the recent epistemology literature between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. A bubble is a structure of omission: you simply never encounter the other side, and supplying the missing material can fix it. An echo chamber is a structure of exclusion: the community maintains standing machinery that discredits outside sources in advance, so exposure does not cure the condition and may reinforce it, and leaving costs the member belonging and credibility. These are ideal types; a real community may be partly both, and a discrediting mechanism can be wildly over-general without being groundless.

I have a second, home-made device I would like tested rather than deferred to. It sorts deception in research by what it recruits: deception that recruits a subject's beliefs, deception that recruits a subject's trust in a person, and deception that recruits a subject's standing in a shared story, by inserting a fabricated character into the narrative a community tells about itself. My suspicion is that the third is not merely a more severe version of the first, that debriefing does not repair it but is rather the moment the injury becomes legible, and that this tells against the persona arm specifically rather than against deception in research generally. I would rather be argued out of this than have it flattered.

Please draft the memo as follows. Bottom line up front: approve as proposed, approve with named modifications, or decline. Then the reasoning, including what the design could and could not establish even if it succeeded, given that a shift in expressed belief cannot by itself distinguish a burst bubble from a successfully manipulated chamber, and given that a successful manipulation is itself a technique that would be published. Give the strongest version of whichever position you reject, not a strawman. If you recommend conditions, specify who does what by when. Address the disclosure problem on its own terms, including the risk that a terminal post triggers a hunt for plants among genuine members. Close with two paragraphs suitable for the committee's public page. Roughly twelve hundred words, addressed to us but written so the investigators can respond to it.
