I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and once a month I run a kind of "ideas lab" for a mixed crowd — reporters, a couple of editors, friends of friends, usually 15 to 25 people, almost none of whom have studied philosophy and some of whom show up ten minutes late. Next month I've got 70 minutes and I want to do Parfit's hitchhiker: the driver gives you a lift only if he thinks you'll pay once you're in town, but once you're in town paying costs you and does you no good, so the person who does better is the one disposed to do the thing that's pointless at the time of doing it.

Design me something actually runnable for that room. Constraints: no real money (I can get a couple bottles of wine as prizes), minimal printing — index cards, envelopes, whatever's lying around. Nobody should hear "precommitment", "rational irrationality", or "expected utility" until afterwards. And the bit I keep getting stuck on: I don't want them to be able to solve it by signing a contract, promising out loud, paying a third party to enforce it, or otherwise buying a commitment device. They should run into the fact that what helps them is being a certain kind of person, not performing a certain action.

Second wrinkle, and this is the one I really want handled: in every version I sketch, players work out over repeat rounds that paying is just worth it for reputation — which is a different lesson than the one I'm after. So arrange the rounds so that reputation runs out while the case doesn't (say, a final round that everyone knows in advance is anonymous and one-shot). If you think that can't be done with a room like mine in 70 minutes, say so and tell me what to do instead.

Then: a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the argument; what will predictably go wrong (I'm expecting at least one person who's read a bit of game theory to announce the equilibrium out loud in round one); and an honest note on which feature of the idea the game can't produce and I'd just have to tell them.

Last thing: flag where your own design is most likely to fail — what you'd see in the room that would convince you it taught the wrong lesson. Under 600 words, terse bulleted rules are fine.
