Our setup, which is idiosyncratic in three ways you should take into account rather than smooth over:

1. Our score is used to allocate a fixed number of places (about 4,000) in a subsidised bridging programme. We do not report points; we report seven bands, and places within a band are allocated by lottery. The score is therefore, for us, a coarse ordering device and not an estimate anyone is shown.
2. After a scandal, our board adopted a hold-harmless ratchet: under a revised scoring procedure, no individual's band may fall by more than one band relative to the old procedure, and no recognised group's mean band may fall at all. This is treated internally as a fairness requirement, and I have never been sure whether that is right.
3. The outcome variable the score is trained to predict — whether a candidate "succeeds" in the programme — is recorded by instructors who can see the band the candidate came in on.

Here is the list of claims I want translated, one by one, into the vocabulary I actually use (true and observed score, measurement error, item characteristic curves, differential item functioning and measurement invariance, linking and equating, cut scores and standard setting, classification consistency, sensitivity and specificity, base rates, predictive validity, criterion contamination, consequential validity, adverse impact):

(a) A score is calibrated within a group when, among people in that group who receive a given score, the actual rate of the outcome matches the score.
(b) A score has equal error rates across groups when the false-positive and false-negative rates are the same in each group; and, separately, when a given score carries the same positive predictive value in each group.
(c) The impossibility result: when the underlying outcome rates differ between groups, a score that is not perfectly accurate cannot have all of these properties at once.
(d) The individual-level requirement, rather than the group-level one: people who are alike in the relevant respects should be treated alike.
