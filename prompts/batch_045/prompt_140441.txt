I spent thirty-five years as a sensory neurophysiologist — a good decade of it recording from nociceptive afferents — and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind, slowly and with more appetite than skill. I now record a small interview series in which I put philosophers to the questions a working scientist would put to them; my listeners are mostly laboratory scientists and clinicians who read no philosophy at all, so every question has to be followable by them without losing its technical bite.

My next guest has written a recent book defending an imperative theory of pain: the claim, roughly, that to be in pain is not to represent damage but to be commanded, in a specifically bodily register, to stop, to withdraw, to protect the affected part — and that what is bad about pain is the issuing of that command, rather than any sensory or representational property of the experience. Her principal empirical witness is pain asymbolia: patients who, typically after damage to insular or prefrontal tissue, still localise a noxious stimulus and scale its intensity, and still withdraw from it, but report that it no longer bothers them.

Please draft me ten to twelve questions, in an order that progressively tightens the screws, none of which lets her simply restate the view, and each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer I think she will actually give. I have put my guesses at her escape routes in brackets; I want the follow-ups to close those routes off rather than to repeat them. Please also add, in a line or two after each follow-up, what a satisfactory answer would have to accomplish, so that I can judge in the moment whether she has answered or deflected. Avoid anything that would require her to cite or name authors, and avoid the usual opening softballs.

The ground I want covered:

1. The interpretive fork. Asymbolia seems to admit at least three readings: the affective component is genuinely absent; the affect is intact but the subject's caring or desire-like response to it is gone; or nothing experiential changed at all and what is damaged is the machinery of evaluative self-report. Ask what non-verbal evidence could discriminate these. Concretely, I want the question to name measurements I could have run in my old lab — electrodermal and cardiovascular responses to a fixed noxious stimulus, escape or avoidance responding under an operant schedule, conditioned place avoidance, withdrawal thresholds — and to ask what pattern of results would tell for her view and what pattern against it. [She will very likely say that first-person report is authoritative here and that I am trying to substitute a third-person criterion for the thing itself.]

2. The mirror-image case. Ask her about a hypothetical patient with the reverse dissociation — no sensory-discriminative content at all: cannot localise the stimulus, cannot scale its intensity, cannot tell a pinprick from a burn — but who is wretched, wants nothing so much as for it to stop, and will work hard to escape it. If imperative content is what makes a state a pain, this subject has pain with no sensation; if the command needs a content, such as protect *this*, where does the content come from? [Expect her to say the case is not genuinely possible, because the command is individuated by the body part or by the sensorimotor situation.] The follow-up should press the asymmetry: why is dissociation in one direction a discovery and in the other a conceptual impossibility?

3. Graded cases. Hypnotic and placebo analgesia appear to pull apart ratings of sensory intensity and ratings of unpleasantness, with unpleasantness falling substantially while intensity is largely preserved. If that is a partial, suggestion-induced analogue of asymbolia, then badness is a graded quantity tracking something we can modulate by dose and by suggestion. Ask whether she wants to say those subjects' pains are literally less bad — which appears to make the badness of pain hostage to whatever we can talk or drug ourselves out of — or that something else in the subject changed. [Expect her to distinguish the phenomenal badness of the state from the subject's caring about it.]

4. The obedience problem. Asymbolic patients still withdraw; on her view the command is issued and obeyed. Ask what is missing such that the state is no longer bad — a second-order desire that the command be satisfied, a global motivational condition, something else — and, if the missing thing is an attitude of the subject's, why the view is not a desire or attitude theory wearing imperative vocabulary.

5. Scope. Nausea, itch, the air hunger of respiratory distress, grief. Ask whether the imperative structure generalises to these, and then either why pain is not the special case philosophers take it to be, or what precisely sets pain apart. [Expect her to say the others are derivative, or that they have no genuine imperative content.]

6. Falsification. Ask what result, in a laboratory like the one I used to run, would make her abandon the theory, and press for a concrete design rather than a promissory note.

7. A short closing question, if it can be made to follow from the rest: if the commanding layer is detachable from the sensory one, does her view give us any reason to think detaching it would be a good thing — and is she prepared to say that a life of asymbolic pain would be missing nothing of moral importance?
