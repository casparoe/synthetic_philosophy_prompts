Hi. I'm a philosophy professor — I work mostly in philosophy of science, but Lakatos has never really been my area — and god knows why, I've ended up chairing my university's internal Strategic Research Fund. We meet three times a year, a dozen or so continuing programmes in front of us each time, and for each one we have to say: renew for three more years, renew with conditions, merge it into something else, or wind it down. Real money, real labs, and people I have to pass in the corridor afterwards. The panel is me, two scientists from completely unrelated fields, somebody from finance, and a dean who can override us for strategic reasons but has to read our one-page rationale before doing so. Per programme we get a four-page self-report, two external review letters, and maybe twenty minutes of discussion. So: no time, no shared expertise, and a hard requirement that the rationale be readable by someone who has never opened a philosophy of science book in their life.

What I want is Lakatos's progressive-versus-degenerating problem shift turned into an instrument my panel can actually run. I know the vocabulary — hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic, novel facts, the difference between predicting something and accommodating it after the fact — so please don't spend the answer explaining the theory back to me; the whole difficulty is the translation. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one given only the material we actually have, where the procedure stops and returns a no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Write the checklist itself in plain language — the panel will not know what a protective belt is, so keep the jargon in brackets or drop it — and be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

Three things I take to be load-bearing in the original and don't want smoothed away in the simplification: first, that a degenerating stretch isn't by itself grounds to eliminate a programme, because on this view you need a rival that does everything the old one does plus something more; second, that a novel fact has to be novel in the right sense, meaning the programme pointed at it before anyone looked rather than absorbed it afterwards; third, that the verdict is retrospective by nature — we are using it to decide about the next three years, which is a substitution, and I'd rather that be flagged in the instrument than quietly hidden.

Here are the cases I need it to survive. One: identifying the hard core. Proponents will call whatever is convenient core or belt depending on the question, so my provisional rule is to infer it from what they have actually refused to give up when pressed — a kind of revealed preference — rather than from what they say it is. Does that survive contact with the theory, and what do we do with a group that says it has no hard core at all, that it just follows the data? Two: counting. I've been toying with a belt-growth check — tally the auxiliary hypotheses and ad hoc adjustments added in the last cycle against the independently corroborated novel predictions the programme produced, and treat parity (one new adjustment per one new fact) as a warning sign. Say what the framework actually commits us to about parity, and where it is silent. Three: programmes that don't predict anything. Some of what we fund produces instruments, datasets, taxonomies, methods — outputs that aren't novel facts in the relevant sense. Does the distinction apply to them at all, and if not what's the least-bad surrogate? Four: gaming. Proponents retro-describe accommodated results as predicted, and our external reviewers often don't know the field's recent history well enough to catch it. What in a four-page self-report would actually discriminate? Five: young programmes with no track record, where the honest answer might just be that the procedure can't speak yet.

Then run it on three from my actual pile. The Computational Toxicology Platform: eight years, high output, and every mismatch with the wet-lab results has been absorbed by adding a correction term; the publications are mostly confirmations; the two reviewers split. The Urban Microclimate group: three years old, no novel predictions yet, an explicit list of what it intends to explain, and a core assumption nobody has tried to break. And the one I'm dreading, Sleep and Metabolic Regulation: fifteen years, degenerating by any honest count as far as I can tell, and no rival programme anywhere that does what it does plus more — the alternative to funding it is that the university simply leaves the area. Run the procedure honestly on that last one even if it tells us to keep paying for it. I suspect that's where the theory goes, but I want to know whether the instrument says so or whether that's just my own reluctance talking.

Finally: close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and tell me how confident you are in each of the load-bearing claims you've made about what the framework commits us to, singling out the one you're least sure of.
