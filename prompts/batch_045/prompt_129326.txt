I want to practise arguing against a defender of liberty as non-domination — the neo-Roman or republican view that freedom is a status of not being subject to another's arbitrary power, rather than merely the absence of interference — and I would like you to play that defender.

Some ground rules. You are a convinced neo-Roman republican, arguing in your own voice: I want your reasons rather than your reading list, so make the case directly and do not lean on the names of contemporary authorities. You hold, and may help yourself to, the following: freedom is a status a person has or lacks, not a count of open options; a power over others is non-arbitrary only where it is constrained to track the avowable interests of those who live under it and where those persons can call it to account; non-domination admits of degrees, and law that binds everyone equally and is answerable to everyone does not thereby dominate; and you are emphatically not a positive libertarian — you deny that freedom consists in self-mastery, in having one's real or rational will satisfied, or in the exercise of collective sovereignty. Concede small points when they genuinely follow, but never concede either that non-domination is simply non-interference made secure, or that it is positive liberty in borrowed clothes. Those are the two outcomes I intend to force on you, and wriggling out of the dilemma without answering it counts as losing. Keep each reply to roughly 250 words, in character, until I say stop.

I will argue as a negative libertarian: freedom is the absence of interference and coercive threat by others, and I am happy to pay for its stability, since what matters is how much of it you can expect rather than where it comes from. My opening case is a small, well-governed jurisdiction I will call Merrow. Every adult resident is assigned a Custodian whose decision procedure is fixed for the resident's lifetime and alterable by no one — not by the Custodian, not by officials, not by the resident. The Custodian may intervene only where it establishes, by published and independently audited reasoning, that intervening serves the resident's own interests; over a century no Custodian has been shown to have intervened on any other basis, and its judgements about a resident's good have proven more reliable than the resident's own. Residents may submit arguments at any time and the Custodian must read them and reply in writing; it need not change anything, and in practice never has. Residents know all of this, feel no fear, and neither defer, flatter, nor self-censor. If some further feature of Merrow matters to your answer and I have not specified it, stipulate it yourself in the way least convenient to you and say that you have done so.

Here is the challenge. Merrow's residents enjoy security of non-interference better than any actual republic's, they are heard, and they are governed only by reasons that track their own good; so if you call them unfree you owe me an account of the residue that is not just anxiety in fancy dress. And the residue seems to be either a probability — which I have already bought — or a capacity to make a difference to how the power over you is exercised, which is self-mastery. So: are the residents of Merrow unfree? If yes, name the remaining badness precisely and say which horn you take. If no, explain why a republican should be untroubled by an arrangement that every friend of liberty would reject.
