Solomonoff induction is the one complete and fully general theory of prediction that we have. The idea is to treat every possible hypothesis about where your data come from as a computer program that prints out that data. Solomonoff's prior gives each program a probability of two raised to the power of minus its length in bits, so a hypothesis that is twice as long is exponentially less probable — which is just Occam's razor finally written down as mathematics. You then condition on the data you have actually seen and average the predictions of every program still consistent with that data, weighted by the prior. The result is a single universal predictor, and Solomonoff proved two things about it. First, convergence: if the data are generated by any computable process whatsoever, the predictor's probabilities converge to the true ones, so it eventually learns the truth no matter what the world turns out to be like. Second, optimality: no other predictor can beat it, on any data, and the total number of mistakes it makes over its entire lifetime is bounded by a constant that depends only on how complex the true hypothesis is and not on how much data it has seen — so it never pays a penalty for being completely general. The one catch is that running it would require simulating every possible program, which takes infinite time, so the method is incomputable. But that is an engineering problem rather than a defect in the theory: you approximate it. And here is the practical upshot. Since two-to-the-minus-length just is a code length, the whole framework is equivalent to data compression: the Kolmogorov complexity of your data is literally the length of the shortest file a compressor can produce, and the best compressor is therefore the best predictor. That is exactly why large language models, which are trained to compress text as efficiently as possible, are the practical realization of Solomonoff's dream, and why scaling them up and training them on more data is a direct route to the optimal predictor.
