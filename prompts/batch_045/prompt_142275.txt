I teach an upper-level seminar on welfare economics and social choice — 14 students, one 80-minute slot, no real money allowed — and I want an activity that makes them feel how much is smuggled in by interpersonal utility comparisons. No lecture up front if you can manage it.

My half-formed idea is to build it around the zero point rather than the usual "can we compare at all?" framing. Give each group the same three people with complete intrapersonal utility numbers over money, but with each person's scale on an arbitrary origin and in arbitrary units. Then have them allocate a fixed budget three times: once with the numbers exactly as handed to them, once after they have to commit in writing to a rule for where each person's zero goes and what counts as one unit, and once on a second case where two defensible anchoring rules — zero at the worst outcome in the choice set versus zero at some shared external baseline, for instance — push in opposite directions. The thing I want them to walk into is that "add up the utilities" and "weight the worse off more" stop being rival policies once the anchor is up for grabs: under one anchor the sum rule mimicks the priority rule and under another it doesn't, so the real moral choice is sitting in a parameter nobody argues about.

Turn that into something runnable: the handout content with actual numbers (including a case where the flip survives small nudges to the figures, so it can't be waved off as contrived), a seperate card for a fourth person who reports every outcome as equally middling, the rules, the group roles, and a timeline with minutes. Then a debrief that maps each moment onto the distinction it's meant to land — intrapersonal cardinality versus interpersonal comparability, level comparisons versus unit comparisons, and the anchor doing hidden work — and that's honest about what the game cannot show them, whether there's any privileged scale to be found and the axiom-and-theorem side of it, so I know what I have to say in my own voice afterwards. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm expecting someone to say "just ask them to rate their happiness from 1 to 10", and someone else to refuse to compare at all.

End with a single last line beginning "Bottom line:" that says in one sentence what the activity makes them do.
