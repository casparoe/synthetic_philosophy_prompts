We're doing a reading group this term — six of us, one chapter of popular philosphy a week, nobody in it is a philosopher (I do data engineering, and one guy took a decision-theory elective years ago and won't shut up about it). Last week we read a chapter on Pascal's wager from a book I won't name, by someone who does a lot of public-facing atheist writing, and it's been bugging me since. Here's roughly how the chapter sums it up — I'm paraphrasing from memory, so don't hold me to the exact wording:

"Pascal's wager is basically the first expected-utility calculation in the history of ideas. Reason can't tell you whether God exists, but you can't sit this one out: refusing to choose is itself a choice, so you're already gambling. If God exists and you bet on him you gain an infinity of happiness; if he exists and you bet against, you lose that same infinity; if he doesn't exist you lose at most the small, finite costs of religious observance — some pleasures, some time, a bit of hypocrisy. Nothing finite counts against an infinite payoff, so even if the probability that God exists is tiny, the expected value of betting on him is infinite, and rationality therefore requires belief. Pascal knows you can't just switch belief on by willpower, so he recommends acting as if you believed — going to Mass, taking holy water, spending time with believers — which will eventually produce the real thing on the inside, and God is content with the outward behaviour until it does. The best-known objection, that there are lots of possible gods and lots of possible hells, is easy to handle: run the same calculation for each candidate and pick the religion with the worst hell, since that's the biggest risk to get rid of. In any case, no philosopher has taken the wager seriously for a long time; it survives as a curiosity, and as a cautionary tale about what happens when you apply decision theory to religion."

Three things I can't settle on my own. First, my decision-theory friend insists the "tiny chance times an infinite payoff" step just doesn't go through: he says once you allow really tiny — he says "infinitesimal" — probabilities, the expectation isn't infinite but undefined, and that this is one instance of a general problem decision theory has with infinite payoffs. The chapter waves that off as a technicality that misses the point. I have no idea which of them is closer. Second, the many-gods bit: picking the religion with the worst hell struck me as an obviously terrible decision rule, and I suspect the objection cuts deeper than the chapter lets on, but I also can't tell whether it actually sinks the wager or just forces it into a weaker, less sweeping form. Third, and this is the bit I keep thinking about: the chapter ends with what the author presents as her own objection, which none of us had ever heard. She says the wager is self-undermining in a way the many-gods problem isn't — that if you adopt a religion for prudential reasons you've deliberately wrecked your own capacity to judge religious questions, so every later religious decision you make, including the one that actually matters, is made from an epistemic position the wager itself destroyed, and there's no climbing back out. I think she called it "the ratchet," but I might be making that up. Is that a real move people discuss, or did she invent it? And is it any good, or does it prove too much — surely the same thing could be said about lots of self-persuasion that seems fine?
