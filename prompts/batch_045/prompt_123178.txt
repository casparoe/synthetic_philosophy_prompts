So I was reading some blog post on philosophy of biology the other day (one of those very long, very confident ones) and it summed up Popper's take on Darwinism like this:

"Popper's demarcation criterion says a theory counts as scientific only if it rules out some conceivable observation. He applied it to Darwin and concluded that Darwinism fails the test: natural selection is circular, because the organisms that survive are the fit ones, and the fit ones are by definition the ones that survive. It can therefore be squared with any outcome whatsoever, which makes it unfalsifiable — and in Popper's book that put it in the same bin as Freud and Marx, i.e. not science, pseudoscience, though he did allow it was still heuristically useful and gave it the polite name 'metaphysical research programme.' Near the end of his life he retracted the whole thing and admitted that natural selection is straightforwardly testable as soon as you specify the environment, so the earlier argument was just a mistake. What's more, by the same logic all of optimality and ESS modelling in behavioural ecology should count as untestable too, since any observed behaviour can always be rescued by redescribing the payoffs."

I'm defintely not sure what to trust here. The bit about survival of the fittest being circular sounds right to me, but the rest I can't evaluate. Can you go through it more or less claim by claim and tell me what's fair to Popper, what's a simplification, and what he'd actually object to? In particular:

- Is the tautology point really the thing Popper was complaining about, or is that a different complaint from his actual one? Because I've seen people say his worry was more about how you'd ever test a claim about a unique historical process.
- A commenter under the post said Popper's target was only the *mechanism*, not whether evolution happened, and that creationists who quote him are misusing him. Is that a real distinction he drew, and does it matter for whether the summary is fair?
- Does the bit about ESS and optimality models follow from Popper's argument at all, or does spelling out the payoffs and constraints put those models in a different situation from the bare Darwinian schema?
- And does his later change of mind, if it really happened, amount to him giving up the earlier position entirely, or was it more of a qualified adjustment?

I don't need page numbers or anything I'd have to look up — I just want to know which parts of this I should believe and whether the blog's implied moral, that falsifiability is a bad criterion because it misfires on our best biological theory, survives the corrections.
