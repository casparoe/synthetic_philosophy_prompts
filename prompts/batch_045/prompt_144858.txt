I'm applying for a nine-month fellowship and need a two-page proposal (somewhere around 800 words) and I've got a half-formed idea I want turned into something fundable.

The idea: everyone sets up James vs. Clifford as a fight about how much evidence is enough, or about whether practical considerations can ever be reasons for belief. I think both framings miss the real fault line. Some propositions have truth-values that depend on the believer's own level of confidence in them — whether a colleague is trustworthy, whether a marriage will last, whether a patient walks again, whether a student can do the work. Call them confidence-dependent. And I want to split them in two: *causally* dependent (being confident helps bring it about, with a gap in between that evidence could in principle have checked — James's mountaineer, the placebo-ish cases) and *constitutively* dependent (the confidence is partly what it *is* for the thing to be so, there's no gap, and no outside evidential check). My hunch is that Clifford's rule is demanding but coherent for the first kind and literally undefined for the second, and that James's permission is really a doctrine about the second kind that he oversells with the faith-helps-create-the-fact rhetoric. Then the part I actually care about: if that's right, the ethics of belief has no category for the attitude we take toward constitutively confidence-dependent propositions. It isn't belief, and it isn't acceptance either (acceptance is too voluntarist and too tied to action). Call it steadfastness, or trust, or whatever — I want the project to argue that a third attitude is needed and to say what norms govern it.

So, in the proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer (I don't want "how should we think about faith and evidence"); say why the standard ways of setting up the dispute leave it open; give the method, i.e. what I'd actually do month by month across nine months, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives (a close reading of the two essays, or just more conceptual analysis of what "sufficient evidence" means); lay out the two or three possible conclusions and which consideration would decide between them; say what would count as failure — especially what would show that my causal/constitutive distinction collapses or does no work; and be honest about scope, what has to be cut to fit nine months.

No bibliography and no citations, I'll drop those in myself, and don't rest anything on sources you'd have to look up — it should stand on the arguments. Lead with the bottom line in the very first sentence: the single sharpest claim the project would establish if it works (not the topic, not the question, the claim), then go straight into the rest.
