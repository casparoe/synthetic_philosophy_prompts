I teach a second-year seminar on formal systems and the limits of mechanism: eighteen students, one eighty-minute session, and a very uneven background — most have seen truth tables, two or three have done a little proof theory, and a handful have read popular accounts of Gödel and know roughly what the incompleteness theorem is supposed to show. I want one class activity whose point is the difference between working inside a formal system and reasoning about that system from outside.

The system I have in mind is the MU-puzzle. The rules, for reference: the only symbols are M, I and U; the sole axiom is the string MI; and there are four rules — from any string ending in I you may append a U; from a string of the form Mx you may pass to Mxx; wherever a string contains III you may replace those three symbols with a single U; and wherever it contains UU you may delete that pair. The question is whether MU is a theorem. It is not, and the usual argument is that the number of I's in a theorem is never a multiple of three, since doubling and subtracting three cannot turn one into a multiple of three.

I have run this before as a straight puzzle and it went badly in two ways. The strong students either had the answer already or brute-forced for ten minutes and then checked out, and in both runs one student produced the counting argument about fifteen minutes in and announced it to the room, which deflated everything — the puzzle became a fact to be told rather than something anyone had to do. So I want the activity structured so that the failure itself is the experience, and so that the student who sees the argument early has something to do with it other than spoil it.

What I would like from you:

A set of rules, roles, materials and a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, with no lecture up front and with the words invariant, metatheory, Gödel and incompleteness withheld until the debrief. Assume a whiteboard, index cards and no technology beyond a projector.

A way of splitting the room so that the mechanical and the reflective attitudes are separated and then brought into contact: for instance one half permitted only to write strings on the board and the other permitted only to make and test conjectures about what can be written, with some controlled channel between them. Tell me what each half will actually discover and why the asymmetry generates the point rather than just slowing things down.

A scoring or recording device — no real money — that makes the difference between my search has not terminated and no derivation exists visible in the students' own behaviour. My thought is a repeated credence report: at fixed intervals each student commits a stake of points to MU is derivable versus MU is not derivable, scored by a proper scoring rule, with a small prize. Design the scheme, say where the reports go in the timeline, tell me what the trajectory of the group's credences should look like if the activity works, and tell me how to keep it from being gamed by someone who knows the answer and bets hard on it immediately.

A companion system, with the same surface look, whose job is to break the inference from failed search to impossibility. Either its target should be reachable but only by a derivation long enough that nobody finds it in the time available, or it should be unreachable for a reason that is not the count of I's — so that what generalises is the technique of finding a property preserved by every rule that the target lacks, and not this particular piece of arithmetic. Give me the companion system, tell me which of the two jobs you gave it and why, and tell me how to sequence the two so the second one lands as a correction rather than as a repetition.

A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the idea: what it is to prove something in a system as opposed to about one; why the counting argument is not a fifth rule and not a longer derivation; what has to be true of a system for an outsider's argument about it to be possible at all.

An honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to say myself. I am particularly worried about the bridge that the puzzle is usually used to build: in the MU case the impossibility is established by an argument the system itself cannot express, whereas what makes the incompleteness argument distinctive is that the undecidable sentence is, under a coding, a string of the system — the jump out of the system is brought back inside. I would like the debrief to set up that contrast rather than cement the lazy version of the analogy, and I would also like your view on whether running the activity at all makes the lazy version more likely.

Finally, what will predictably go wrong: the student who already knows the answer, the student who thinks impossible just means we were not clever enough, the group that treats the invariant as one more move available inside the game, and the student who concludes that the session has refuted mechanism. For each, tell me how to handle it in the room.
