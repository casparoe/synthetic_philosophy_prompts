I'm a TA for a first-year ethics module and I'm supposed to be writing next week's seminar handout on Williams's critique of consequentialism — the integrity objection and that "one thought too many" bit — and I've realised I use the two complaints interchangebly, as if they were the same point said twice. I suspect they aren't. I'm also, unhelpfully for my concentration, in the middle of a family row about whether we move my mum into a care home: my brother keeps doing the impartial arithmetic out loud ("obviously better for everyone if she's somewhere with 24-hour staff, it's not close"), and every time he does it I want to throw a plate at him, but I genuinely can't tell whether what's wrong with it is the thought itself or what the thought is going to end up making us do.

So: build me two cases that pull the two complaints apart, one in each direction. Case 1: the impartial thought really is one too many — someone close to the agent would be entitled to be hurt or resentful if they found out the agent was having it, and it's the wrong kind of thing to be weighing — but the agent's integrity is not threatened: nothing in the consequentialist verdict asks her to abandon, betray or hollow out a project or commitment that gives her life its point, and if she just acted on the partial consideration instead, nothing much would be lost. Case 2: her integrity really is on the line in the Williams sense — the consequentialist verdict demands something that costs her a ground project, and that alienation is the substance of the complaint — but nobody would say she's had one thought too many; reasoning in impersonal terms is apt here, maybe even required by the situation, and no one in the story would be insulted or hurt by the fact that she's doing it.

Constraints, because last year's handout got torn apart for being lazy. Use the same protagonist in both cases — someone with a job where impartial calculation is sometimes exactly the right mode and sometimes not (a ward nurse? a GP? a school head? you pick) — so that what varies is the structure of the situation and not the person. Each case one paragraph, realistic and contemporary: no trolleys, no drowning, no burning buildings, no desert-island hostages, and no reheating of Williams's own examples. The consequentialist verdict in each case has to be one a smart, non-fanatical consequentialist would actually endorse — no demandingness absurdities, no "give away everything you own" — since the whole point is that the complaints bite even when the consequentialist is being reasonable. The integrity loss in Case 2 must not just reduce to "she feels bad about doing the right thing" or "she's asked to do something she finds personally icky."

Then show the cases work. For each one, say why the one-thought-too-many verdict goes the way it does and why the integrity verdict goes the way it does, derived from features of the case rather than announced by label — and name the single feature that flips each verdict, plus what happens to the case if you remove it. Then give me: (a) a control case in three or four sentences — a minimal variant of one of yours where both complaints fire at once, with a note on what you had to add; (b) the best available consequentialist reply to each case, not a strawman; and (c) your honest verdict on whether these are one argument in two registers or two arguments that merely travel together. If you think one of the two directions can't be built honestly, say which one and why, and give me the closest near-miss instead — I'd rather have that than a case that just stipulates the verdicts. My students have only read a short excerpt in the course reader, so don't assume any knowledge of the secondary literature.
