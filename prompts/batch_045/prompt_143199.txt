I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and I help run a small Sunday reading group that works through moral philosophy at a fairly slow pace. We're doing a unit on the ethics of aggregation, and our last session went badly: everyone restated their slogan, nobody conceded anything, and the one person who'd actually read the primary sources won by name-dropping. For next week I want a handout in dialogue form that we can argue with instead.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, equally competent, who disagree about the separateness of persons objection to aggregative ethics. Call them Iris (who presses the objection) and Marcus (who defends aggregation). Setting: their panel at a conference has been canceled, they've been left with a whiteboard and ninety minutes, and they decide to use the time on each other. Minimal stage directions, distinguishable voices, roughly 1,400 to 1,900 words.

What I want from it:

1. No strawmanning and no talking past each other. Each should land at least one point the other can't answer on the spot, and there should be at least two places where one of them concedes something and then reframes or revises rather than collapsing. Neither gets to win by restating a principle louder.

2. Cover the load-bearing familiar material, in their own words and without quoting or citing anything: the Rawls-style charge that aggregation treats many people's satisfactions as if they were the satisfactions of one person spread across a life; the question of why a sacrifice that seems plainly fine when one person bears both the cost and the benefit looks different when the cost and the benefit land on different people; the Nozick-style point that the balance is not experienced by anybody in particular; and the hardest press on the objector, namely that refusing to aggregate also has victims — declining to impose a very small risk on many in order to avert a large harm to a few identifiable people is itself a choice with a body count.

3. The reason I'm commissioning a new dialogue rather than handing out something published: I want them to work through a case I've been chewing on that I don't think gets handled explicitly. Suppose a bit of fictional technology — call it a confluence link — that can make two subjects share a single stream of consciousness to a degree f between 0 and 1. At f = 0 they are ordinary separate people. At f = 1 there is one phenomenal field taking input from both bodies, while each still controls one body. At intermediate values, a fraction f of each subject's experiences are jointly experienced, felt from both sides at once, and the remainder are private. Then have them fight about:

   - Whether the permissibility of trading pain in one body for pleasure in the other scales continuously with f, or whether there's a cliff somewhere. Make them locate the disagreement precisely: is the operative variable who feels what, whose agency or consent is in play, or whether there is a standpoint from which the package is chosen as a single life?
   - The counting question. If a partially fused pair is, in the relevant sense, more than one subject but fewer than two, does the objection commit you to a fusion premium — to weighting their combined welfare more heavily than you'd weight the same welfare in two separate people? If Iris rejects the premium, Marcus should press her on whether her view has quietly become a rule about identity rather than about separateness of experience.
   - The reverse case. Consider one person whose future self is as psychologically discontinuous from them as a stranger is: total change of values, no anticipation, no identification. If intrapersonal compensation is supposed to be the model case where trade-offs clearly work, does Iris's account predict that compensation should stop working inside a life at the point where continuity breaks? And then: does she want the continuity case and the f-spectrum governed by the same variable, or by two different ones? Marcus gets to say which answer would make the objection look like a metaphysical thesis wearing a moral costume, and she gets to respond to that.

4. Let them try to locate the real disagreement near the end: is it first-order, about whether aggregation is permissible, or is it a disagreement about what the metaphysics of persons is and how much normative work it can do? Don't let it end in agreement to disagree — have each say what would actually move them, and let the other say whether that's ad hoc.

After the dialogue, add a moderator's note of about 250 to 350 words, in your own voice rather than the characters', doing three things. First, separate what is dialectically standard from what is live: say plainly where the dialogue restates something most people who work on this would grant (for instance, that the objection targets the structure of aggregation rather than the value of welfare, or that intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs differ in some respect) versus where it takes a side in an ongoing fight (whether the objection secretly leans on a thesis about personal identity, whether the ex ante or impartial-standpoint reply rescues aggregation, whether the pull we feel in the f-spectrum is evidence about separateness or an artifact of imagining a technology we don't have). Second, flag any place where you made a character concede more or less than actual defenders of that position would concede, or where you relied on a contested reading of the standard moves. Third, if either character leans on a formal result — an impartial-spectator or aggregation theorem, expected-utility machinery — state it only as precisely as you can reconstruct it unaided, and mark reconstructions as reconstructions rather than as claims about what anyone proved.

No citations, bibliographies, page references, or quoted passages from primary texts: the handout has to stand on its own for people who won't have the reading in front of them, and I don't want anyone in the group to be able to win by name-dropping.
