I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on individually imperceptible contributions to collective harms — a tonne of carbon here, a course of unnecessary antibiotics there, the water draw and tail risks of large compute infrastructure — and the torture-versus-dust-specks comparison keeps reappearing as the limiting case that any view like mine has to be able to handle. I keep being told my commitments can't all be true, but every time I try to find the inconsistency it slides away from me, so I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. I also can't tell whether the thing I find suspicious is the thing that's actually load-bearing, so please don't take my own suspicions as a guide to where the problem is.

Roughly, and as precisely as I can state it, here is where I've landed. (1) Continuity: there is no metaphysical joint in the space of aversive experience — for any unpleasant experience there is a slightly less unpleasant one, and one can walk from a dust speck in the eye to decades of severe torture by steps no subject could discriminate. (2) Unbounded mild aggregation: mild harms really do sum, and there is no point at which adding further specks stops making the outcome worse; 10^30 dust specks, spread across 10^30 people, is worse than a stubbed toe, worse than a broken arm, worse than a broken arm for everybody. (3) A ceiling: no number of dust specks, however enormous, is worse than one person's decades of severe torture. (4) Additive separability across persons: the badness of a distribution is the sum of the badnesses of the experiences composing it, and it makes no difference to the total whether a given quantity of mild discomfort is concentrated in one life or spread thinly over a billion. (5) Representability: I want the ordering over outcomes to be complete, transitive and expressible in ordinary real numbers — I don't want to say that speck-totals and tortures are "incommensurable," which I take to be a way of refusing the question — because my other chapter runs expected-value arguments over very small probabilities of small harms in aggregate, and I don't know how to do that with any richer structure. (6) A case verdict I am stubborn about: offered a certainty of 10^30 dust specks or a one-in-a-million chance of one person's decades of torture, I take the specks, and I don't think taking them makes me irrational. (7) A methodological commitment: I think the stepwise "spectrum" style of argument is a legitimate way of discovering what I am already committed to, and that once each step has been granted the endpoint has to be granted too; this is part of how I arrived at (2).

What I want is the following. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — people keep telling me that (1) and (3) are flatly inconsistent and I genuinely do not see the argument, so if that one dissolves I want to know what extra premise it would need. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the package, especially in the expected-value machinery of the other chapter and in my confidence about (6). Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most, and whether any item in the list is doing no work at all. Two further things. My supervisor has floated a way out that I find both attractive and suspicious, and I can't tell whether it is the crux or a red herring: drop (4) and let mild harms aggregate with diminishing marginal contribution toward an asymptote, so that the total climbs without ever crossing into the severe tier — she sketches it informally as a saturating function of the number of specks, with a ceiling set just below the severe tier. Assess whether that actually delivers (2), (3), (5) and (6) together, and if it does, what it costs: in particular, does it commit me to saying that the ten-billionth speck matters less than the first, and does that undermine the claim my applied chapter needs, namely that any individual's imperceptible contribution still counts? And separately: if the severe tier really is lexically dominant in the way (3) suggests, am I thereby committed to the kind of view that must forbid ordinary air travel or driving on the ground that they impose a nonzero chance of severe harm, or is there a principled place to stop short of that?
