ok so slightly odd request. My book club is doing a month on capital punishment and I volunteered to write a "what would Camus say" piece for the newsletter. I want it to be about the stuff he couldnt have had a view on because it didnt exist yet — he died in 1960 — rather than just a summary of his old aboltionist essay.

Here's the setup I want him dropped into. Say its 2031 and some mid-size country (call it Vereda, totally made up) has kept the death penalty but fixed everything abolitionists used to complain about:

- before any death sentence is carried out, an automated second review layer re-runs the case and re-tests the physical evidence; the government says the residual risk of killing an innocent person is under one in ten million, and the audit is public. So the fallibility argument basically doesnt bite anymore.
- the killing itself is inert-gas hypoxia in a sealed room: no audience, no press, no last-meal theatre, no one there but a technician. And the prisoner is told the sentence but not the date, so theres no multi-year wait on death row either.

The alternative on offer in Vereda is the thing that in the real world actually replaced execution after his death: life imprisonment with no possibility of release, in an ordinary-ish prison.

What I want from you: about 900 words in his voice — essay shaped, not a bullet list — taking a position on Vereda. Not just "hed oppose it because he opposed the death penalty." I want to know where exactly hed locate the objection once the fallibility, the cruelty and the spectacle are all gone. Is the problem the killing as such, or the states claim to a killing, or the fact that it has now become clean and administrative? Would he think the sanitised version is somehow worse than the guillotine, or would some part of him think its less bad than what it replaced?

Two more things to work in, because theyre where his own machinery seems to strain:

1. Would he accept life-without-parole as the humane trade, the way a lot of real abolitionists did — or would he hear it as the very same punishment stretched out over forty years and refuse the deal?

2. The awkward one: given how he thinks about death elsewhere — the universal, natural, unavoidable thing, with the real horror located in what we build around it — does his own framework leave him room to prefer a quick invisible death to fifty years in a cell? If you think hed bite that bullet, say so plainly and defend him. If you think hed squirm, change the subject, or quietly revise something he said, say that instead and explain why.

No citations or reading list please, I just want the argument.
