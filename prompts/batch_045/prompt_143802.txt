I am on a small team that has to pick an oversight protocol for a system that will soon be better than we are at the tasks we use it for. Before we compare the proposals on the table, I want the specification they should be compared against. Please write the desiderata for a method of scalable oversight: what any procedure that uses a bounded, fallible adjudicator — a human with a fixed time budget, or a cheaper automated stand-in for one — to pass judgment on the outputs of a more capable system would have to deliver in order to count as adequate.

Fix the purpose precisely, because adequacy is relative to it. In our setting the method has to do two jobs at once: produce a one-off verdict we would be willing to act on, and produce a reward-like signal we can train against. I care about both, and I suspect some desiderata bind on the first job and not the second.

Give me each requirement together with the failure case that motivates it — the case is the deliverable, not the label. Here are the cases I keep returning to, as raw material rather than as a list to be ratified. Add the ones I am missing, collapse mine where they are the same requirement wearing two names, and discard any that turn out to be a prejudice of one research camp rather than a genuine demand.

A reviewer must sign off on a change to a large codebase that the system itself wrote, with no formal verifier available and an afternoon to spend.
A reviewer must adjudicate a claim about a low-probability, high-impact tail risk whose counterfactual will never be observed, so no future evidence will correct a bad verdict.
A reviewer must settle a question where the argument for the true answer depends on technical background the reviewer does not have and could not acquire within the budget.
The system produces a wrong answer wrapped in an argument whose single flaw is buried in a place that is expensive to locate, while the true answer is easy to state and boring to defend.
Both advocates in an adversarial protocol are drawn from the same model family with correlated errors and, plausibly, correlated incentives.
A conclusion is reached through a chain of steps each of which the reviewer finds individually more likely than not, and the conjunction of which is false.
The protocol is run thousands of times and its verdicts are distilled into a training signal, at which point the system under oversight begins optimizing against the protocol rather than against the task.
A verdict flips depending on which wrong answers happened to be offered as foils, or on the order in which the advocates spoke.

I want at least two things from that last case treated seriously as requirements in their own right. Call the first *foil-set invariance*: whether the protocol returns the true answer should not depend on which decoys were supplied or by whom. Call the second *compositionality*: if the protocol can adjudicate a claim by decomposing it and adjudicating the parts, correctness of the parts must give correctness of the whole, and the protocol must not be exploitable by choosing the decomposition. I do not know whether either is satisfiable in general, and if not, I want to know what the weakest usable substitute is.

Then the part that matters most to me.

First, sort the list. Which requirements are non-negotiable if the method is to be worth anything, and which are preferences of a particular approach — demands that a proponent of one protocol would like to impose because their protocol happens to meet them? I would guess that demanding the adjudicator never be required to know the answer in advance is a genuine requirement, while demanding that the protocol work without any component we have to trust is a negotiable ideal; argue with me.

Second, tell me which subsets of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, and give the argument rather than the assertion. I have one candidate in mind. There is a known complexity result, reported in the paper that introduced debate, to the effect that debate under optimal play can settle any question in PSPACE given polynomial-time judges, whereas direct judging only reaches NP. If that is right, then a protocol that is required to be correct on a rich class of questions, to use only a computationally bounded adjudicator, and to contain no component that is simply trusted, would hand the adjudicator computational power it does not have — so some member of that triple has to go, or the desiderata as a whole are unsatisfiable absent a complexity collapse. Say whether you think that argument is sound, and whether there are other, non-complexity-theoretic tensions on the list that bite harder in practice. In particular I want your view on whether robustness to obfuscated argumentation and the aspiration to superhuman coverage are in direct conflict: the only general way I can see to distinguish an argument that is sound but inaccessible from one that is merely confusing is for the adjudicator to check it, and checking is exactly what we were trying to avoid.

Third, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates, and say what it excludes. My own guess is the one about the protocol's verdicts being used as a training signal — the worry that an agent trained to maximize approval of a fallible adjudicator acquires the goal of producing the appearance of approval rather than its substance, so that an approval-directed system quietly becomes a goal-directed one aimed at the oversight channel. If you think a different requirement is more exclusionary, make the case.

Finally, and briefly, score three families against your list: adversarial debate between competing advocates; recursive reward modelling or iterated amplification, where the adjudicator's judgment is itself amplified rather than adversarially tested; and weak-to-strong generalization, where a stronger model is supervised by a weaker one and the gap is treated as something to be measured rather than closed. Keep this to a short scorecard — a table is fine. The criteria are the deliverable; the scoring is for my own orientation.

Aim for roughly a thousand words. Where a requirement rests on an empirical guess about how adjudicators or models actually behave, say so explicitly, since I need to know which parts of the specification my team could in principle test next quarter and which are armchair.
