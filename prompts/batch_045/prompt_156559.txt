My lab submitted a paper last month on an architecture we built, and the reviewer reports have dropped me into a disagreement I am not equipped to settle. The system has an encoder that maps its input onto a sequence of codes drawn from a finite learned codebook (a vector-quantized bottleneck), a learned transition model over those codes, and a decoder. It was trained on a compositional task with certain combinations held out, and it generalizes to unseen combinations at roughly ninety percent accuracy; if we substitute one code in an intermediate sequence with a code extracted from a different input, the output changes in the way the obvious semantics would predict. My coauthor thinks this is a language of thought, learned rather than innate. The reviewer thinks it is not: the codes are continuous embeddings selected by gradient descent, nothing in the system has content in the relevant sense, and our systematicity is an inductive bias of the architecture doing the work that genuine constituency was supposed to do.

I want the underlying issue clarified rather than the exchange won. Please explain what the language of thought hypothesis is committed to, over and above the bare claim that cognition has internal structure, in a way that makes clear what would falsify it for a system like ours. Work through the standard marks one at a time — systematicity, productivity, compositionality, and the requirement that a representation's constituents make a causal difference to which states the system moves into — saying what each was meant to rule out and whether our architecture meets it. Then say where the disagreement bottoms out: is it an empirical question about the network, a question about how demanding the notion of a symbol ought to be, or a question about whether a merely partial and graded form of systematicity counts at all? If it is the last, say what one further experiment would have to show to settle it. Reason from the mechanics of the system rather than from the literature, and do not spend time on citations.
