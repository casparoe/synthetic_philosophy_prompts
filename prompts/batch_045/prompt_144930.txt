I teach a first-year module on the philosophy of history, and next term I am screening Robert Bolt's *A Man for All Seasons* — Zinnemann's 1966 film, though the 1960 stage version is what I'd be glad to have the argument work from where the two differ — as a case study in Butterfield's complaint about the Whig interpretation of history. What I want is a reading of the work through that lens: not a summary of what Whig history is, and not a plot recapitulation.

The reason it interests me is that the film does something Butterfield's category does not obviously cover. His target is a complaint about success stories: beginning from the present and reading backwards, abridging the record into a drama with an apparent moral clarity, and sorting the agents of the past into those who furthered what we now have and those who contributed nothing to the making of it. Bolt's More belongs to no such story. He loses, he is executed, and the cause he defends — the universal visible Church under the papacy — is not vindicated at any later point in the film's own terms; the break with Rome, which did so much to produce the modern state and arguably modern liberty, is on the wrong side of the drama. Yet almost everything else in the work is a machine for producing identification: we are meant to recognise ourselves in More, to hear in his defence of conscience an early version of something we now hold, and to see Cromwell, Rich and Wolsey as men who left nothing worth inheriting.

So I want four things worked out.

First, the taxonomy I should be putting on the board. I have been distinguishing teleological whiggism, in which the sequence is narrated so that the present looks like its point; motivational anachronism, in which an agent is given a motive he could not have had; anticipatory anachronism, in which he is credited with foreseeing what came after; and a fourth thing for which I have no good name, in which a figure is cast as our ancestor electively rather than causally, needing neither to have foreseen nor to have intended anything, and not needing to have won. Is that fourth thing genuinely distinct, or does it collapse into the second or third? And is it what the film is doing?

Second, what is the film actually committed to? More cannot have meant by conscience what the film's More means: his conscience was bound to the authority of the visible Church, and the man who wrote polemics against Tyndale, and who held the chancellorship while Protestants were burned in England — how directly implicated he was is itself something historians argue about — did not take private judgement to be the last court of appeal. Does the film require us to think otherwise? Or can it claim him under the fourth heading, as a causal ancestor of a doctrine he would have repudiated, on the ground that martyrs for conscience became, over the following two centuries, part of the case for toleration? I want to know whether unintended lineage is a defence against the charge of whiggism or only a subtler version of it.

Third, what to do with the Common Man. My hypothesis, which I would like you to argue with, is that he is not merely a Brechtian narrator but the play's way of licensing the anachronism: a figure who steps out of the action, plays servant, boatman, gaoler, foreman, headsman, tells us he is no hero and only wants to keep his skin, and thereby names the distance between 1530 and the audience in order to make that distance safe to ignore. If that is right, the device is a confession that buys permission, and a knowing whiggism is still whiggism. If it is wrong — if the Common Man does something to the structure of the work rather than to our complicity in it — give me the better account.

Fourth, where does the work resist the reading, and where should I say in the seminar that the honest critic has to let it go? I have in mind More's insistence that even the Devil is entitled to the protection of the law, which is not obviously a modern liberal point at all; the play's thorough anti-authoritarianism, which looks more like Bolt's own century than More's; and the sacramental, hierarchical world the film's More actually inhabits.

Close by saying what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not, if it adds anything. My suspicion is that it shows the pull of ancestry-selection to be not merely an error but something a narrative about the past has to exercise if it is to matter to anyone, and that a criticism which can only forbid it is less use to me than one which says what we are allowed to want from a usable past. Name the scenes you would put on screen beside Butterfield, and don't pad the answer with plot.
