I am a senior at a public high school and I compete on my school's policy debate team. Last month our squad hosted a public showcase round on the resolution that the world is becoming more peaceful. Afterwards, at the reception, my uncle, who does actuarial modelling for an insurance company, got into an argument with my AP European History teacher, who had come to watch. I had spent two weeks researching the topic, so the two of them turned to me to settle it, and I could not: I kept agreeing with whichever of them was speaking at the moment. They have asked me to tell the squad who won. I would be grateful if you would go through the exchange move by move, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say where one of them is changing the subject rather than answering. Please do not try to check their numbers; assume them for the sake of argument and tell me where an argument still fails even granting them.

The exchange, as close as I can reproduce it:

[1] My uncle: Homicide rates in Western Europe have fallen by a factor of ten or more since the late Middle Ages, and deaths in war per head of world population are far below what they were in the 1940s. A person alive today is much less likely to be killed by another person than someone in the fourteenth century. That is what less violent means, and on that measure the resolution is obviously true.

[2] Ms. Ferrante: You have picked your measure and then described it as the answer. Violence is not one natural kind with a single trend; it is an aggregate we assemble out of things that move independently of each other: death in battle, homicide, assault, domestic and sexual violence, police and prison violence, the force used to pacify and administer colonies, hunger produced by policy. Your aggregate happens to include the two that fell and to exclude the rest. If the aggregate is built to yield the trend, the trend is a fact about your bookkeeping, not about the world. And per capita is not neutral bookkeeping either: with a growing population, the rate can fall while the absolute number of victims stays flat or rises, so which of the two you report decides the answer in advance.

[3] My uncle: That cuts both ways, and some of your categories are not violence at all. A famine caused by bad policy is a catastrophe, but calling it violence stretches the word until it means harm I disapprove of, at which point there is no fact of the matter and the whole question dissolves.

[4] Ms. Ferrante: I am not trying to stretch the word; I am pointing out that the boundary decisions are doing the work. And even inside the categories you accept, part of what you are measuring may not be violence either. The long-run homicide figures come from small samples of burials and court rolls from a few well-excavated regions, and the more recent part of the decline may partly reflect trauma care, since the same attack now gets recorded as an aggravated assault rather than a homicide because the victim survives. So one component of your trend is medicine and another is which sites happened to be dug up.

[5] My uncle: I will grant you both, and I will say the decline is real but smaller and more recent than the headline numbers suggest. But notice what just happened: you attacked the trend by attacking the measure, and every measure can be attacked that way. If no trend survives that treatment, then the argument was never about the world to begin with, and the honest thing is to say the best available numbers support the decline and stop there.

[6] Ms. Ferrante: There is one more problem, the one I actually care about. Every measure you use is a count of realized violence, a body count. But what makes us safer may be a state's monopoly on force, which neither removes violence nor merely relocates it; it also accumulates destructive capacity in very few hands. A world in which a handful of people could kill hundreds of millions in an afternoon has a very low realized count and an enormous expected one. Whether that world is more or less violent than a world of frequent small raids seems to me to have no answer until you say whether you are counting realized violence or risk-weighted violence, and I do not think the question has a determinate answer in advance of that choice.

[7] My uncle: If unrealized capacities count as violence, then the invention of agriculture, or of standing armies, made the world enormously more violent the moment it made mass killing possible, and a hermit sitting on a loaded weapon is more violent than an actual brawl. You may be happy to say that, but then you have changed the subject from whether people are being hurt to something else, and the word violent is doing no work.

[8] Ms. Ferrante: I do not think anything goes, which is why I started with the boundary question. The honest test is whether the measures cluster. If homicide, assault, war death, and domestic violence all fell together, there is a robust fact that the world got less violent, and my objections only trim the trend. If they diverge, then the right answer is a disaggregated one and the resolution our squad was debating was simply ill-posed. What I deny is that there is one clean thing out there, trending downward, that we are both trying to estimate.

Beyond the move-by-move verdict, I would like to know what both of them are missing, whether the disagreement at the end is factual, conceptual, or verbal, and what I should actually say to the squad on Monday in two or three sentences.
