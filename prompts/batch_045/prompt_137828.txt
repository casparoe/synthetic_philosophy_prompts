I am writing a seminar paper on the bootstrapping problem and the easy-knowledge argument against reliabilism, and a study partner and I have constructed a version of the argument that seems to us to prove a great deal more than either of us is willing to accept. I would like your help identifying which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

The case. An agent has a digital thermometer of unknown provenance and no independent way of telling what the temperature of a room is: no other instrument, no memory of what the temperature felt like, no testimony. On each of a hundred occasions she reads the thermometer, forms the belief that the temperature on that occasion was whatever the thermometer said, and records this in a list. From the list she infers, by what looks like perfectly ordinary enumerative induction, that her thermometer is reliable; combining that conclusion with today's reading, she concludes that the room is twenty degrees. Call this the bootstrapping procedure.

The argument.

(1) Reliabilism: a belief amounts to knowledge if it was produced by a reliable process, and nothing in this requires the agent to have independent reason to think the process reliable.

(2) In the case above, the intermediate beliefs recorded in the list are themselves produced by the thermometer. If the thermometer is reliable, they are knowledge, and knowledge is available to its possessor as evidence.

(3) If those intermediate beliefs are available as evidence, then the inference from the list to the reliability of the thermometer is an ordinary induction from a sample to a statistical generalization about the sample's own source, and is licensed in just the way any such induction is licensed.

(4) So reliabilism entitles the agent to argue from the thermometer's output to the reliability of the thermometer, and thereby to know the temperature of the room without any independent check. Knowledge is easy.

(5) The standard reply is that the argument is epistemically circular: the list was compiled by the thermometer itself, and a record compiled by the very instrument whose reliability is in question cannot support the claim that the instrument is reliable.

(6) But the same description seems to fit every chain of measurement in the sciences. As far as we can tell, no instrument is checked against the truth directly; it is calibrated against other instruments, which are calibrated against others, and the network either regresses without end or closes into a loop of inter-laboratory comparisons. If epistemic circularity is fatal in (5), then no systematic error is ever detectable and measurement never yields knowledge at all.

(7) Nor is measurement special. Our only evidence that enumerative induction is reliable is its record of past success, and that record is compiled using induction. Our only evidence that a rule of deductive inference is valid is the apparent correctness of instances of it, assessed by that very rule. If epistemic circularity is fatal, then there is no knowledge by induction or by deduction either.

(8) The reply we keep being offered to (6) is that actual measurement escapes circularity by cross-checking independent instruments, built by different manufacturers and exploiting different physical principles. But this seems to relocate the problem rather than remove it: the claim that the two instruments' errors are probabilistically independent is a substantive empirical claim of exactly the kind the procedure was supposed to establish, and the only way to test it is by more measurement of the same circular sort.

(9) So we appear to be forced into a dilemma. Either reliabilism is true and knowledge is easy, bootstrapping being legitimate after all; or we impose a general ban on epistemically circular support, in which case (6) and (7) take measurement, induction and deduction down together, and skepticism follows.

(10) What makes us suspicious of our own argument rather than of reliabilism: if you model the agent in (2) as a Bayesian whose prior spreads credence over hypotheses about the thermometer's reliability, then the readings do raise the probability that the thermometer is reliable, and they do so by perfectly ordinary conditioning. So the complaint cannot be about the update. It must be about which priors an agent is entitled to start from, or about which items she may treat as evidence at all — which is the original dispute moved one step back rather than settled.

We have tried two distinctions and abandoned both. The first is between using a method in the course of arguing that the method is reliable and assuming the conclusion among the premises; this looked to us like a relabelling of the disagreement, since whether the bootstrapping argument belongs to the benign or the malignant class is precisely what is at issue. The second is that the bootstrapper's evidence could not have been adverse, since she formed each recorded belief from the reading itself; but that seemed to us equally true of any evidence gathered by a method one currently trusts, which would make the distinction useless.

The verdicts we would most like to keep are these: that the belief produced at the end of the bootstrapping procedure is not knowledge, and that ordinary measurement and ordinary induction do yield knowledge. If we cannot have both, we would rather give up the first than the second, but we would prefer not to choose at all.

So: which step of the argument do you reject? Explain why that step looked innocent, and what each of the alternatives costs. In particular, say what a principle would have to look like in order to condemn the bootstrapping procedure while leaving the calibration chains in (6), the inductive and deductive reasoning in (7), and the cross-checking in (8) intact, and tell us whether that principle is one a reliabilist can accept or whether accepting it means giving up reliabilism anyway.
