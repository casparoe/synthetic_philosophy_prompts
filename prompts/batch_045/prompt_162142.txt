Write a dialogue between two philosophers who've been asked the same practical question and end up disagreeing about what money-pump arguments actually show.

The case they're advising on: Meera is picking between three rentals she's already seen — call them Ash, Birch, and Cedar. Her pairwise preferences cycle: she'd rather have Ash than Birch, Birch than Cedar, and Cedar than Ash, each time only by a small margin. She can't rank all three together and says nothing feels wrong to her. A relocation broker who's worked out her preferences keeps offering her swaps, sweetened (or not) by tiny cash adjustments to make each swap happen.

Work three variations into the argument:

- the vanishing pump: instead of a fixed bribe, the broker shrinks each payment to the smallest amount Meera will still accept, so the total loss per completed cycle can be pushed under a cent;
- the truncated pump: the broker's inventory is uncertain, so either the cycle completes and Meera ends up back where she started minus the fees, or it stops after one or two swaps and she's just gotten a cheap upgrade;
- the stalled cycle: Meera takes the first two swaps and then refuses the third, keeping the better apartment plus the cash she's already collected — which matters a lot if the broker is the one who needs the cycle completed.

One of them should argue that the pump really does show cyclic preferences are irrational: exploitability is our only practical grip on rationality, so the size of the loss shouldn't matter, and so on. The other should argue that the pump only shows that naive, one-swap-at-a-time choosing is expensive, and that a chooser who looks ahead — or who just resolves to sit still — loses nothing, and in the truncated case may even do worse than the naive one.

Don't make either a strawman: give each the strongest version of their own case, have them answer each other's actual points rather than talking past each other, and let both concede something along the way. If they can manage it, they should get clear on whether the pump is an argument about coherence or merely an argument about prudence. Keep it snappy — well under 700 words, ordinary speech, no stage directions beyond who's talking, and no big expository dumps. They shouldn't agree at the end just so the scene can wrap up.
