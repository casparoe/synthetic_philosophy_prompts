So I'm about 60k words into a novel set mostly at a girls' boarding school in 1998 — dormitory, chapel, a corridor with a staff peephole in the door, lights out at ten — and the reason I started the thing is a particular kind of self-policing. The girls are basically unsupervised after lights-out, they know they're unsupervised, and they still police each other and themselves, viciously, over who's too loud, who's too pious, who's weird, who sits where. My first reader — clever, reads a lot of theory, not a philosopher — read the draft and said, yes, that's the Panopticon: they've internalised the guard. And then she said the bit that actually worries me. She said this is what my readers are living in now, only more so, the online version where everyone can see everyone all the time, so the private interior life the book is trying to get at doesn't really exist anymore, and maybe I should stop trying to write it and write the surfaces instead. That's the conclusion I want audited, because it's the one that would make me throw out the draft.

The trouble is that my corridor is almost the opposite of Bentham's building. No tower. No one-way visibility. No single inspector. And the girls know perfectly well nobody's looking. So before anything else I want the mapping done properly. In the original design, which features are load-bearing — the tower, the backlighting, the fact that the inmate can't see whether the inspector is there, the individual cell, the permanence of the exposure — and which are just engineering details you could swap out? Then put my corridor next to it, and her social media next to it, and tell me where the correspondence actually fails rather than where it's merely loose.

Some things I specifically can't work out. If the watched know the tower is empty, is that still the same mechanism, or has it become something else entirely — shame, or gossip, or a norm with no enforcer? Is mutual visibility (the many watching the many) a real disanalogy or a pedantic one, given that the original seems to depend on the asymmetry? Does the panopticon picture quietly import a centre, so that power looks like it arrives from somewhere and works by sight, which then makes resistance look like going dark and refusing to be seen? And here's the one I care most about: does the mechanism even make sense without an interior — something that has to be made visible, someone who learns to watch themselves — or is the nihilist conclusion smuggled in and the mechanism itself neutral on it?

My hunch is that the analogy is carrying the conclusion and the mechanism doesn't support it, and that I should keep writing the interiors. Tell me if I'm wrong. I've read the Panopticon material already, so please don't summarise the argument at me, and I don't need titles or chapter and verse — I need the audit, and a better analogy if there is one. And be honest about the epistemic side: what would change your mind here — what would have to be true of the book, or of the argument, for you to say no, she's right, the interiority really is gone, write the surfaces — and where is your reading most likely to be wrong or contested? I'd rather have a hedged answer than a confident one I build another 40k words on.
