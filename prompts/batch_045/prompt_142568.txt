Hey — I'm writing up a short funding recommendation for a small pot of money: about six philosopher-years spread over three years, conceptual work only (no surveys, no field experiments, no empirically estimating demand elasticities), and the funder's mandate is oddly specific. The work should change what we end up telling individuals who ask whether it's even worth it. You know the cases: buying meat, taking a long-haul flight, voting in a big national election, chipping in to a lobbying campaign, signing a petition, offsetting. So what I need is a defended agenda, not a survey: which open questions in the individual-contribution-to-collective-harm area should that money go to, in what order, and why?

Here's where my own head is, so you can push back on it — I'd rather you tell me one of these is a dead end or already basically settled than have it politely included.

- Threshold granularity. The standard expected-difference-making reply is usually framed in terms of tiny probabilities of making a difference. But the mechanisms that aggregate individual acts differ a lot in how finely they count. Votes get tallied one at a time, so my chance of mattering is small but spread smoothly over the distribution of close elections. Supermarket restocking, hatchery orders, and flight scheduling happen in lumps: a pallet, a batch of tens of thousands of birds, an extra aircraft rotation on a route. Where aggregation is lumpy, my act doesn't have a small chance of a small effect; it has a mostly-zero effect and occasionally a whole-lump effect, and the lump is big. That turns the practical question into something more like: how much can I know about where I sit relative to the next threshold? Which is an epistemology-of-thresholds question rather than a small-probabilities question, and it's not obvious the existing machinery about tiny chances transfers cleanly. I think this might be upstream of a lot — until you know whether lumpiness matters, the reassurance that expected value is still positive looks unsupported. But maybe it's downstream of something else, or maybe it washes out because the expectation is the same either way.

- Membership versus impact. A lot of the unease people report isn't about outcomes at all: it's about being one of the people who did this. That looks like a different question from whether my act changes anything, and it's not clear to me that an expected-utility reply even addresses it — it might answer the impact question while leaving the membership question untouched, or it might turn out the membership question is just impact in disguise. Is there a real distinction here, and if there is, which half should the money go to?

- Which 'it doesn't matter' is on the table? The phrase gets used for at least four claims: no duty, no blameworthiness, no rational requirement, and no point or significance. These seem to come apart — an argument that kills one might leave the others standing — and advice to individuals is usually about the third while the arguments are aimed at the first. Is disentangling them a priority, or is it bookkeeping that won't change any conclusion?

- Does the objection climb levels? If convincing one person to change also fails to make a difference, then the inefficacy worry generalizes to advocacy, movement-building, and funding the advocacy, and it's not obvious where it stops. Either there's a fixpoint somewhere or the whole thing collapses into a global quietism nobody accepts. Where does it stop, and is that an interesting question or a cheap regress objection?

- The portfolio problem. Thousands of individually negligible expected impacts, across causes and across a life, arguably sum to something large, and the 'nothing I do matters' conclusion then never applies to anyone's total conduct. Is that a genuine dissolution of the practical worry, or does it sneak in an assumption about aggregation across decision contexts that the rest of the debate doesn't grant?

What I actually want from you:

1. A ranking of these plus whatever I've clearly missed, ordered by what six philosopher-years should go to. The reasoning matters much more to me than the list.
2. Explicit criteria and explicit trade-offs between them: how many other questions turn on the answer, whether it's tractable for people with only conceptual tools inside three years, whether it's neglected or already crowded, and how much a clean result would change the advice we give individuals.
3. Which of these are pseudo-questions, or would produce a result without changing anything downstream. My suspicion is that some of the heat in this area is verbal and I can't tell which parts.
4. What would move things up or down the list — not what people would read, but what kind of result, if someone got it in eighteen months, should reorder the agenda.
5. And the bit I'm least sure about: which alleged implications of this debate do people lean on but don't actually follow? My sense is that 'so nobody is blameworthy', 'so it's really just about keeping your own hands clean', and 'so only collective political action can ever matter' each overreach in different directions, and I can't work out which is most worth attacking head-on. If one of them is the load-bearing bad inference behind a lot of confident advice, that's what I want to know.
