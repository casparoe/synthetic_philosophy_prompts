Here's roughly where I've landed on natural kinds, which I'm writing up for a reading group next month:

1. There are real, mind-independent divisions in nature, and the reason I think so is basically the success-of-science argument: the classifications our best sciences use let us induct, explain, predict and intervene in ways that would be miraculous if those classifications were just ours. So my working criterion is: a classification carves nature at the joints just when it earns its keep in successful induction and explanation.

2. But I'm also a pluralist. I don't think there's one True Classification of the furniture of the world. Chemical, biological, neuroscientific and even everyday functional ways of sorting things can each latch onto something real, and it genuinely doesn't bother me that they cross-classify the same stuff. "Joints" doesn't have to mean a unique set of joints.

3. For the paradigm cases I'm broadly Kripke–Putnam: kind terms are rigid, reference is fixed by underlying nature and not by the descriptions ordinary speakers associate with the term, so the water/H2O identity is necessary but only knowable a posteriori, and it's possible to discover that a term like "jade" never picked out a single kind at all — it turned out to straddle two different minerals, so there's no essence there to be had.

4. For most of the interesting cases in the special sciences I go homeostatic property cluster: a kind is a bundle of properties held together by some contingent mechanism, with vague edges, no deep essence, and the capacity to drift over time.

5. Species aren't kinds. They're individuals — historical, spatiotemporally bounded chunks of the genealogical nexus rather than classes — so "tiger" isn't a natural-kind term and "tigers are striped" isn't a law in the strict nomic sense. I'm happy to say "tiger" still rigidly designates; it just designates an individual rather than a kind.

6. Race is not a biological natural kind; there's no biological structure out there that racial categories track. At the same time I think race is causally and socially real, that racial categories really do earn their keep in epidemiology and in clinical medicine, and that we shouldn't stop using them there.

7. And on the positive side I want to say that a lot of medical and psychiatric categories — type 2 diabetes, schizophrenia — are bona fide if messy natural-kind candidates, even though my guess is they're pretty heterogeneous underneath.
