Act as a professional moral philosopher who also knows your way around decision theory under deep uncertainty. Take your time with this one — brainstorm a bit before you commit to a line, and please don't just hand me a survey of the evidence on insect minds or a list of positions. I want a diagnosis.

I sit on a university extension pest-management advisory group — growers, an entomologist, a toxicologist, a couple of regulatory people — and for about two years we've been circling the same argument without anyone moving. It comes up every time we revise a spray recommendation.

Here's what I think is striking about it. Nobody in the room disagrees about the biology. Insects have nociceptors, they learn to avoid noxious stimuli, they show what looks like motivational trade-off behaviour, they groom injured body parts; they don't have anything remotely like mammalian pain pathways, and their lives are short and mostly end in being eaten, frozen, starved or poisoned. Everyone grants all of that. We also all grant that if insects matter morally at all, the numbers are astronomical, and that we deliberately kill an enormous number of them. And still:

- Two or three people treat the whole thing as a non-issue: on no credible account of consciousness is an insect a subject of experience, so the only real questions are crop loss and non-target ecological effects.
- Two treat it as possibly the largest issue in applied ethics, because even a small credence in insect sentience multiplied by those numbers swamps everything else we care about, vertebrate farmed-animal welfare included.
- Most of the group wants a threshold instead: some credence below which we ignore it, plus some mechanism-level property — speed of kill, whether the compound paralyses for days before it kills, stomach poison vs contact — that we should prefer no matter which side is right.

My own hypothesis, which I'd rather you stress-test than flatter: I don't think we're arguing about insect minds at all. I think we're arguing about whether to run expected-value reasoning with tiny probabilities and astronomically large stakes, and that this is a decision-theory fight wearing a biology costume. But there's a wrinkle I can't place and haven't seen addressed anywhere.

The wrinkle: conditional on insects being sentient, how intense is the experience? The "big issue" people seem to multiply a small credence by something like full mammalian intensity of suffering. But if your reason for doubting insect sentience is that the neural machinery is simple and largely reflex, that same reason should lead you to expect the experience, if there is one, to be a thin low-grade aversive signal rather than agony — and if you think the machinery is rich enough for agony, you should probably be less uncertain in the first place. So the two factors may not be independent, and if they're coupled the expected value deflates a lot. I've started calling that the coupling question, but I don't know whether it's a real problem or just me not understanding conditional expectation.

Two more things that might be the same disagreement or might be separate. First: nobody on the group reasons about a single insect the way they reason at policy scale — swatting one mosquito feels like nothing, writing a statewide spray schedule feels like something, and nobody has reconciled the two. Second: one member keeps saying that killing is beside the point, because if we wipe out a colony something else expands into the vacated niche, so population-level effects of any particular intervention are roughly nil — we should be arguing about suppression versus replacement, not killing versus not killing. That one feels like it should reframe everything, and both camps ignore it.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it one disagreement or three? Is my decision-theory diagnosis right, or is it really about standards of evidence for other minds, or about the word "pest" doing moral work, or two different questions — individual act and institutional policy — sharing a name? Where does the coupling point actually belong, and is it a genuine objection? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction I'm missing, something formal about tiny probabilities and huge stakes, an actual experiment on insects someone could run, a change of question — or should we expect to stay stuck, and why? An honest "this is ill-posed, here's why" is more useful to me than a tidy answer.
