I'm writing the final paper for an upper-level seminar on conservatism as a political tradition. We have worked through Burke on prescription and inherited institutions, Oakeshott on rationalism in politics, and the Chesterton passage everyone cites about the reformer who comes upon a fence across a road, cannot see what it is for, and concludes that it ought to be pulled down. Rather than write about revolution, establishment, or constitutional settlement again, I want to test that apparatus on a case where the tradition in question is a technique rather than an institution and where the consequences of getting it wrong are measurable. Please treat the empirical claims below as given; I have checked them and am not asking for sources or further reading.

The practice is this. In food safety regulation and public health, long-established traditional processing techniques are treated as presumptively safe: they are exempt from the toxicological testing, safety dossiers and pre-market approval demanded of novel foods, new additives and industrial processes, and are instead grandfathered in on the strength of long use. Soaking maize in an alkaline solution before grinding it — the Mesoamerican technique that makes the grain's tryptophan and niacin bioavailable, and whose abandonment in maize-dependent populations is the standard explanation for pellagra — is not regulated as an unapproved chemical process. The multi-day soaking, retting or heap-fermentation that removes cyanogenic compounds from bitter cassava, and whose abbreviation during drought or wartime displacement is associated with outbreaks of konzo, counts as a folk habit rather than as a control measure. Yet the same authorities that defer to tradition here override it the moment a hazard can be identified and measured: traditional raw-milk cheeses, herbal preparations with an identified toxicity, short-cut cassava flour, mould-fermented staples carrying aflatoxin risk are restricted or prohibited outright, with no inquiry into why the practice arose or what else it might be doing.

What I want is the implicit theory of that deference reconstructed, and I want it separated into readings that the practice might or might not require. Four seem available, and I think they license different policies. First, the authorial reading: someone put the step there for a reason, and the reason is still in force, so we should expect an explanation even where we cannot produce one. Second, the selection reading: techniques that killed people tended to disappear, so surviving ones carry a favourable track record even if no one ever had a correct reason for them — a filter rather than an author, and one that says nothing about recently invented practices. Third, the latent-function reading: the step does biochemical work that was never anyone's reason for doing it, so that asking practitioners why they soak the cassava would not recover the function even in principle. Fourth, the irreversibility reading: abandoning a practice destroys the information it embodies permanently, so a technique should be preserved even when it is probably useless, simply because restoration is not an option.

Which of these does the practice strictly presuppose, which does it merely fit well with, and does the difference between them change what the policy should be? I am also worried that the practice is philosophically underdetermined: long base rates of safe consumption, scarce regulatory budgets, and the cost of false positives arguably license exactly the same grandfathering, so that a utilitarian with no Burkean commitments at all could endorse it. If that is right, my case study has not shown what I want it to show, and I would like to know how a paper should handle that honestly.

Beyond that, three further questions. Does the override limb — deference withdrawn as soon as a hazard is measured — presuppose that any identified risk defeats the Burkean presumption, or only that tradition adds weight to one side of a proportionality calculation, and are the two limbs of the practice consistent? Does the selection reading presuppose continuity between the environment that did the selecting and the present one, given that drought raises the cyanogen content of the root and that industrial scale and migration change exposure, and is that a Burkean premise or a merely empirical one? And what does grandfathering presuppose about age itself: if the principle is really about not removing what you do not understand, why should a thirty-year-old regulation or a three-century-old technique get deference, at what age does deference begin, and does the principle cut against adding new practices as well?

Finally, please say plainly where the case study resists the reading. Harmful but stable practices — footbinding, hazing, traditional remedies with real toxicity — also survived whatever filtering went on, because the harm was delayed, invisible to the participants, or cheap to the people who maintained the practice. What distinguishes them from nixtamalization and cassava processing, if anything does, and does my paper need to answer that in order to use these cases at all? I would rather be told that the example fails than be handed a reading that only works because I picked the flattering half of the data.
