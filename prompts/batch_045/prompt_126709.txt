I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working on a paper about moral worth and acting for the right reasons, and I've hit the point where I can't tell whether my framework has the resolution to handle the cases I actually care about. My supervisor thinks the whole scalar approach is a mistake and that moral worth is at bottom a binary matter of whether the right-making features figure in the explanation of the action in the right way; a friend from a conference last month thinks that any case where the agent gets things right for a derivative or second-best reason is really the same case, and that the apparent differences are artifacts of how I describe them. I suspect both are partly wrong, but I keep failing to say why. I've been working with four components and I'd like you to stress-test them rather than replace them wholesale: (i) Rightness — the action is in fact morally right or at least permissible; (ii) Guidance — the features that make it right figure non-accidentally in the causal and explanatory history of what the agent does, rather than being an after-the-fact rationalization; (iii) Conception — the agent represents those features as reasons, or as right-making, and not merely as motivating; and (iv) Robustness — across nearby variations in which the features the agent actually cites are absent, weakened, or swapped for other right-making features, the agent would still have acted rightly. I'm especially unsure about the fourth, and about whether (iii) should require the agent to represent the features as *normative* rather than merely as considerations she happens to weigh.

Here are ten cases I've been using. For each, assume the action is in fact morally right unless stated otherwise, and assume the agent is an ordinary adult with no unusual excusing conditions: (1) an analyst donates a large share of her income to a highly cost-effective charity, but her actual motivating reason is that giving is the only thing that reliably quiets a guilt she feels about her job; she verified the charity's effectiveness only because she wanted the guilt-relief to be genuine, and she would have given the same amount to whichever charity a colleague happened to recommend; (2) a field surgeon works brutal shifts out of absorbed professional habit and a real taste for the craft, and when asked why, says something about the patients needing her and her being able to help — an answer she would retract under mild conversational pressure and never actually uses to guide her; (3) someone returns a found wallet with a large sum and clear ID because she believes God has commanded returning lost property, while also believing that the content of God's commands is arbitrary and that had God commanded keeping found property, keeping it would have been right; (4) a researcher keeps a burdensome promise to co-author a paper solely because his professional reputation is the main asset he uses to do good work elsewhere, and his belief about that is correct; (5) a man dives into a canal and pulls out a struggling child without any deliberation at all, and afterwards cannot reconstruct any reason beyond some variant of she was there and she was mine — there is no detour through an impartial principle, not even the one that critics of impartial moral theories complain would be one thought too many; (6) a donor gives to an effective charity because she has read, incorrectly, that its principal effect is raising school attendance, when in fact its principal effect is reducing malaria; she would not have given had she known the mechanism, but would still have given had she been told the true amount of good per dollar; (7) a juror votes to convict on the strength of the evidence, is disposed to do so across the board, and also took a small bribe that she believed had no effect on her judgment and which in fact had none; (8) a nurse stays late with a dying patient partly because her ward manager is watching and she needs a decent performance review, and partly because she believes staying is what the patient needs and would have stayed had she been certain no one was watching — as it happens she was not certain; (9) a hospital's automated triage system recommends the ethically correct allocation of scarce beds and produces a justification citing the right considerations, where we stipulate for the sake of argument that nothing in the system counts as taking those considerations as reasons; (10) an activist turns out to protest a genuine injustice, but does so because the community she identifies with is turning out for it; had that community turned out for a different and comparably unjust cause she would have gone to that instead, and had it stayed home she would have stayed home too.

What I want concretely. First, rate each case from 0 to 10 for the moral worth of the action, with a one-sentence note on why it lands where it does; then sort all ten into tiers such that cases inside a tier are, on your view, within a point of each other. Second, give me a single number between 0 and 1 for what fraction of the total spread in moral worth across these ten cases is explained by the reasons-condition as opposed to the action's independently being right and its producing good consequences — and tell me which two cases do the most work in setting that number. Third, if one of my four components is doing no work, or is double-counting another, say which and re-rate the ten without it, flagging which cases move and by how much. Fourth, identify the two cases that a reasonable rival account would rate at least three points apart from your own rating, state the crux that separates the two ratings, and say what kind of further case — described concretely enough that I could write it up — would settle which rating is right. Finally, if there's a distinction the scheme needs that my four components don't capture, name it, define it operationally, and tell me which of the ten cases it discriminates between that I currently have to treat as equivalent.
