Two colleagues of mine — one in statistics, one in philosophy — spent the better part of a department lunch arguing about a question a friend of mine had asked one of them, and neither of them conceded anything. I was there for the whole exchange and genuinely cannot tell who won. I would like a move-by-move adjudication.

The setup. My friend has been offered a senior role at a company founded about two and a half years ago and has to decide whether to move her family for it. She asked what sounds like a simple question: what is the probability the company still exists, in something like its current form, three years from now?

1. Marcus said: do not answer with the headline base rate. The rule the frequency tradition arrived at for exactly this case is to use the narrowest reference class for which one still has usable statistics — here, companies of this size, in this sector, at this funding stage — because that rule uses the information one has rather than discarding it, and because the headline rate answers a question about a population she is not a member of.

2. Dana said the class he chose is contaminated: whether a company is still hiring, or still raising, at thirty months is causally downstream of not having failed yet, so conditioning on it builds survival into the class and inflates the frequency. Marcus's rule cannot see this, she said, because "narrowest class with usable statistics" is indifferent to whether membership in the class is a cause of the outcome, an effect of the outcome, or a symptom of a common cause — and every one of those three relations pulls the frequency in a different direction.

3. Marcus accepted the contamination point and switched to features fixed at the company's founding, but said that cannot be Dana's real objection, because her real objection is the old one: any individual case belongs to indefinitely many classes yielding different frequencies, and no refinement rule says which features to record in the first place. His reply was that this version is solvable in principle: do not pick a class by intuition, pick a *rule* for picking classes, and score candidate rules by how well they would have predicted cases they were not built from. That turns a standoff into model selection with an empirical answer.

4. Dana said the move fails, and fails instructively: out-of-sample scoring needs its own reference class — a set of past prediction problems taken to be relevantly similar to this one — so the original difficulty reappears one level up as a class of classes. She added that it reappears in a worse form, since the historical record of prediction problems is thin and itself selected: reference-class guesses that failed quietly in someone's meeting never enter the record.

5. Marcus conceded the regress but not the conclusion, and changed the question. For a decision with a lumpy downside — she would be pulling two children out of school and leaving a stable job — the central rate is the wrong summary statistic. What matters is the bottom of the distribution of outcomes: what share of outcomes in the broad class are catastrophic, and how bad. Two classes can share a mean and differ entirely where it matters. Asking "what is the probability" was malformed; asking "what spread of outcomes would I be walking into" is not.

6. Dana agreed the reframing was an improvement and denied that it helped: an expected-utility calculation, or a look at the tail, still needs probabilities over outcome categories, and those still come from some class, so the bump has moved rather than gone. She then pressed a point neither of them resolved: my friend's question is not about a class of companies observed from outside. If she takes the job she becomes part of the causal process whose outcome she is asking about, so the class she really wants — companies that hired someone with her profile into a role like this one — is one whose membership is altered by the decision itself, and "probability of survival" and "probability of survival given that I join" are different quantities that no observed reference class gives her directly.

Please adjudicate it as argued. Specifically: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each; then say who is more right overall and, more importantly, what both of them are missing. I would particularly value your view on four things: whether Dana's regress argument is sound or Marcus's model-selection move genuinely breaks it; whether a causal-homogeneity requirement — that an admissible class must screen the case off from further relevant information — is the right extra constraint on classes and whether it resolves the problem or merely relocates it into causal modelling; whether the decision-theoretic point in move 5 dissolves the problem or presupposes a solution to it; and whether the intervention-dependence in move 6 shows that some single-case probability questions are not answerable from frequency considerations at all.

Two further wrinkles you should feel free to use. First, my friend's question said "in something like its current form," and it occurred to me later that the outcome event is itself vaguely specified — acquisition, pivot, rename and downsizing are all survival in one sense and not in another — so there may be a second indeterminacy prior to and independent of the choice of class. Second, both debaters seem to assume throughout that base rates are the main evidence available; I do not know whether that assumption is itself the thing to attack, since she could in principle learn about runway, burn rate, customer concentration and the founders' plans. If the right verdict is that for some of these questions there is no fact of the matter, say exactly which, say why, and say what my friend should be told instead of a number.
