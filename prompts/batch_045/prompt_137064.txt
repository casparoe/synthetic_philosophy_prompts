I've gotten weirdly into making my own Bongard problems lately — the ones with six little figures on the left that all obey some rule and six on the right that break it — partly for a puzzle night and partly because I wanted to use them in a first-year reasoning course as a hands-on example of underdetermination (theory by data, all that). But I keep having the same argument with two friends and I don't have the vocabulary to say what's bugging me.

Friend A says any rule that correctly sorts all twelve figures is a solution, full stop. Friend B says no, there's the rule the problem was built around, and a rule can sort correctly and still be the wrong answer. My first instinct was to file friend B's complaint under the old grue-style worry: some candidate rules are gerrymandered, or obviously reverse-engineered off this exact dozen of figures, or just wouldn't hold up on a new figure, and I get why those feel like cheats. But I've hit cases that don't feel like cheating in that way at all, and I can't tell if it's the same complaint in disguise or a genuinely different one. Here's one: the left box has six figures, each a big closed shape with a smaller shape strictly inside it, not touching the outline anywhere; the right box has the same kind of pairs, but the small shape always pokes through or is tangent to the outline. Two rules both seperate the boxes: "the small shape lies strictly inside, with no contact," and "the small shape's area is under 40% of the big one's" — I drew the right-hand ones with chunkier inner shapes, so the threshold works, and it's not a gerrymander: it's a single continuous feature that would apply fine to a new figure and would survive any grue-ish test I can think of. Still, it's plainly not the answer. The best I can say about why is that the right-hand figures look like someone took a left-hand figure and changed exactly one thing, and the good rule is the one that names the thing that got changed, while my area rule picks up a difference that just happens to be there. Same shape of thing in a second case: left box has a triangle and a circle with the triangle's tip aimed at the circle, right box has the same two shapes with the tip aimed away — and a rule like "the two shapes' centroids are farther apart than the triangle is tall" also sorts them, also isn't arbitrary, also isn't what's being asked.

So: is there an established concept for this — for the difference between a rule that merely seperates the two classes and a rule that answers the contrast the problem set up, one that's the thing the right-hand figures are near-misses *of*? Related things I've half-heard of and want distinguished rather than just named: the grue/projectibility business (is contrast-insensitivity just a species of non-projectibility, or independent of it?), the linguistic idea of a minimal pair, the thought that a why-question comes with a contrast class or a focus (I remember something roughly like that from a philosophy of science class), and — from an ML friend — "shortcut learning," which doesn't seem right either, since my area rule isn't a spurious correlate that will break on new data, it's a real feature that genuinely tracks the intended class and still misses the point. And note the wrinkle that not every problem of mine has the near-miss structure: sometimes the right-hand figures are just assorted things that lack the property, and there the thing I'm griping about seems to evaporate and collapse back into plain simplicity or naturalness. So if there's no name for it, I'd like you to coin one, define it tightly enough that I can apply it, and tell me where its edges are — including whether it's really a property of the rule, of the pair (rule, problem), or of the person who drew the figures.
