You are a philosophical logician and formal epistemologist who works at the intersection of the paradoxes of self-reference and the epistemic foundations of game theory. Write a careful, self-contained essay of roughly 1,200–1,500 words on the problem set out below.

Before you draft anything, do the mathematics. Work out the tails, the hazards, and the relevant optima for every procedure you discuss; check your arithmetic twice; and make sure each quantitative claim in the essay is one a reader could verify by hand from the numbers you give. Start by brainstorming: list three or four candidate procedures the teacher might use and their rough surprise-profiles before you settle on which one is optimal. Take the setup exactly as stated — do not quietly substitute an easier variant of the paradox, and do not lean on what you remember of the published literature. You may allude to a well-known position in passing, but the argument must stand entirely on its own, with no citations, footnotes, quotations, or named authorities.

The problem. On Friday afternoon a teacher tells a student: There will be exactly one exam next week, on one of Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday or Friday. It will be a surprise — when it comes, you will not have been able to see it coming. Assume (i) the student is an ideal Bayesian whose credences come from conditionalizing one prior on the announcement and on the observed absence of the exam on earlier days; (ii) the student knows the teacher's selection procedure, i.e. the distribution p over the five days, the teacher knows that she knows, and so on; and the teacher's truthfulness is likewise common knowledge. Treat (ii) as the load-bearing assumption of the whole discussion and say explicitly what happens when it is relaxed. Assume (iii) a scoring rule: at the start of at most one day the student may announce that the exam is that day, and she wins if it is. The teacher's payoff is 1 if the exam is a surprise on the day it occurs, plus 0.1 for each day the exam is deferred (Monday = +0, Friday = +0.4).

You are to adjudicate the announcement, and to do that you must first say what surprise comes to. Here are three precisifications, for a fixed threshold t (take t = 1/2, but say whether the choice of threshold is doing any real work, or whether the same dialectic runs for every t < 1):

(C) Occurrent credence. The exam on day d is a surprise iff the student's credence that morning, the hazard h(d) = p(d)/T(d) where T(d) is the tail Σ_{e≥d} p(e), is at most t.

(A) Attainable predictive success, assessed at the time. The exam on day d is a surprise iff, on that morning, the best she can still do — max over e ≥ d of p(e)/T(d), since she may guess that day or any later day — is at most t.

(E) Attainable predictive success, assessed ex ante. The exam is a surprise iff her best plan from Sunday night, max over d of p(d), is at most t.

Compare at least these two procedures, and any others you think competitive: (U) the uniform distribution over the five days; (G) a fair coin tossed each morning from Monday onwards, heads meaning the exam is that day, with the exam on Friday if the first four tosses are all tails (so p = 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/16).

Questions your essay must settle. Under (C) and (A) the last day with positive probability always carries a hazard of 1, so no procedure known to the student can make the announcement guaranteed true; work out how well the teacher can do instead — what is the maximum attainable probability that the exam is a surprise, and which procedure attains it? Is (G) optimal, or can some other distribution beat it? Does the answer turn on whether the threshold is read strictly or non-strictly, and is there anything special about t = 1/2? Notice that (G) holds the hazard flat at 1/2 until Friday and say what that knife-edge case reveals about the mechanism behind the classical elimination of Friday, then Thursday, and so on. Under (E), by contrast, ask whether the announcement collapses at all: note that (E) is a property of the procedure as a whole rather than of the day, and that the plan that is best ex ante may not be the plan the student would want to follow once she has actually reached a later day; then say what that dynamic-inconsistency point does to (E)'s credentials as an explication of surprise. Separately, ask whether the classical elimination argument is an instance of the familiar claim that common knowledge of rationality forces backward induction in finite games of perfect information: do the standard worries about that claim — in particular that the induction credits the student with rationality at nodes that the optimal path of play reaches only with small probability — carry over here, or is the disanalogy substantive, given that the exam argument is a fixed-point condition on a self-referential announcement rather than a solution concept for a game tree? Finally, given the teacher's payoff, what will a rational teacher actually do, and is the announcement true or false at that choice?

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. "Setup, notation, and the assumptions I am making" — about 150 words. Fix your notation and flag which assumptions you will later revisit.
2. "The graded collapse: what the classical argument really establishes" — about 350 words. Include a small table giving the tails and hazards for (U) and (G) so the arithmetic is checkable.
3. "The case that the announcement survives" — about 350 words. The (E) reading, the ex-ante-versus-interim point, the counterfactual or off-path objection to the elimination argument, and the game-theoretic reading of the teacher's choice.
4. "Verdict" — about 300 words. Commit: which precisification is the right one for adjudicating the teacher's announcement; whether, under it, the announcement is satisfiable with certainty, satisfiable only with high probability, or not satisfiable at all; and whether the classical no-surprise-exam argument is sound, unsound, or sound but not genuinely paradoxical. Do not split the difference between the readings.
5. "What would change my mind" — about 200 words. Name the single argument or consideration that would most likely reverse your verdict, and locate precisely where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong: a mis-specified threshold, the common-knowledge-of-the-procedure assumption, the ex-ante-versus-interim distinction, the treatment of the announcement as a fixed point, or a slip in the arithmetic.
