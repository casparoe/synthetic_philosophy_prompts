I'm in the second term of a graduate seminar on population ethics and suffering-focused ethics, and I have to submit a short thesis prospectus in a fortnight. The thesis itself would be about nine months of work, single-authored, roughly twelve thousand words, and it has to end in a defended verdict rather than a survey: whether some version of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism should guide cause prioritization, i.e. whether the reduction of extreme suffering really does swamp the production of ordinary happiness, or whether that conclusion is an artefact of how the view is set up.

Let me state the target view in my own terms so we don't talk past each other. It has four parts: (1) the only non-instrumentally good or bad features of a life are pleasant and unpleasant experiences, understood as admitting of scalar intensity and duration; (2) below some intensity threshold, pleasures and pains are commensurable and trade off against each other on a single cardinal scale; (3) above that threshold, the disvalue of pain is lexically dominant, so no amount of sub-threshold pleasure, however vast or however distributed across persons, can compensate for a single episode of suffering above it; (4) the view is negative in that the avoidance and relief of suffering is where the practical action is, and the threshold is precisely what is supposed to stop it collapsing into the familiar reductio that we ought to painlessly extinguish everyone, since ordinary lives contain a good deal of mild suffering.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a literature review and not a reading list. I can find and read the sources myself, so please don't give me citations or suggestions for further reading. Here is the candidate pool as I currently see it, though part of what I want is for you to tell me which of these are the real cruxes and which are noise.

The calibration problem: the threshold has to be set so that ordinary human lives clear it, on pain of reinstating the extinction implication, but so that the worst suffering we have decent testimony about falls above it, on pain of the view being extensionally indistinguishable from total utilitarianism in every case anyone cares about. Can that be done non-arbitrarily, or is the threshold always going to look like a dial turned to whatever the theorist's prior conclusions were?

The question whether the lexical claim is axiological or deliberative: whether it says which worlds are better, or only which ones an agent may bring about or permit. These can come apart, and I suspect the distinction quietly does a lot of work in the debate.

Two aggregation questions that I think people conflate. Whether the threshold blocks compensation within a single life, so that a life containing a few minutes of agony plus eighty years of contentment is not worth living, is a different question from whether it blocks compensation across lives, so that no number of mildly contented lives could justify one episode of agony. A view can take quite different shapes depending on whether it is intrapersonal, interpersonal, or both.

A decision-theoretic worry I haven't seen handled: if our credences over the intensity of some experience are continuous, then lexicographic orderings are not representable by anything like an ordinary expectation, and infinitesimal probabilities seem to be doing all the work. So what does the view actually recommend to an agent who is uncertain whether a given state clears the threshold?

And the question my supervisor takes to be upstream of all of it: whether pleasure and pain are commensurable in the first place, whether intensities are interpersonally comparable, and what it would even mean to settle that.

Two variants of my own I'd like you to assess rather than humour. The first makes the view symmetric by adding a floor: experiences below some intensity contribute nothing at all, so there is a dead zone at the bottom of the scale, and I want to know whether the floor and the lexical ceiling could reasonably be set independently or whether symmetry is unmotivated. The second replaces the sharp cut with a narrow band of intensities over which the disvalue of pain rises very steeply but continuously, which removes the cliff-edge objection at the cost, I suspect, of removing the lexicality too.

Rank the four or five questions you think a nine-month thesis should actually engage, and defend the ordering. I care about the criteria at least as much as the list: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for one person with nine months and no empirical data, which are neglected rather than merely unresolved, and what argument or consideration would move something up or down the list. Tell me if one of my two variants is a dead end, and tell me if my framing of the view has smuggled in something it shouldn't. If you think the commensurability question really does swallow the others, say so and say what I should do with nine months if that's true.
