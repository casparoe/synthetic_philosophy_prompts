I sit on the standing review panel of a national research council's high-risk "Frontiers" scheme, and we are mid-term reviewing a five-year programme grant, *Pre-Selection Without Words*, which asks whether any non-human animal preselects among possible actions — trying out candidate moves in imagination, as it were, and letting the bad ones die cheaply — rather than merely repeating whatever a history of reinforcement has stamped in. The interim report describes what the applicants call the "costly check" result: in a novel apparatus where the food is visible from the start and where lifting a weighted inspection flap costs the bird both time and priority of access, most of the crows lift the flap before committing, on their very first encounter with the apparatus. The applicants read this as preselection: the birds pay a real price to eliminate a possibility before acting on it. One of our panel members reads the same data as a Skinnerian creature doing exactly what a long reinforcement history for investigating anything novel would predict, and says the experiment was never designed to tell the two apart. Both readings feel genuinely available to me, and I cannot see what would break the tie.

What makes it worse — or more interesting — is a wrinkle raised at our last meeting. The apparatus is itself informative: the flap's weight, the transparent panel, the way the reward sits in the tube, all encode the answer in a form a bird could simply read off the world rather than compute in its head, so that what looks like foresight may be the environment doing the preselecting for a creature that only has to respond to the result. If that is right, we seem to have a mind that achieves Gregorian performance — real leverage from information-bearing structures outside the skin — without the Popperian machinery that the familiar four-rung ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures (Dennett, I gather; I came to it secondhand) treats as its prerequisite. The rungs stop being cumulative, and our welfare working group's quiet assumption that these classifications track a single scale of inner richness comes apart. That matters concretely here, because the council intends to use this programme's outcome to set enrichment and husbandry standards for corvids kept by member institutions, and possibly to decide which species go onto an enhanced-protection list; if the competence lives in the niche rather than in the animal, the practical requirement may be to preserve the scaffold rather than to credit the mind.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to our board before it meets on 12 March. It should recommend one concrete course of action on the second tranche — release it, release it re-profiled, or withhold it pending a specified change — with a date or milestone attached, and it should state plainly what result eighteen months from now would settle the question either way. Assume the memo will be shared with the applicants, so the decision rule has to be something they could actually go and answer in a laboratory rather than an invitation to produce a deeper philosophical argument. It should also engage seriously the objection I expect from our comparative psychologist: that no behavioural signature can distinguish preselection occurring inside the animal from preselection performed by a structured environment, and that in any case welfare protection should track what an animal can suffer rather than where it sits on a ladder of cognitive kinds — so that, as she put it at our last meeting, both our research question and our enrichment debate are expensive ways of asking something we already know the answer to. If you think she is right, say so and recommend accordingly; if you think she is partly right, say which part and what follows for the tranche.
