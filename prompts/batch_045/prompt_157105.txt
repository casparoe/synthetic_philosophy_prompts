I am a novelist, and I am some way into a book whose central scene is a single long meeting of a flood-relief committee in an invented northern town. The committee has one grant left. It can pay for an expensive operation that will save one named child's sight, or it can fund a drainage scheme that will spare several hundred households a winter of damp, ruined plaster and chronic coughs. What interests me is not the outcome but the shape of the arguments, and I have been trying to let the committee argue in the idiom of the contractualism associated with T. M. Scanlon, on which an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that no one, suitably motivated, could reasonably reject.

My device is this. The chair, an ageing parish clerk with no patience for theory, insists that any decision be defensible door to door: the committee must be able to send one of its members to the house of every single loser, alone, and give that person a reason she could accept. Halfway through the book a rival councillor proposes the opposite, which I call the town-hall procedure: the justification is delivered once, to everyone assembled, and what it has to answer is the cumulative complaint of the room. I did not invent these as allegories, but I have noticed that they track a dispute I keep meeting in the literature, and that characters who accept one procedure and reject the other simply cannot argue with each other at all.

The dispute runs roughly as follows. Contractualism appears to weigh objections person by person, setting the strength of one individual's complaint against the strength of another's, and so the numbers seem to drop out entirely: no sum of trivial harms can outweigh one serious harm. Almost nobody accepts that result, and contractualists have proposed various ways of letting numbers back in, by comparing the many severally rather than adding them up, by appealing to some residual tie-breaking consideration, or by confining the view to a narrower domain. Their critics answer that these manoeuvres either smuggle in the consequentialism the view was meant to replace or leave the numbers doing no real work, and that this is a reason to give up the framework; the contractualists reply that the critics have silently helped themselves to a consequentialist picture of what a moral reason is. As far as I can tell this exchange has been running for some decades and nobody has moved.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about numbers at all but about the address of a justification: whether a moral reason must be one that could be given to each person taken separately, or to the affected people taken together; and that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading. But I would like that hypothesis tested rather than confirmed.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it verbal, a clash of bedrock intuitions about the separateness of persons that neither side can argue for, a substantive disagreement about the nature and force of reasons, or a hidden question — about what a principle is for, perhaps — doing all the work? Does my door-to-door and town-hall distinction isolate the real crux, or is it novelistic scenery that misdescribes it? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all, and why? And finally, the part I care about as a writer: if the disagreement is of the kind you say it is, how should I stage it so that a reader feels the two procedures failing to engage, rather than my merely reporting that philosophers disagree?
