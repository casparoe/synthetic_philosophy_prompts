I work on data infrastructure — distributed systems, a fair amount of variance decomposition, and a lot of arguments with colleagues about whether a regression on observational data licenses any causal claim. I have no philosophy background beyond that. I recently got pulled into a discussion about what "heritable" means, discovered that I had strong gut reactions to a series of inferences people draw from heritability numbers and no idea what principle I was applying, and started writing the cases down. I would like to know whether my verdicts add up to anything.

To keep this from turning into a fight about the empirical literature, I have written the cases with invented traits and invented numbers. Please take the numbers as stipulated. By h² I mean the usual ratio: genetic variance over total phenotypic variance, in a specified population, over the range of environments that population actually occupies.

1. Baseline. In population P, h²(T) = 0.8. Claim: "Most of the differences in T among people in P track genetic differences." — Fine.

2. Individual decomposition. Same number. Claim: "So for any given person in P, 80% of their T comes from their genes and 20% from their environment, and a sufficiently good measurement of both contributions would return that split." — Not fine. It feels like a category mistake. But I do not want to conclude that the 0.8 is meaningless, because I accepted it in (1).

3. Nothing to decompose. A clonal population C under tightly controlled conditions: no genetic variance, essentially no environmental variance, only measurement noise. Depending on how the noise is handled, h² is undefined or around zero. Claim: "So T isn't genetic in C." — Not fine; the trait is produced by the same genes there as anywhere.

4. Zero heritability, wholly genetic. Trait U: whether a person has the standard number of digits on the left hand. In P the variance comes almost entirely from accidents and surgery; h²(U) ≈ 0.02. Claim: "So genes have nothing to do with U." — Not fine.

5. Malleability. Back to T, h² = 0.8 in P. An intervention is introduced — a nutrient, a training protocol, pick whichever you like — and the mean rises by most of a standard deviation, the whole distribution shifting. Claim, made before anyone knew about the intervention: "h² is 0.8, so there is not much room for environmental interventions here." — Not fine. I would go further and say the estimate gives no evidence at all about that claim, which is stronger than "weak evidence," and I am not sure I can defend the stronger version.

6. Between groups. P divides into two subpopulations differing in average environment. Within each, h²(T) ≈ 0.8. The means differ by 0.9 standard deviations. Claim: "So the gap between them is genetic." — Not fine.

7. Same conclusion, different evidence. Individuals are moved at random between the two subpopulations' environments and re-measured; the gap is unchanged. Claim: "At least part of the gap is genetic." — Fine, and I would act on it.

Pair (6)/(7) is one of two that trouble me. I reject what is essentially the same conclusion in (6) and accept it in (7). If the answer is "heritability is a within-population statistic, and (7) is an experiment," that is true but not yet satisfying: I do not know what it is about an experiment that licenses the causal conclusion here, and I suspect that whatever it is will also tell me something about (5).

8. The number moves when the environment does. A trait with h² = 0.5 in P. Environments are made uniform across P and the trait re-measured: h² is now 0.8. Claim: "The genes got stronger; the trait became more genetic." — Not fine. But I also do not want to say the new estimate is wrong, since it correctly describes the new situation. Related claim: "Since h² went up, the equalization did not help." — Also not fine, though this looks like a restatement of my reason for rejecting (5), and I want to know whether it is the same point or a different one.

9. The pathway case. Trait V is hours per week spent practicing a skill. h²(V) = 0.6 in P, and the mechanism is entirely indirect: a genetic propensity leads some children to seek out practice, and it is the practice that produces the skill. Is V genetic or environmental? My verdict: I am happy to say both that 60% of the variance in V is attributable to genetic differences and that V is, causally speaking, an environmental variable, and I do not think these conflict. This is the second case that troubles me, but for an odd reason: in my own work, if someone told me that 60% of the variance in database time was explained by request size, and that request size was in turn set by client configuration, I would have no discomfort whatsoever. So either the discomfort here is purely psychological, a leftover from the nature/nurture framing, or something is genuinely different, and I cannot tell which.

10. Crossing. Genotype A outperforms B in environment E1; B outperforms A in E2. Measured over P, which contains a mix of E1 and E2, h² = 0.4. Verdict: 0.4 is a perfectly good description of P, but "A is genetically better" has no answer, and there is no environment-free fact of the matter about how genetic the trait is.

11. Scale. h²(T) = 0.6, while h²(log T) = 0.35. Verdict: uneasy, and I cannot tell whether this is a real problem or a quibble. Tell me if it is a quibble and I will drop it.

My tentative principle, which I do not think survives my own list: a heritability estimate is a statement about the actual sources of variance in a specified population occupying a specified range of environments, and it licenses no inference about individuals, about other populations, about environments outside that range, or about what would happen under any intervention. I like this right up to the point where I notice that it appears to make heritability nearly useless — and I do not believe that, because I treat the number as informative in (1), in (7), and in the selective-breeding case I have not listed. So either the principle is too strong or I do not understand what it commits me to.

What I would like from you:

a) A principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could implement it as a rule — given an estimate and a claim, say whether the estimate licenses the claim — with the case that motivates each clause.

b) Which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which you would overturn. Be explicit about (6)/(7), about (5) versus (8), and about (9).

c) What h² positively licenses. If it is not individuals, not other populations, and not interventions, then what is it good for? I would like this in terms of what I could do with the number, not only in terms of what I must not say.

d) Verdicts on two cases I have not given verdicts on. First: a within-family design that compares siblings and so holds between-family environment constant returns a much lower h² than the population estimate — which of the two is "the" heritability, and what does the gap between them mean? Second: an intervention pushes an environmental variable outside the range it currently occupies in P — is the existing estimate evidence about its effect, and how much?

e) If what I actually want is some counterfactual quantity — how far T would move if we moved this variable — then say what that quantity is, in your own terms, without appeal to references or to what it is called in the literature; whether it is estimable from the kinds of data heritability estimates come from; and how it relates to h². If it is not estimable from those data, say that plainly. And if the honest conclusion is that h² is the wrong statistic for every question I actually care about, tell me that too.
