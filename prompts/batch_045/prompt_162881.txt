I'm a novelist, about ninety pages into a first-person thing narrated by a retired estate manager who is quietly lying to himself about something that happened thirty years ago, and my beta readers keep saying two contradictory things at once: half of them are convinced he's being ironic, half are convinced he's sincere, and one of them wrote 'I can't tell whether he knows he's doing it.' That's more or less the effect I'm after, but I'd like to understand the machinery before I commit to three hundred more pages, because right now I'm just doing it by ear. Someone in my writers group said I should look at Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day with Grice in hand, so: read that novel through Grice on implicature and speaker meaning. I've read the Grice material before — cooperative principle, the maxims, flouting versus violating, particularized versus generalized implicature, calculability, what is said versus what is implicated — so please don't re-explain it from scratch; I want the cases where it bends. I've been using a rough three-way taxonomy in my notes and I'd like you to take it apart: narrator-implicature, where the fictional speaker means something he'd deny meaning; author-implicature, where the real author means something the narrator manifestly does not; and what I've been calling 'residue,' where the reader infers things nobody in the chain intends, mostly from background knowledge Stevens doesn't have about the 1930s and about what actually became of Lord Darlington's reputation. Does Grice's apparatus, which ties speaker meaning pretty tightly to a speaker's reflexive intentions, have any room for the second and third of those, or do you have to posit two speakers to make the novel come out? And what about the 'extended' case: Grice's own examples are single remarks between people who are, broadly, cooperating — the recommendation letter, the stranded motorist — whereas here the understatement is systematic across a whole book, and the reader's inference is distributed over two hundred pages of small evasions, so it's not obvious the implicature is calculable from any one utterance. If Grice is the wrong tool, say so; if he's the right tool and my narrator is just doing a cruder version of what Stevens does, say that too.

I'd like it anchored in actual scenes rather than a general vibe: the business with his father dying upstairs while he's serving at the conference, the way he handles Miss Kenton and the sentimental novel she's reading, his dithering attempts to define dignity, the stuff about practising bantering at the end, and the pier scene. Which of those actually repay a Gricean reading, and which would you leave alone? Also be honest in the other direction — where does forcing Grice onto the book flatten it, and what does the novel know about meaning that the theory doesn't? My own half-formed suspicion is that the book is quietly arguing that a life can mean something its owner didn't mean, and that this is a kind of implicature with no speaker behind it, which sounds like it might be nonsense but also sounds like the reason the ending lands. Please don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll fill those in myself, and don't quote lines at me — I've got the book right here and I'd rather you describe the scenes than get wording slightly wrong from memory. What I really want at the end is the practical version: what Ishiguro is calibrating that I should steal, and which of his tricks depend on this particular narrator and would definitely fall apart in mine.
