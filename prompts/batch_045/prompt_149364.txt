I'm on a three-person committee rewriting the house style guide for our engineering standards docs at work, and I keep hitting the same conversation-stopper.

We proposed swapping out the old man-compounds (manpower, man-hours, unmanned) and dropping generic he. One of the senior engineers -- smart guy, genuinely not being a jerk about it -- said: man is just the unmarked form, the unmarked form is the default, defaults are invisible, so using it doesn't say anything about anybody. When we pushed back he added: you can't fix the world by editing the dictionary. And that was more or less the end of the discussion, which bugs me, because I think one of those two lines is doing all the work and I can't tell which.

So: what are the precise things a person could be claiming when they say man is just the unmarked default? I want a menu, not a single verdict -- from the boring, almost-trivial readings up to the wild ones. For each one, please say (a) in plain terms what it actually asserts, and whether it's a claim about words, about speakers, about hearers, about society, or about how people think; (b) whether it's a platitude, an empirical claim that could turn out false, or something probably false; and (c) whether it would actually get my colleague to so leave the wording alone, or whether it cuts the other way -- i.e. whether the default being real is itself the problem. I dont really have a linguistics background, so don't assume I know what any of the technical vocabulary means.

Three things I'd like you to use while you're at it, because these are the bits I can't seperate myself:

1. A friend of mine distinguishes a live default from a fossil default. A fossil is just a historical leftover that nobody's grammar or comprehension actually consults any more -- whatever is going on in a word whose etymology nobody knows, say. A live default is what you actually get when the context doesn't decide it for you, and the thing you have to spend effort to override. Which of the readings need the default to be live, and which are fine with it being a fossil? And is there any cheap way to tell the two apart in a particular case, or is that just an empirical slog?

2. Maybe the default isn't in the language at all. Most of the engineers in our field are men. So when somebody reads a sentence like the operator should check his gauge before startup and pictures a guy, that could be base rates doing the work rather than the word. If it's mostly the world and not the lexicon, does that change which reading of the slogan is true? It seems like it should also mean the default would quietly evaporate once the field's demographics change, but I don't trust that inference.

3. There's an old satire about this -- I'm fairly sure it's by Douglas Hofstadter -- where somebody writes a prissy, self-important defense of purifying English by replacing man with person everywhere (chairperson, personpower, personhole covers, that sort of thing), and the joke is that it reads as absurd and preachy in a way the original wording doesn't. I feel the sting, but what does the sting actually show? That the default really is male-as-norm? Or just that any unfamiliar variant is marked and costs more to process -- in which case the feeling is about novelty and not about gender at all? Here's the control I thought of: compare flipping to a female default with flipping to a neutral one, womanpower versus personpower. If both feel equally weird, weirdness seems to be tracking unfamiliarity; if the female one is worse, that's something else. Is that control any good, and what result would support which reading?

Also, someone on our internal listserv pointed at old statutory interpretation rules -- the kind that say masculine-gendered words are to be read as including the feminine, with no clause going the other way -- as proof that the masculine really is the neutral default in English. I don't know whether that's even accurate, but suppose it is: that looks to me like a stipulation somebody wrote down rather than a discovery about the language, and I want to know which reading of the slogan, if any, a rule like that actually supports.

Last thing: which of these readings do you think people who say this line are usually aiming at? And is the can't fix the world by editing the dictionary line even about the same subject, or did he quietly switch topics on us?
