Six months ago I was appointed to the allocations committee of the Mercer Valley Community Health Trust, which disburses roughly four million dollars a year in small grants across our twelve districts: housing navigation, mobile clinics, school nursing, benefits advice, a meals service, a home-visiting programme for new parents. We meet four times a year and typically have to rank about thirty proposals against a fixed pot. I have no training in philosophy; two of my colleagues are clinicians, one is a retired accountant, one is a county councillor, one runs a food bank. Every decision we make has to survive three audiences: the board that ratifies it, the applicants we turn down, and the residents who lose a service because we moved the money.

I am writing because of what happened at our last meeting. We very nearly approved a proposal whose main effect was to close an existing outreach service in our two least-deprived districts and use most of the saving to fund a disparities monitoring post, so that the measured gap between the best-served and worst-served districts would narrow. No district would have gained any service. Several thousand residents would have lost one. When I objected that a redistribution cannot be an improvement if it leaves some people worse off and nobody better off, however much more equal the result looks, I was told that narrowing the gap is simply what equity means, and that if I disagreed I should come back with a rule the committee could actually apply. I have been asked to bring that rule to our September meeting.

Since then we have provisionally agreed to score proposals in a prioritarian way: estimate the benefit a proposal produces for its recipients, then multiply that benefit by a weight which is larger the worse off the recipients are, with the weights declining smoothly as well-being rises. I want that idea turned into something we can run. Two features of our situation make this harder than the idea sounds, and I want both handled rather than waved at.

The first is our data. The only well-being information we have is the Trust's deprivation index, which orders districts and places them in deciles; it does not tell us, even roughly, how far apart two districts are in anything like a common unit. So we can either attach our priority weights to the district's rank or decile, or we can invent a cardinal scale that the index does not really support. I need to know what this choice costs us. In particular, I have a worry I cannot settle myself: if the weight a benefit receives depends on where its recipients sit relative to other people, have we quietly bought back the relational view that my objection above was supposed to rule out, or does a rank-relative weighting remain immune to it? Say which, and say what the procedure should test for instead of testing for that.

The second is that our founding document commits us to narrowing disparities, and the board will not amend that sentence. So the procedure cannot simply forbid relational considerations; it has to tell us how to recognise a component of a proposal whose whole point is to change the gap rather than anyone's condition, what to do with such a component, and how to write the reason for rejecting it in a paragraph the board will accept.

What I am asking for, concretely:

A written procedure a committee member can work through on a single proposal in about twenty minutes, with the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it hands the decision back to a human being because the question is one of values rather than of fact. If there is exactly one such value question — I suspect it is how steep the priority weights should be, that is, how much a small benefit to the very worst off is worth compared with a much larger benefit to the merely badly off — I want it identified as such, and I want the procedure to be honest that it cannot supply the answer rather than smuggling one in.

The procedure needs to say what we do when a benefit cannot be estimated at all. This matters to me because the proposals aimed at the worst off are reliably the ones with the weakest evidence and the least measurable outcomes, and a procedure that demands a number will systematically defund them; but I also do not want uncertainty converted into a blank cheque.

It also needs to handle proposals that change the deprivation ranking they are scored against. A successful home-visiting programme in a poor district will move that district up the index over several years and therefore reduce the priority weight its future benefits receive. Is that a cost, a benefit, or neither, and how should we score it?

Then apply the finished procedure to three cases.

One: two proposals to be ranked against each other. A home-visiting nursing programme reaching about two hundred families in our two most-deprived deciles, costing nine hundred thousand dollars, with modest but unusually well-evidenced benefits per family. Against a region-wide falls-prevention and vaccination outreach reaching about eleven thousand residents, mostly in the middle deciles, costing six hundred thousand dollars, with large and well-measured benefits per resident. Give the numbers whatever common unit of benefit you think we can defend, show the ranking the procedure yields, and — this is the part I most want — give us the range of the steepness parameter over which each proposal wins, including the crossover value at which the ranking flips. If the honest answer is that the procedure ranks them one way at any steepness we might reasonably choose, say so; if it is that the answer depends entirely on a number the procedure will not give us, say that instead.

Two: a proposal of the kind that caused the argument — one that improves no one's condition and narrows a gap by withdrawing service from better-off residents. I want to see the procedure reach and state a no, and I want the one-paragraph published reason that goes with it.

Three: a case where the procedure tells us something we do not want to hear. Our flagship meals service for isolated elderly residents in the worst-off districts is the proposal the committee is most attached to; its cost per unit of benefit is very high. If the procedure rejects it even at the steepest weighting we could defend, I would rather learn that from you than from an applicant.

Finally, tell us what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements it will leave exactly as it found them, which of its steps are judgement dressed up as arithmetic, and where a committee like mine can expect to go wrong by trusting it too much.
