I'm writing a term paper for a reading group on the unity of the proposition, and I keep getting stuck on a distinction that looked crisp in my notes but frays the moment Bradley's regress turns up. Borrowing some vocabulary I've made up for the purpose, I've been separating what I call *binding unity* — whatever it is that turns a plurality of items (a particular, a relation, another particular) into one structured item rather than a mere list or aggregate — from what I call *truth-evaluable unity* — whatever it is about that structured item in virtue of which it is the sort of thing that can be true or false at all, and true or false in a determinate way, so that a proposition built from the same constituents in a different order is a different proposition even when the two cannot come apart in truth value, and so that a proposition that happens to be false is no less unified for that. My supervisor's complaint is that I've merely given two names to one problem, and I'd like to know whether he is right.

The comparison I want is between two readings of what the regress is actually an argument for. On the *binding* reading, the complaint is about composition: however many further relations we add in the hope of tying the original relation to its terms, each addition is just one more item in the plurality, so the demand for structure is regenerated at every step and no structured whole ever emerges; the moral is that unity cannot be contributed by a constituent, and the familiar Fregean–Russellian cures — treating the relation as unsaturated and hence not an item of the same logical kind as its terms, or insisting that relations simply do relate rather than needing to be related — are best understood as attempts to switch off the regenerating step rather than to satisfy it. On the *truth-evaluable* reading, the complaint is not about how the items get fastened together but about how the result could ever say anything: how it could be the kind of item that bears a truth value, or that represents one term as bearing the relation to the other rather than the other way about. On this reading the regress is a symptom of an explanatory demand rather than of a compositional failure — for every relational truth a ground, and for every ground a further relation, without end — and the natural cure is not to demote the relation from full-fledged itemhood but to refuse the demand that the obtaining of a relation be grounded in a further relational fact, so that the regress, though perfectly real, is vertically shallow and harmless. Please compare these two readings: say which is closer to what Bradley himself was after, given that he took the argument to show relations to be mere appearance, and which of the two has the better resources in its own right.

To keep the comparison honest, I'd like each reading tested against three cases that seem to me to pull them apart. First, a proposition that is unified but false: whatever unity is, it cannot simply be the obtaining of the corresponding fact, yet a story about grounds and explanations has an obvious pull towards saying exactly that. Second, a pair of distinct but necessarily equivalent propositions made from exactly the same constituents differently arranged, where there is no difference of ground for a regress to trade on. Third, a relation that seems to hold of necessity given the nature of its own terms, so that the demand for an extra ground looks idle at the very first step, before any regress gets going. If one reading handles all three and the other does not, say so and explain why; if neither survives, tell me what third thing the regress might be an argument for, and whether that third thing is still a problem about propositions in particular or about relational facts in general. Please write your answer in British English as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just paragraphs of the sort you would put in a long email to a student who has got himself tangled up.
