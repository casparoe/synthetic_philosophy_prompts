I have been an amateur student of military history for about twenty years — campaign histories, battlefield sites on my holidays — and somewhere along the way I became more interested in the monuments than in the fighting. I keep noticing the same shape of story in places that otherwise have nothing in common, and I would like to know whether it has a name. I have no academic background, so I will describe it in my own words.

The shape is this. A community suffers a total, irreversible defeat, and then builds an identity not around what it lost but around the fact of having lost. The loss is treated as an achievement: the suffering is recounted as proof of worth, and the story gets told in such a way that the defeat itself, rather than the cause, is what makes the group admirable. I see it in the American South, where most of the Confederate monuments went up decades after the war was over, put up by people who were, by most measures, doing perfectly well; in the Serbian legend of Kosovo, where the prince is said to have chosen a heavenly kingdom over an earthly one, so that losing the battle is narrated as a higher kind of winning; and, more awkwardly, at Masada, where the defenders killed themselves rather than be taken and where my impression, which may be wrong, is that the story was nearly dormant for centuries before it was revived and made central.

Four things about it strike me as odd, and they are the reason I suspect a single word will not settle it.

First, it does not fade. It seems to get stronger as the people who actually remember the event die off, and stronger as the group becomes more secure and more prosperous.

Second, it keeps two sets of books. When the group wants recognition or sympathy, the defeat is a catastrophe of the first order; when the group is criticised for what it was fighting for, the same defeat becomes a purely spiritual matter, and anyone who raises the original cause is being crude or literal-minded.

Third, and this is the part I have never seen discussed: it is hostile to being fixed. An apology, reparations, a reversal of the old outcome, even an honest reckoning with what actually happened, would take the thing away. The community appears to need the injury to stay open, and to need an oppressor to remain available in the story, which makes me think the grievance is doing something other than what it says it is doing.

Fourth, it can flourish in a cause that mostly won. In the American case the side that lost the war obtained a great deal of what it had wanted in the decades afterwards, and the defeat-story seems to let a group hold the benefits of winning while also holding the moral standing of losing.

I have been told this is just resentment, but that does not seem right: resentment needs someone to resent, and it needs the resenting party to be underneath, whereas here the group often holds real power and is choosing to keep the injury. It is not ordinary nostalgia either, since nostalgia does not need an enemy. And Dunkirk — a withdrawal remembered as a kind of triumph, even though the prime minister of the day pointed out that wars are not won by evacuations — feels adjacent but wrong: nothing was really lost there, and nobody claims the evacuation was a moral achievement.

So: is there an established concept for this, one that covers the need for the wound to stay open and the way a lost cause can be put to work while it is quietly being won? If there is only a family of near-misses, tell me which ones, and how my case differs from each. If there is nothing, coin a term and define it tightly enough that I can tell which commemorations count and which do not, and say what a borderline case would look like. Please do not just hand me a label; the carving is what I want.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no closing summary.
