I work in workforce analytics for a large hospital group, and I have been asked to sit on the internal group that will decide whether generation should be used as a variable in our retention models and as an organising category in our management training. The question has become personal in two ways I did not anticipate. I was born in 1981, so I am either a Gen Xer or a Millennial depending on which scheme the consultants use; my deputy, born in 1996, is either a Millennial or Gen Z. And I am the person who would have to defend the variable if it went in.

My professional instinct is that adding a five-level generation variable to a model that already contains continuous birth year buys nothing, and that a training profile telling a ward manager what her Gen Z staff are like is a stereotype with a spreadsheet attached. The argument I built to justify that instinct, however, now seems to me to prove far more than I am willing to believe, and I cannot locate the point at which it overreaches.

(1) A category may be used in explanation and prediction only if membership in it makes a causal difference: there must be causally relevant structure that its members share, in virtue of which the generalisations hold. Categories that merely sort people — employees whose surnames begin with M — can be useful for bookkeeping without being explanatory, and I take it that if generational categories are of that sort, they have no business in a model or a training profile.

(2) The only candidate for such structure, in the case of a generation, is shared formative exposure: the events, technologies, institutions and labour-market conditions a person met at particular developmental stages. Call the dated record of these a person's formative exposure profile.

(3) So a generational category is, if it is anything, a grouping of people by similarity of formative exposure profile.

(4) The individual exposures do not flip at year boundaries. A technology arrives in one adolescence and in another person's early twenties; two people two years apart leave school into very different points of the same cycle; a widely witnessed public event is encountered at thirteen by one and at fifteen by another.

(5) Different exposures cut at different places. The exposure that best separates people who grew up with a permanently networked computer in the house from those who did not has a different boundary from the one that separates people who entered the labour market before a given recession from those who entered after it, and both differ again from the boundary that matters for a given piece of legislation or a given change in the housing market.

(6) Hence, for any standard generational label and any exposure cited on its behalf, there are people on both sides of the label's boundary who share that exposure, and people inside the label who differ in it.

(7) If a category's boundary is arbitrary with respect to each of the causally relevant properties cited for it, taken one at a time, then it is arbitrary with respect to all of them together, and the category fails the test in (1).

(8) Therefore no standard generational label — Boomer, Gen X, Millennial, Gen Z — is a real kind. They are administrative conventions with a public-relations life of their own. This is the conclusion I wanted.

(9) But nothing in (4) to (7) depends on the labels being coarse. Take any grouping by birth at any resolution — a single year, a quarter, the cohort that started secondary school in a particular autumn — and steps (4) through (6) can be run again: time is continuous, exposures are graded, and for every boundary there is a finer one at which the exposures differ less and a coarser one at which they differ more. I have started calling the underlying trap the granularity trilemma: a scheme for grouping people by birth can be fine-grained enough to track exposures, in which case it fragments into dozens of micro-cohorts and stops doing the work a shared label does; or coarse enough to be usable, in which case its boundaries are to some degree arbitrary; or neither, in which case it is idle. Each horn seems to me to be generated by (4) to (7) rather than by anything specific to the popular labels.

(10) So, by (7), no grouping by birth or by exposure-window is a real kind — including the ones I would have staked money on: people who were in utero during a famine; children who received a particular treatment before the age of two; the cohort that left school into a particular recession; the survivors of a war.

(11) Yet these are exactly the groupings for which cohort effects are best confirmed. What happens to a body or a biography at a particular stage shows up in outcomes forty years later, and demography and epidemiology rely on it routinely.

(12) So either (1) is too demanding, or (7) is invalid, or my sense that (10) is absurd is mistaken.

That is where I am stuck. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (8) than accept (10), and I would rather keep (1) than keep (7): (1) is the only thing that ever made (8) plausible, whereas (7) is doing quiet work I never examined. But I cannot say what goes wrong in (7) without making it so weak that (8) no longer follows, and I cannot see what else separates the decade labels from famine cohorts besides the accident that someone has run a study on one and a marketing campaign on the other.

One of my colleagues says I have been asking the wrong question: that the labels are real, not as groupings of exposure profiles but as categories that act on the people classified under them, because managers treat staff differently once they are labelled and staff respond to being treated that way. I do not know whether that helps. It may only describe how a bad category becomes socially load-bearing, which is not the same as being a kind, and it does not tell me what to put in the model.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? And what does rejecting it cost me — both for what I have to tell my group next month, and for the larger claim that the familiar decade labels are bogus? If there is a principled line between the cohort effects in (11) and the categories in (8), I would like to know what it is, whether it is a line about fuzziness or a line about something else, and whether drawing it forces me to concede that some birth-year groupings my colleagues will dislike — people who came of age in a particular recession, say — really are kinds, with everything that follows for how we are allowed to talk about them.
