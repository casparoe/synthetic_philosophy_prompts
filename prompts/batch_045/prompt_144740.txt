I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas — and I'm working on a piece about whether the AI race actually forces labs to ship things they shouldn't, or whether that whole story is a bit of a bogeyman that people in the field repeate because it makes regulation sound urgent. So I want a number from you, but more than the number I want to see where it comes from.

Here's the proposition I want your credence on, stated so it should have a truth value by 1 Jan 2031:

At least one lab training frontier-scale AI systems will, before the end of 2030, give broad external access (API or product, not internal use) to a system that its own pre-deployment safety evaluation had flagged as above the catastrophic-risk threshold it was publicly committed to — and it will do that primarily because waiting would have cost it its competitive position.

If a lab hasn't published such a threshold, count its internal one. If nobody is training frontier systems by then, or capability growth has obviously stalled, count it as not happening; I'll take that.

My own credence is around 0.55. An economist I interviewed — works on R&D races, doesn't want to be named — put it at 0.15, and her argument was basically this: the cost of waiting is bounded. Worst case you lose your lead, maybe you lose the firm, and that's a finite number. The cost of shipping something that actually causes a catastrophe is unbounded, and it lands on you too. So any firm with even a modest estimate of that downside should be willing to eat a delay, and the race story only works if you assume implausibly short time horizons or implausibly small perceived risk. I couldn't shake it and I also couldn't quite believe it, and it's been bugging me for weeks.

So: give me your number, but decompose it. I want at least these terms, each with a number and one line of justification, and then how they combine:

1. That the thing people call the alignment tax is big enough to bite at all — that making a frontier system safe enough costs something on the order of a year of capability lead or a double-digit capability hit, rather than being a rounding error or a fixed engineering cost you pay once and then amortize forever.
2. That it stays big rather than collapsing — the story where safety work is mostly a one-time fixed cost, after which the safe system and the unsafe one converge and nobody's lead is at stake anymore.
3. That competitive pressure is genuinely binding on the decision, as opposed to labs being willing to wait if they believed everyone else would wait too, or the real constraint being internal (investors, hype, recruiting) rather than rival labs.
4. That a lab would even be in the position of knowing it had crossed its own threshold — that internal evals are sharp enough to detect the relevant risk, and that anyone would trust them against the commercial pressure to re-read them charitably.
5. Whether there are forces pushing the other way that I'm not modeling. One I keep hearing from a founder friend: paying the tax early is strategically good, because whoever builds the safety technique first gets to define what regulators later mandate everyone to do — so the race is to be safe, not reckless. Call it the standardization option; I don't know if it has a real name. Is that reasoning sound, and how much should it move me?

Then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to — the one where flipping it would move you furthest. And tell me where you think my 0.55 and her 0.15 actually disagree, because I have a suspicion it's all in (1) and (5), and that we're both treating the tax as one scalar when it's really a bundle of cheap measures and expensive ones — but I want to hear what you think. And if you think the proposition as I've written it is unanswerable in principle rather than just hard, say that too — but give me the number anyway, and tell me what I should have asked instead.
