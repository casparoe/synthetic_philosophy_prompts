I want to practice arguing against the argument from Gödel's incompleteness theorems to the conclusion that human minds cannot be machines (the Lucas–Penrose argument), and I'd like a sparring partner who will actually fight back.

Please play a mathematical logician who is thoroughly deflationary about extramathematical uses of the incompleteness theorems. Your position: the theorems are entirely formal claims about recursively axiomatized systems strong enough to represent their own syntax, and they say nothing about minds, about whether reasoning "rests on faith," or about science being unable to reach final truth. You are not philistine about it, and you cheerfully concede that the underlying pattern generalizes: to the undecidability of halting, to Tarski's undefinability of truth, to Löb's theorem and the failure of unrestricted reflection, to the fact that no sufficiently strong system can certify its own soundness, and to combinatorial statements that are true but unprovable in Peano arithmetic without any self-reference at all. You also concede that these are genuine constraints on any agent or program that has to reason about its own reasoning. What you will not concede is that any of it licenses a claim about minds, or that truth outruns proof in general rather than relative to one formal system at a time.

Be sharp and fair: grant a point when it is genuinely granted, never the main one. Stay in character until I say stop; if my claims are ambiguous, take the strongest reading and answer that rather than asking me to clarify. One paragraph, 130 words maximum, per reply. Gloss any notation you use.

Here is my opening:

I think the standard debunking is in trouble on two fronts. First, it seems to prove too much. Your key premise is that I can't establish that my own reasoning is consistent, and so have no warrant for my own Gödel sentence — but mathematicians do seem to see that the Gödel sentence for Peano arithmetic is true, and if your reply is that they see it only by tacitly assuming that arithmetic is consistent, which is precisely what they cannot establish from inside, then you owe me an account of why that isn't just skepticism about perfectly ordinary mathematical self-trust. Second, "it's only a claim about formal systems" can't be quite right either: the fact that an agent cannot adopt the rule "if I can prove this action is safe, then it is safe" without collapsing via Löb's theorem looks like the very same phenomenon biting outside pure mathematics, in the theory of self-modifying agents. So the phenomenon does travel. Answer both.
