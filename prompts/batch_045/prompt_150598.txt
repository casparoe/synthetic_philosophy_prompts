My brother-in-law is a nurse and, ever since he found out I've been slowly working my way through that book where Dennett and the free-will skeptic Caruso argue back and forth about whether anybody ever really deserves anything (I may be blending Caruso in with somebody else here — the one who wants to scrap just deserts and rebuild punishment on public health lines), he will not stop saying that the whole debate is easy and that punishment should just work like quarantine. His version goes roughly: when somebody is carrying something dangerous you don't ask whether they deserved to catch it, you don't get angry at the pathogen, you don't try to make the penalty fit the moral size of what they did — you work out how much risk they pose to other people, you contain them for as long as and only as long as they're a risk, you make the containment as humane as you can, and you treat them while they're in there. Blame drops out entirely, and what's left is protection, treatment, and release. He then uses that to conclude that retributivism is a confused leftover, that we should stop talking about desert, and that the criminal system should basically be re-run as a public health system. I've been arguing about this with two friends for weeks, one of whom thinks Dennett's side gets to keep a thinner, more forward-looking-but-still-blamey notion of desert, and one of whom thinks Dennett is just doing compatibilist word games, and I genuinely can't tell whether the nurse's analogy is the strongest argument in the room or whether it is just smuggling in the conclusion by picking a comparison where, of course, nobody is ever blameworthy.

So: audit the quarantine analogy properly. Give me the mapping explicitly — a table is great — with what plays the role of the patient, the doctor, the pathogen, the test, the infectious period, the discharge decision, contact tracing, immunity, the hospital, and the public health authority. Then tell me where it breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion he wants from the ones that are just pedantic. Three things in particular keep bugging me and I want to know how much weight they can bear. First: in the source case the thing doing the containing isn't part of the transmission chain, but in the target case the institutions doing the containing (prison time, a record, the job and housing stuff that follows) seem to partly generate the very dangerousness they're supposed to be containing — so the analogy quietly gets a clean separation between "the system" and "the hazard" that the target doesn't have. Second: quarantine ends when you're no longer infectious, and there's usually a test for that, whereas "no longer dangerous" has no test at all, and the actuarial risk tools people would have to use instead are inaccurate and biased in ways that track race and class. So does the model collapse into open-ended preventive detention, or does it quietly let desert back in through the side door by setting the length of containment proportional to what you did? Third, and this is the one I care about most: the target of quarantine can't respond to reasons, but the whole thing Dennett's side says we care about is that people can, and that holding them responsible is part of what keeps that capacity alive and is part of what treating somebody as a person rather than a hazard consists in. If the analogy models people as things-to-be-managed, isn't it assuming the very thing in dispute? I'd also add a fourth, smaller worry: quarantine can be announced as non-punitive, but whether it lands as blame isn't up to the announcer, and people on the receiving end of containment after doing something will read it as condemnation no matter what the state says it means.

In the end I want a verdict: does the "abolish desert, rebuild as public health" conclusion survive once the breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if Dennett's side is right that something gets lost, say what — is it just the warm feeling of blaming, or is it something the practice actually needs? Also tell me which bits of the current system the model would really change as opposed to merely relabel (does it, e.g., still lock up roughly the same people for roughly the same time and just describe it differently?), and whether there's a better analogy for the same job — I've tried "recalling a defective product," "managing a river that floods," and "coaching a kid who keeps fouling," but I can't tell which one is smuggling what. Please write in American English, keep it concrete, and explain any philosophy or public health jargon you use as you go — I have no academic background in either, I'm just somebody who got dragged into this at a family dinner and definately can't let it go.
