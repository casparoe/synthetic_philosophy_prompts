I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and one of the recurring threads there is the question of whether the axiom of choice — or, in the form it actually gets used, Zorn's lemma — is really true. The modal answer in those threads is a shrug: choice is fine, every vector space having a basis is a cost you should be willing to pay, Banach–Tarski is a curiosity about non-measurable sets rather than a problem, and if you remain uneasy you can always track where choice got used in a proof. I have never found that satisfying, because the word fine is doing all of the work.

I'm putting together a one-year, self-funded independent research proposal — roughly fifteen hours a week, no advisor, my maths is good to graduate level but I am not a set theorist — and I want the question pinned down before I commit a year to it. My half-formed idea is that the standard justifications for accepting Zorn's lemma are aimed at the wrong target, and that splitting the target in two dissolves part of the dispute and sharpens the rest.

The split I have in mind is this. Question one: is the full-strength principle true, or justified, or to be accepted? Question two: how much choice does the mathematics that our best empirical theories actually invoke really need? The indispensability-style argument — we are committed to whatever the best-confirmed theories quantify over, and mathematics is indispensable to those theories — looks built to answer question one by way of question two. My suspicion is that it cannot, because the choice-flavoured theorems that physics, statistics and economics lean on mostly sit well below full choice: much of applicable analysis is fine with countable or dependent choice, and the Hahn–Banach theorem, which is the one that keeps coming up in the foundations of quantum mechanics because you want a rich dual space, follows, as I understand it, from the Boolean prime ideal theorem (equivalently the ultrafilter lemma), which is strictly weaker than choice and does not even deliver countable choice. So the indispensability route may only ever hand you a fragment, and the live question is whether the leftover strength has any non-pragmatic justification at all.

Two further strands I want the proposal to deal with rather than dodge. First, the intrinsic-justification strand: the iterative conception of set plus a maximality heuristic are often said to give you choice, but the heuristic looks univocal only until you notice that rivals of choice — determinacy principles, which hold in canonical inner models and flatly contradict full choice — are also naturally described as maximising, in a different direction. If maximality motivates both, it cannot be what justifies choice specifically. Second, a deflationary hypothesis I want tested rather than assumed: is the question of whether choice holds more like the continuum hypothesis (a determinate question we may merely be unable to answer) or more like the parallel postulate (a question that dissolves once you stop assuming a unique intended model)? The version of that I care about is normative: if the deflationary reading is right, the only remaining question is instrumental — which set-theoretic environment makes applied reasoning cheapest, given that the applications cannot tell them apart. The objection I most want the proposal to confront is that this reading has to explain why set-theoretic practice itself (the search for new axioms, forcing axioms, inner model theory) looks like inquiry into one structure rather than a menu.

The deliverable I have in mind is a two-page proposal, about a thousand words: a precise statement of the question; why the existing positions leave it open; the method, month by month; the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing. One component of the method should be a small audit — take a fixed basket of, say, eight to ten results that working science actually invokes, and classify each by the weakest choice principle that suffices for it: ZF alone, ZF with countable or dependent choice, prime-ideal strength, or full choice (or not known to be weaker). The point of the audit is to separate choice that is load-bearing from choice that is merely convenient, and to see whether the indispensability argument survives losing the convenient cases.

Please do not include a bibliography or a citation-based literature review; I will fill those in myself, so refer to positions and arguments by their content, not by where they were published. Do flag any place where a background claim of mine is wrong or shakier than I think — especially the relative-strength claims about Hahn–Banach, the ultrafilter lemma and countable choice, and a half-remembered result to the effect that even Hahn–Banach brings the Banach–Tarski paradox along with it, which I mention only because if it is right it makes convenient-case losses more expensive. Please also tell me candidly if the year is too short for the parts that need forcing or inner-model machinery I would have to take on testimony, and what the project looks like if those parts are cut.

Finally: for each load-bearing claim in the proposal, say how confident you are in it, and single out the one you are least sure of.
