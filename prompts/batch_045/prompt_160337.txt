I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend — a poet who translates from French — about a question neither of us could settle and both of us care about: whether a translation that keeps a short poem's stanza shape, rhyme scheme and syllable count but quietly swaps out some of its imagery is *more* faithful to the original than a prose rendering that preserves every image, every shift of register and every turn of syntax but abandons the music entirely. We started from the sixteenth-century Marot poem that Hofstadter made the stalking horse of his 1997 book on translation, but by the fourth hour we were trading invented examples: a poem about being confined written in a deliberately cramped stanza form; a poem whose rhyme-words pair the very things its content insists are opposites; a poem whose line-lengths shrink as the speaker's options do. Her position is that the prose crib is a gloss and not a translation, that rhyme and metre are not ornament but the thing that makes the object the object it is. Mine is that rhyme schemes are local conventions with local effects, that the music does not survive the crossing, and that what a translation owes us is a chance at the experience the poem gave its first readers, which sometimes requires throwing the form away. We each produced exemplars. Neither of us moved. I have had versions of this argument for fifteen years and so, I assume, has everyone else, which is why I now think the interesting question is not who is right but what kind of stalemate this is.

My hypothesis, offered as one, is that it is not a single dispute but several stacked on top of each other, and that the stacking is what makes it interminable: (i) a verbal layer, where "faithful" means fidelity to the text for me and fidelity to the reading for her; (ii) a substantive but unstated layer about the identity conditions of a poem — whether its phonological and metrical form is constitutive of the work or an accident of the medium it happens to be written in; (iii) a hidden question that looks factual rather than conceptual, namely how often a poem's form is semantically load-bearing rather than merely conventional, and whether translators and critics can reliably tell which they are dealing with in a given case; and (iv) a bedrock divergence about what translations are for and for whom — the dead poet's authority against the living reader's use — which neither of us can argue for at all. My friend says (iv) is the whole story and (i) through (iii) are post-hoc rationalizations of a temperamental difference she has no interest in defending. That strikes me as too quick, mainly because of two pieces of apparatus I have been sketching, which I want you either to use or to reject.

The first is what I am calling a preservation profile: a stated vector of the formal features a given translation actually preserves — line count, stanza shape, rhyme scheme, rhyme position, syllable count, stress pattern, the alternation between rhymes ending in a mute vowel and rhymes that do not, rhyme-richness, refrain placement, alliteration, initial letters — so that two renderings can be compared dimension by dimension instead of globally. My suspicion is that neither of us, and nobody in the wider argument either, ever states a preservation profile; each of us is comparing a translation that preserves X with one that preserves Y while both of us use one word, "faithful," and the chosen examples are quietly doing all the work. If that is right, a large share of the stalemate is a bookkeeping failure, and the remedy is taxonomic rather than philosophical.

The second is a test for whether form can ever be content. In the cramped-stanza poem and in the poem whose rhymes pair what its sense opposes, preserving the form *is* preserving part of the meaning; in a poem whose form is conventional scaffolding, it is not. So the question of how much form is load-bearing seems to decide how much of the disagreement is real trade-off and how much is confusion — and it is a question about particular poems, not about Translation with a capital T. There is a cheaper probe I keep returning to as well: take two translations of the same poem that differ only in whether they preserve some extremely local formal feature, such as that mute-vowel alternation, and see whether anyone's judgment of faithfulness tracks the difference. If judgments do not track it, then "fidelity" is not tracking formal preservation at all, and the formalist rhetoric about music is doing something other than describing what my friend actually values.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, argued for rather than surveyed, and anchored in what each side actually says. No bibliography, no citations, no reading suggestions — I know that literature badly enough to fill in names myself, and I would rather you reason than recollect. Is the dispute verbal, substantive-but-hidden, two questions under one heading, an empirical question in disguise, or bedrock? What, concretely, would discriminate between those descriptions? Is my stacking hypothesis right, and is my friend's deflation of it defensible? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a test, a change of question — or should we expect none, and why not?

After the prose, give me the diagnosis as structured data: a Markdown table, one row per candidate diagnosis, four to six rows, ordered by how much of the stalemate you think each explains, with no cell longer than two sentences and exactly these columns:

- diagnosis — a short label plus one sentence stating what the disagreement really is on this description
- mechanism — what my friend and I are each actually doing, and what each of us would say if this description were correct

Above the table, one sentence giving your bottom line, and below it one sentence naming the single question I should put to my friend the next time this comes up.
