My first monograph — eleven years' work, 388 pages — is at page-proof stage, and my editor has asked me to do two things by Friday: sign the author's warranty affirming that the book contains no assertion I have reason to doubt, and supply the customary preface sentence in which I take sole responsibility for whatever errors nevertheless remain. Read together, the two requests are ones I can satisfy only one at a time, and I have to reply to her this week.

Please write the document I wish I could send her: a deadpan, straight-faced "modest proposal" of roughly 750 words, in the voice of the chair of a university press's Committee on Editorial Assurance, arguing that the preface apology should be abolished and replaced by a lottery-administered Designated Error — one claim per manuscript, selected at random by the press, which the author may then officially disbelieve and for which she is contractually indemnified.

The humor should come from getting the epistemology exactly right, so that a formal epistemologist recognises the view being sent up. In particular, build the argument on the real mechanics of the lottery and preface paradoxes rather than on anything invented: a fixed threshold for rational acceptance; the fact that an author's confidence in each individual claim can clear that threshold while her confidence that the conjunction of all of them is true does not; the base-rate observation that books of such-and-such length essentially always contain at least one error, from which it follows both that some particular claim is false and that no particular claim can be identified as the false one — if she knew which, she would have corrected it. That last point is what makes the lottery, rather than any assay of the manuscript, the appropriate allocation mechanism, and the paper should say so with genuine bureaucratic satisfaction. Let it also register, without ever breaking character, the awkward corollary the committee has to manage: that the author can be certain the lottery has a winner while being unable to accept of any given claim that it is the winner.

Include the apparatus such a scheme would need: a worked calculation with explicit numbers for a book of N assertions at a stated error rate, so that the individual claims clear the threshold while the conjunction collapses; a rule governing how referees' reports may be aggregated given that the committee cannot simply conjoin what it accepts; a short schedule of fees, exemptions (fiction; edited volumes; second editions, where the previous Designated Error has already been retired) and appeals; and a closing budgetary paragraph totalling the savings from foreclosed errata disputes and retracted warranties.

Do not let the chair wink, hedge, or hint that the scheme is absurd. The reductio should land on its own: by the end, a reader should feel that what has really been exposed is the assumption that acceptance is governed by a probabilistic threshold plus closure, and should be left with the live question of whether the preface apology was ever the expression of a belief at all, or only a politeness, a legal form, or a way of owning error without locating it.
