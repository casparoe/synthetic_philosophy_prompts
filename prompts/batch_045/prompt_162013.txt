I run a small, pretty informal reading group on chance and credence, and this week I want to stage a fight I cant stage with the actual readings, because the case I want them to fight over isn't in any of them. Write me a dialogue, somewhere around 900–1200 words, between two people who both take the chance–credence link seriously but think it's basic for different reasons:

- Call her the Deferrer: she thinks deference to objective chance is a primitive requirement on rational credence — given admissible evidence, your credence in A conditional on the chance of A at t being x should just be x — and thinks the only real work left is saying what counts as admissible.
- Call him the Deriver: he thinks that's a theorem, not a axiom. The reason chance has authority over credence is something like an accuracy or calibration argument — matching the chances is what maximizes expected accuracy, or keeps you calibrated, or spares you a certain kind of guaranteed loss — so chance's authority only extends as far as that argument actually reaches.

Both of them should be good at this, and neither should be a punching bag: I want them answering each other's actual points, conceding what really deserves conceding, and stealing each other's moves where it's fair. Assume they've already agreed about the usual undermining cases — they both accept that outcomes can be evidence about what the chances were, and that this breaks the naive version of the principle — so don't spend the dialogue there. Start them past that.

The case I want them on is this. There's a device I'll call the Echo: at time t it reads your current credence in some outcome A, then sets the objective chance of A at t equal to that number, after which the outcome is produced by an ordinary chancy process with exactly that bias. Nothing else about A is touched. So if you're at 0.7 the chance really is 0.7, and if you could get yourself to 1 the chance would be 1 and the outcome would be certain.

Have them work through the following, in whatever order feels natural, and don't let either of them just announce a verdict:

1. What does each of their accounts even say here? For the Deferrer: is knowledge of your own credence admissible, given that the machine is about to convert it into the chance? Does she end up committed to any credence at all being permitted — radical permissivism — and is that a bullet she can bite? For the Deriver: his accuracy or calibration argument looks equally inert, since whatever you pick is perfectly calibrated by construction. Does his account have anything left to say, or does it quietly assume chances don't depend on credence?
2. Whether either of them can rule out the extreme fixed point, where you talk yourself into credence 1 (or 0) and the chance follows you there. If that's rationally fine, is something else in trouble?
3. The variant I actually care about more, the Anti-Echo: the device sets the chance of A to one minus your credence. Now there's exactly one self-consistent credence, a half, and you can get to it without learning anything about the world — purely by noticing the fixed point. Does either of them have a principled story about why consistency is doing normative work here, or is this consistency in a bad sense?
4. Where they come apart on a third variant: the device sets the chance to your credence or to one minus your credence, but you don't know which, and you're fifty-fifty on which one you're hooked up to.

Then, after the dialogue, add a short note — a couple hundred words — on where each of them is closest to giving in, the single argument or consideration that would actually change each of their minds, and where you think the reasoning on each side is most likely to fail. Also flag any place where the Echo setup is doing more work than it earns, or sneaking in an assumption about chance, or about self-knowledge, that the other side wouldn't grant.

Casual is good — they can interrupt each other, and it should read like two people who have had this argument before.
