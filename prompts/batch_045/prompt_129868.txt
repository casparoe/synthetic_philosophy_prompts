I spent much of last night arguing with a friend about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and I'm not sure either of us won. Her position was that if the mathematics shows that no risk score can satisfy every reasonable fairness criterion at once, then the choice among criteria is arbitrary and one might as well maximise predictive accuracy and stop pretending otherwise. My position was that the results are claims about which combinations of conditions can hold simultaneously in a given joint distribution, and that they are silent about which condition one ought to prefer — which is a question about what the score is for and about who bears which error. We went round in circles on whether "arbitrary" follows from "not jointly satisfiable," and left it there.

I now have to grade a problem-set answer on exactly this material, and I'd like a careful second read rather than my own tired one. The question (20 marks)

Throughout, A is a binary group variable, Y a binary outcome, and S a real-valued risk score intended as an estimate of the probability that Y = 1.

(a) [7 marks] State the central impossibility result for risk scores — the one involving calibration within groups, balance for the negative class, and balance for the positive class — defining each of the three conditions carefully. Specify what must be true of the score and of the two groups for the conflict to arise, and identify the degenerate cases in which all three conditions hold simultaneously. In one closing sentence, say what the result does not show.

(b) [7 marks] A compliance team proposes the following way out. The conflict, they observe, only arises when the two groups differ in their base rate of Y. They therefore propose to importance-weight the training sample until the two groups' base rates are equal, retrain, and then report all three conditions as satisfied. Assess this proposal. In particular: does equalising base rates in the training data make the conflict disappear for the classifier as it will actually be deployed? And is equality of base rates in the deployed population, together with the score's being non-trivial, sufficient to guarantee that all three conditions hold? If not, say what else would have to be true.

(c) [6 marks] Now consider a stronger requirement. Call a score uniformly fair if error-rate balance is required to hold at every threshold rather than at the single threshold the agency happens to use, and calibration is required to hold at each score value rather than within bins. Someone argues as follows: if error-rate balance must hold at every threshold, then the distribution of scores must be the same in both groups conditional on Y, i.e. S must be independent of A given Y; and once S is independent of A given Y, the score carries no information about group membership, so calibration within groups comes for free; hence uniform fairness escapes the impossibility result. Is this argument sound? What does requiring error-rate balance at every threshold actually force, and does uniform fairness escape the conflict?

The student's answer

(a) Calibration within groups says that the score means the same thing in both groups: for any value s, of the people in group a who are assigned the score s, a fraction s actually has Y = 1. Balance for the negative class says that the average score received by people with Y = 0 is the same in the two groups, and balance for the positive class says the same for people with Y = 1.

The impossibility result says that no score can satisfy all three at once. More precisely, when the two groups have different base rates of Y — different values of P(Y = 1 | A = a) — a score that is calibrated cannot also satisfy both balance conditions. The degenerate cases in which all three hold are those in which the score is perfectly predictive, and those in which the groups' base rates are equal.

What the result does not show is that fairness is unachievable generally, or that we must abandon all of the criteria at once: any two of the three can be satisfied together. Nor does it show that the data are biased or that anyone has been negligent — the conflict is a fact about the joint distribution of (A, Y, S) and arises even for a perfectly specified model trained on perfectly recorded outcomes. And it does not tell us which criterion we should adopt, since that depends on what the score is used for and on who bears which kind of error.

(b) The compliance team is right that unequal base rates are the engine of the conflict, but wrong to think they can switch the engine off by reweighting the training sample. Importance weighting changes the weights in the training loss, but it does not change the fitted score, so the function they deploy is the one they would have had anyway. And the three conditions are not properties of the training procedure at all: they are properties of the joint distribution of (A, Y, S) in the population where the score is used. If the deployed population still has unequal base rates, the conflict is still there no matter what was done upstream.

Note also that balance for the positive class is just the requirement that the positive predictive value be equal across groups: among those who receive a high score, the proportion who actually have Y = 1 should be the same for the two groups. This is why the compliance team's proposal is worth taking seriously in the first place.

Suppose, however, that the deployed population really did have equal base rates. Then, as the degenerate case in part (a) shows, the conflict disappears: once the base rates match, any calibrated and non-trivial score automatically satisfies both balance conditions, so all three hold. The practical moral is that the trade-off is a fact about prevalence, and prevalence is something an institution can in principle change by intervening on the world rather than on the classifier.

(c) The argument is sound in its first step. If the false positive rate has to match at every threshold, then for every threshold the upper tail of the score distribution among people with Y = 0 must carry the same mass in both groups, which is to say that the conditional distribution of S given Y = 0 is the same in both groups; and similarly given Y = 1. So uniform error-rate balance requires S to be independent of A given Y. Note that this is much stronger than ordinary error-rate balance, which only requires the two groups' ROC curves to pass through a common point.

And once S is independent of A given Y, the score carries no further information about group membership: conditional on the outcome, scores are distributed identically in the two groups. Since calibration is a condition on what the score means — that a score of s corresponds to a risk of s — and the score's distribution no longer differs between the groups, calibration within groups follows automatically. Uniform fairness therefore escapes the impossibility result, and an agency that wants to avoid the trade-off should require S to be independent of A given Y by construction; training on a group-balanced sample and then checking that the two ROC curves coincide is enough. One might object that equality at every threshold is impossibly demanding in practice. But that is a practical objection rather than a mathematical one, and the point of the construction is that the impossibility result is an artifact of fixing a single threshold.
