I'm teaching the second Untimely Meditation — Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — and I want to spend a session on the Confederate monument fights, mostly Charlottesville in 2017 and the wave of removals that came after. Don't give me the history of the Lost Cause or the timeline of when the statues actually went up, I've got that. What I want is the mapping: monumental, antiquarian, and critical history as three ways a living culture can stand to its past, applied to people pulling down and defending a bronze general on a courthouse square.

My working hunch, which you should push on: a lot of the fight is a fight about which kind of history the statue is doing. One reading has it address you as an example — be like this, this is the great thing, it is repeatable. Another has it address you as an ancestor — this is where you come from, and piety is owed to it. Those are different claims on a person, and my sense is that each side hears the other reading and thinks the other side is being obtuse. Is that a real distinction in Nietzsche's terms or am I inventing a fourth category he doesn't have?

Then the part I care about more: what does the case do to the essay? Nietzsche wants the three in balance, says the critical mode is only healthy when it clears ground for something to be built, and worries about a culture that has taken in more history than it can digest. So: what would he say belongs on those pedestals, if anything? And is the honest answer that the whole framework quietly assumes a single we with a single past — so a country containing the descendents of the enslaved alongside the descendents of the people who put the statues up is a case the essay never imagined? Flag as well where the three categories just go silent, where there's a feature of this episode they can't sort at all.
