I am drafting a proposal for a second legislative chamber selected by lot, and before I take it further I would like the position set audited as a package rather than one commitment at a time. Where I stand:

1. **Term limits.** I support a hard limit of two consecutive terms in any elected office, and I justify it mainly by the diagnosis that long tenure produces a class whose skills are the skills of winning re-election rather than of governing, and whose judgment is captured by funders, staff and lobbyists.
2. **Sortition on epistemic grounds.** I want one chamber drawn by lot from the adult citizenry. I reject the mirroring argument — that a chamber must demographically resemble the population to be legitimate — and rest the case instead on the claim that a randomly selected body, once properly briefed, deliberates better than elected politicians on long-horizon problems such as climate and pensions, because electoral incentives systematically discount the future.
3. **The diversity claim in its strong form.** I hold that the advantage of such a body is cognitive and perspectival diversity itself: a heterogeneous group of moderately informed people can outperform a homogeneous group of better-informed experts. This is why I give little weight to the objection that sortition hands power to the ignorant.
4. **Professionalisation anyway.** I want members serving full-time for pay, with independent staff and expert briefing, and I favour a 200-member chamber with staggered six-year terms renewed by halves every three years, where twenty seats in each incoming half are reserved for members of the outgoing half, who mentor newcomers and chair committees.
5. **Accountability as a side-constraint.** Any governing body must be answerable to those it governs, in the sense that they can remove or sanction it; I treat this as a constraint rather than something to be traded off against good outcomes. At the same time I reject recall elections and referenda as accountability mechanisms, which I regard as plebiscitary and worse than representative ones.

Colleagues keep telling me the set is unstable, but the only pair they point to — my dismissal of mirroring in (2) against my reliance on diversity in (3) — looks to me like no conflict at all, since demographic resemblance is only a proxy for the diversity that does the work. So I cannot locate the real fault line myself.

Please audit the set: which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be, and what each available repair would cost me. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: the genuine tensions ordered from most to least severe, each with what would have to give and what that costs; then any pair that only looks tense, with the reason it dissolves; and as the final item, the single revision that buys back the most consistency at the least cost.
