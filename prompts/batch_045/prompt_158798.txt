QUESTION 3 (answer TWO questions; each question carries 20 marks)

Prioritarianism holds that the value of an outcome is a weighted sum of individuals' well-being, where a unit of well-being counts for more the worse off the person is to whom it accrues. In its standard, "absolute" form, the weight on a unit of well-being is a decreasing function of that person's own absolute level of well-being.

(a) State the levelling-down objection. Explain why the standard form of prioritarianism is normally held to escape it, and why views that assign intrinsic value to equality are held not to escape it. [8]

(b) Some writers have floated a "relative" variant of prioritarianism (RP). On RP, the value of an outcome is

V = Σᵢ v(uᵢ − ū) · uᵢ,

where ū is the mean level of well-being in that outcome and v is a positive, strictly decreasing function of a person's deviation from that mean. In what follows take v(r) = 1 − r/60, and assume throughout that all deviations lie strictly between −60 and 60, so that every weight is positive.

Consider these two-person outcomes, with well-being measured on an interval scale:

X: A has 0, B has 100.
Y: A has 0, B has 60.
Z: A has −2, B has 60.

(i) Compute V(X), V(Y) and V(Z). [4]

(ii) State what your computation shows about whether the levelling-down objection applies to RP, and explain carefully whether the argument you gave in part (a) transfers to RP. [8]

CANDIDATE 4021 — ANSWER TO QUESTION 3

3(a) The levelling-down objection targets views that treat the pattern of a distribution as valuable in itself. If equality is intrinsically good, then a move to a more equal distribution is, in one respect, an improvement, even when it makes some people worse off and no one better off. That is the objection: it is absurd to suppose that we could improve an outcome by making people worse off, and any view that implies otherwise is treating equality as a fetish rather than taking seriously the people whose lives are at stake.

Egalitarian views are stuck with this, because for them the value of an outcome is not exhausted by the well-being of the individuals in it. There is a further value — equality — which can go up while no one's life goes better.

Prioritarianism avoids the objection because it has no such extra value. On the standard version, the weight on a unit of well-being is a decreasing function of the recipient's own absolute level, and every person's well-being enters the total with a positive weight. So if you lower someone's level and raise no one's, every term in the sum either falls or stays the same, and at least one term falls: the total must fall. A levelling down is therefore not better in one respect and worse in another; it is simply worse. This is why prioritarianism was put forward as a rival to egalitarianism, and it is also, incidentally, why the view respects the Pareto principle, which egalitarianism of the relevant kind does not.

3(b)(i) V(X): mean 50. A deviates by −50, so v = 1 + 50/60 = 1.83; B deviates by +50, so v = 1 − 50/60 = 0.17. V(X) = 1.83(0) + 0.17(100) = 16.67.

V(Y): mean 30. v(−30) = 1.50, v(30) = 0.50. V(Y) = 1.50(0) + 0.50(60) = 30.00.

V(Z): mean 29. v(−31) = 1.52, v(31) = 0.48. V(Z) = 1.52(−2) + 0.48(60) = −3.04 + 28.80 = 25.76. Using unrounded fractions gives 25.97; the difference does not matter for what follows.

(ii) These numbers might look like a levelling-down result, but they are not one. The argument I gave in (a) did not depend on any particular assumption about where the weights come from; it depended only on each person's well-being being counted with a positive weight, and v(r) is positive throughout the range in which we are working. So lowering B from 100 to 60 cannot raise the total, and the rise from 16.67 to 30.00 must have some other explanation.

It does. In Y, A's relative position has improved: A is no longer 100 units below the best-off person but only 60 below, and because RP weights by relative position, A's situation has genuinely improved. So the move from X to Y is not a case of making some people worse off and no one better off at all. It makes one person better off and another worse off, which is an ordinary trade-off of exactly the kind that prioritarian views exist to adjudicate.

What the example really shows is only that RP is sensitive to relative position, and someone who thinks that only absolute levels matter will object to that. But that is a complaint about RP's weighting function, not the levelling-down objection, which is an objection specifically to views that assign intrinsic value to equality. RP assigns no such value — it merely weights well-being — so the objection gets no grip on it.

Notice, finally, that RP ranks Z (25.97) below Y (30.00), which shows that it is not simply maximizing equality: it is willing to sacrifice some equality when absolute levels fall.
