I run a fortnightly discussion evening in a village hall: about sixteen adults, ages from roughly twenty to seventy-five, almost none of them with any university mathematics or economics, and one ninety-minute session with a short break in the middle. I have no academic background myself. I got into questions about fair division through an argument in my own family over a shared inheritance, and I have been reading about them on my own since, which is why I may well have some of this slightly wrong.

The thing I want the group to feel is a disagreement between two rules for splitting a joint gain. The first rule I think of as the equal-fractions rule: fix what each side gets if talks break down, fix the most each side could possibly get if the other side were completely generous, and then choose the feasible division at which both sides have travelled the same fraction of the distance from their breakdown position towards their best possible outcome. The second rule is the biggest-product rule: take the division that makes the product of the two sides' gains above their breakdown positions as large as possible. On paper these look like two ways of saying much the same thing. I have become convinced that they are not, and that the difference is one a person can feel as unfair rather than merely understand.

Please design a session in which the participants walk into that difference before anyone names it. What I have in mind is a negotiating game in pairs, three or four rounds, played with tokens or cards rather than money, with a prize that is real but trivial — say the right to choose next month's topic, plus a tin of biscuits. The rounds should differ in what divisions are available and possibly in what happens if a pair fails to agree. The round that matters should add an option that no pair would ever seriously settle on — enormously good for one side and enormously bad for the other — and my guess is that pairs who had settled comfortably in the previous round will find their settled split dragged towards the side that the unusable option favours, even though nobody chooses it. If that is the effect you are aiming at, say so plainly; if you think it will not survive contact with a village hall, say that instead and give me the version you think will actually work.

Please give me: what participants are told before each round and what they are kept ignorant of; a payoff scheme that makes the disagreement show up in what they actually do and can see, rather than in what they say afterwards — I imagined each pair posting its settlement on a wall chart after every round so that any drift is visible to the whole room; a timeline for the ninety minutes; a debrief that maps particular moments on to particular features of the two rules, with the words I could actually say; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it in the room; and a frank note on which part of the disagreement the game cannot stage, so that I know what I have to state myself instead of expecting them to discover it. Keep the arithmetic to round numbers nobody has to write down. No theory names, no formulae and no jargon before the debrief: they should meet these ideas as a nuisance in the game first. In the debrief section only, if the rule I described above is the one that goes by two particular surnames in the literature, tell me so; and if my description does not match it, correct me — I would rather find out that I have misremembered it.

I would also rather have a design that is candid about its weak points than one that reads cleanly. So tell me which assumption in your design is most likely to be false — about how people actually bargain, or about what they will notice — and tell me what I would see in the room that would show the design's central claim was wrong.

Please write in British English.
