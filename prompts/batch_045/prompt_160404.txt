Next month I am giving oral evidence to a constitutional reform commission on a proposal I will be defending, and I would like a preparation sheet for the questioning rather than a defence of the proposal in the abstract.

The proposal. I am arguing for an Agenda Assembly: a chamber of three hundred members selected by stratified random sample from the electoral register, serving staggered three-year terms with one third of the seats rotated each year. The Assembly could not draft, amend, or veto legislation. It would have exactly two powers: first, to set a binding order in which the elected chamber must take up bills that have completed committee stage; second, to discharge any bill from committee after nine months, compelling a recorded floor vote within sixty days. Before each docket vote the Assembly would sit in plenary for two days, working from briefing packs prepared by a permanent secretariat, and would rank bills without altering them.

The argument I will give. (1) The familiar epistemic objection to sortition, that randomly selected people lack the competence to govern, is aimed at a chamber that writes law; a chamber that only ranks unfinished business by urgency is doing a task for which the lived experience of a representative sample is a kind of expertise rather than a substitute for one. (2) Agenda control is where legislative power already sits, and at present it sits with party leaderships, so the reform transfers an existing power instead of inventing a new democratic actor and should be judged against the realistic alternative rather than against an ideal. (3) The case from descriptive representation does its work at the stage at which a grievance first becomes visible to the legislature, not at the drafting stage, and that is precisely the stage at which electoral competition performs badly, since parties compete over an agenda that has already been filtered. (4) Staggered rotation supplies institutional memory, because two thirds of any sitting cohort will be in their second or third year, without creating anyone who must face re-election.

The panel. Seven commissioners will question me: a professor of political economy who works in formal social choice theory; a political theorist associated with the deliberative-democracy literature on minipublics; a former appellate judge; a former party whip of twenty years' standing; a survey statistician seconded from the national statistics office; a trade-union organiser; and a political columnist who will be asking, on the record, whether any of this is intelligible to an ordinary voter. All of them have read the general literature on citizens' assemblies and on replacing elections with sortition, so nothing is gained by rehearsing the general case. The session is livestreamed and I will have roughly two minutes per answer.

What I want from you. Reconstruct the eight or so objections this specific panel will raise, in the order in which they are likely to arrive, and for each: say who raises it; phrase it in that person's vocabulary rather than in mine; rate how much damage it does to the proposal as designed rather than to sortition in general; give the two-minute reply; and note explicitly where that reply is weakest. Where an objection bites harder against some variant of the design than against the design as I have stated it, say so. I would also like separate advice on three things: what I should concede in the first two minutes in order to be taken seriously by this particular room; the one reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the session; and the objection I am quietly hoping nobody raises, together with the least-bad answer to it.

Three vulnerabilities I already know about and want treated as live: the gap between the sample drawn and the sample that actually accepts the summons; the secretariat's effective control over what the Assembly reads; and the possibility that a chamber with no power to amend is simply routed around by procedural manoeuvre in the elected house. Assume the commissioners are sharp enough to find these on their own, and give me the strongest version of each.
