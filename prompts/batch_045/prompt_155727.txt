I work in machine learning, on probabilistic forecasting and causal inference, and I want to understand Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals in the vocabulary I actually use rather than the one they are normally stated in. My working vocabulary is: a measurable space of states, a distribution P over it, events as measurable sets, conditioning on an event A as the operation of reweighting by the indicator of A and renormalizing, quantities of interest as functionals of P (some linear in P, like an expectation, some not, like a ratio of expectations), estimators of those functionals, proper scoring rules and calibration, forecasts that are evaluated against resolving events, and the standard tricks for enlarging a space — auxiliary independent randomness, twin networks, potential-outcome variables, interventions that define a new distribution rather than select a subset. I am fluent in this vocabulary and not in possible-worlds semantics.

Here is my current reconstruction, which I suspect is a parody in places. Adams's thesis, as I read it, says that there is some event (some measurable set, depending only on the pair A, C) whose probability under P equals P(C given A) — i.e. that a ratio of two expectations, E[1_A·1_C]/E[1_A], is always expressible as a single expectation E[X_{A,C}] of a fixed indicator, with one such X for every pair of events in the algebra. The results are then supposed to show that this cannot be done in any space that is not degenerate.

Please do the following.

First, translate the setup and the result(s) into my terms as faithfully as you can, and say which of my sentences above are translations and which are distortions. In particular: state precisely what "trivial" comes to in my vocabulary (I take it the conclusion is that every event has probability 0 or 1, because every pair of events ends up independent — confirm or correct this); say whether the impossibility is a claim about a single distribution over a sufficiently rich algebra, or a claim quantified over distributions, since in my vocabulary those are very different claims and I do not know which one the result is; say what the logical assumptions about the conditional (roughly, that A together with C guarantees "if A then C", and that A together with "if A then C" guarantees C) translate into — my guess is hard support constraints on the joint law of the indicators, analogous to architectural guarantees rather than penalties; and say which assumption is doing the work, and whether the later strengthenings that drop those logical assumptions change the shape of the argument or only its premises.

Second, settle a confusion of mine with a concrete case. Take the four atoms of the algebra generated by two events A and C, with probabilities 0.3, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4 assigned to A∧C, A∧¬C, ¬A∧C and ¬A∧¬C. Then P(C given A) = 0.75, and no event in that algebra has probability 0.75. My own view is that this is not the result at all: it is an artifact of having only four atoms, and I can always enrich the space with an independent coin to manufacture an event of measure 0.75. So tell me what the real result adds over and above a demand that the algebra be rich, and express the extra content as a claim I could check on a joint distribution.

Third, translate the main escape routes, one by one, into my vocabulary: treating the conditional as a three-valued object that is true where A∧C, false where A∧¬C and undefined elsewhere, with the thesis recovered by conditioning away the undefined case (this looks to me like a classifier with a reject option, and I want to know whether the analogy is exact or whether the renormalization is quietly changing the subject); denying that conditionals have truth conditions at all and giving them only probabilities (which in my vocabulary would be a forecast with no resolving event, normally a defective object — except that I issue exactly such forecasts all the time in causal inference); moving to a richer space, e.g. ordered pairs of a state and a selected A-state, which is structurally what I do when I introduce potential outcomes or twin networks, and which seems to give back both propositionhood and the thesis; and treating "if" as a restrictor on a quantifier or modal rather than as a binary operation on events. For each, say what it costs in my terms, and whether the cost is one my framework has a name for.

Finally, give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical dispute translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? I have three candidates for genuine residue and I want your judgement on each: (i) the normative element — the claim is not merely representational but says what a rational agent's credence in a conditional ought to be, and my vocabulary describes what estimators do, not what anyone should believe; (ii) the requirement that the conditional be the same kind of thing as A and C, so that it can be negated, embedded in longer sentences, and taken as the object of attitudes; (iii) the distinction between being truth-apt and being score-able, which my framework mostly collapses. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to something the philosophers' claim was never clearly saying. And be explicit where my reconstruction above smuggles in an assumption of my own framework and presents it as a translation: I would rather be told that than be given a smooth restatement.
