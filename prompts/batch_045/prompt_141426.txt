I'm giving a 40-minute talk with a 25-minute Q&A at a rationalist-adjacent conference next month, on a decision-theory track, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The thesis is that the familiar risk-weighted alternative to expected-utility maximization has been applied at the wrong level of description, and that this is why it looks pathological in sequential cases.

Here is the position in the form I'll present it. Standard risk-weighted utility: for a gamble with outcomes ranked worst to best, you compute value by integrating utility not against the objective cumulative probabilities but against a distorted one — the weight on the top outcome is r(p) rather than p, where r maps [0,1] onto [0,1] strictly increasing with r(0)=0 and r(1)=1, and risk aversion shows up as r lying below the diagonal, so that r(p) < p and the agent prefers a sure thing worth the gamble's expected utility to the gamble itself. My claim is that r should be applied exactly once, to the cumulative distribution of *total* discounted utility induced by a *policy* over the whole decision tree, rather than once per decision node to the local gamble. Call this composed risk weighting. I'll argue for four things: (i) in genuinely one-shot decisions it returns exactly the verdicts of the node-by-node version; (ii) over a stream of gambles it yields a "portfolio effect" — the risk penalty attached to any single gamble shrinks with that gamble's share of the aggregate, so one can coherently reject a gamble in isolation and accept a stream of such gambles, and can buy both insurance and high-variance research bets for the same reason; (iii) it is plan-stable, since a policy maximizer's later selves have no grounds to deviate and the usual diachronic exploitation arguments don't get a grip; and (iv) it escapes the standard calibration worry on which expected-utility risk aversion fitted to small-stakes behavior is supposed to entail absurd rejection of large-stakes gambles, because the stake-sensitivity is carried by the aggregation step rather than by the curvature of utility.

The room will be about thirty people. Roughly half are hardline expected-utility types, longtermist in orientation, who think risk aversion over lives saved is a bias to be trained out, reach for dominance and money-pump arguments by reflex, and have all been through the fanaticism arguments many times. Three or four are sympathetic to risk weighting as a theory of rationality — they've read the main book-length defense, they think the sequential-choice objections to it are answerable, and they are the ones most likely to think I've either reinvented it or misread it. Two or three come from the Kelly-betting / growth-rate / time-average camp and will say that both expected utility and risk weighting are wrong in kind about repeated decisions. One or two think tail probabilities aren't estimable at all and that the whole apparatus is premature. Two people are working grantmakers who will only care whether this changes what they do with a portfolio of grants. And there is always someone who asks whose risk attitude is supposed to matter when the stakes are other people's lives.
