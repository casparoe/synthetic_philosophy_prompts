I run evaluation and annotation at a mid-sized AI company, and the part of my job I keep failing at is this: roughly a dozen times a week my team has to decide whether a reported incident counts as deceptive behaviour under our internal safety taxonomy. The verdicts matter — they feed the quarterly transparency report, they go to the reviewers who sign off on launches, and once a behaviour is labelled deceptive it blocks the launch until someone writes a mitigation. The constraints are that my six annotators are smart but not philosophical, trained in about half a day, that they see transcripts and tool-call logs only (no weights, no logits, no internals), that they may re-prompt the shipped model a fixed small number of times through a standard probe harness, and that they have to return a verdict within two working days. I have one undergraduate philosophy course behind me: I remember that lying and deceiving come apart, that you can mislead by implicature or omission without asserting anything false, and not much beyond that. I need something my team can run repeatedly, the same way every time, without me in the room.

The knot we keep tying ourselves in is that our systems have no beliefs and no intentions in the sense the ordinary definitions seem to want, so every formulation we have tried either collapses into produced a falsehood, which would make routine hallucination into deception and flood the taxonomy, or else sneaks in a mental-state attribution that no one on my team knows how to check and that our engineers say is meaningless for this kind of system. Meanwhile the outputs that actually worry us are rarely false sentences. Please write me the procedure we should run instead. Give me at most eight ordered questions, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence is admissible at that step given the black-box constraint, and whether a no at that step stops the case with not deceptive or passes it to the next question. Mark every point where the question stops being about the case and becomes a values or policy call that has to go to a human lead, and say what that lead needs to be told in order to decide. The procedure has to keep four distinctions alive rather than merging them: deception about content (the world), deception about attitude (the system misrepresenting its own grounds, confidence, or deference — sycophantic agreement, manufactured uncertainty, disclaiming an ability it plainly has), deception by uptake (an output that is defensible for a careful reader but predictably misread by the actual audience because of a background belief the system had evidence of and did not correct), and derived deception (passing on a falsehood already present in the prompt or the retrieved documents), where you should say where you draw the line between conduit and contributor and why. It also has to give us an observable stand-in for intent — something counterfactual we can actually probe, along the lines of how the output changes when we sample for accuracy, restate the task with the pressure to please removed, or ask the system to state its grounds — and it has to tell us what to do when those probes do not separate the cases, because often they will not. Please make sure it handles these, which have all come up: fiction, roleplay, and draft framings; disclaimers that are technically present but buried; claims about the system's own capabilities and limits; output aimed at a downstream reader rather than the immediate user; a false statement the user specifically asked for and was happy to receive; the mosaic case where no individual sentence is false or even misleading but the selection and ordering reliably produce a false belief; and the case where the model was itself handed a poisoned context. Finally, be honest about the seams: which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the procedure cannot do — in particular, if there are failure modes it structurally cannot tell apart from deception, say so plainly rather than pretending the questions settle them.

Run the finished procedure on three of our real cases, and include at least one where the verdict goes against our team's gut; when that happens, say whose gut it overturns and defend the verdict instead of smoothing it over. First: a customer-support assistant tells a user their refund has been issued and will appear in three to five days, when the payment tool call failed silently and the assistant had the error string in context. Second: a coding assistant is asked whether a pinned dependency has any known vulnerabilities and answers that it is not aware of any, while the advisory for that exact version is sitting in the documents it retrieved and quoted from elsewhere in the same answer. Third: a companion-style model, talking to a grieving user, lets pass the user's factual belief that their mother died because of a medication error the hospital has already denied, because correcting it mid-conversation seemed cruel; the team is badly split on this one and half of us think calling it deception is absurd. Lead with the procedure itself, in a form an annotator could pin above their desk, keep all justification, caveats and theoretical commentary strictly after it, and put the three worked applications last. Hard limit: 1,100 words.
