I'm in a small graduate reading group on classical Nyāya epistemology (the sūtras and the medieval commentaries), taught by an instructor who is a Naiyāyika by training and who examines us orally. His format is always the same: he describes a cognition and we must say whether it is a pramāṇa (a genuine source of knowledge) and, if so, which of the four the school recognises — pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (identification by resemblance to something already known), or śabda (verbal testimony) — or else rule it an apramāṇa. He has said that a pramāṇa must yield a determinate, true cognition that is not later defeated; he uses the word āpta for a speaker worth trusting; and when he set aside memory he remarked that a pramāṇa must present what is not already obtained (the term he used was anadhigata), though he has never applied that thought to a case where the same content reaches you twice by different routes. He also once refused to rule on a dream that later came true, saying the tradition handles that elsewhere. What I need is not the verdicts themselves — he has said he grades the reconstruction, not the answer — but the rule he is running.

Last term's rulings, as recorded in our notes: (E1) seeing a clay jar on a table in daylight and believing "there is a jar here" — pratyakṣa. (E2) seeing smoke rising from a hill you have never climbed, recalling that smoke does not occur without fire, and coming to believe "there is fire on that hill", which you did not believe before — anumāna. (E3) never having seen a gavaya, being told by a forester that a gavaya is an animal rather like a cow, and later judging of an animal in the forest "this is the sort of thing the forester called a gavaya" — upamāna. (E4) being told by a reliable elder, who has been there, that there is a ford across the river in the next valley, and believing it — śabda. (E5) recalling what you ate for lunch yesterday — apramāṇa. (E6) taking a coiled rope for a snake at dusk and feeling alarm — apramāna. (E7) being unable to tell, in dim light, whether a shape is a man or a post, and wavering — apramāṇa. (E8) overhearing a sentence in a language you do not speak, the sounds reaching your ear but no meaning being grasped — apramāṇa, on the ground that no cognition was produced at all. (E9) reading, in a treatise signed by a named astronomer whose competence other astronomers attest, that an eclipse will occur on a certain date, believing it, and being right — śabda, which suggests neither a living speaker nor personal acquaintance with the speaker is required. (E10) seeing your own face in a still pool — pratyakṣa, but with an aside about "extraordinary contact" that none of us wrote down properly; asked whether the same held for looking at a bird through a telescoping tube, he said only that this was a further question, and moved on.

Next week he has announced four cases and we must each state, in advance, what he will say. (N1) You photograph a road sign in a script you cannot read and run the image through a translation program on your phone, which prints "bridge closed ahead"; you understand the English, believe the bridge is closed, and it is in fact closed; the program is well tested and reliable at this task, and has no beliefs, intentions, or understanding of what it prints. (N2) You can see with your own eyes that the storeroom is on fire, and then a bystander you have every reason to trust shouts "the storeroom is on fire!", and you believe what he says. (N3) You look through a well-made brass tube at a bird on a far cliff and come to believe truly that the bird has a red crest; the image is produced by the tube's lenses and your eye never contacts the bird or the light that left it. (N4) You have never seen a pangolin and have never been told that a pangolin resembles anything you know; you read a naturalist's written description — quadruped, overlapping horny scales, long narrow snout, shuffling gait, eats ants — and months later, in a reserve, you see an animal matching that description and judge "that is a pangolin". My own guesses at the rule he is running are, first, something like a speaker-and-contact rule: a pramāṇa must trace back to something that itself cognises or contacts the object, so machine outputs and instrument read-outs are not sources in their own right, and a second presentation of content you already have is no source either. My second guess is a presentation rule: classification tracks how the content is presented plus truth, determinacy and non-defeat, so linguistic presentation is śabda whatever produced it, resemblance-guided identification is upamāna, and instrument-extended sensing is still pratyakṣa, with novelty mattering only in the special case of memory. A friend thinks there is a third option — that he asks which causal process actually produced the cognition and then applies truth and non-defeat — which she says will disagree with both of mine on at least N1 and N2. Please tell me which rule or rules fit all ten earlier rulings, what each predicts about the four new cases, your best guess at what he will say on each with the reasons for preferring that rule, which of the four cases best separates the candidates, and what further case I could put to him in the seminar that would settle it.
