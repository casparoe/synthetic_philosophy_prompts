ok so i need someone to settle an argument I keep having with my friend about Malthus.

His thing was: population compounds, food production only adds, so any improvement in living standards gets eaten up by more mouths, and wages get knocked back down to subsistence by famine, disease, war, whatever. That obviously didn't happen where I live — food per acre went up by insane multiples after 1800 rather than crawling up in a straight line. My friend says fine, Malthus was wrong, case closed, stop bringing him up.

But i keep running into people saying he was actually right but early: the mechanism is intact, we just bought two centuries of headroom with coal and fertilzer and high-yield crops, and the recokning is merely postponed. That sounds like immunization to me. If you can always slide the date forward, the claim was never falsifiable in the first place, so by Popper's own standard it was never really science — and I don't get why "it's coming later now" is any different from a doomsday cult rescheduling.

So I want an actual rule, not vibes. Here's mine: every time you push out the date on a failed prediction, the theory has to pay for it — narrow its scope, or give up some other claim it used to make about how strong the effect is, or about who it applies to. If you can move the deadline forever without ever losing anything, you've got a pseudo-theory. I call it the moving-goalpost tax.

Does Malthusianism pay the tax or dodge it? Is my rule even coherent, or does it punish exactly the kind of vague long-run structural claim that can't name a date? And part of me suspects there isn't one theory here at all — that people are sliding between something boring and obviously true about any population with limited resources, and a much more specific claim about human wages and human fertility that actually did get falsified. If that's right, then "was Malthus right but early" might just be a badly posed question, and i'd like to know what the well-posed version is.
