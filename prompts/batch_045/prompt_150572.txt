I work on the identity platform team at a mid-size consumer app company, and I have no philosophy background beyond a handful of podcasts. On one of them a guest sketched a view — I've seen it called the "center of narrative gravity" view, and I think it's associated with a philosopher named Dennett, though I can't look that up right now and I'd rather you not lean on the attribution — according to which a person's self is not a thing inside their head but an abstraction defined over the stories told about them and by them, roughly the way a body's center of gravity is a real, locatable, counterfactual-supporting thing that is not made of any additional matter, that you cannot find by dissecting the body, and that can even lie outside the body altogether. This came up at work because we are finally deprecating our old `self` service, and the argument in our team channel split between people who think we are deleting a real thing that users have and people who think we are deleting nothing at all, only a cache. I want to settle it by writing it up, and I want to write it up as a joke.

So: write me a parody internal RFC — call it something like "RFC-0187: The Self as a Derived View" — about 800–1000 words, in the deadpan voice of a staff engineer's design doc at a large consumer-tech company, with the usual section headers (Summary, Background, Non-goals, Design, Edge cases, Migration plan, Open questions) and at least three inline reviewer comments from named colleagues: one skeptical staff engineer who keeps trying to smuggle a real self back in, one person from Legal, and one outside consultant hired for "ontology review." The humor has to come from the engineering being *correct* about the metaphysics, so that someone who actually knows this literature laughs rather than winces. Concretely: the self should have a location and support predictions and counterfactuals while being unstorable; the user's own first-person reports should be weighted as more events in the stream rather than flagged as ground truth; and the abstraction should be defended the way you'd defend any abstraction that earns its keep, with an eval showing that a model consuming the self-vector beats one consuming raw events at predicting behavior, plus one honest admission of where it fails. Please also use the center-of-gravity analogy the way it is actually meant to be used, including the fact that a center of gravity can fall outside the object — so give me an edge case where a user's computed self resolves to a point outside their own body (say, a minor celebrity whose narrative mass sits almost entirely in other people's heads, so the self's coordinates land in a fan wiki's data center) and one where a user's stories bifurcate into two clusters with no narrative bridge, and have the doc refuse to adjudicate which one is the real user while still having to decide what to serve and how to bill it.

The part I care most about is that the authors never cheat. They must not appeal, even once, to a hidden inner fact of the matter — no "the real user underneath," no true preferences, no winking at the reader — even in the places where that would make the design easier, and the jokes should come from how far they have to go to stay consistent. That includes the low-narrative-mass cases: infant accounts, a user in a coma, a user with advanced dementia, where defining a mass threshold below which no self is served quietly decides who stops being served at all. Let the migration plan and the open questions make that cost visible without ever commenting on it. Use "mass" and "gravity" as straight technical terms after at most one deliberate groaner, keep the corporate register intact throughout, and don't cite or name any real philosopher or paper anywhere in the document itself — invented internal people only.
