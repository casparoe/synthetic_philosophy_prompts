I've been reading about program equilibrium — the result that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma where each player submits a program that can read the other player's source code, mutual cooperation becomes available as a Nash equilibrium: a program on the order of "cooperate with any program that cooperates with me" is a best response to a counterpart of the same shape, with no repetition, no external enforcer, no communication of intent, and no altruism. (Making the self-reference work takes a fixed-point trick — quining, or keying cooperation to provability rather than to literal identity of source — and the bad equilibrium in which both sides submit "always defect" is present too, so the result makes cooperation available rather than inevitable.) What I keep coming back to is that the operative mechanism is the legibility of a decision procedure, not the content of anyone's will.

Now hold that against competition law, which almost everywhere punishes a price-fixing *agreement* while treating merely parallel conduct as lawful. Concretely: a supermarket chain posts a public, standing policy — "find a lower price locally and we'll beat it by ten percent" — and its rival posts the same. Economists have argued that such guarantees are precisely what sustain high prices, since they remove the point of undercutting: any cut is matched, so no one gains share, and the cooperative outcome holds. Yet this is unilateral conduct and is treated as benign, even pro-competitive. Meanwhile an exchange of messages between the same two firms — "we're raising prices in March, and we'd welcome it if you did the same" — is close to a criminal agreement, even if the messages change nothing about the conditional policies the firms were already running. And in between sits the case of a firm that publicly announces its future prices in a form designed to be read by its rivals, or publishes its pricing rule in machine-readable form rather than in prose addressed to anyone.

What I want is the implicit theory. What must an agreement be — a meeting of wills, a communication with a particular illocutionary force, the exercise of a normative power to bind oneself, a partial transfer of control over one's future conduct — for the practice of punishing the messages but licensing the posted guarantees to be anything more than moral luck about the medium? Reconstruct that theory explicitly, and then tell me which parts of the practice it *strictly* presupposes and which it merely fits comfortably: is the agreement requirement really a piece of the philosophy of action, or is it equally well explained by worries about proof and administrability, or by a desire not to chill conduct (meeting a rival's price) that usually helps consumers? Where exactly is the doctrine underdetermined, so that two or three quite different theories would license the same pattern?

Two further things I'd like you to take on. First, is there a defensible version of the will-based view that survives program equilibrium — for instance one that marks the boundary at each party's retained power to revise its own policy at will, so that revocability is what makes conduct unilateral? That looks unstable to me, since the equilibrium depends on the policy being fixed at the moment of play, but I'd like to see it worked out rather than gestured at. Second: if we replaced the intention-centered theory with a structural one on which coordination consists in the interlocking of decision procedures, what would enforcement actually have to look like, and what incentives would it create? My worry is that it would make illegibility into a defence — a firm could hardly be said to have reached a structural understanding with a rival whose algorithm it could not read — and I want to know whether that incentive is worse than the present one, which is an incentive not to talk.
