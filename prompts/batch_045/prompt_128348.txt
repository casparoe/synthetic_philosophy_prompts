i'm TAing intro ethics this term and we're at the end of the free will section, on Frankfurt-style cases and whether the principle of alternative possibilities survives them, and I want to close the last class with a poem instead of another handout. The reason I care more than I should: last year I did something fairly shitty and I keep describing it to myself as "I couldn't have done otherwise," and it's strange how little that sentence actually comforts me — which is, I think, more or less the intuition the cases are built to squeeze. My flatmate, who's a compatibilist about everything, says this just shows the sentence was never doing the excusing; I'm not sure shes right but I can't say why.

Anyway, here's the variant I came up with and can't get out of my head, and I'd rather the poem be built on it than on the usual neuroscientist-with-a-chip setup. In mine the agent does the thing in the actual sequence for her own reasons, fine, but the counterfactual intervener is keyed not to some prior twitch or blush or change of heart but to hesitation itself — the instant she starts to waver, or even starts to try to waver, that's the cue, and the device quietly settles her. So she gets curious and decides to test it: she'll half waver on purpose, see whether she's stopped, and thereby find out whether she's the kind of person who could have done otherwise. The test is self-defeating of course — trying to waver just is wavering, so the device closes on her and she never learns a thing. What I like about it is that it seems to shut the flicker-of-freedom reply as well, since the single thing she could have done instead is the single thing that's rigged, and yet it also doesn't feel like it proves anything, because the alternative it takes away isn't the one anyone was leaning on in the first place. I genuinely can't tell whether that's an argument or just a mood, and I'd like the poem to hold that ambiguity rather than pick.

Form: I want a sestina — six rotating end words plus the envoi. The ones I keep circling are hesitate, sign, hand, could, otherwise, mine, but swap any out if better ones occur to you. The reason I want a sestina specifically is that the rotation should be doing the philosophical work: the same six words coming back in new slots until each has occupied every position is more or less what this debate has done with its handful of concepts for fifty years. The turn shouldn't land at a fixed line; it should be wherever the reader realises she's testing the device and that the test itself is what triggers it. First person, her voice. The envoi has to get "I could have" and "I could not" next to each other and refuse to choose between them. Two things matter to me beyond that: it should be accurate enough that someone who actually works on this wouldn't wince — the intervention stays counterfactual, the actual sequence runs on her own reasons, and nothing in the poem should imply the device made her do it — and it shouldn't lean on the abstract vocabulary. If you can get through the whole thing without the words freedom, choice, responsibility and will I'd be thrilled; if one has to appear, make it earn its place. Don't resolve it, don't turn it into a sermon, I want people still arguing in the corridor. Somewhere around 30-45 lines. If the sestina really fights you, a dramatic monologue in blank verse at about the same length with the same constraints is fine, but try the sestina first.
