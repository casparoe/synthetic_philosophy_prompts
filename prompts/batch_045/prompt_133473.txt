I've been going to a small reading group on strong longtermism — the view, roughly, that because the long-run future could contain vastly more well-being than the present does, the effects of our actions on that future dominate everything else in the moral accounting — and I've noticed that my gut verdicts on concrete cases don't line up with how the view is usually presented. I'd like you to help me reverse-engineer whatever principle I'm implicitly using. To keep it concrete, picture the same $5M grant each time, and here is what I find myself thinking: (1) A program that shaves a one-in-ten-thousand slice off the chance that civilization ends this century — clearly worth it, and it feels like the most urgent thing I can imagine funding; I'd fund it over almost anything. (2) A program that would pull the date of large-scale interstellar settlement about twenty-five years closer, which by the same style of arithmetic should reclaim an enormous amount of otherwise-unrealized value — this one merely feels fine, like a nice-to-have; I'd rank it far below (1) and I can't say why. (3) An intervention that makes durable global institutions slightly better, so that everyone who lives over the next ten millennia is, say, a hundredth of a percent better off — I feel more confident about this than about (1), even though the expected magnitude looks smaller and the causal story is sketchier. (4) An endowment designed to survive for centuries, holding certain moral ideas in trust and re-disbursing when its trustees judge the moment right — this strikes me as faintly absurd and probably inert, despite having a causal chain of the same length and vagueness as (3). (5) A proven global-health charity that would save on the order of a thousand lives in the next decade — I feel bad skipping this in a way I don't feel bad skipping (2), which seems inconsistent given that I take the far-future stakes in (2) to be larger. (6) A policy that changes which people come to exist in the future but makes nobody better or worse off — I register this as worth nothing at all. (7) Work aimed at making it likelier that, if the long future is largely populated by digital minds, those minds' lives go well rather than badly — this feels almost as important as (1), even though it is entirely about beings who would not otherwise exist, and even though my reaction to (6) was that identity questions don't matter. (8) Reducing the small chance that whichever civilization first expands beyond Earth locks in a bad set of values permanently — this feels overwhelmingly important, and yet it gives me the same queasy unverifiability that made me dismiss (4).

The only thing I can offer as a candidate is something I've started calling navigability: a future good counts fully only if the causal path from my action to it runs through agents who remain able to reassess, revise, or redirect what has been passed to them, so that goods I lock in by momentum or ship to strangers down a channel nobody is steering count for less. Please test that candidate against cases (1) through (8) and tell me where it works and where it obviously fails, and then either repair it or replace it with something better: state the principle precisely, clause by clause, saying which case each clause is there to accommodate, and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save and would therefore force me to give up. Two more things. First, apply whatever principle you arrive at to the case I genuinely have no stable verdict on — spending to increase the number of flourishing beings in the far future rather than the quality of any of their lives; my number goes up enormously and my gut goes blank, and I'd like to know whether the principle says anything here or whether it runs out. Second, generate one further case the principle has a surprising verdict on, and tell me whether that verdict is a cost or a selling point. I'm also interested in whether what I've been describing is really a version of longtermism with an extra filter bolted on, or something that quietly undercuts the whole framework — for instance, if my principle turns out to be a discount for causal or evidential distance, why doesn't it also kill case (1)?
