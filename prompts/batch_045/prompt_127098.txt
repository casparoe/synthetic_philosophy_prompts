Tranquilism is the theory of welfare on which a person's well-being is determined entirely by the extent to which they are free of craving and free of suffering: nothing else — pleasure, achievement, knowledge, love — makes a life better in itself, though such things can matter instrumentally, insofar as they quiet cravings or keep suffering away.

A post on a philosophy blog I came across recently presents the view in these terms:

    Tranquilism says that pleasure and pain are not what really matter; what matters is whether you want things you don't have. Suffering is best understood as an especially intense unfulfilled desire, so the theory really has just one currency — unsatisfied desire — and, since it is a purely negative theory, it has no way to make sense of a life going positively well. A person without cravings is already at the maximum level of welfare, and any two craving-free people are equally well off, whether one has written symphonies and raised children and the other has spent fifty years in a bare room feeling nothing in particular. That is also why the theory is refuted by the case of the contented patient who has been quietly drugged into wanting nothing: if we want to say that something has gone badly for her, we have to give up tranquilism.

I suspect this is not entirely fair, but I don't know where to draw the lines. Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a defender of tranquilism would accept, what they would call a distortion or a common oversimplification, and whether the closing verdict — that the drugged-patient case refutes the view — survives once the mischaracterizations are corrected.

Two points I would especially like addressed, since I can't tell how the theory is supposed to handle them:

1. Suppose a craving can be removed in either of two ways: by getting the thing craved, or by a pill that simply deletes the craving while leaving you without the thing you wanted. On the theory's own terms, is there any difference in welfare between these two outcomes, and should that answer be counted as a cost of the view or as something it can comfortably live with?

2. The summary reduces suffering to a form of unsatisfied desire, and treats craving as nothing more than an unfulfilled want. If both of those reductions are rejected — if suffering can come apart from desire, and if not every desire is a craving — how much of the summary's case is left standing?
