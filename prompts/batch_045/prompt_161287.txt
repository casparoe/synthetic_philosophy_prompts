I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and I'm on our in-house evidence panel — the little group that gets called when an editor and a fact-checker disagree about whether a claim is actually supported. Right now we've got a piece that's been through three rewrites and still isn't resolved. The reporter's central claim is that a federal science agency shelved a proposed safety rule mainly because of an internal cost estimate it never published, after months of industry lobbying. Her support is: one on-the-record interview with a mid-level official who was in the room but has since been reassigned and won't repeat it on tape; two anonymous sources, one of whom is a former contractor whose firm lost work when the rule was first floated and who definately has an axe to grind; a slide deck that came through the same encrypted channel as three earlier batches of documents that all checked out, but that we can't authenticate on its own; a projection from a 40-site pilot study up to a national figure; and a comparison to a 2010s case where a similar rule died after a big industry comment campaign. The news desk wants to run it as news tomorrow. One editor wants to hold until we can authenticate the deck. Another wants to run it now but reframed as reported analysis rather than as a claim about what actually happened. The reporter will read whatever we write, so I need reasons she could actually argue with, not an edict.

Here's my angle, which is why I'm asking you instead of just drafting it myself. I got into an argument last weekend with a friend who's finishing a PhD on classical Indian epistemology, and she said our whole sourcing checklist — single source, two sources, hard document, on the record — is crude next to the Nyāya framework, which apparently sorts reliable ways of knowing into four kinds: perception, inference, comparison or analogy, and testimony from a reliable person. The parts that stuck with me were that testimony is treated as its own kind of warrant rather than as a species of inference; that there's an elaborate apparatus for when the generalization that licenses an inference (she used a Sanskrit word that sounded like vyaapti) fails to hold; that knowing is supposed to involve some second-order check on your own cognition; and that there's a separate puzzle about memory not counting as knowledge. I've probably garbled all of that. But it struck me that our handbook has nothing useful to say about most of what this story actually turns on, and that a memo organized around those distinctions would be worth more than another round of arguing about whether we have two sources.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should land on one recommendation — run as news, hold pending some specific step with a date attached, or reframe as analysis — and it should work through, in whatever order makes sense: what makes someone count as a reliable testifier for our purposes when our best source is anonymous and interested; whether the slide deck is perception, testimony, or something else, and why that classification should change how much weight it gets; how strong a linking generalization has to be before it can carry a flat assertion versus a hedge, applied concretely to the "agencies kill rules under industry pressure" generalization; whether the historical parallel is doing any real epistemic work or is just rhetorical garnish; and finally whether you'd recommend we rewrite the evidence section of the handbook along these lines at all, including how a fact-checker with a deadline would actually apply it. Please give the strongest version of whichever option you're rejecting — the news desk will push back hard if we hold, and I'd rather answer that in the memo than in the room. And keep it usable: no Sanskrit, no seminar, nothing a senior editor will skim past.
