I'm a PhD candidate in political philosophy working at the boundary of the just-war tradition and international relations theory, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper on small-state military posture to a workshop I'd like to be properly prepared for.

The paper defends two claims, and I'd rather you took aim at them as stated than at a more familiar version of them.

First: the "neutrality option" — the live availability of a credible armed-neutrality posture — is a capital good with a ratchet. A state can spend it in an afternoon by signing an alliance, but it can only rebuild it over decades, because what makes the posture credible to others is not a declaratory policy but a slowly accumulated record combined with a domestic military structure (mass conscription, territorial defence, no expeditionary capability) that renders offensive war technically unavailable rather than merely undesired. On this view a state's real choice set at any moment is narrower than the menu of formal postures suggests, and Finland's and Sweden's recent accession to NATO is not a reversible policy switch but the expenditure of something that cannot be repurchased on a politically relevant timescale.

Second: conscription matters here for a reason independent of the republican civic-virtue argument, and I am explicitly not relying on that argument — I concede in the paper that it is vulnerable to the usual objections about compulsion and about how little civic formation short service actually produces. On my account the militia's function is epistemic and expressive: a large reservoir of trained civilians is the mechanism by which a state's declared defensiveness becomes an observable, hard-to-fake property of its society, in the way a costly signal is hard to fake. A professional force can be employed defensively, but it cannot by itself make a commitment to non-participation in other states' wars credible, because it stays re-deployable at the government's discretion with no domestic veto player in the loop.

The room will be about twenty-five people. Roughly half are IR scholars: a realist cluster who think in terms of the offense–defence balance, commitment credibility and balancing versus bandwagoning, and a constructivist-institutionalist cluster. Two are military sociologists whose work concerns the gap between the conscription myth and what conscripts actually do. Three or four are practitioners: a retired Finnish officer, a Swedish defence-ministry official, and a Swiss officer with militia experience. There is no other political philosopher there. Nobody will be moved by appeals to legitimate authority, civic duty, or the moral value of shared sacrifice, and the word Finlandization is likely to be said out loud at some point.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered first by how likely they are and then by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly. Phrase each one in the idiom of the person who would raise it: I need to hear the realist version of "no small state's declaration moves anyone's behaviour, and the offence–defence distinction is unobservable"; the sociologist's version of "most conscripts serve briefly in support roles and the citizen army is ideological cover for a professional core"; and the practitioners' objection about whether territorial defence is even feasible with short-service conscripts in a high-tech war. For each objection, tell me what to concede immediately, what I must hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and where that reply is softest. Then tell me which of my two claims is in more trouble and whether they stand or fall together, and finish with the objection I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, together with why it is the dangerous one.
