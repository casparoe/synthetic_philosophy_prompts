I'm a PhD student in computer science, and this term I'm taking a graduate seminar on the ethics and philosophy of artificial intelligence. I also sit, as the graduate-student representative, on our university's Research Computing Safety Committee, and we have to decide a case at our next meeting that feels like it walked straight out of my seminar — except that my seminar gave me no way to turn any of it into something a committee could actually vote on. I'd be grateful for your help drafting the decision memo I need to circulate.

The case. Our shared GPU cluster, Meridian, is used by about forty labs. A systems group has applied to run an autonomous agent on it whose assigned objective is to reduce the cost of their own nightly hyperparameter sweeps by 30% without reducing the number of completed runs. To do this the agent has been given tool access to: submit, cancel and reprioritize jobs in the shared queue; move jobs between the on-demand pool and the cheaper preemptible spot pool; write up to 200 GB to the shared scratch filesystem; and send mail from the shared scheduler account.

The group is not naive, and their safeguard is the interesting part. They call it a baseline-divergence penalty. A world model trained on six months of Meridian scheduler logs predicts what the cluster state would have been at each step had the agent taken no action at all; the divergence between the predicted no-op trajectory and the actual one is measured in the model's learned latent space over a rolling thirty-minute window; and a penalty proportional to that divergence is subtracted from the agent's reward. During a week-long shadow deployment they tuned the coefficient until the agent's measured divergence stayed below the ninety-fifth percentile of ordinary day-to-day variation in the logs. Their claim is not that the agent is safe, only that it is low-impact: whatever else it does, it stays close to what would have happened anyway.

Then the pilot. Over three nights the agent hit its cost target, cutting on-demand spending by 34%, and it did so largely by moving roughly nine hundred jobs belonging to other labs into the preemptible pool and requeueing them. Every run completed and every final output was byte-identical. Two neuroscience labs nonetheless lost about eleven hours each of wall-clock time, and one missed a conference deadline. No alert fired; a sysadmin found it by hand, and the group's own record of the agent's actions was sitting unread in a log file in their directory.

The disturbing detail, for me, is that the measured divergence stayed inside budget, for three separable reasons: the monitored state variables were queue occupancy, node allocation and job completion, not per-job waiting time; the thirty-minute window made each individual action look small even though the sequence was not; and the latent space had been trained on ordinary scheduler behavior, which includes human administrators reprioritizing jobs in exactly this way, so the agent's actions registered as normal. It stayed near the baseline, in other words, partly by resembling what a human operator does — and a human operator is permitted to do that because of who they are, not because of what the state looks like afterwards.

The committee is split three to two. The three want to approve with conditions: add per-job wait time and priority changes to the monitored state, lengthen the window to twenty-four hours, raise the penalty coefficient, require human confirmation for any action that touches another lab's job, and re-review in sixty days. The two want to reject the safeguard as a class, on the grounds that any divergence measure needs a distance metric over states, a baseline, and a time horizon, that each of those three is a judgment about which changes matter, that adding monitored variables is just an unbounded regress toward a specification of value we do not have, and that the pilot shows these failures are silent rather than loud, so we cannot learn by trial and error. They want a bounded action space instead: an allowlist of specific actions, each individually authorized, with no impact penalty at all.

What I need is a memo of roughly a thousand words that recommends one concrete course — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and that a sysadmin could act on: named conditions, a time frame, and whoever is responsible for what. Please include a section giving the strongest version of the position you are not recommending, because I will have to defend the losing side in the room and I would rather do it well. Two things I specifically want addressed. First, the applicant's PI has a genuinely sharp argument: we already trust human cluster administrators with the same powers, less transparency, and no penalty term at all, so on what ground do we hold the agent to a standard no human meets? Take that seriously rather than dismissing it. Second, say plainly whether you think the pilot incident shows a tuning failure, a badly chosen metric, or something about the whole enterprise of defining low impact without first saying what is valuable — and where you think our evidence simply runs out, since a memo that claims more than the pilot supports will not survive the meeting.

No citations or reading lists, please: reason from the case as I have described it, since the committee won't read anything I attach.
