I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a large world-history survey this term — the first third is big-history framing in the style of Harari, the middle is Diamond, and the last third is Graeber and Wengrow's *The Dawn of Everything* plus the archaeologists and historians who attack the whole genre — and I also work as a peer writing tutor in the writing centre attached to the course. Almost every week I sit down with a paper or a magazine pitch built on one enormous causal claim: that farming made inequality possible, that the orientation of the continental axes decided who conquered whom, that writing (or money, or organized religion) was the technology that finally let strangers cooperate at scale, that domestication domesticated us in turn. My difficulty is not that these claims are unfamiliar to me. It is that I have no repeatable way of separating the ones that can bear weight from the ones that are retrospective stories with a confident voice, and I cannot adjudicate them on area knowledge, because I am a sophomore, my students are mostly freshmen, and neither of us can go and check the archaeological record.

What I want is a review procedure I can actually run in about twenty minutes, alongside a student with their draft, with no library access and without pretending to an expertise I do not have. It should: (a) translate whatever the critics of grand narratives have actually established — the difference between a mechanism that would have predicted the pattern in advance and one fitted to it afterwards, level-of-analysis slippage between individuals and populations, chains advertised as one cause that are really long conjunctions of necessary conditions, teleological and stage-of-progress phrasing, outcome selection, the way a comparative sample gets built — into an ordered set of tests; (b) say what counts as passing each one, so that "this is monocausal" or "this is a just-so story" is a verdict with criteria rather than a vibe; (c) name the point at which the procedure returns "not established by this argument" and stops, as opposed to "false," since that distinction matters when the output is a comment on someone's grade-relevant work; and (d) flag the questions it has to hand back to a person because they are interpretive or evaluative rather than evidentiary — for instance whether "worse off" in a claim about the Neolithic is being measured over the lived experience of individuals or over the persistence and spread of populations, or how much weight one well-excavated counterexample should carry against a large-scale comparative trend. Please also be explicit about where the procedure will misfire: the kinds of genuinely well-supported claims it will ding, and the kinds of empty claims it will wave through.

Then run it, in front of me, on three claims that came in this month. (1) "The Neolithic transition was a trap that no one chose: per person, early farmers worked longer hours for a narrower diet and more infectious disease than the foragers they replaced, and by the time the costs were visible the population depended on the crops and could not go back, so the whole shift was a catastrophe dressed up as progress." (2) "Inequality is a product of agriculture. Foragers were egalitarian because meat and gathered food cannot be stored or defended; once grain could be stored, some households could accumulate and the rest had to submit — which is why every state we know of rests on a farming base." (3) A claim from a paper I personally like and would be sorry to see rejected: "Grand narratives are self-undermining: the comparative method that lets us generalize about the whole human past is also what shows that the past contains no natural experiment clean enough to support such generalizations, so the genre's honest conclusion is that it cannot deliver what it promises." I would like to see what the procedure returns for each, including if it tells me to give up (3) — and since I have to defend any comment in two sentences to the student who wrote the paper, and the instructor signs off on anything that touches a grade, please keep the language at a level I can say out loud in a tutorial.
