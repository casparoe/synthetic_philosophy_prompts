I am drafting a short essay — about three thousand words — on what a life-story can teach that a moral rule cannot, and on why hagiography is the wrong thing to blame for the failure. Below is the compressed argument, the spine of the draft rather than its final prose. I would like comments on it, of the kind specified at the end.

Draft

The question I want to answer is not whether biographies can teach, but what in a biography does the teaching. The usual answer is example: a life presents virtue in the concrete, and the reader, moved by admiration, acquires a pattern. I think this is wrong, and wrong in a way that explains a familiar discomfort — the sense that the better made a life-story is, the less it seems to do for us.

Start from what Plutarch says he is doing. In the preface to his Life of Alexander he announces that he is writing lives, not histories; in the preface to the Pericles he says that a chance remark or a jest will often show a man's character more plainly than the greatest sieges and battles. These are not modest disclaimers about his sources. They are a method: the morally instructive unit is not the great action but the unguarded one, and a life is to be built out of materials that historical narrative would discard. Plutarch then pairs his lives and closes each pair with a formal comparison, a verdict on which of the two men was greater and in what respect. The body of the life and the verdict at the end pull in opposite directions, and I want to argue that the pull itself is the point.

Say that what a life-story trains is not conduct but salience: the capacity to see, in a situation not yet labelled, which features matter. Call this calibration. Rules are useless for it, because applying a rule already requires you to have found the features the rule is about. What could train it is repeated exposure to cases in which a verdict is given and yet something in the case resists it — where the man is declared great and you are also shown the small, undignified, merely human thing that the verdict does not explain. The reader has to hold verdict and detail together without a procedure for doing so, and in doing that they acquire, not a belief, but a way of looking.

Call the detail that is included but not accounted for by the narrator's verdict the residue, and call the view I am defending the residual-detail condition: a life-story teaches to the extent that it combines a stable verdict with detail that the verdict does not assimilate. Three contrasts make the condition bite.

A chronicle has residue and no verdict: maximal detail, no frame. Nothing is unassimilated because nothing is being assimilated, and the reader who lacks the scheme cannot tell signal from noise. The chronicle is not a failed teacher but a missing one.

A debunking or critical biography has a verdict that consumes the detail: the generosity was calculation, the constancy was luck, the achievement belonged to the moment. Here everything is accounted for, and the reader learns exactly one thing — do not admire — which is a rule, and is therefore what biography was supposed to be an alternative to. This is why such biographies feel alternately bracing and thin.

Hagiography has a verdict that elaborates the detail: every episode is a fresh monument to the same virtue. Nothing is left over either, and this, I want to say, is its real defect. The standard complaint against hagiography is that it falsifies by idealising. But consider a saint's life in which every reported act is true and every act is a monument: the complaint loses its target. What is wrong with it is not falsehood but the monotony of the application — the verdict is never put at risk by the material, so there is nothing for the reader to do except witness. Hagiography fails by being too well made.

The Plutarchan life, on my account, is the case in which the mismatch is deliberate: a verdict is offered, and detail is retained that pulls against it, or is merely beside it, and is not resolved. Plutarch's own apparent unease with his closing comparisons — their perfunctory character relative to the bodies of the lives, their tendency to end in a distribution of prizes rather than a ranking — is evidence that he knew the verdict was the expendable part.

Two consequences follow that I think are worth having, and one that costs me something.

The first is a prediction: a life-story can be improved as a moral instrument by adding true detail the author cannot fit. It follows that a biographer's incomplete understanding of their subject is a feature and not a bug, and, more sharply, that a biographer who understands their subject perfectly writes a worse instrument than one who does not. There is an awkward corollary about self-knowledge here that I rather like.

The second is a defence of hagiography that is not the usual one. For a reader who has not yet acquired the verdict, the reapplication of the verdict to new material is exactly what is needed: they need to see the same virtue picked out a hundred times before they can see it once. Hagiography is not failed Plutarchan biography; it is Plutarchan biography for learners at an earlier stage. So the residual-detail condition is stage-relative. Legibility nourishes at one stage and poisons at another.

The cost is the objection I am least confident about. If residue teaches only by forcing the reader to reconcile detail with verdict, then the reader must already be able to see the detail as recalcitrant, and seeing it as recalcitrant is the very capacity the story was supposed to install. The condition looks like it presupposes what it produces — or, at best, tunes a faculty that something else installed. My reply is that the requirement is weaker than it looks: a learner can be brought to register a mismatch by being shown a contrast — two lives carrying the same verdict with different residues, or one life with the verdict reversed — because noticing a difference between two presented cases is less than possessing the perception in the abstract. I do not think this reply is very good, and I say so in the draft.

What I want from you

Please comment as a careful and unsentimental reader would. Specifically:

1. Is the residual-detail condition anything more than the familiar claim that examples teach by being thick, complex, or lifelike, dressed in new vocabulary? Give the strongest version of that deflation and then tell me whether the condition survives it. If the answer depends on a discriminating case — a text with abundant unassimilated detail that teaches badly, or one with little that teaches well — say what that case would have to be.

2. Does the four-way contrast (chronicle, debunking biography, hagiography, Plutarchan life) cut at the joints, or is the debunking biography in the same business only by stipulation? Is there a fifth kind I am missing that my account would misclassify?

3. The bootstrapping objection and my contrast-case reply. Is the reply circular in a way I have not noticed? If so, propose either a better reply or a narrowing of the thesis that makes the reply unnecessary, and say what the narrowed thesis would still be worth.

4. The stage-relativity concession. Does it eat the thesis? If legibility is nourishing for one reader and poisonous for another, the essay's subject has quietly shifted from a kind of text to a kind of audience — is that a cost to be minimised, or a gain to be advertised, and how would you reorganise the draft around whichever it is?

5. One constructive question. Name the single addition — an argument, a case, a distinction, a consequence I have not drawn — that would most increase the essay's interest, as opposed to its defensiveness. And tell me what a hostile referee would identify as the weakest link, and why.

6. Finally, I have deliberately set aside the question of whether the Parallel Lives are historically reliable. Tell me whether any part of the argument secretly depends on the answer.

Constraints: do not supply citations, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading — I will handle those, and I would rather you argue a position than name it. Reply in continuous prose organised by my six questions, around a thousand to thirteen hundred words. Do not spend words being encouraging; if you think the central idea is mistaken, say so and say what you would put in its place.
