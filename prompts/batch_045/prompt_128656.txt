I'm giving a short lecture next month on whether there's anything to be said for legally shortening the working week, and I want to get the background framing right rather than repeat something I half-remember. A long read on the history of work I came across online sets it up like this:

Modern people work as hard as they do because Calvinism taught that worldly success was a sign you were among the saved. Hard work began as a way of quieting religious anxiety rather than for any economic reason, and Weber closes his famous book by saying that this spirit now prowls through our economic order like the ghost of a religious belief that has died — which is really just to say that the work ethic is a religious leftover with no rational basis. The obvious reply is that the whole story is false anyway: capitalism was flourishing in Italian and Flemish cities well before the Reformation, so the Reformation cannot have produced it. Even so, the leftover does real damage. France decided to work less, cutting its statutory week to thirty-five hours and accepting lower output in exchange for more time off, while Japan never shed the same compulsion and now compensates families for deaths from overwork, which is simply the cost of a culture that never learned to say no. Once you see that the work ethic is a contingent religious inheritance rather than common sense, there is no serious argument left against simply mandating a shorter week.

Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful historian of the Protestant ethic would accept, what is the popularizer's simplification, and what would get me laughed at if I said it from a lectern. I suspect the bit about the Italian cities is too quick, but the "religious leftover" line sounds right to me. Finally, does the piece's closing conclusion follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction? Where your verdict turns on a genuinely disputed empirical claim about the French or Japanese experience, say that rather than picking a side.
