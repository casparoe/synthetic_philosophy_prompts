I've been asked to give a short "where I stand" talk to a joint group of philosophers and theologians, and a colleague who read the draft told me that some part of the package has to go. I can't see which part. Here is the position, in five theses:

(1) Religious belief is not a hypothesis. To believe in God is not to hold a claim about how things are that evidence could confirm or disconfirm; it is to live by a picture that stands fast for the believer, and the security involved is closer to the framework certainties of the later Wittgenstein's On Certainty than to the confidence of a well-supported conclusion. Believing here is groundless in the sense of not resting on reasons.

(2) None of this makes religious utterances less than true or false. God is merciful, if it is true, is made true by how things are with God and not by the practices of the community that uses the sentence, and it could be false even for a community whose whole form of life depends on it.

(3) Theistic belief is nonetheless warranted without argument: it is the output of a cognitive faculty — I mean the sort of thing the Reformed epistemologists call proper functionalism, together with their talk of a sensus divinitatis — that is reliable when it functions properly in the environment it was designed for, so that theistic belief can amount to knowledge while nothing at all counts as propositional evidence for it.

(4) The amount and distribution of suffering in the world is a genuine intellectual objection to theism. It does not refute it, but it is the strongest thing on the other side, and a thoughtful theist who fully took it in ought to be less confident than they would otherwise be.

(5) The fact of religious diversity is not a defeater at all. That other traditions rest on very different pictures, held with the same kind of groundless security, gives me no reason to doubt mine, since these pictures are not competing explanations of a common subject matter — and I do not think I owe any account of why my community's picture rather than theirs is the correct one.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look as if they do, and what is the least costly way to make the set hang together? I don't want a survey of the literature, and I don't want to be told who to read or which thesis I ought to find most attractive — I want the audit, tracing what each commitment commits me to.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Items 1 to 3 should be the most serious real tensions in the set, in descending order of severity, each naming the theses that clash, saying why the clash is real, and saying what exactly has to give if I insist on keeping both members of the pair; if you think there are only two real tensions, say so in item 3 and explain why the next candidate is not one. Item 4 should be the apparent tension that dissolves once the theses are read carefully, with a sentence on why it is only apparent. Item 5 should be the single revision that would remove the most tension at the least cost to what I actually want to say, together with what that revision costs me.
