I am writing a commentary for a volume on the philosophy of spacetime, and one section of it is a disciplined exercise in "what would Einstein have made of that?" I need you to commit yourself: give me one reconstruction, defended, rather than a menu of alternatives with their pros and cons.

The background I am working from can be taken as settled. In 1913, while still hunting for the gravitational field equations, Einstein constructed the "hole" argument: take a region of spacetime devoid of matter, hold the fields fixed outside and to the past of it, and note that a generally covariant theory will admit two solutions that agree everywhere outside the hole yet differ inside it. He concluded that general covariance was untenable, because it spoiled the unique determination of the field by its sources. From the 1980s onwards the argument was revived and re-aimed — the revival is associated with Earman, Stachel and Norton — this time at manifold substantivalism, the view that the points of the spacetime manifold are individuals that exist independently of the fields defined on them. The revived argument leans on Leibniz equivalence (diffeomorphic models represent one and the same physical possibility) and then argues that, for a substantivalist, the theory goes indeterministic: a complete state on a Cauchy surface, together with the field equations, fails to fix which of two diffeomorphic continuations obtains. There is also a quieter verificationist horn: the difference between the two models would be in principle unobservable.

The part I want help with is a variant I have been calling the fading hole. Instead of a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a bounded region, consider one that is the identity on and to the past of some Cauchy surface but differs from the identity at every point to its future, while its displacement from the identity decays smoothly — faster than any inverse power — as one goes to spatial infinity on each later slice. So there is no walled-off hole: the two models differ everywhere in the future, but they differ by less than any assigned amount outside a sufficiently large bounded region, and they agree exactly at infinity. Standard practice treats diffeomorphisms that reduce to the identity at the boundary as gauge, and those that act non-trivially there as genuine physical symmetries; the fading hole sits precisely on the seam, since it is trivial at infinity yet has no localised hole.

So, concretely:

1. Would Einstein have taken the fading hole to be a case his own argument covers, or would he have said that the absence of a bounded hole makes all the difference — and which way would he have thought the difference cuts?
2. On which horn would he have pressed: the threat to determinism, or the threat that the theory posits unknowable facts? He was, after all, a physicist whose sympathies ran against unobservable structure; say which consideration he would have weighted more heavily and why.
3. Would he have accepted Leibniz equivalence as a premise, granted that in his own 1913 use of the argument he appears to have treated the differing solutions as genuinely distinct? Is there a coherent path from the 1913 Einstein to the post-1980 conclusion, or would he have had to repudiate something he believed?
4. Where would he have placed himself in the resulting landscape — relationalism, straightforward substantivalism, or the position on which the manifold's points are individuals only up to the structure the fields induce? Give me one answer, with the reason that would have weighed most with him.
5. Would he have welcomed the later use of the hole argument as a general template for detecting surplus structure in theories quite unlike general relativity — in particular in attempts to quantise gravity background-independently, where there may be no manifold of points to drag around at all — or would he have thought the template over-extended?

Please be explicit about which premise you think he would have rejected, and flag the single step in your reconstruction that you take to be most speculative, with a word on why it is shakier than the rest. I do not want citations, references or a reading list: the piece is for a general readership, and what I need is conceptual reconstruction from the case as I have set it out. Please answer in British English.
