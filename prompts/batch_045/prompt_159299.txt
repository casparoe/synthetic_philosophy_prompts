So I'm a backend dev, never took a philosophy class in my life, and I've got an argument going in a group chat that I now want to turn into a blog post — and I've realized I don't have the vocabulary for the thing I'm actually arguing about, which I think is the old fight about whether an author's intentions decide what a text means.

The setup: The Matrix (1999), written and directed by the Wachowskis together. Everyone's seen it, so I won't recap the plot. In the 2010s both sisters came out publicly as trans women, and somewhere around then I half-remember one of them saying in an interview that the movie had always been partly intended as an allegory about gender transition. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real quote or something the internet collectively hallucinated, so plese treat it as a hypothetical if it isn't. Either way, nowadays a big chunk of the audience reads the film that way: the red pill / blue pill choice, Agent Smith stubbornly calling him "Mr. Anderson" while everyone else calls him Neo, Neo's body being rebuilt in the pod, the "the body cannot live without the mind" bit, "Know Thyself" carved over the Oracle's kitchen door. And meanwhile basically everyone in 1999 read it as Plato's cave plus cyberpunk plus a Christian/Gnostic messiah story — Neo is an anagram of "one", there's Trinity and Zion and a resurrection scene — and the film openly invites that reading too.

So here's what I want: a reading of the film through the authorial-intention debate, faithful in both directions. Not a survey of the positions, and no reading list or citations — I can't tell a real citation from a made-up one, so just argue.

Three hypotheses of mine that I'd rather you attack than agree with:

1. I think the fight is mostly about which of three different things people are calling "what the movie means": (a) what the authors were trying to do, (b) what the finished artifact supports once you know how to look at it, (c) what a competent viewer at the time would have taken it to mean. I think this film is interesting precisely because all three come apart here. Is that a real distinction philosophers make, or am I reinventing something, or is it just confused?

2. My version-control analogy: once you ship v1.0, the author can publish updated docs telling future users how to use it, but can't retroactively change what you shipped. Applied here: a later statement about what the film "was always about" guides future readings but can't make 1999's film have meant something it didn't. But the opposite intuition also feels right — that an artwork isn't a shipped binary, it's something the author keeps living with, and her later understanding is authoritative. Which of those does this case actually support, and is my analogy doing any work or is it a toy?

3. Even granting the intentionalist that intention fixes meaning, how much evidential weight should a twenty-years-later self-report get? If you ask me why I wrote a line of code in 2019, what you get is a reconstruction, not a memory, and I'd guess directors are the same about their own movies. So maybe the live disagreement is epistemic — about the quality of a certain kind of evidence — rather than metaphysical at all.

Two more wrinkles I want dealt with: it's a co-authored film, so what does "the author's intention" even mean when there are two of them — a joint intention, or each one's separately, and what if they came to disagree? And Lana Wachowski directed the 2021 sequel on her own, which goes back to the red pill and puts Neo in therapy with a guy who keeps handing him blue pills; does one co-author returning to the text alone get to reframe the original?

Be honest about where the reading is a stretch. I'd rather have a critic who says "this scene carries it and that one doesn't" than someone who flattens the movie into an illustration of a theory.
