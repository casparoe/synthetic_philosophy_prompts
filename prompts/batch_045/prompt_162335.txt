I'm a graduate TA for an upper-division seminar cross-listed between history and philosophy, nominally on explanation in the historical sciences. For the last three weeks we have been working on whether non-human things — pathogens, climates, volcanic eruptions — can be credited as actors in a historical explanation, or whether that way of talking is either an empty flourish or a slide into environmental determinism. The professor has been running the discussion with a rough three-way distinction: a *background condition* sets the terms within which agents operate and contributes the same whatever anyone does; a *trigger* initiates a sequence at a particular time; and an *interactive cause* is one whose contribution to the outcome varies with what other agents do in response to it, so that the causal story has a loop in it. Alongside this she has insisted on a separate contrast between causes that *push* (an invading army, an order given) and causes that *filter* (a disease environment that removes some variants of a population and spares others), her claim being that only the first sort can be said to do anything, while the second merely removes options. We have applied all of this to the Tambora eruption and the famine years that followed it, to falciparum malaria and the sickle-cell trait, and to louse-borne typhus in the retreat of 1812. I find the push/filter contrast suggestive but I am not convinced it does the work she wants from it, and I am trying not to let that leak into my marking.

I have read it twice and cannot settle on a mark: it is the most fluent prose in the pile by a distance, and I specifically do not want to reward fluency, but I also do not want to manufacture faults in an answer simply because it is confident. The question, which every student saw:

(a) Talk of non-human "actors" is sometimes defended and sometimes dismissed. In two or three sentences each, give the strongest reason to resist calling a pathogen or a volcano an actor, and the strongest reason to allow it.
(b) Choose one case in which a non-human cause is often said to have decided an outcome. Explain what would have to be true for that claim to hold without the explanation collapsing into environmental determinism.
(c) If the non-human cause in your case really was decisive, does that change what we should say about the responsibility of the human agents involved? Say why or why not, and be explicit about which kind of responsibility you mean.

The answer:

(a) The strongest reason to resist the language is that acting, in the sense that matters here, is doing something for a reason, and neither a bacillus nor a volcano has reasons: it has no ends, no alternatives it deliberated among, nothing it could have done otherwise. Call such a thing an actor and you have either quietly redefined *actor* until it means nothing more than *cause*, or else smuggled purposes into inanimate nature.

The strongest reason to allow it is that causes do not all enter explanations in the same shape, and the word is marking a difference in shape rather than a difference in metaphysics. The location of a mountain range sits in a story as a fixed background: what it contributes does not vary with what anybody decides. An epidemic does not sit like that. It spreads faster when troops are crowded into barracks and slower when they are dispersed; it kills newcomers and spares the acclimatised. Its contribution is a function of what human agents do and of what it does to them in turn, which is the loop structure we associate with a second actor on the scene. Since what historical explanation needs is the shape of the causal story, the word earns its keep. It is no objection that the pathogen does not deliberate; that objection would only bite if the term were being used to attribute intentions.

(b) Consider the destruction of the French expedition to Saint-Domingue in 1802–03. Yellow fever, carried by mosquitoes breeding in the coastal towns the French garrisoned, killed an enormous share of the force, including most of the reinforcements who arrived in the hot months; the survivors withdrew, and the colony became Haiti. For the claim that the epidemic decided this outcome to be true without lapsing into environmental determinism, three things must hold. First, the epidemic must be causally necessary to the collapse: subtract it and a professional army with command of the sea would very probably have restored control. That counterfactual is a plausible one. Second, its necessity must be compatible with the efficacy of human decision, and it is, because the disease reached the soldiers only through a chain of decisions — to send a European army at all, to campaign through the hot months, to hold the ports. Third, and decisively, its contribution must vary with those decisions rather than override them: the disease is an interactive cause, not a background condition. This is also why the push/filter contrast does not settle the question on its own. Yellow fever filtered the expedition, removing week by week the option of continuing the campaign; but what it removed was an army's options, at a rate that depended on how that army was disposed, and that is precisely what an interactive cause is.

Determinism would be the claim that the disease environment dictated the result whatever anyone decided. That claim is false, and the case shows why: the decisions supplied the exposure. But the case is instructive in the other direction too. Once transmission was established in the camps, the decisions stopped mattering: from that point the epidemic did the work, and Leclerc's dispositions were only the channel along which it ran. So the honest conclusion is that here the pathogen, and not imperial policy, was the decisive cause. Human agency explains how the disease got in; the disease explains what happened next.

(c) If the epidemic really was decisive, the responsibility question is settled by the same fact, and settled in one direction: it disappears. Responsibility in the sense that matters — blame, the kind of accountability that justifies censure — attaches to what an agent controlled. Once we have established that the outcome turned on a pathogen, the human agents belong to the causal story but not to the moral one. We can say the expedition was destroyed; we cannot say that Napoleon, or Leclerc, was at fault for its destruction. Nor is it to the point that high mortality among Europeans in the tropics was commonknowledge long before anyone understood how the disease was carried: knowing that men die is not knowing what kills them, and without the mechanism there was nothing to weigh and no precaution whose omission could be blamed. This, I take it, is the real difference between a cause that decides an outcome and one that merely colours it. The first removes the action from the domain of blame; the second leaves it there.
