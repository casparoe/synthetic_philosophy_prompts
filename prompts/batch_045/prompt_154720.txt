I sit on the naming review committee of a small university, and we owe the Board of Trustees a recommendation in two weeks on two petitions we heard together, which is why I want one principle rather than two separate rulings.

Petition A asks us to remove the name of a living donor from our School of Public Health. The naming honoured a 1997 gift; the donor had no connection to the field, and the name was given in gratitude for the money and nothing else. In the years since, this donor has become the largest funder of a national campaign to dismantle the public-health agencies the school exists to train people for, and now says publicly that the school's own faculty are part of the problem. The gift agreement names the school in perpetuity, but only so long as the institution remains true to the purposes for which the gift was made. A final pledged installment, which would fund roughly forty scholarships a year for first-generation students, has not been paid, and the donor's lawyer has hinted that it depends on the name staying up.

Petition B asks us to name an admissions building after a civil-rights organizer who is not an alumna. The dean supports it. A group of faculty opposes it, on the ground that the university has missed every enrollment target it set for itself over the last decade and that putting her name on the building lets the institution display a commitment it has not actually made; one of them calls this commemorative laundering, the use of a name as a substitute for the reform the name celebrates.

I want a memo that handles both at once, and I would rather it not apply the familiar script about whether an honoree's bad deeds outweigh their good ones. The donor in Petition A has done nothing disgraceful by the standards usually applied in these disputes, and the honoree in Petition B is uncontroversial. The question, it seems to me, is what a name is doing: whether it records a past act, asserts a continuing endorsement, or does something to the people who have to work and study under it every day.

Please draft the memo I will circulate, roughly 700 to 900 words, with the following properties.

- Lead with the bottom lines in the first two sentences: what you recommend for A, and what you recommend for B. No preamble and no restating of the case.
- Give the principle you are using, stated tightly enough that we could apply it to the next petition without you in the room, and say what it implies about whether names should be reviewed on a schedule rather than only when someone complains.
- Give the strongest version of the argument you are rejecting in each case, not a summary of it.
- Separate, explicitly and in a form we could quote to the Board: first, what anyone who has thought about this would grant; second, what is genuinely contested among people who work on the ethics of commemoration; third, the points where the committee simply has to make a judgement that no principle settles. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
- Say what we should do about the unpaid installment, and what we should say to the donor and to the faculty group.
- No citations, case law, or reading lists; we will supply whatever documentation we need.
