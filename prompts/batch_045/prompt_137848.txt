I've been reading on my own, with no background in philosophy or biology, about the idea that minds come in grades rather than arriving all at once: creatures whose design is entirely the work of natural selection over generations; creatures that can additionally be reshaped within a single lifetime by trial, reward and punishment; creatures that can try candidate actions out inside their own heads and let the bad candidates die there instead of dying themselves; and creatures that are handed their mental tools by other creatures, so that they need not rediscover what their predecessors already worked out. The book I picked up labels the grades after Darwin, Skinner and Popper, and the last one after a psychologist named Gregory, but I care much less about who proposed the ladder than about whether it is a good one.

The reason I want it is not the reason it was built. I want to apply it to things that are not animals at all: a large language model that was first trained to predict text and then tuned against human approval ratings, an ant colony, a market, a scientific field. And I have a candidate grade of my own that I cannot place. I have been calling it the Ulyssean creature, after the man who had himself tied to the mast: a creature that can reorganize the learning it is going to do in the future — hand over its wallet before reaching the bar, take a drug that turns drinking into nausea, write a constitution that is deliberately hard to amend, impose a hard ceiling on how far a trained system may drift from where it started, delete in advance the option it expects later to be unable to refuse. My friend says this is not a new grade at all, only the tool-using grade turned inward on the creature's own future reinforcement; I say that if that move is permitted, the ladder collapses, because a creature that can rewrite the schedule it is being shaped by is not obviously the same kind of creature as one that cannot.

So before I read any further I would like the specification rather than another tour of the grades. What does a taxonomy of this sort have to deliver to be worth using for a purpose like mine — which cases must it classify and which is it allowed to decline, which distinctions must it hold open rather than collapse (a difference in speed versus a difference in kind, having a capacity versus routinely exercising it, the individual versus the lineage versus the group, what a creature can learn versus what it can pass on), and what findings from biology or from engineering would it flatly contradict if it got them wrong? Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp: must the grades be nested, must they form an order, must each grade bring its own characteristic new way of going wrong as well as its own new power, must the scheme apply to composites such as colonies, markets and trained artifacts, must it track anything that could matter morally? Most of all I want to know which pair of requirements cannot be satisfied together — my suspicion is that "the grades must be sharply separated" and "the grades must apply to fuzzy composites like colonies and trained models" pull against each other, and that if so the valuable output here is the incoherence of the list rather than any verdict it delivers. Then, judging by the list and not by tidiness, tell me what to do with the Ulyssean case: a genuine fifth grade, a sub-case of the fourth, or a sign that I posed the question badly.
