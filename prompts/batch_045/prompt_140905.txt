hi — I'm a novelist, which I only bring up because my whole working life is writing about people who fully intend to do something and then absolutely dont do it. Resolutions, promises, vows made at 2am: that's what plots are made of. So I've got skin in this game.

A philosopher turned up at a reading of mine last month, we had a beer, he fed me Kavka's toxin puzzle (Kavka's, I'm told — I never wrote the name down) and now it's eating my book. Setup as I understood it: a billionaire puts a vial in front of you. Drink it and you'll be wretched for a day, no lasting harm. He hands you a million dollars at midnight tonight if, at that moment, you intend to drink it tomorrow morning. You never actually have to drink it. He's a perfect judge of what you intend. So: can you do it?

My gut says no, and the scene I'm stuck on needs the answer to be no. But when I tried to write out why, the argument ate my own characters. Here it is:

(1) If you intend to do A, you believe you'll do A.
(2) In the vial case, at midnight I already know that by morning the money is in my pocket and drinking is pure cost, and I know I'll see that when I see it — so at midnight I believe I won't drink.
(3) So I can't intend to drink it. (1, 2)
(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) turns on the vial, the money, or the being sick. The only thing doing work is: at the moment of forming the intention, I've already got a reliable prediction that future-me won't follow through.
(5) I've got predictions like that constantly, everyone does, and so does every character I've ever written. I know I'll blow off the 6am swim. Ines knows she'll knock on that door anyway.
(6) So nobody with any realistic self-knowledge ever intends anything they expect to botch. Which is nuts — forming an intention is exactly the thing you do *because* you know you'll drift otherwise; it's the only tool anyone has against their own future weakness.

If I have to choose I'd a thousand times rather keep (3) than (6). But (6) really does look like it follows if (4) and (5) are honest, and I can't see which step is doing the smuggling.

So: name the step, say why it looked fine, and tell me what each way out costs. In particular, can I keep (3) and still let my characters intend things they'll probably fail at, or does keeping (3) commit me to something about how strong the prediction in (2) has to be, and where that line sits? And does it matter at all that the billionaire is a perfect judge rather than a guy with a polygraph?

One more thing I can't straighten out, which I think is a separate knot: my friend says (2) is too quick anyway, because once the intention is formed, actually drinking is what makes you the sort of person whose intentions mean anything — so intending manufactures a reason you didn't have before, and a decent self-predictor would foresee that and drink. If that's right the puzzle seems to dissolve, but it also seems like it'd let me intend anything at all on the same logic, which is worse. Is that move worth anything, or is it cheating?
