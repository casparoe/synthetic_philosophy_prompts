I'm teaching a 16-person seminar on cooperation and rational choice this spring (mostly philosophy majors, a few CS minors), one 80-minute slot, and I can't count on laptops — whiteboard, index cards, and one volunteer's phone at most. I want them to feel the force of program equilibrium without hearing the phrase until the debrief: that in a one-shot prisoners' dilemma, mutual cooperation can be stable once what you choose isn't an action but a rule that gets handed the other player's rule.

Rough shape I have in mind, fix it if there's something better. Round 1: blind simultaneous PD against a randomly drawn anonymous partner, points toward a quiz bonus — they'll defect. Round 2: same one-shot structure, new random pairing, but each person now writes a rule on an index card in a tiny fixed language with exactly three primitives — output C or D; compare your own rule text to the other player's; simulate the other rule on a specified input, with a step limit and default D on timeout — and the volunteer executes both. Then the bit I'd like to add: round 3, I run every rule through a "compiler" that renames variables and reorders independent lines before execution, and I plant one extra rule of my own that behaves like a copycat but is written in a visibly different style. I want the students whose rules relied on literal text-matching to crash into mutual defection against a rule that is dispositionally just like theirs, and have to rebuild on simulation instead.

What I need from you: the three rounds as actually runnable rules (exact language, a length cap, how pairs are formed and scored, how the simulation primitive gets adjudicated so nobody can game the timeout); a debrief that maps particular moments onto the formal points — dominance in the action game versus equilibrium in the rule game, why deviating to always-defect is self-defeating here, the fact that mutual defection is also an equilibrium so nothing selects cooperation for you, and what has to be true of the world for any of this to matter outside the seminar room (reputation, contracts, verifiable commitments, trading agents reading each other's code); what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I handle each case (the resident clever CS student writing a rule that cooperates when simulated and defects when actually run, students importing tit-for-tat habits, someone defecting for laughs); and an honest note on what the activity can't show. My guess is it can't deliver the general existence result, and I suspect it can't settle whether submitting a cooperative rule is what a rational agent *would* do rather than merely something that, if matched, nobody regrets. Tell me where I'm wrong about that too.
