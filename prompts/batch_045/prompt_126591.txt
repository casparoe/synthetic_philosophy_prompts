I'm in a philosophy of economics reading group and we've burnt something like four sessions on the fight over what "utility" and "preference" actually mean, and I want to understand why it's stuck rather than who's right. Here's how I'd put the two sides. On one side, the choice-first people: preference is nothing over and above patterns in actual and hypothetical choice, "utility function" is just a numerically convenient representation of those patterns, and any question of the form "yes but does she really prefer A, deep down?" that can't be cashed out as something she'd pick under some specifiable circumstances is empty. On the other side, the state-first people: preferences are real comparative evaluations or desires that cause and explain choices, roughly the way a belief and a desire explain an action; choice is only ever fallible evidence for them, because of akrasia, inattention, framing, menu effects and so on; and if you define preference as choice you've turned the theory into a tautology that can't be tested and can't do the welfare work — interpersonal comparisons, compensation tests, "would these people be better off?" — that economists quietly want it to do anyway. What's weird is that everyone in the room agrees on the formal side: that any finite body of choice data satisfying the standard consistency conditions can be represented as maximizing some utility function, and that the representation is pinned down only up to a transformation, so the data by itself can't tell you whether the function is tracking well-being, desire-strength, or nothing much at all. And everyone agrees on the anomalies: adding a clearly dominated third option changes which of the original two gets picked, intransitivities show up in the lab, choices shift depending on what else was on the menu. Then, having agreed on all of that, both sides go home exactly where they started.

My own hypothesis, which I cant seem to get the group to engage with, is that this isn't a dispute about the mind at all but two job descriptions sharing one word: the choice-first side wants a quantity that lets you predict behaviour and design institutions, the state-first side wants a quantity that can bear the weight of "better off" inside an ethical argument. If that's right, neither side's evidence is even aimed at the other side's question, which would definately explain why nothing lands. But maybe it's really an empirical disagreement about whether there are such states, or a disagreement about what would count as evidence for a psychological state, or just bedrock intuition on both sides that nobody can argue for.

To stress-test whatever you think, here's a case I made up, because every real case just gets swallowed by both camps. Suppose someone builds a scanner — call it the oracle — that from a brain scan taken before any options are on the table predicts a person's binary choices with 97% accuracy, beats the person's own verbal self-report, and does something the person's actual choices don't: its outputs are stable and satisfy the usual consistency conditions even in the decoy and menu-effect cases where real choices go funny, and they move when and only when you change what the person believes about the options, not when you change how the options are framed. It looks exactly like a measurement of a coherent, belief-sensitive, comparative state of which choice is a noisy readout. My friend, who leans choice-first, says this would change nothing: her side will just say "great, you've found one more behaviour to be revealed by," the other side will say "at last, a measurement," and everyone goes home happy. Is she right that a result like that couldn't settle it? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a theorem about what can and can't be read off behaviour, an experiment like the oracle, splitting the word in two and banning the other use, or nothing? And be honest if the better answer is that "what does utility really mean" is ill posed and we wasted four sessions.
