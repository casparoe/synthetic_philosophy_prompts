I'm a journalist working on a long piece about decision-theory puzzles for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I've got myself into a muddle that I'd like help clearing up before I try to explain any of it to readers. I keep coming back to the two-envelope setup — one envelope contains twice the other, you pick one, you may keep it or take the other — and I've been polling my own gut on a family of variants, in the hope that my verdicts will reveal the rule I'm unconsciously using. Here they are. (i) Envelope unopened, no information whatever about how the amounts were chosen: I'm indifferent; switching is pointless. (ii) I open and find €40, and I still have no information about how the amounts were chosen: my gut says switch, because the other envelope holds €20 or €80 with equal probability and that's €50 in expectation — though this is precisely the verdict I trust least, since I'd apparently want to switch whatever I saw. (iii) Same, except I'm told the smaller amount was drawn uniformly from €10, €20, €40 and €80: I open €40 and switch, for an expected €50. (iv) Same setup as (iii) but I open €160: I absolutely keep it, since €160 can only be the larger amount. (v) The smaller amount is ten euros times a power of two, with the probability halving at each doubling, and I open €40: I work out that I'm exactly indifferent, and after checking the arithmetic my gut agrees. (vi) Same, except each doubling costs only a quarter of the probability mass rather than a half: I open €40, work out that I'm something like 57% confident I'm holding the *larger* envelope, and still conclude that switching is worth about €5.71 in expectation — a verdict I find deeply uncomfortable. (vii) Back to the uniform setup of (iii), except that the sums are small relative to my wealth, so I treat my utility for money as roughly logarithmic: the pull to switch disappears and I'm indifferent again. (viii) The pair was chosen by a television producer who knows in advance which rule I'll follow and would prefer me to end up with less: no rule helps me, I'm indifferent, and in particular I reject any policy of the form switch whatever you see.

The rule I keep falling back on is: switch if and only if, conditional on everything I've observed, it's more likely than not that I'm holding the smaller envelope. That rule seems to get (i), (iv) and (viii) right, but it flatly contradicts my verdict in (vi), and it doesn't obviously deliver the knife-edge in (v) or the shift between (iii) and (vii). So here's what I want, if you can: test my rule against the eight cases, tell me exactly where it goes wrong and how it should be repaired — I suspect the threshold it sets is wrong, and that the right threshold depends on quite how much I gain if I'm holding the smaller envelope relative to how much I lose if I'm holding the larger one, but I'd like that stated precisely, in a form that also covers curved utility for money. Then tell me which of my verdicts the repaired principle cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn: I'd guess (ii) is the casualty, since with no information about the generating process there may be no probability distribution for the threshold to be applied to at all, but I want to know whether that's really the right thing to say or whether some non-probabilistic decision rule can save it. I'd also like to see how a single principle reconciles (iii) with (i): if switching really is profitable conditional on seeing €40, how does that fail to make switching profitable before I look, given that I know in advance that whatever I see I'll want to switch? And please say what the resulting account predicts for two cases I haven't put on my list: the three-envelope version, where the envelopes hold some amount, twice it and four times it, I see what I hold and may trade for one of the other two at random; and the version where, having already switched once, I'm offered the chance to pay a euro to switch back.
