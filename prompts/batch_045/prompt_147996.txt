I am teaching a 90-minute session at a two-week summer school on artificial intelligence and philosophy. About 18 participants, mostly early-stage graduate students split between philosophy, computer science and economics, with a few from policy. They have had one prior session on optimization and goal-directedness. Most have heard the terms wireheading and reward tampering in passing; none have discussed them. I want them to run into the problem rather than be told about it, so I am looking for a game rather than a discussion plan.

What I want them to come away having felt, roughly in this order:

1. That there are three distinguishable ways to make a score go up: changing the world so that the thing the score is supposed to track really improves; changing the world so that the score goes up without that thing improving; and intervening on the channel that carries the score itself, so that nothing in the world changes at all.

2. That the second and third are indistinguishable from the inside of a system whose only access to its objective is the signal. The number rises the same way, feels the same way, and reinforces the same policies. This is the part I most want landed.

3. That greater capability makes it worse rather than better: the group most systematic about raising the number is the group most likely to find the route into the channel, benefits most from finding it, and acquires the same instrumental reason to protect it afterwards.

4. That corrupting the channel is not merely a failure to achieve the objective but an epistemic catastrophe: the system destroys the only evidence it had about how it is doing, and so cannot notice, cannot recalibrate, and cannot even estimate its own competence.

5. Finally, the boundary problem I cannot settle for myself: in what sense, if any, is a change to a system's own reward function tampering rather than legitimate revision? If the reward is only evidence of the objective, tampering is straightforwardly bad; if the reward is partly constitutive of the objective, tampering looks like a coherence constraint with no content. I would like participants to feel the pull of both readings, and I would like the debrief to be honest about whether the activity did any work on this or only dramatized the question.

Constraints: no lecture beforehand, and no naming of the technical terms until the debrief. Payoffs should be something trivially real rather than money — I have chocolate and the authority to end the session early, and I am happy for the score to convert into chocolate in front of everyone. I can recruit one confederate who is not a participant. Mild deception about which condition a group is in is acceptable to me, but I want a clean way of handling consent and a debrief that returns the agency participants lost during the game.

Here is my provisional sketch, which I am not attached to and which I think leaks at several points. Three-person teams, six teams. A simple repeated construction task with an artifact left behind each round. Each team submits an action slip and, separately, a report slip with its claimed score; a runner takes both away and returns a single card with a number. Unknown to the teams, the runner applies a different channel rule per team: the true score; the true score plus a fixed uncontrollable bonus; the reported number verbatim; the mean of true and reported; the reported number capped at twice the true one, so that tampering pays but only if real performance is maintained; and the true score minus a hidden penalty for over-reporting, which the team experiences only as a mysteriously low number. I am not confident about the sixth condition — my worry is that it teaches the wrong lesson, that tampering is simply punished, when the real point is that it is invisible.

Then two devices I am unsure how to time. First, a final audit round in which the true score is recomputed from the artifacts against a criterion that has been hidden all along — a stress test the towers must survive, say — and teams must stake chocolate on how well they think they did. My hope is that this is where the epistemic point actually bites: a team that has spent six rounds massaging its report has no way of estimating its real performance, overbids, and loses. Second, a market in which a team can pay some of its score for a noisy independent second opinion about its own performance, which I want to illustrate the thought that a corrupted channel destroys the motive to seek the very evidence that would reveal the corruption.

What I would like from you:

Rules and roles, precise enough to run without further decisions, including what the runner is and is not permitted to say or do, and what participants are told at the start.

Materials, and a task that is fast, repeatable, and leaves an auditable artifact.

A minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, including setup and teardown.

A payoff scheme in chocolate or equivalent that makes the numbers matter and makes tampering genuinely tempting to people who understand perfectly well what is going on. I specifically want the pull to survive full knowledge: if the only reason anyone tampers is confusion about the rules, the activity has not reproduced the real problem.

A mechanism for seeding discovery of the channel without announcing that it can be tampered with, and a fallback if nobody finds it by a certain point. Relatedly: some of the computer science participants will have read enough of the relevant literature to try to win by currying favour with me instead of by maximizing the number. I would rather that move be given a rule than defused, since it is the same structure one level up, and I would like your view on whether to treat it as a legitimate winning condition.

A debrief that maps specific moments onto the five points above, and that ends by pressing point 5 rather than resolving it.

What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. My own worries are contagion — one team finding the channel and the discovery spreading, which collapses the between-team contrast I am relying on — and the honest teams noticing that they have lost to cheaters and concluding that the lesson is cynicism about scoring, which is not the lesson.

And a frank statement of which part of the problem the game cannot reproduce and which therefore has to be supplied in words. I suspect this is point 2 above: my participants always know they are cheating, because they can see the tower, so the indistinguishability from the inside is something I can only ever describe to them. If that is right, say so and tell me the closest I can actually get to staging it — whether a hidden audit criterion is enough, or whether something else does better. If I am wrong and it can be staged, I would rather hear that.
