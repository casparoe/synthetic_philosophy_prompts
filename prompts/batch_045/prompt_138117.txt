Hey, I need a hand drafting something. I run a small interdisciplinary reading group (two people who lean evolutionary-psychology, two sociologists, me, and various hangers-on) and the two camps have been going at each other for about two years over the gender-equality paradox — you know, the much-reported pattern that countries scoring high on gender-equality measures, the Nordics especially, tend to have *more* gender-skewed educational and occupational choices, not less: a smaller share of women in engineering, physics and computer science, a bigger share in nursing, teaching and care work, than in plenty of countries that are much less egalitarian by any index you like. I've finaly got them to agree to write one document together instead of another round of point-scoring, and I want you to draft it.

The two signatories: call them the constraint-removal camp (roughly: once you strip away the material, legal and familial constraints, what's left is at least partly average differences in interests and temperament that have some biological component, and the paradox is more or less what you should expect) and the social-mechanism camp (roughly: the paradox is produced by something else that travels along with equality — what the indices actually measure, the security that comes with a rich welfare state, the different social meaning of a choice in a post-industrial society — not by released natural preference).

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section: what the pattern actually is and isn't (no overclaiming in either direction), what a cross-country correlation of this kind can and cannot establish, and what each side concedes about the other's strongest case. I want at least one claim per side that that side will find genuinely uncomfortable to sign, and I'll know if you've softened it.
2. A disagreements table: each row a disagreement stated precisely, with the wording of each side's own position that that side would accept, plus a flag for whether it's substantive or merely verbal. My suspicion is that a good chunk of the heat comes from using "choice", "preference" and "free" differently while the headline fight is staged as being about how much of the variance is biological — but don't force that reading; if you think the core disagreement is real and deep, say so and locate it.
3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that a clear answer would visibly move at least one of them. For each crux give a concrete test, dataset, natural experiment or comparison group that both would accept as genuinely bearing on it — nothing that needs a laboratory that doesn't exist, so think cross-country comparison, within-country change over time, cohort effects, immigrant or second-generation populations, countries that changed fast, whatever is actually available — and then each side's prediction written down in advance, stated sharply enough that it could come out wrong. Include, for each side, what result would make them give ground and what they'd be tempted to explain away.

Where I want you to put your own weight: I have a pet hypothesis neither of them has engaged with, and I want it forced into the document as a third position both have to answer. Roughly this: what if the variable doing the work isn't equality at all, but how much a career's payoff depends on continuous, uninterrupted, credential-heavy accumulation? High-equality countries are also rich countries with saturated higher education and long training pipelines, so fields where skill and seniority compound — engineering, surgery, physics — become maximally unattractive to anyone who expects a career break, which is still mostly women, whereas fields with flatter, more re-entry-friendly payoff profiles punish breaks much less. On this story you get the paradox with no appeal to innate interest whatsoever, and it also predicts the gap should show up in any group facing career discontinuity, not just women, and that it should shrink wherever re-entry is made cheap. Make both camps state what they predict if that story is right, and what single observation would kill it for them.

One more section I want handled rather than dodged: the "so what for justice" question. If the constraint-removal camp is largely right, does gendered occupational sorting stop being a justice issue and become a matter of taste to accomodate? If the social-mechanism camp is right, does that by itself license intervention, given that steering people's choices in a liberal state is its own problem? Give me, for each side, a short list of policy conclusions that do *not* follow from their view — the inferences they should resist — not just the ones they like.

Keep it in the register of something two actual academics could put their names to: no victory language, no straw men, no "more research is needed", and where a crux genuinely can't be settled with present data, say so plainly and say what kind of thing would settle it.

Last thing: at the end give me a short note on how confident you are in what you've put in each side's mouth — where you're fairly sure the position really commits them to it — and single out the one claim you're least confident either of them would actually sign.
