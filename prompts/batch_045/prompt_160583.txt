Exam question (25 points):

"A field engineer, Mira, is posted to an isolated research station whose inhabitants speak a language no one on her team has deciphered. She has no translation manual, no bilingual informant, and no prior knowledge of what the inhabitants believe. All she can do is log, for each utterance, the publicly observable circumstances in which it is produced, and record the inhabitants' assent and dissent behaviour — the observable responses that would count as accepting or rejecting a sentence — along with the events in the shared environment that appear to prompt them. She cannot open anyone's head: no private mental states, introspective reports, or neural recordings are available. The station's environment contains drifting swarms of reflective particles ('motes') that continually fuse with and split off from one another.

Call the project of constructing a theory of meaning for the inhabitants' language on this evidence base 'radical interpretation'.

(a) What evidence does radical interpretation allow the interpreter to appeal to, and why does Davidson set the problem up this way? (8 points)
(b) Why, on Davidson's account, can this evidence not determine belief and meaning independently of one another? What work does the principle of charity do here? (9 points)
(c) Suppose Mira succeeds: she produces a theory that fits every observation she could make. Does this show that the inhabitants mean one determinate thing by each of their sentences? Explain what Davidson's notion of the inscrutability of reference commits him to, and what it does not commit him to. (8 points)"

Student answer (unedited):

"Radical interpretation is the project of assigning meanings to a speaker's sentences, and beliefs and desires to the speaker, without help from translators, dictionaries, or prior knowledge of what the speaker thinks. Mira at the station is the pure case.

(a) The evidence she may use is strictly public: which sentences the inhabitants hold true, and the observable circumstances — the objects and events in the environment she shares with them — in which those hold-true attitudes arise. She may not help herself to their private mental states, to introspective reports, or to neural data. Davidson restricts the evidence this way because meaning is not a hidden inner item that could be detected apart from behaviour; interpretation has to be answerable to what any observer could in principle check, and it is the shared environment that makes what people say answerable to something outside both speaker and interpreter. Interpreter, speaker and common cause together form the triangle Davidson calls triangulation. Charity aside, nothing but such public evidence can constrain a theory of meaning.

(b) The reason belief and meaning cannot be settled separately is holism. A sentence's meaning is fixed by its place in a whole theory, and a belief is identified only through its relations to other beliefs and desires, so Mira cannot first read off the inhabitants' beliefs from their behaviour and then solve for meanings, or the reverse; the two have to be determined together. Charity is what gets her into this circle. She interprets so as to maximize agreement: she attributes, by her own lights, mostly true beliefs and a largely coherent pattern of belief and desire, and then treats the residual failures as the points at which a meaning differs from her first guess. Charity is an empirical hypothesis of an ordinary kind — it says that creatures sharing an environment with us mostly get things right, and it is confirmed exactly as any other hypothesis is, by the predictive success of the interpretations built on it. It is therefore open to refutation: if Mira found a community that was massively and systematically wrong about the world around them, the correct response would be to set charity aside and simply record them as holding false beliefs.

(c) No, success does not deliver uniqueness. All of Mira's evidence is behavioural, and the same behaviour is compatible with different assignments of reference: the mote-word might pick out motes, or temporal stages of motes, or undetached mote-parts, and no observation of the kind she can make distinguishes these. Davidson takes this over from Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and the upshot is the same: there is no fact of the matter about what the inhabitants mean. Inscrutability therefore reaches the level of whole sentences, so that two theories fitting every possible observation may assign flatly incompatible truth conditions to the very sentence Mira hears uttered in the presence of a mote, and nothing — not even triangulation — could adjudicate between them. Communication then succeeds only when the interpreter's theory happens to coincide with the speaker's, which is a lucky convergence rather than something the account explains. Interpretation is a matter of choosing whichever theory is most convenient."
