I'm writing the second section of a paper on whether dreams are experiences, and I want to get the specification down before I score anyone against it: I keep noticing that every desideratum I draft turns out to be something only one side of the Malcolm–Dennett dispute would ever have wanted, and I'd rather have that exposed now than hidden in the scoring. So: write me the list of requirements that an adequate theory of dreaming has to meet. Fix the purpose as follows — dream reports are used as data in the science of consciousness (lab reports as a measure of phenomenal content, reports or sleep behaviour as evidence about what infants and non-human animals experience, reports of dreamless sleep as a no-experience baseline), so the theory needs to be good enough to license or refuse the inference from a report, or from what a sleeper does, to an experience during sleep, and to say what would make one report better evidence than another.

Here are the candidate requirements I have so far, with the cases I had in mind — keep the ones that are genuine, discard the ones that are just camp prejudice, and add the ones I've missed. A theory has to say whether the question did this sleeper have an experience during that stretch of sleep? has a determinate yes-or-no answer for an arbitrary dream, or whether it can be indeterminate or a matter of degree; a sleep researcher I argue with thinks many dreams are aborted or degraded simulations that never compose into anything, and that pressing for a verdict in those cases is a mistake rather than a discovery, and I want to know whether demanding a verdict is itself a desideratum or a prejudice. It has to give an account of the reporter's epistemic position: reports are produced in a different state from the one they describe, memory for dreams is wretched, and the awakening itself may be when the content takes the shape it gets reported in (the deferred-construction possibility), so it must say what a report is evidence of and how much the transformation matters. It has to handle the full range of the phenomena rather than the lab-friendly centre: dreams reported from non-REM sleep as well as REM, so-called white dreams where the subject insists something was happening but there is nothing to describe, hypnagogic imagery, nightmares, and enactment of dream content by people whose sleep behaviour is otherwise abnormal. It has to handle lucid dreaming with agreed signalling from verified sleep — and say whether that settles the question or merely relocates it — and it has to handle subject-side oddities: dreams with no unified standpoint, where the dreamer shifts between being a participant and an observer; dreams in people blind from birth; dreams reported by people who deny having imagery when awake. It has to respect distinctions rather than collapse them: having an experience versus seeming now to remember one; the metaphysical question versus the evidential one; dreaming as an activity versus a dream as an item; whether one dreamt at all versus what one dreamt; whether the dream unfolded in something like real time, and whether it has to. And it has to leave room for both first-person authority about one's own dream life and the possibility of being wrong about whether one dreamt at all — imagine a protocol, which as far as I know nobody has actually run, in which sleepers are woken briefly at twenty-second intervals through a single REM period and asked only what is happening right now, with the successive fragments stitching into one continuous narrative: whatever the theory is, it should be able to say what that corpus would show and what it could not.

What I want from you is the criteria and the cases behind them, not a survey: mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp, tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and flag any pair or triple that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — my own suspicion is that reports are our only evidence, reports are unreliable in the way the theory itself predicts, and every dream gets a determinate verdict cannot all three be had, and if that is right then the list is itself a result and I want it stated as one. Keep the scorecard to a short paragraph or a small table at the end: Malcolm's line that the very idea of a dream experience is conceptually confused, Dennett's (including the cassette-theory target he attacks and his later willingness to treat the facts about dreams as indeterminate), and something in the neighbourhood of my friend's degraded-simulation view.
