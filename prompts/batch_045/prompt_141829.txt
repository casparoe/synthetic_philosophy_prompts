I used to work as an actuary and I now help run a small reading group at the public library, where we work slowly through popular books on mathematics and philosophy. Last month we did a chapter on the infinite hotel — infinitely many rooms, all full, and yet one more guest fits, and then infinitely many more fit, and then you can empty every other room and still have a full hotel. What has been nagging at me since is not whether any of that is true but something about *how* the surprises are produced.

The standard story, as I understood it, is that two collections count as the same size whenever you can match each item in one with exactly one item in the other, with nothing left over on either side. It seemed to me that whether you get a surprising verdict depends entirely on which matchings you are willing to count. So I started playing with a stricter rule: allow a matching only if it never moves an item very far from where it started — specifically, as you go further out along the collections, the distance any item gets shifted must become a vanishing fraction of how far out that item is. Under that rule, as far as I can work out, the hotel still takes one more guest, and would still take a million more, and would not notice if you turned out the residents of the first thousand rooms; but if the guests in every second room check out, the hotel really is smaller afterwards, and there really are fewer square numbers than counting numbers, which is what everyone's untutored instinct says and what the standard story spends its time explaining away. I also notice my rule leans on the collections coming already arranged in an order, which the usual matching rule does not, and I cannot decide whether that is a fatal flaw or the whole point.

What I want from you is the lay of the land, in at most 400 words and with no reading list or references: does this way of splitting the difference have an established name, or a family of established theories it belongs to, and if so which of them fits my rule and which only look as if they do? If it is genuinely unnamed, give it a name and a definition, and tell me which cases sit right at its edges — including whether it is really anything more than just tracking the proportion of the first so many numbers that belong to a collection, dressed up in the language of matchings. I will not be satisfied with being told the word for the standard matching rule; I want to know what my rule is, what it is not, and where exactly it departs from each of its neighbours.
