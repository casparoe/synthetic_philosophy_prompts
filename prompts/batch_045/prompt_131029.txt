My brother-in-law has moderated a busy online forum about betting for about a decade, and one of its running features is his verdict on hypothetical cases that readers send in. He files each one as either "a setup" — the reader is being taken advantage of and should walk away — or "nothing wrong with it." I have been going through years of these posts trying to work out what test he is actually applying, and I have boiled his track record down to five rulings that seem to pin it down almost, but not quite. (I'm told arguments of this general shape are called Dutch book arguments; I'd rather you reason from his rulings than from anything you know about that literature, and I don't want citations or reading suggestions — his rulings and the cases are all the evidence I have.)

His five rulings:

1. A reader is offered, at the same time, $1 bets on each of three mutually exclusive outcomes that between them exhaust the possibilities, at $0.40 each. Setup.

2. A reader posts his own fair prices on the same three outcomes; they add up to $0.90; the bookie gets to choose which side of each bet the reader ends up on. Setup.

3. A reader takes a single even-odds bet that he himself regards as exactly fair, from a bookie who knows more than he does and who would not have offered it unless he expected to profit. The reader loses. Nothing wrong with it.

4. A reader buys a bet at his own fair price on Monday; on Tuesday he revises his odds on the same proposition without having learned anything he counts as relevant, and sells the bet back at his new, lower price. Setup.

5. A bookie offers a reader a run of bets each of which the reader considers generous, where taking all of them would lock in a certain gain. The reader takes none, and the bookie gets nothing. Nothing wrong with it.

The case I actually care about: my two sisters share a joint account and both bet from it. On the same coin toss, one of them is 60% on heads and the other 60% on tails; each thinks her own number is right, each knows the other's number and knows the other is being offered an analogous bet, but they make up their minds separately. The bookie sells each of them, at her own fair price, a $1 bet on the side she favours. Whichever way the coin lands, the account is $0.20 down.

What I want from you: the two or three rules that fit all five rulings about equally well; what each of them implies about the sisters; your single best guess at his verdict, with the reason that rule beats the others; and the smallest variation on the sisters' case that would separate the rules that disagree, since I would like to send him that version next and see whether he flinches. I have a suspicion it turns on where their disagreement comes from, but I want your version before I say more. Please also flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and tell me which single detail of the five rulings, if I have reported it wrong, would most change your prediction. Finish with one sentence naming the rule you are betting on and the verdict you predict, then a final line containing exactly \answer{setup} or \answer{not a setup}, with nothing after it.
