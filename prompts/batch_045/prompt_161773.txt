I'm in a seminar on the philosophy of law, and we've been working through the dispute over just deserts as it plays out in the Dennett–Caruso exchange. The move I can't get clear on is the analogy Caruso's side leans on: the dangerous offender is presented as like a carrier of a communicable disease, and incapacitation as like quarantine — forward-looking, protective, and carrying no claim that anyone deserves to suffer. The analogy is doing real argumentative work: it is meant to make a wholly non-retributive criminal justice system sound like plain common sense, and to take the sting out of the charge that giving up desert means letting wrongdoers off.

Suppose we take it literally and press it at the edges. On one side: offenders whose past act was grave but whose predicted future dangerousness is low — call them cold carriers. On the other: people assessed as dangerous who have not yet acted on it. Audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the pathogen, the carrier, the infection, the quarantine, the health authority, the cure, and the discharge — then the disanalogies, sorted into those that actually block the work the analogy is being asked to do and those that are beside the point. Say what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks: deference to expertise, the patient's own interest in being cured, the absence of any message being sent by the confinement. Then tell me whether the non-retributive conclusion survives once those are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it; whether there is a better analogy available, for either Caruso's side or Dennett's; and whether the failure of this analogy, if it fails, is evidence about retributivism or only about its packaging.
