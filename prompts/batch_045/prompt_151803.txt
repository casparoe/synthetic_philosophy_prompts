I work as a claims adjuster at a regional insurance company, and once a month I run a forty-minute lunch-and-learn for whoever turns up — people from underwriting, IT, the call center, and HR, a whiteboard and nothing else. Almost nobody there has maths or programming beyond what they did at school, and one or two are openly suspicious of anything labeled AI. Last month we did compound interest and it went well; next month I want to do inverse reinforcement learning. My brother-in-law, who works on warehouse robots, told me over Thanksgiving that this is where the field's whole hope of teaching machines what humans want is staked, and I have been turning it over since and think I half understand it and half do not. What I need from you is not an explanation of IRL for me — I can read that up on my own — but a plan for the forty minutes: the order the ideas have to arrive in and what depends on what; the one concrete example that should carry the whole thing; the misconceptions this audience will form and the exact point in the sequence where I should head off each one; the question I can ask in the last five minutes that tells me whether the idea actually landed rather than whether they liked the session; which technical words to introduce and which to keep out of their mouths; and the things I should openly decline to explain at this level, naming them as refusals.

Two constraints on the example. Please build it around a home-care aide named Marisol who is showing a household robot how she wants a client's flat tidied: she is competent but not optimal, she is being filmed by her supervisor as she works, and on Friday afternoons she is exhausted and quietly leaves things half-finished. The case needs to make vivid that the learner sees only behavior and never the fatigue, the audience, or the reasons, and that one stretch of visible tidying is compatible with a whole family of different reward functions — she may be tidying to her supervisor's standard, to her own, to what the client actually likes, or to finish fast and sit down. It should also carry a wrinkle I have started calling the Potemkin preference: a demonstrator who knows she is being recorded as a standard-setter may perform a tidier version of her own values than she actually lives by, so what gets inferred from her is an officially sanctioned caricature of what she cares about. If you think that wrinkle belongs later in the sequence rather than inside the example itself, say so and say where, and tell me what short phrase I should put on the board for it.

The misconceptions I am braced for, which you should correct, reorder, or add to: that the robot ends up wanting what Marisol wants in the sense that matters; that watching more behavior will eventually pin the reward function down uniquely, which I suspect is a non-identifiability point I do not know how to make land without maths; that IRL assumes the demonstrator is optimal and that this is a fixable bug rather than a load-bearing feature; and that inferring preferences from behavior is just the old revealed-preference idea from economics with new vocabulary. Tell me which of these, if any, I should let the audience hold for a while and then break deliberately, and which I should inoculate against up front. Begin directly with the plan — the one-sentence arc of the session in your first sentence, then the sequence — with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for. American English, please.
