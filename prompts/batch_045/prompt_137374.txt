I'm the only undergraduate in a graduate seminar on historical injustice, and on Monday I have to hand in a two-page position statement that the rest of the seminar will then try to take apart. Before I write it up I want to know whether the pieces I've assembled actually fit together. Here they are, numbered so you can refer back to them.

1. Ground. A duty of repair is justified by the wrong and by what the wrong did, not by the good that paying would do now. I don't want to argue that reparations are owed because they would reduce present inequality, improve relations between groups, deter future wrongs, or express a valuable commitment. If those were the only available arguments, I would take that as a reason to doubt the claim rather than as a different route to the same conclusion.

2. Ground, continued. I think the non-identity objection is fatal to the most natural backward-looking argument. Present-day claimants normally exist only because the injustice happened: their ancestors' lives would have gone differently, different people would have met, and different children would have been conceived. So we cannot say of any present-day claimant that they are worse off than they would otherwise have been, and harm to them cannot be what grounds the duty. I take the honest replacement to be unjust enrichment: the wrongdoer's side still holds something that was never rightfully theirs, and that present holding is what does the work.

3. Bearer. The duty belongs to the persisting institutional agent — the state, the university, the firm — and not to individual citizens merely as such. Nobody alive today owes anything personally in virtue of being a co-national or a descendant of the wrongdoers. They did not do it, and in a great many cases they did not gain by it, and I don't think bare membership in a nation or a descent group can generate a moral debt.

4. Discharge. The state may nonetheless permissibly discharge that duty out of general taxation, coercively, including from people who owe nothing personally. I mean this flatly: their tax bill can go up to pay a debt that is not theirs.

5. Quantum. What is owed is set by the magnitude of the wrong — roughly, what it would take to put the wronged party in the position they would have been in had it not occurred. It makes no difference to the size of the duty whether the beneficiaries still hold what they gained or whether the gain has long since been consumed.

6. A case, which I take to be the central rather than the marginal one. In 1900 a company seized a tract of land from a family with no colour of legal right; a later regime formally acknowledged the seizure as unlawful. The land was sold in 1930 at full market value to a buyer who knew nothing of the seizure and had no connection to the company, and the company was dissolved in 1960 with its remaining assets distributed to its shareholders. The buyer and then her heirs have farmed the land since, and their own labour and investment have roughly tripled its value. My verdicts: the heirs owe nothing, because they paid value in good faith to someone with apparent title; the descendants of the original owners nonetheless have a valid claim; and what they have a claim to is that land as it now is, improvements included, with no reimbursement to the heirs for the improvements. I also think most live claims look more like this than like a living wrongdoer still holding a living victim's asset, so a theory that handles this case only as an afterthought isn't really a theory of the problem.

I can't locate the fault myself. Each of these has seemed obviously correct to me for a long time, separately, which is exactly why I've become suspicious of them together. So I'd like an audit rather than a lecture, and I'd like it done against my own list rather than against the literature's usual menu of positions.

Concretely: rank the tensions in descending order of severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each one say which of my commitments are pulling against each other, why it is a genuine inconsistency and not a verbal or apparent one, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set. Say explicitly if any pair that looks in tension to me is not, and why. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and don't spare my favourites — I'd much rather be told that commitment 2 is doing the damage than be told that everything is in mild tension with everything else.

No preamble, and no restating my position back to me.
