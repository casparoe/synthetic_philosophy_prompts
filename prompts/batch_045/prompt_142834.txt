Hiya — I'd like you to write me a dialogue that works through something I've been chewing over, rather than picking a winner in the first paragraph and then justifying it.

The setup. I've started calling these things "commitment races" — I dont know if thats the usual term, it's just what I call them. Roughly: several parties can each make a binding, expensive-to-reverse commitment, and what decides who comes out best isn't who commits to the best policy but who commits *first*. The bit I can't get out of my head is that commitments are normally the *cure* for this kind of mess — you tie your hands Ulysses-style and the collective action problem goes away. Here tying your hands is the disease: everyone binds themselves early and badly because earliness is what pays.

Concretely. Two frontier AI labs, Callow and Merrivale. Both expect a binding international compute treaty in about two years, and everyone expects the treaty to codify whatever measurement and verification standard is already in widest practical use, because drafting a fresh one from scratch is slow and the diplomats would rather not. Either lab can, today, irrevocably commit itself (escrowed bond, published audits, the lot) to a compute cap defined under a measurement standard of its own choosing. Whoever registers first gets their standard treated as the de facto one, and so effectively writes the rules the other side will end up being judged by. Neither wants to be measured by the other's yardstick. The catch: whatever you lock in now you're stuck with, and right now nobody really knows what their training runs will actually look like in two years.

So: write me a dialogue between two advisors to Callow's board, in the room where the decision has to be made. One thinks they should register this week, and thinks the whole thing is really just an arms race with extra steps — the advantage is real, it isn't manufactured by anyone's beliefs, and the only lever that does anything is changing what the treaty drafters default to. The other thinks the rush is self-inflicted, that the "prize" is largely an artefact of expecting the other side to rush, and that the real move is a joint or conditional commitment — one that only fires if nobody else has registered in the window — which would make the first-mover advantage evaporate.

Please dont make either of them thick, and make them actually answer each other rather than delivering speeches past one another. In particular I'd like them to get into: whether the advantage is more like a patent race (a genuine positional good that's there whoever believes in it) or more like a bank run (self-fulfilling, and gone if nobody expects it); whether it's first-mover at all, or whether the sweet spot is being second but early, copying the other side's standard with a tweak and letting them eat the design risk; whether, since there's arguably no last moment at which "commit just before they do" still pays, the deadline should just collapse inwards — and if it does, whether that shows the situation is pathological or shows that the race model is the wrong model; and whether there's anything specially bad about racing to bind your own future agency, over and above the usual badness of an arms race, or whether that's just moralising about something that's only instrumentally bad. If they end up agreeing on the diagnosis but not on the fix, that's fine — that's roughly where I am.

At the end, outside the dialogue, a short note: which of the two you think has the better of it, how confident you are in that, and the single step in the argument you'd bet on least. Flag anywhere the dialogue quietly helps itself to a contested assumption too — including in my scenario, which I may well have rigged without meaning to.
