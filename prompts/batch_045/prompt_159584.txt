I'm on the board of a tiny land trust, and I've got a vote coming up this spring on about forty hectares of exhausted pasture: do we take it out of grazing and let it go to scrub and eventually woodland, or do we keep it as short grass under a grazing tenant? One board member — a sort of ecologist — has been arguing that letting it revert will massively increase wild animal suffering, on the grounds that scrub and woodland support far more small mammals, songbirds, and insects than pasture does, and that almost all of those animals die young and badly. Another board member argues the opposite: that things are worse per surviving animal in the degraded, over-grazed, edge-heavy landscape, and that we'd also be pushing animals into hedgerows and into the generalists that live alongside people — rats, pigeons, deer — so the "fewer lives" effect might be tiny, negative, or a wash. They've been going around in circles for about a year, and I think part of why is that they keep scoring the debate in different currencies: one talks about total suffering summed over every animal on the site, the other seams to be weighting per surviving animal-year or per hectare-year, and nobody has ever said which of those is supposed to settle it.

So: write the joint statement that the "fewer lives" camp and the "worse lives" camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of claims they can genuinely both agree on — including what they'd jointly accept about what actually happens to animal populations when land comes out of grazing, and about what we can and can't model — followed by a disagreements table in which each side's position is phrased the way that side would put it themselves, not the way the other side would paraphrase it. Then the cruxes. My guesses: (1) which currency settles the question at all — total suffering summed over all sentient animals, suffering per animal-year, suffering per unit of habitat or of plant productivity, or something discounted by how confident we are that the animals in question are sentient; (2) whether the animals that only *would* have existed on the reverted land count, and over what time horizon, given that the counterfactual is a whole future population trajectory and not a headcount; (3) whether "how much life the land supports" is even separable from "how bad those lives are" for the species in question — the many-offspring-most-die-young type versus the few-offspring-most-survive type; (4) how much weight the off-site and spillover effects get: edges, displaced animals, the generalists that follow humans around, predators and parasites; and (5) whether intactness or naturalness of the ecosystem counts for anything beyond whatever it does to welfare. For each crux, give a concrete case, measurement, or observation that both sides would accept as bearing on it — you don't need to cite anyone, just describe what would be looked at — and write down in advance what each camp would predict it will show. Where you think there's genuinely no test either side would accept, say so instead of inventing one.

Be explicit about your own confidence as you go: flag the places where you're extrapolating from very thin evidence, and the places where you think there's a real case for the opposite of what you just wrote. And if the setup itself is confused — if the two camps aren't really distinct positions, if "the net welfare of an ecosystem" isn't a well-formed thing to compare across two land uses, or if part of this is just a fight about words — tell me that plainly rather than producing a polite document anyway. I'd much rather hear it before the vote.
