Got a favour to ask for next week's seminar: second-year epistemology, 16–18 students, 80 minutes, I've got a projector and my laptop but no internet in the room, and I'm willing to bring a bag of cheap chocolate as stakes. They've done justified-true-belief, Gettier, and a first pass at safety-style accounts; they've done nothing at all on higher-order knowledge, and I don't want to name any of it up front — no lecture before the activity, they shouldn't hear the labels until the debrief.

The topic is luminosity and the KK principle, and what I want them to run into in their bones is not just "sometimes you're right without being able to tell" (they'll grant that in thirty seconds) but the stronger thing: that the gap is *forced*. If a condition can change slowly, there has to be a place where you're in it but can't tell you're in it. Here's the argument I want the session to be built around, so you can aim the activity at its joints:

Say a condition C is luminous iff whenever you're in C you're in a position to know you're in C. KK is the special case where C is knowing that p. Take a long series of cases c0 … cn that differ only by tiny steps, such that you're in C at c0, you're not in C at cn, and each adjacent pair is indiscriminable to you — you can't tell ci from ci+1. By luminosity you're in a position to know you're in C at c0. So there's a last case ci at which you're in a position to know you're in C; at ci+1 you're not. But ci and ci+1 are indiscriminable, and being in a position to know shouldn't come apart between two cases you can't tell apart, so if you're in a position to know at ci you should be at ci+1 too. Contradiction. Nothing that can come and go by small degrees is luminous, so either knowing can't come and go by small degrees or KK is false.

What I need designed:

1. A first-order task with a genuinely fine-grained difficulty knob. I was thinking rapid forced-choice judgements on the projector — which of two grey patches is lighter, or a between-two-and-four-second flash of dots and "were there more than 100" — with difficulty stepped in increments small enough that accuracy slides from roughly 95% down to roughly 55% with no visible step anywhere. Give me the actual stimulus recipe (what varies, by how much per step, how many steps, how many trials per step, timing, and how I stop the front row having an easier time of it than the back row). If you have something cleverer than grey patches, use it, but the two constraints are: adjacent items must really be indiscriminable to a human, and the difficulty knob must not be confounded with response bias or with how much time people take.

2. A second-order move after every item that is a real decision under stakes, not a confidence slider. I specifically don't want a 1–7 confidence scale: that turns the session into a calibration lecture and I lose the room. I was thinking something like "bank it" versus "pass", where banking pays +3 chocolates if you got the item right and −5 if you got it wrong, and passing pays 0 either way. Give me the actual payoff numbers — I want a ratio such that the right call depends on your real accuracy in that region, so that there is a band where banking is correct and a band where passing is correct, and a band in the middle where people are genuinely torn. Then tell me how to set the difficulty steps so that the torn band is well populated rather than two or three lucky trials.

3. The bit I'm least sure about, and the bit I care about most. I want them to try to find their own cutoff and fail to find it. My rough idea: after the first pass, each student privately marks the last item — by difficulty level, not by trial — that they'd be willing to say in advance, without hedging, "I'll know that I got that one right". Then we run a second pass with fresh tokens, same difficulty levels, shuffled so the levels don't come in order and so they can't memorise individual items. Design that second pass properly: how many trials, how to shuffle without them spotting the banding, what I record, and what the class-level data should look like if the argument is right. My expectation is that first-order accuracy will still be decently above chance somewhere above their marked cutoffs while second-order discrimination is at chance, and that the cutoffs themselves won't be stable from pass one to pass two. Tell me how to make that visible to them fast, with no statistics software, and tell me what to do if the data comes out the other way.

4. Something I want pressure-tested rather than assumed. Two things actually, and I don't know if either can be built:

(a) Iterated drift. If knowing needs a margin, and knowing-that-you-know needs a margin on top of that, then the band where you can't tell that you got it right should be strictly wider than the band where you can't get it right, and the third-order band wider still. Can the activity be rigged to test that — a three-level ladder of banking decisions (bank on being right / bank on knowing you're right / bank on knowing that you know you're right) with the same underlying trials — and if so, what does the widening pattern predict, and could the activity in principle distinguish "the bands keep widening without limit" from "they widen a bit and then stop"? My guess is the second is untestable in a room with 18 people and 80 minutes, but I want to hear where exactly it breaks.

(b) A contrastive variant. The indiscriminable neighbour case is literally sitting in the stimulus set, right next to the item they just judged. So instead of asking whether they can know that they know, ask whether they can know *that they know this one rather than merely getting that one right by luck* — a contrastive higher-order claim, where the contrast class is the adjacent difficulty level they just saw. Does that version dodge the argument, or does the slow series kill it too? And if the activity can discriminate between the plain version and the contrastive version, I want that in there, because it's the point I personally find hardest.

Then the debrief. What I want from it:

- The fork I expect to get the most pushback on: the data is compatible with two stories. Story one: KK is false — in the drift band you know and just can't know that you know. Story two: KK is true, and what the band shows is only that knowledge is fragile and has a margin for error built into it, so in the band you never knew in the first place.
