I'm a PhD student TAing a first-year metaphysics module, and next week's tutorial is on identity — specifically the bit where we get to Max Black's two-spheres argument against the Leibnizian principle and half the room says obviously there are two spheres, the other half says obviously there can't be, and then we run out of things to say by minute ten. What I want is a sorting quiz I can hand out: eight to ten questions, each a concrete little case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key at the back that maps answer patterns onto the actual positions, so students get sorted by what they say about cases rather than by which label they fancy. No reading lists or citations, I already have those.

The rules I care about: no question may use the vocabulary of the debate — no naming the principle, no talk of thisness or haecceities, no bundles or substrata, no discernibility talk, and definitely no straight-out "do you accept it?" Each question should be a case someone could answer in five seconds without having done the reading. And I don't want the usual cases recycled. Here are the ones I want used. (1) A universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres — crucially, no landmarks, nothing else in it, and the total state of the whole universe is completely unchanged if you swap them. (2) A third sphere, slightly dented, exists alongside them for a while and is then destroyed: is the world now like the first one again, or has something stuck to the two survivors? (3) The two spheres slowly orbit and exchange places: when the swap is finished, is the sphere now on the left the one that started on the left? (4) A universe with nothing in it but two mirror-image hands. (5) Two electrons in a set-up where every measurable quantity comes out symmetric between them, and a question about how many things there are in the box. Put (5) last, and tell me whether it needs its own branch or whether answers to (1)–(4) already settle it.

Then the taxonomy half: build the tree from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and only afterwards locate the real positions in that space — at minimum a leaf for people who think the spheres are told apart by trivial identity-involving properties (with variants for allowing that but refusing to count it, and for counting it but holding it makes the principle vacuous), a leaf for people who think an irreflexive symmetric relation like being a mile from the other one does the discerning even though both spheres satisfy it, a leaf for people who think the numerical difference is just brute, a leaf for people who think there are two of them but no fact of the matter which is which, and leaves for whatever the bundle-of-properties and bare-substratum options turn into down at the bottom. Give each leaf its usual name if it has one; otherwise write "unnamed" and say in one sentence why nobody sits there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves my questions can't actually seperate, and tell me which questions end up doing no real work because the disagreement at that node is verbal (my guess is that a lot of it hides in what counts as a property, but show me if it's somewhere else). Finally: which branch does the real disagreement live on? I want to know what to spend the second half of the tutorial arguing about.
