I'm a novelist, and after three books about people I made up I've spent the last two years on something I no longer know how to describe: a novel about an invented nineteenth-century woman — call her Mary Aldiss — who spent eleven years as a human "computer" at a small observatory, was quietly dismissed when the director took credit for a star catalogue she had largely compiled, and who in her last decade stopped answering letters about astronomy and asked her sister to burn her notebooks. Her sister burned most of them. What survives is about forty pages of fragment and a great deal of silence. The irony is not lost on me. The book I want to write is about a woman who withdrew, and the only way I can see to write it is to give her a childhood, a lover, a private vocabulary, a final confrontation with the director that almost certainly never happened — and then to be thanked, presumably, for giving her back her voice. My publisher's contracts department has been delightful and useless on the point, and I've started daydreaming about the document that would actually tell me what I am permitted to do, which is how I arrived at this request.

I'd like you to write that document, as a piece of deadpan satire. Give me an internal compliance instrument from the legal department of a studio or publishing house — call it Form 17-B, "Dramatization Consent Waiver and Legacy Impact Assessment (Deceased Subject)" — running to roughly 800 words, laid out as numbered clauses with checkboxes, blanks to be filled in, defined terms, and cross-references that lead nowhere, in the flat and entirely self-assured register of real production paperwork. It should be written from inside the institution, which has solved this problem and is faintly impatient with anyone who hasn't. Among the things I hope to find in it: an Overwrite Coefficient estimating the degree to which the finished work will permanently replace the real person in public memory, with a worked example showing that a successful depiction drives the coefficient to 1.0 and therefore resolves the question; a Willed Obscurity Election for subjects who appear to have chosen not to be remembered, together with the box for establishing that they did not mean it; a Ten Percent Doctrine capping invention at ten per cent of screen time or pages while permitting it to include all motivation, interiority, and last words; Composite Character Certification, allowing three real people to be merged so that no one of them is defamed by what the composite does; an Empathy Floor requiring that the subject remain sympathetic for at least sixty per cent of act two; a clause noting that the subject cannot withdraw consent; and an Estate Satisfaction Guarantee under which any element a rights-holder finds unflattering may be reshot, so that the truth of the depiction is whatever the estate will license.

Two further requirements. First, the humour has to come from the actual ethics of the practice rather than from invented absurdity: every comic clause should exaggerate something dramatizations of the real dead genuinely do or genuinely assume — that a flattering lie is a form of respect, that a life has to be given the shape of a plot in order to be worth telling, that fame or obscurity is itself a kind of consent, that the disclaimer at the front of the film is doing moral work, that we may invent freely provided we invent inspirationally. No winking, no named real people, no real statutes or real cases, and no citations: the instrument should be entirely invented, so that nothing in it needs to be looked up. Second, I'd like the form's own logic to arrive somewhere I recoil from. A schedule of Progressive De-Identification, offering the one depiction guaranteed to wrong no one by degrees until nothing recognisable remains, crossed against Marketing's requirement that the subject stay recognisable, would do it — as would a clause that briefly states the worry I actually have and a following clause instructing that such questions are not to be raised in writing. A reader who has never thought about posthumous harm should still understand what is being mocked, and should finish it unsure whether signing would be wrong, and whether "we made her inspiring" is an answer.
