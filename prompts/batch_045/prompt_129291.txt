I hold a version of the identity of indiscernibles that I think survives the standard counterexamples, and I want a map of what would have to be true for me to give it up.

The version I hold: necessarily, no two distinct particulars share all their properties, monadic and relational, where the discerning properties must themselves be qualitative rather than of the form being identical to A. I also want the thesis to have teeth. If two distinct objects were indiscernible, then swapping them would leave every qualitative fact unchanged; so the thesis amounts, I think, to the claim that no such swap is ever available, that every possible world is rigid in the sense of having no nontrivial symmetry. On that reading, Black's two-sphere world — a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres — is not a counterexample at all. It is a demonstration of a different point: that a complete description cast purely in qualitative and relational vocabulary, never using identity or singular reference, cannot settle how many things the world contains. My position is that this exposes a limit of description rather than a metaphysical possibility. The two-sphere world is not possible, and the ease with which I seem to imagine it is no evidence that it is.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of these a committed defender of the principle would himself concede as counting against it and which he would dismiss as question-begging. In particular, I want five things adjudicated rather than listed.

First, the rigidity consequence. The thesis apparently entails that no world is exactly symmetric in any respect: no world with two exactly similar objects, but also, it seems, no perfectly homogeneous plenum, since any translation of such a world would be a symmetry. Is that a cost or a badge, and does the defender have an out in the plenum case? Related: consider two spheres whose radii differ by a shrinking amount, converging to zero over an hour. If distinctness always requires some difference, the thesis seems to predict either that this process cannot complete or that there is a last instant of distinctness followed by a fusion event that nothing in the local physics corresponds to. Is that prediction embarrassing, or can it simply be accepted?

Second, exact duplication. Take two causally isolated perfect replicas of some universe. Cross-world pairs share every qualitative property, so the thesis seems to forbid duplication across worlds outright, or else to force itself to be indexed to a single world — in which case I want to know whether the indexed version still does any work or has become a tautology.

Third, actual physics. If our best physics represents some states as containing two particles sharing every monadic property and related only symmetrically, that would be an obtaining instance and not an imagined one. How much should that move me, and is the reply that such particles are weakly discerned by a relation that is irreflexive yet symmetric a genuine answer or only a restatement of the thesis in relational vocabulary?

Fourth, individual essences. The thesis appears to require that everything have a purely qualitative individual essence, some conjunctive property nothing else could instantiate. Is that a real liability once the essence must be exhaustively specific?

Fifth, collapse. In a symmetric world the only available discerners look haecceitistic. Does the qualitative version of the thesis then either forbid the world or concede the point to its opponent, so that the interesting thesis falls even while a trivial one survives?

Finally, the thing I most want checked. My two defensive moves — descriptive underdetermination, and the unreliability of apparent conceivability — look like they could be wheeled out to shield any necessary thesis from any apparent counterexample. If so, I have insulated the view into unfalsifiability. Say plainly whether that is what I have done, whether it is fatal or merely acceptable for a thesis of this kind, and what sort of consideration could still reach me. For what it is worth, what I would personally find decisive is not another armchair scenario but an argument that some actually obtaining, scientifically described configuration forces the two-sphere structure on us, or else that no such configuration could be described at all without identity smuggled back in.
