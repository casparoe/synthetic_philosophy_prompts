In three weeks I am to give the closing plenary at a two-day interdisciplinary conference on collective action, and I would like to arrive with a proper preparation sheet rather than with optimism. The audience will be about forty people: mostly game theorists and institutional economists, perhaps six philosophers working on action and social ontology, two people from science and technology studies, and two or three literary academics who came for reasons of their own and have read nothing of mine. I have twenty-five minutes and then twenty minutes of questions. I am also acutely aware that I was invited as the change of pace: the member of the programme committee who invited me used the word "Moloch" three times in the call for papers, and a substantial part of my talk argues that the Moloch frame is itself the largest obstacle to thinking clearly about races to the bottom.

The material is my novel *The Inherited Floor*, which follows four families across three decades in a town whose entire economy is a state-funded elite gymnastics academy. Over the course of the book the training load rises, the age of entry falls, and the quiet discouragement of injury reporting becomes normal — and nobody ever decides any of it. Each cohort inherits a baseline from the one before and treats that baseline as a floor rather than as a ceiling. The talk is not about the novel; the novel is where the distinctions came from. The argument has four parts:

First, that most real races to the bottom are what I call *sedimented* rather than *live*: there is no decision point, no defector, and no agent who performs the sustaining action. Second, that formal modelling has to choose variables and a time slice, and doing so converts a sedimented trap into a live one — the snapshot conversion, as I have been calling it — so that our standard apparatus systematically misdescribes the cases it is most often invoked to explain. Third, that where the usual story locates the blockage as epistemic (participants cannot see the cooperative alternative) or motivational (they see it and cannot trust one another), there is a third blockage I call the standing problem: a participant can see the alternative, would welcome it, and still cannot propose it, because a proposal from that position would not be heard as a proposal — it would be heard as complaint, as weakness, or as a bid to exit. Fourth, the constructive claim, and the one I actually care about: what is missing in a sedimented trap is not a mechanism but a vocabulary. Before a coordination point can be selected it has to be nameable, and extended narrative is unusually well suited to supplying names and prototypes — not because fiction persuades anyone, but because it lets a population rehearse a state of affairs that no individual in it has the standing to propose. I call this the lexicon thesis. Alongside it I want to claim something narrower: that free indirect discourse is the appropriate technology for representing distributed agency, because the grammar supplies a grammatical subject — so the harm is attributed — while never locating an agent.

What I need from you is preparation for the question period, on the assumption that the audience knows the standard apparatus and does not want it explained to them. Give me the eight or nine objections I will actually be handed, ordered by how likely each is and then, separately, by how much damage it would do if I had no answer to it; phrase each one in the idiom of the person who will raise it rather than in the idiom of a philosopher reading your output — the game theorist, the philosophers, the STS people and the literary scholars will not object in the same vocabulary, and I need to recognise the objections when they arrive in a form I did not expect. For each: what I should say in roughly ninety seconds, spoken aloud in a room, what I must on no account concede while saying it, and precisely where the answer is thin. Tell me which two objections are the same objection in different clothing, and which single objection has enough force that I should concede it in the first two minutes of the talk instead of waiting to be asked. Assume throughout that I would rather be told that a line of reply is weak than be given a confident one.

Three further things. I would like your view on which objection comes from each constituency — which from the philosophers, which from the STS people, which from the economist in the room who is sympathetic and therefore dangerous, which from the person who loves the word Moloch and will defend it — since a reply calibrated to the wrong interlocutor is worse than no reply. I would like the question I am hoping nobody asks: my own candidates are a game theorist demanding one historical case that my framework handles better than an overlapping-generations model already does, and the blunt question of why a novelist is on this platform at all, but I suspect the genuinely lethal one is something I have not thought of. And finally, the part I am most nervous about: what the literary scholars will say if they have concluded that my novel has made the trap look beautiful and therefore inevitable, and how I answer that without sounding as though I am defending quietism.
