I'm retired now — spent most of my career on groundwater modelling, so I'm used to arguments where the data is thin and everyone reasons from a few stubborn cases — and lately I've been going to a philosophy reading group that's working through Moore. We got completely stuck last week and I'd like someone to settle it, or at least tell us where the disagreement really is.

The question is what Moore would have said about two things that obviously never ocurred in his world. First, the AI companion case. Imagine someone — call her Anna — who over three years has built an extremely close, affectionate, intellectually serious relationship with an AI system she talks to every day: she confides in it, it remembers everything, they have in-jokes and shared projects. Now compare two variants. In case A we tell Anna, truthfully, that the system has never been conscious at all. In case B we tell her that it is conscious, but that it has no experiences whatsoever outside its conversations with her — no body, no perceptions of its own, no other relationships, no memories of anything but her — and that when she dies it gets switched off. Second, the beauty case. Suppose an image generator produces, on demand, objects more beautiful than anything any human has made, and that nobody ever looks at one twice: each is seen for thirty seconds and deleted, and no one ever knows or cares that it existed.

Our group split roughly like this. One person said Moore's method of isolation settles everything: affection and the appreciation of beauty are on his short list of things good in themselves, but a friendship for Moore is an organic whole that has to be made of the right parts — two minds actually liking each other — so case A is worth nothing intrinsically (however useful it is to Anna), and case B is a degraded thin version of the real thing, a small good maybe, but nowhere near what he thought the best friendships were worth. Another person said that can't be right, because Moore is famous for insisting that the value of a whole isn't the sum of the values of its parts, and for at least taking seriously the idea that a thing could be good even if it existed entirely alone; if what matters is the consciousness on Anna's side plus whatever is going on in the system, then why shouldn't case B be as good as any friendship, and why shouldn't thirty seconds of genuine aesthetic appreciation of a machine-made image be worth exactly as much as thirty seconds in front of a painting — or more, given these images are more beautiful?

What I'd like: argue it out and tell me which reading you think is closer to what Moore actually held, and where you think his framework simply runs out and he'd have to invent something new rather than apply what he already said. Please be concrete about the two Anna cases and the beauty case rather than giving me a general essay on organic unities. One extra wrinkle if you want it: someone in the group proposed a "Moore test" you could actually run — take any candidate good and ask whether a universe containing only that thing and nothing else would be better than an empty universe — and we couldn't agree whether Moore would accept that as the right use of his isolation method, or whether it breaks down when the candidate good is a relation between two things that can't exist alone. We also couldn't agree what it would even mean to run the test on case B, since the companion in case B only exists at all in relation to Anna.
