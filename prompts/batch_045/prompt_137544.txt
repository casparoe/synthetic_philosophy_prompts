I invented a game for our school's mathematics club and have since been using it as a test case for an argument about strategy stealing that I only half understand. The two now contradict each other, and I cannot see which one to surrender.

The game, which I call Weld, is played on a 4×4 grid. Light and Dark alternate placing one stone of their own colour on an empty square, Light moving first, until all sixteen squares are filled, so each player places exactly eight stones. When the board is full, a player's weld count is the number of pairs of squares that share an edge and both contain that player's stones; sharing only a corner does not count. The player with the higher weld count wins. If the two counts are equal, the player who made the final move wins. No draw is possible.

I think I can prove that Dark, the second player, wins. Pair every square with the square it lands on when the board is given a half-turn about its centre; a 4×4 grid has no centre square, so no square is paired with itself. Dark's rule is to play the partner of the square Light has just taken. That reply is always available: before Light's move the position is symmetric, in the sense that Dark's stones are exactly the half-turn images of Light's, so if the partner square already held a Dark stone then by symmetry Light's square would already have held a Light stone, and if it held a Light stone then Light's square would already have held a Dark stone; either way Light could not have just played there. So Dark can always reply, the symmetry is restored after each reply, and at the end Dark's stones are a rotated copy of Light's. The two weld counts are therefore equal, Dark made the sixteenth move, and Dark wins.

Against that I have the following argument, which is the one I learned from the standard strategy-stealing reasoning and which seems to show the opposite.

(1) Weld is finite, deterministic, has perfect information, and every play has a winner, so one of the two players must have a winning strategy.

(2) The rules favour neither player: Light and Dark place stones under exactly the same restriction, both win under exactly the same description, and no rule mentions either player by name.

(3) An extra stone of your own colour can never hurt you. Take any finished position and switch one square from the opponent's colour to yours: your weld count rises by the number of your stones edge-adjacent to that square, theirs falls by the number of their stones edge-adjacent to it, so your margin moves by the degree of the square, which is at least two anywhere on the grid. An extra stone of your own can therefore never turn a position you win into one you lose.

(4) Suppose Dark had a winning strategy. Light could place a first stone anywhere, treat it as a spare, and from then on answer Dark's moves exactly as that strategy prescribes for the second player, ignoring the spare when reading the position. If the strategy ever names the square the spare sits on, Light plays on any other empty square instead and treats that stone as the spare from then on. By (3) the spare can only help, and the strategy wins against anything Dark does, so Light wins.

(5) So Dark has no winning strategy, and by (1) Light has one.

Step (5) contradicts the half-turn argument, and nothing in (2) to (4) seems to depend on the board being 4×4, so the same reasoning would apply on the 6×6 and 8×8 boards, where the mirror works just as well.

I have checked the steps I can check. Step (1) looks solid. Step (3) I am fairly confident of, since I can prove the margin computation, and it is the step I would least like to give up. That leaves (2), (4), or something the argument needs but never states.

So my questions. Which step do you reject, and — the part I would find most useful — why does that step look like it uses only what (1) to (3) state, when in fact it does not? If the argument needs an extra condition, what is the weakest form of it: something like the outcome having to depend only on the final distribution of stones, and on that distribution symmetrically in the two players? Does the argument survive if I change the tie rule — if equal weld counts are declared a draw, if they are awarded to the player who moved first, or if they are broken by a further positional comparison such as who has more stones in the topmost row where the players differ? Does the mirror strategy survive those changes? Does the size of the board matter to the diagnosis? On a 5×5 board I can find no mirror strategy at all, since any way of pairing off twenty-five squares leaves one square over, and there I do not know whether the stealing argument is sound or not.

Finally, what does rejecting the step cost me? I would especially like to know whether the strategy-stealing pattern still tells us anything substantial once the missing condition is added, or whether the condition quietly confines it to games in which a second-mover advantage has already been excluded some other way — i.e. whether this style of argument can ever show that a second player cannot have an advantage, or only that they cannot have one of a particular kind.
