I work in a small unit inside a ministry of justice — I would rather not name the country — that has just been handed the job of administering a new funding arrangement for religious and other worldview bodies, and I have to draft the assessment procedure. The background: our constitution has no established church and a clause, inserted as a compromise during the last revision, saying that the Republic recognises the public contribution of communities of belief and of conscience. From next year, taxpayers will have a payroll deduction of 0.6 per cent; those who name a qualifying body send their share to it, those who tick no affiliation go into a residual cultural-heritage fund unless they actively name a body anyway, and there is talk of a later opt-in for redirecting the residual share to secular charities. Bodies can apply for recognition on either of two tracks, one for worship-centred communities and one for non-theistic convictions, and the government has not yet decided whether recognition should be a single gate that carries everything with it or a tiered status where the levy, the right to conduct legally valid marriages, and the right to teach in state schools come apart. We have looked loosely at how England, Germany, France and the United States handle these things, and at the Italian and Spanish tax-designation schemes, but my minister has been clear that we are copying nobody. My director's own line is that we are not in the business of grading religions, which I find sounds right and decides nothing.

What I need from you is a written procedure my case officers can run. They are lawyers without training in theology or the philosophy of religion, they get about thirty applications a year, they have roughly three weeks a file, and every decision is politically visible, signable by the minister and reviewable by a constitutional court that applies a proportionality and equal-treatment test. So: the questions to ask in order, and what a sufficient answer to each looks like; the points at which the file stops with a no; the points at which the procedure has to hand the decision to a named person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. It should never require an official to judge whether a doctrine is true, or how sincerely it is held, but it does have to cope with bodies whose internal governance is undemocratic, or discriminatory in ways that would be illegal in any other employer, or funded from abroad, or that exist mainly as parody. I also want you to say explicitly which distinctions from the church-and-state literature survive being turned into a checklist and which ones you have dropped as unusable at this level, and to tell me whether you think the gate should be single or tiered.

Then apply the procedure to three of our pending files, please: an Ethical Culture and Humanist Association with three thousand dues-paying members, Sunday assemblies, a trained celebrant corps and no creed, which is currently the only body that will conduct non-religious funerals in rural districts; a federation of forty legally independent immigrant congregations, formed last year, some of which receive money from a foreign government's religious-affairs ministry and none of which has a governing hierarchy above the congregational level; and the centuries-old national church, which ordains only men, will not solemnise same-sex marriages, and runs something like a third of the country's hospices and elder care. I would like at least one of the three applications to come out somewhere I will not like, and I would rather hear it than not. Finally, be honest about what this kind of procedure cannot do — where the appearance of administrative neutrality is doing work that actually belongs to a legislature, and where it is just hiding a substantive view about which communities a liberal state should want more of.
