I teach a first-year seminar on philosophy of language and mind, and I'm reworking the syllabus for next term. I want to screen the 1962 film of The Miracle Worker — Gibson's play, Anne Sullivan spelling into Helen Keller's hand, the pump scene — alongside Wittgenstein's private language argument, and I want to know whether that pairing is actually doing any philosophical work or whether its just decoration.

Here's the thought I can't shake, and I'd like you to take it seriously as a hypothesis rather than as my conclusion. The private language argument, in the form where someone makes a mark each time a certain sensation occurs and calls it a name, seems to have teeth only against signs that purport to say or report something. Signs that are addressed to someone or something are a different animal: prayer, talking to the dead, letters you never send, a castaway talking to a volleyball. An address isn't competing for correctness in the same way, so the argument's usual machinery — no criterion for right and wrong, no distinction between thinking you follow a rule and following it — doesn't obviously grip it. I want to call it the difference between allocutive and descriptive signs, though Im probably reinventing something.

So the reading I want: take the film through the private language argument — the beetle in the box, the impossibility of a private ostensive definition, the claim that an inner process stands in need of outward criteria, the point that ostension needs a stage-setting or a technique before it can do anything — and tell me where it illuminates the film and where the film resists it. Is the pump scene best read as a picture of Wittgenstein's positive claim, a case where naming fails until an entire practice is in place, or is it a picture of exactly the thing he attacks, an inner dawning that then just gets a label stuck onto it? Does the film assume there was a fully formed mind in there waiting for the word, and does it have any resources to question that assumption about itself, given that it's a sentimental therapeutic drama built on a breakthrough? What does the reading do with the earlier scenes — the doll that gets smashed, the fight at the breakfast table — and with the question of whether Helen has thoughts before language? And where does my allocutive/descriptive distinction actually earn its keep, and where is it just me inventing a category so the film will behave?

I'd rather hand students a reading that admits a scene fights back than one that irons it out. Tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims and flag the one you're least sure of. And please, no headings, bullets, bold or tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
