I am rewriting the notes for the two-week unit on conditionals in my graduate seminar, and I have reached the point where I use Lewis's triviality results as the hinge of the whole thing: everything before it sets up the Ramsey test, and everything after it is supposed to be the scramble for an escape route. A colleague in the mathematics department listened to me run the argument over lunch and said only that he did not see why it forced the conclusion I drew, and I could not produce a decent answer on the spot; a referee on a related paper then made a remark that I now suspect was aimed at exactly the same step. So I would like to set out the argument as I actually teach it and have someone tell me which inference in it is bad, if any — I would much rather be told it is wrong than be given a way of making it look right.

The argument, compressed. The Ramsey test says that my confidence in "if A, B" ought to be P(B|A). Lewis showed that there is no proposition, in any non-trivial credence function, whose probability always equals the corresponding conditional probability; and the proof apparently needs only that the conditional contributes something to a Boolean algebra over which a classically additive probability function is defined, plus the assumption that the relevant cells A∧B and A∧¬B have positive probability. Since the Ramsey test is the datum any theory has to respect, and since there is nothing whose probability could be P(B|A), the conclusion I have been drawing is that indicative conditionals do not express propositions at all: the honest form of a Ramsey-test view is a non-cognitivism of the familiar suppositional sort, and saying that conditionals have "truth conditions of a non-classical kind" is just a way of avoiding that word. Two corollaries have been following from this in my lectures. First, that the result is also what finally retires the material conditional: it is the one truth-conditional analysis compatible with Lewis's argument, since it never identifies P(if A, B) with P(B|A), but it buys that compatibility at the price of making the probability of a conditional absurdly high whenever the antecedent is improbable, so the probability data plus the triviality argument leave it with nothing to say for itself. Second, that a construction I have been toying with for years is merely notational: take "if A, B" to denote an object that takes the value 1 on A∧B, 0 on A∧¬B, and no value at all on ¬A, so that its expectation is P(B|A). If that object escapes the result, it can only be because it is not a proposition; and that, I have been telling myself and my students, is non-cognitivism written in different symbols. I have been dismissing the standard complaint that such an object cannot occur inside negations, disjunctions and embedded antecedents on the grounds that the embeddings which cause the trouble are philosophers' contrivations rather than things speakers do.

What I want is the structure of the argument, not pointers to the literature — I have the literature to hand, and what I lack is a clear view of the logical geography. Specifically: is the choice really a straight dichotomy between truth conditions and the equation, or have I conflated being a proposition in a Boolean algebra with having truth conditions? What exactly does the proof take as hostage, and is a restriction of the equation to a privileged class of antecedents, or a non-classical notion of content, a genuine option or a verbal manoeuvre? Is my three-valued object a real third position or a restatement of non-cognitivism, and what precisely does it cost me? And am I wrong that the triviality results are what defeat the material conditional, rather than something quite independent of them? I need something I can put in front of students for a one-hour session and defend in the question period afterwards.
