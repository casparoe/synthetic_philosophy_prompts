QUESTION. In this course we've been working with the following two-dimensional framework. Every expression has (i) a primary intension, a function from possible worlds considered as actual to extensions, and (ii) a secondary intension, a function from possible worlds considered as counterfactual to extensions, where the reference is fixed by what the expression picks out in the actual world. We then read off two statuses: a sentence is a priori (for a speaker) iff its primary intension is true at every world considered as actual; it is metaphysically necessary iff its secondary intension is true at every world considered as counterfactual. For a natural-kind term such as 'water' this means: the primary intension is the function that takes each world considered as actual to whatever plays the watery role there (the clearish, drinkable liquid in the lakes and rivers, and so on), while the secondary intension takes each counterfactual world to H2O.

(a) Use this machinery to explain how 'water = H2O' manages to be both metaphysically necessary and a posteriori. [5]

(b) Here is a new case. Veridia is a planet in another solar system whose lakes we have only seen through telescopes, and V₂O is the chemical that in fact makes up most of the liquid in those lakes. Suppose we introduce a term by stipulating: let 'vater' name whichever substance actually makes up most of the liquid in the lakes of Veridia. Here 'actually' is a strong actuality operator: even when we evaluate 'vater' with respect to some other world considered as actual, the operator still reaches back to the real actual world. (Ignore the alternative treatment on which 'actually' is evaluated along the diagonal of the matrix.) Give the primary intension and the secondary intension of our term 'vater', and classify 'vater = V₂O' as we use it — necessary or contingent, a priori or a posteriori — justifying each verdict briefly from the definitions above. [8]

(c) The Vicians live on Veridia, and like us they have a word 'vater', but theirs was not introduced by any actualized description: for them it means something like 'the liquid in the lakes on Veridia', with no actuality operator anywhere in it. Does 'vater = V₂O' have the same two-dimensional profile for them as it does for us? If not, say exactly where it differs and why. Finally, say what this shows about whether apriority, on this way of setting things up, is a property of sentence-types or of something else. [7]

And here is the student's answer, copied out pretty much verbatim (typos are theirs):

ANSWER. (a) The trick is that the two intensions pull apart. The primary intension of 'water' sends each world considered as actual to whatever plays the watery role in that world — on Twin Earth, considered as actual, that's XYZ rather than H2O. So the primary intension of 'water = H2O' is false at some worlds considered as actual, which by the definition above makes it a posteriori: to know it you have to go and find out which stuff is the watery stuff round here. Meanwhile the secondary intension of 'water' is H2O at every counterfactual world, and the same goes for 'H2O' itself, so the secondary intension of the whole sentence is true at every counterfactual world and the sentence is metaphysically necessary. There's no contradiction in calling one and the same sentence necessary and a posteriori; the two claims are claims about different intensions.

(b) The strong actuality operator makes the reference-fixer insensitive to which world we consider as actual. At any world w considered as actual, the description still reaches back to the real world and picks out V₂O. So the primary intension of 'vater' is the constant function with value V₂O everywhere. 'V₂O' likewise picks out V₂O at every world considered as actual, so the primary intension of 'vater = V₂O' is true at every world considered as actual, which makes it a priori for us. For the secondary intension we hold the actual reference fixed but ask what happens at counterfactual worlds: if the Veridian lakes had been full of liquid methane instead, then the substance that makes up most of the liquid in them would have been methane, and 'vater' would have picked out methane, so the secondary intension of 'vater' varies across counterfactual worlds and 'vater = V₂O' is contingent. All in all our sentence is a priori but contingent — the exact mirror image of 'water = H2O', which is a posteriori but necessary.

(c) For the Vicians it's the other way round. Their 'vater' has no actuality operator in it, so its primary intension sends each world considered as actual to whatever fills the Veridian lakes in that world; at a world where those lakes hold H2O the sentence comes out false, so its primary intension isn't necessary and the sentence is a posteriori for them. Its secondary intension, though, is constant — once the actual reference is fixed to V₂O, the term designates V₂O at every counterfactual world — so the sentence is necessary for them as well. So the very same sentence is, on this framework, a priori and necessary for us while being a posteriori and necessary for them, and that shows that apriority here can't be a property of sentence-types; it has to be a property of utterances, or of a sentence taken together with the speaker's idiolect and the way the terms in it had their reference fixed. The sentence-type itself has no apriority status at all, and any view that tries to read apriority off the shapes of the marks on the page is going to give the wrong verdict in a case like this.
