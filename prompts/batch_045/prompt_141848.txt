I'm drafting the closing chapter of a short popular book on Johannes Kepler. The chapter is a counterfactual: Kepler is shown the state of astrology in the twenty-first century and replies. I need that reply written as a piece of sustained reasoning rather than as pastiche — no period diction, no invented aphorisms — modern prose in which every prediction is anchored to something he actually held.

For the purposes of the exercise, treat his position as follows. He regarded astrology as a genuine but badly corrupted body of knowledge that had to be rebuilt from accumulated experience rather than from authority, and he was willing to throw out most of the received apparatus — the houses, the dignities and debilities, the traditional rulerships — as untested tradition. What he retained was the doctrine of aspects: the claim that what matters is not which sign a planet occupies but the angular separations between planets, and that only some separations are effective, because they correspond to divisions of the circle that are geometrically constructible and musically consonant, which is why he expanded the classical set with aspects such as the quintile and the biquintile. He did not think the influence operated by mechanical push; he posited something in the Earth — a perceiving, harmonically responsive principle rather than a passive body — that registers the configuration and is stirred by it, and he took terrestrial, especially meteorological, effects to be the clearest case. He held that the heavens incline rather than determine. And he earned part of his living from calendars, prognostications and nativities for patrons while speaking with open contempt about astrology as it was actually sold.

Set him against four things he could not have encountered.

1. Physics. There is no known interaction that could carry the influence. The gravitational and electromagnetic effects of Mars on a newborn are orders of magnitude smaller than those of the people in the delivery room, and nothing in current physics distinguishes an angular separation of 72 degrees from one of 73. Note the tension: in his astronomy he demanded physical causes and replaced the moving souls of earlier theory with a quasi-magnetic force, while his astrology rested on something explicitly non-mechanical.

2. Statistics. Suppose we show him a study in which a team, working with a national birth registry linked to occupational records covering roughly 1.8 million people, reports that those who reach the top of a handful of professions are born slightly more often than chance when a particular planet stands near the horizon or the meridian at their birth. The excess is one or two percent over baseline, survives the team's own cross-validation, was found only after searching across dozens of candidate angles, planets and professions, and disappears in a pre-registered replication on fresh data.

3. Psychology. The apparent accuracy of horoscopes is explained by documented facts about us: people accept vague, flattering personality sketches as uniquely apt to them, and remember the hits and forget the misses.

4. The rebranding. Astrology has been largely reclassified as a wellness practice. Its defenders in the popular press no longer claim the planets cause anything; a horoscope is a frame for self-reflection, a mirror, a language, and demanding evidence of a causal influence is, they say, a category mistake.

What I need from you:

For each of the four, say what he would concede and what he would reject, and which of the commitments above is doing the work. Where you extrapolate, identify the commitment you are extrapolating from.

On the first: say whether the absence of a mechanism has any force against him at all, or whether it presupposes a conception of causation he never accepted — and if the latter, whether he can say so without conceding that his astrology explains anything and everything.

On the fourth: say whether the symbolic, non-causal defence is one he could avail himself of, or whether it undercuts the very thing he thought made astrology worth defending.

Say whether, in his hands, the aspect doctrine stands or falls together with natal horoscopy, or whether he can detach them — and if he detaches them, which way he goes: keeping the aspect-and-weather work and abandoning character readings, or the reverse.

Say what specifically he would demand of the statistical study, given that he insisted on testing astrological rules against large numbers of carefully recorded cases and had no patience for coincidences noticed after the fact.

Finally, tell me where the exercise stops being prediction: at what point would attributing a view to him mean attributing one he had no resources to form?

Write about 700 to 900 words. No quotations, footnotes, citations, or suggestions for further reading — I will check the history myself, and I need prose a general reader can follow. If you are unsure whether he actually held some detail I have given you, flag it in a parenthesis rather than building on it.
