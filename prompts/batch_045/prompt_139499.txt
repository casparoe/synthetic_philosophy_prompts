I'm in the final year of a joint Philosophy and Computer Science degree, and this term I'm taking a seminar called "Computation, Proof and Mind." For my assessed essay I need to work through an argument that has been nagging at me since week three, and I would be grateful for a draft I can then argue with.

The seminar has spent a lot of time on one claim: that P versus NP is not merely a technical question but a philosophical one. The idea is that the class NP supposedly captures the difference between checking an answer and finding one, so that if it turned out that P = NP, discovery (mathematical proof included) would in principle be no harder than verification, and something we associate with creativity would turn out to be mechanical after all. Call this the creativity argument.

I want to test that argument against a scenario I have been calling the Existence-Only Resolution. Suppose that next year someone publishes a proof that SAT admits a deterministic algorithm running in time O(n^4), but that the proof is non-constructive in a rather brutal way: it shows that one member of a finite though astronomically large, explicitly specified family of circuit families decides SAT within that bound, without identifying which member, and without offering any method for recovering it. Assume the proof is correct and universally accepted, and assume that even a century later nobody has extracted a runnable algorithm from it. Suppose additionally that a separate group produces what I will call a horizon-optimal searcher: a general procedure for finding proofs or solutions that is provably the fastest possible up to an unknown multiplicative constant, where the constant is finite but nobody can bound it and, for all anyone knows, it is astronomically large.

So there seem to be two things one might mean by "tractable." I will call the first tractability-in-principle: membership in a nice asymptotic complexity class, even if no one can exhibit or run the relevant algorithm. I will call the second tractability-for-us: a procedure that can actually be specified, implemented, and whose runtime can be estimated at the instance sizes that creatures like us, with our bounded lifetimes and hardware, actually care about. Please use those two labels in your answer.

The essay I need should answer two questions. First, does the Existence-Only Resolution vindicate the creativity argument, refute it, or neither? Second, and more generally, ought the philosophical weight in this debate to be carried by tractability-in-principle or by tractability-for-us — or does the entire framing rest on a mistake about what complexity-theoretic classification is for? Please also engage one wrinkle: several people in my seminar hold that even a genuinely fast solver that emitted verified certificates for arbitrary conjectures would not touch what is valuable about mathematics, because the certificate would explain nothing; it would be a black box. Does that reply rescue the creativity argument, or does it quietly change the subject?

Practical requirements. Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words. Write in British English. Pitch it at a seminar that is half philosophers and half computer scientists, so define any technical term the first time you use it. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I will supply those myself, so argue from the stipulations above and from first principles rather than from what any particular author has claimed. Above all, do not end on a non-committal note: I want a verdict you would be willing to defend out loud in class, and I want you to state the strongest version of the consideration you are rejecting before you reject it.
