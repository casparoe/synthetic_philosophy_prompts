Next term I am teaching an upper-level seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and I would like to spend one session on Le Guin's short parable of Omelas — the radiant city whose happiness, beauty and summer festival are held, by a bargain the narrator refuses to explain mechanically, to depend on one child kept in filth and darkness in a locked room, shown to the citizens when they come of age, at which point a few of them, without protest, argument, or any attempt at rescue, simply walk out of the city and into the mountains, never to return. I have taught it for years as the usual utilitarian exercise — count the child, count the city, notice that the numbers seem to favour the arrangement and ask what that shows about the theory — and I want to do something different this time: read it through Buddhist ethical thought, and in particular through the ideas that what karma consists in is volition or intention rather than outcome, that action is assessed by the roots from which it springs (greed, aversion, and delusion, and their opposites) rather than by its生産 ...

Let me restate that more carefully, since I want the reading to be technically accountable. Read the story through the Buddhist moral psychology in which the ethically salient unit is not the state of affairs produced but the quality of will expressed and the dispositions it cultivates: the claim, familiar from the canon, that karma just is cetanā, volition; the classification of action by the three unwholesome roots and their counterparts; the place of non-harming and compassion; and the very different ideals represented by the arhat, who has extinguished the causes of his own suffering, and the bodhisattva, who returns to the world out of compassion. The point I want pressed is this. A consequentialist has a ready complaint about the walkers: they do nothing for the child, they do not even try, their going is at best expressive and at worst a form of moral self-indulgence, a purchase on one's own purity bought at no benefit to anyone — and the story's own narrator makes this worse by refusing to say where they go or whether their going has any point at all, only that they seem to know. A Buddhist reading has to be able to say what the consequentialist cannot: that the difference the walkers make is real and is located in the withdrawal of the sustaining volition, in the refusal to let one's own ease be carried by an intention directed at another's misery, and in what that refusal does to the agent's character and future action. I want to know whether that difference is genuinely different in kind from consequentialist reasoning, or whether it is simply consequentialism with a more generous account of which outcomes count — states of mind, dispositions, what one is disposed to notice — and therefore vulnerable to the same collapse into the charge of self-indulgence. My colleague in the religion department thinks the story is essentially a renunciation narrative and that the consequentialist framing is a category mistake; I am not convinced, and I would like the disagreement settled as far as it can be.

Two further complications, which I ask you to take seriously rather than set aside, because I think they are where a Buddhist reading either earns its keep or fails. First, a variant I have been turning over: suppose the city's dependence on the child is real but no one is ever told — the citizens live their whole lives without knowledge of the child, never visit, never consent, and the walkers' option does not exist. Call it unvisited Omelas. The consequentialist should say that nothing morally important has changed: the same suffering, the same happiness, less distress among the citizens, and no one's will is directed at the child at all. Does the Buddhist account then have to say that a great deal has changed, because there is now no intention to withdraw and no delusion to be dispelled — and if so, is that an embarrassment, since it seems to make the wrongness of Omelas depend on who is thinking about it rather than on the child? Second, a figure Le Guin does not give us: the person who leaves, comes to see something, and then comes back — not to accept the arrangement but to work on it from inside, at the cost of remaining a beneficiary of it. The bodhisattva-shaped option. Does the story's silence about such a figure betray an assumption that there is genuinely nothing to be done, and would a Buddhist ethicist be under pressure to supply the missing ending in a way that a consequentialist would not? I would also like you to say, honestly, where the story resists the reading: whether its narrator's pluralism about what the terms really are is as resistant to a Buddhist construal as it is to a utilitarian one, and whether — the question I care about most — the parable dramatizes something about the relation between self-regarding liberation and other-regarding benefit that the philosophical arguments on both sides of the consequentialism debate do not.
