I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar: fourteen students, a single eighty-minute session, a room with movable chairs and four large tables, and no screens — I would rather the activity be run with index cards, string, and tokens than with anything digital. It is the third week. The students have encountered the words grounding and fundamental in casual discussion but have read no technical material, and I do not want them to hear either term, or the name of any position, until the activity is finished.

What I want is one structured game, played by four groups of three or four, in which participants run into the following three things by trying to win, and only afterwards learn that these things have names: first, that a relation of dependence is cheap — for almost any fact you can find something else it depends on — so the real question is not whether a fact depends on something but which of the dependencies are the constitutive ones; second, that a principle saying every non-fundamental fact is grounded, combined with pressure to posit as few fundamentals as possible, squeezes you toward one of three uncomfortable outcomes: a single all-purpose ground that explains nothing, a stock of fundamentals whose membership looks arbitrary, or a regress; third, that fundamentality is not the same as being smallest, earliest, or most causally upstream.

The mechanics I have in mind — please develop them, correct them, or replace them with something better — are these. Each group receives a deck of about twenty-four fact cards phrased in plain language, mixing five kinds: intrinsic facts about material objects; dispositional or modal facts; existence facts about composites or about abstract objects; normative facts; and, dealt only at the halfway point, three gold cards stating a conjunctive fact, a negative fact, and a fact to the effect that the cards on the table are all the facts there are. Groups lay the cards out on their table and draw arrows, understanding an arrow only as meaning obtains in virtue of. Each group is given exactly four BRUTE tokens, to be placed on cards that are allowed to have no incoming arrow. Scoring should make completeness and parsimony fight: points for each card that traces downward along arrows to a BRUTE token, a larger bonus for each unused BRUTE token, a penalty for any card left dangling, and a sharp penalty for a cycle, which the group must cut in public while saying which arrow they sacrificed and why. After that, an adversarial audit round: one group challenges another group's arrow by producing a near miss, a card that would have been cited as the ground had the target fact been slightly different, and the arrow is voided unless the defending group can say why their ground is the right one and the near miss is not. Then the gold cards are dealt, with the rule that they must be placed in the structure like any other card.

Please give me: a materials list; complete rules and a payoff scheme that needs no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline across the eighty minutes, including what the facilitator says at the start, which should not include any theory; a short list of example cards for each of the five kinds, written as a student would read them; and a debrief that maps specific moments the game predictably produces onto the parts of the idea — the audit round onto the requirement that a ground make a difference, the cut cycle onto the assumption that dependence is asymmetric and well founded, the argument about whether a disposition is grounded in its categorical base onto the distinction between constitutive and causal or nomic dependence, the gold cards onto the limits of the completeness principle, and the moment a group spends its last BRUTE token onto the choice between an ungrounded remainder and a hierarchy with no bottom.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each case: the group that declares everything brute and banks the parsimony bonus; the group that builds a long chain of mere redescriptions; the group that grounds every card in the initial state of the universe, or in God; the group that finishes in five minutes while others are still arguing; and the student who points out, correctly, that the scoring is arbitrary. Finally, be frank about which feature of the view the game cannot reproduce and which step the facilitator must therefore supply in the debrief — in particular, say how I should get from our accounting practice ran out to there is a structure in reality, and whether the game can distinguish a fact that is ungrounded because it is fundamental from a fact that is ungrounded because it is not the kind of thing that enters into the relation at all.
