Ok so I've somehow ended up as the facilitator for an adversarial collab between two people who've been sniping at each other about peer disagreement for years — one is a full-on conciliationist (equal weight, split the difference, the works) and the other is a steadfast theorist (roughly: if my read of the first-order evidence was justified before I met you, it's still justified after I met you). They've agreed in principle to write a joint document and I have to circulate a draft skeleton before the first meeting, so I need the actual artifact: not my summary of the debate, but text that both of them could put their names to without wincing.

Here's what I want it to contain.

1. The agreed claims, numbered. And I don't mean the trivial stuff — skip anything so thin that neither of them would ever have denied it. I mean the real common ground: that disagreement is a genuine piece of evidence and not just a psychological fact about two heads; that there's some case where both of them agree you should move a lot (I'm thinking of the jointly-botched restaurant bill, where you both know you're unreliable at mental arithmetic); that "peer" is a matter of degree rather than a switch that flips on; and, if you can get them to sign it, that the standard disputed-calculation setup is a bad model for the cases they actually care about, because in the interesting cases there's no independent check to appeal to afterwards.

2. A disagreements section as a table, where the conciliationist's formulation and the steadfast theorist's formulation sit side by side and each one is worded so that that side would accept it as their own view — not the opponent's caricature of it. If some row collapses into a fight over words (justification vs rationality vs permissibility, or "must change your mind" vs "must change your confidence"), say which row and why, and then give the substantive version underneath.

3. The cruxes, and for each one a case or argument both of them accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down before the fact. I have hunks of what I think the cruxes are, but tell me if I've got them wrong:

- The closed-room case. Two people with the same training, handed the same evidence packet, allowed unlimited joint deliberation in which every consideration either of them has goes on the table, and they still come out at different credences. The conciliationist wants to say the survival of disagreement through all that is itself evidence of a fault somewhere in the pair. The steadfast theorist wants to say it's evidence of a brute, non-truth-tracked difference in starting points. Is there anything both accept would tell them which of those is happening?

- The scale question, which I never see addressed and which I think has to be settled before the rest can even be stated. Is conciliation supposed to be an operation on credences, on log-odds, or on the odds directly? Splitting the difference in credence and meeting in the middle in log-odds give different numbers, and they come apart more the further apart the two peers start. Whichever way each side answers, does the answer cost them anything — and is there a formulation of conciliationism that's neutral about the scale, or does the view secretly need one?

- The ratchet. If I conciliate towards you and you conciliate towards me and then we each learn that the other has conciliated, do we update again, and again? If yes, both of us walk to a compromise neither of us ever had reason to hold; if no, the conciliationist seems to be treating the fact of disagreement as evidence on the first pass but not the second. Both of them should write down what they say here and what they predict about it.

- Correlated error, which I think is where the real action is. Call an echo panel a group of peers whose mistakes are correlated because they were trained on the same sources or the same flawed model, and whose disagreement with you therefore carries less independent information than their number suggests. The conciliationist should say how much independent-error versus correlated-error disagreement there is in the cases they care about; the steadfast theorist should say whether their view gets more attractive as correlation goes up. Give me the jointly accepted test — I'd guess something like forecasting teams who see each other's numbers, plus the echo-panel variant — and each side's prediction about what it shows, plus what each of them would count as a result against their own side.

Two more things. Each of them has to write down, in their own words, what would count as evidence against their own position, and I want that in the document rather than left implicit. And I suspect at least one of the four cruxes is mostly verbal while another one is where the whole thing actually turns — tell me which is which, and don't hedge it into a mush.

Format: numbered agreed claims first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. Don't pad it with a lit review or any pointers to papers; the whole value here is in the wording, and I want to be able to send it to both of them as-is.
