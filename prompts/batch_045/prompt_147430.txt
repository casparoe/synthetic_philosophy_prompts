Writing a novel at the moment and one of my characters is close to a saint — gives away most of what she earns, is always on call for everyone, has basically no hobbies — and I want to write her as admirable but also slightly unlivable, without turning the book into a tract. A friend who did some philosophy told me the argument about "moral saints" goes like this: someone who is as morally good as possible would have to drop every non-moral project and passion, would end up bland, joyless and boring, and nobody would want to be that person or want their kids to be them; so morality shouldn't be the main thing a life is organized around, and we should all cut ourselves some slack. Is that fair to the argument? I dont want sources or a reading list. What I want: which parts someone who actually holds the view would accept, which parts they'd wince at, and whether my friend's last bit — the "so we can relax about morality" part — really follows or is a separate claim nobody argued for. And the bit that actually matters for my book: does the argument, taken on its own terms, really rule out a saint who is also a compelling character — say one whose personal projects happen to line up with what morality wants, or who genuinely enjoys the sacrifices — or does it only bite against a narrow version of the type? If the diagnosis is that her interests are all instrumental, is the fix to give her non-moral goods she values for their own sake, or does that just make her less of a saint?
