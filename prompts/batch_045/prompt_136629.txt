i keep bumping into the same sliding move in arguments and i have no word for it, so here goes.

Case one: a friend, arguing about whether some big thing in his life was "meant to be". I say that's just comforting, comfort isn't truth. He says: if believing it makes me a better dad and gets me out of bed, in what sense is it not true? I say, ok, so a belief turns true by being pleasant to hold? He says no, that's not what I mean, I mean the belief we'd end up at if inquiry went on forever with all the evidence in. Then ten minutes later he's back to "well, it works for me."

Case two: a meeting about which metric to adopt. Someone says "this is true for us" and she means the people in this room happen to accept it — but when I point out that most of the room was hired last year and might just be wrong, she defends it as if she'd meant the long-run thing, and then uses the authority of the long-run thing to shut down the question about the room.

Case three: someone arguing about free will, or about whether there's an external world, saying we couldn't deliberate / act at all without presupposing it, therefore it's true. That one feels like it's in the same family as the others but I can't quite line it up with either of the first two.

The thing I want a name for isn't just "people are sliding around." It's that these look like three or four genuinely different claims — (a) something about the effects of my holding the belief now, (b) something about where inquiry would land if it went on forever, (c) something about what you can't act without, (d) something about who happens to be in the room — and they all get advanced under one slogan, and the slide is always toward whichever one happens to answer the objection I just made. And it isn't equivocation in the ordinary sense, or at least I can't make it come out as equivocation: the same belief could be both useful-to-me-now and the one inquiry would converge on, so someone could consistently identify truth with either, and no single step in the argument is invalid. What breaks is the argument as a whole, and I don't know how to say what the breakage is.

They also disagree on things I care about: whether the same sentence can be true for me and false for you, or true now and false later; whether a claim can be true and permanently unknowable; whether truth can change.

So: is there an established concept for this family and for the slide between its members? Someone told me the two guys who started the tradition attached to the slogan "truth is what works" actually couldn't stand each other's version, and I have a feeling that maps onto my (a) and (b) — if so, which is which, and where do (c) and (d) belong: do they have names, are they somebody's actual view, or did I invent them? But what I really want is the carving rather than the label. What are the axes these differ along, which difference is the one that actually matters, are there members of the family I've missed (a version where the "use" accrues to a group rather than a person? a version where the long-run inquirers aren't ideal?), and above all: what single question can I ask mid-argument that forces the speaker to commit to one of them, so I can tell which one is doing the work?
