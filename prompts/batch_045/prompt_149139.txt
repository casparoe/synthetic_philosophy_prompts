I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of mathematics — mostly philosophers plus one set theorist who shows up intermittently — and I'm the only person in it without a mathematics degree. One member of the group has been pushing an argument about Skolem's paradox that I can't find the exit from, chiefly because every individual step looks like something I've seen asserted in a textbook or lecture handout. I'd very much rather not accept his conclusion, but I want to know what refusing it costs me, and so far the group has given me two incompatible diagnoses.

Here is the argument, more or less as he wrote it on the board:

(1) If a countable first-order theory has any infinite model at all, it has a countable model.
(2) ZFC is a countable first-order theory, and if it is consistent it has an infinite model.
(3) So there is a countable model M of ZFC. If we want M to be well founded, we can take a countable elementary submodel of a well-founded model and collapse it — that assumption is stronger than bare consistency, but harmless for the argument — so we may as well suppose M is a countable transitive set whose membership relation is the real membership relation restricted to it.
(4) Inside M, Cantor's theorem holds: M contains an object p that M takes to be the powerset of its natural numbers, and M satisfies the claim that there is no function from ω onto p.
(5) M is countable from the outside, so every element of M is countable from the outside, p among them; in particular a genuine surjection from the real ω onto p exists.
(6) Whether a set can be surjected onto from ω is an intrinsic feature of that set — a matter of how many members it has — not a matter of which model we happen to be viewing it from. The number of eggs in a box does not change depending on who is counting.
(7) So p is both countable and uncountable. Nothing about p changed between (4) and (5); only who was looking.
(8) Therefore the claim that there are uncountably many reals does not express a determinate proposition about a determinate subject matter. ZFC — and, by the same reasoning, any first-order theory — fails to pin down how many things it is talking about. Cardinality is relative to a model, and the intended interpretation of set theory is not something we can single out formally.

What bothers me is that (8) seems to prove considerably more than he wants, and in two directions at once. First, if the model is not required to be transitive, the same style of reasoning appears to make finiteness relative as well: a non-well-founded model can contain an object that it counts as a natural number and as finite, while from outside that object has infinitely many predecessors, so we would have to give up the determinacy of 'finite' and of 'natural number' too, which is far more than the paradox is normally advertised as showing. Second — and this is the part I keep coming back to — the statement in (5) that M is countable is itself a cardinality claim, evaluated in some background theory; if cardinality is relative, that claim is only true relative to a further model, which has its own countable model, and so on, and it is unclear whether the view can even be stated, let alone argued for. It is also unclear to me why the 'external' standpoint is supposed to be philosophically suspect when ZFC itself, together with the assumption that ZFC is consistent, proves that a countable model of ZFC exists.

The set theorist, when she was last there,offhandedly said that for a transitive model the situation is asymmetric: if such a model thinks a set is countable then the set really is countable, but if it thinks a set is uncountable the set may still be countable, because the bijection that witnessescountability need not belong to the model. I don't know whether that observation defuses the argument or merely relocates it, and I don't know whether it is even right as I've written it down.

So: which step should I reject? One person says the trouble is at (6); another says (6) is fine as stated and the real mistake is the slide from (5) and (6) to (7), or perhaps an equivocation buried earlier. I can't tell these apart. For what it's worth, I would like to keep (4) and (5) in some form — I don't want to give up Cantor's theorem about the intended reals, and I don't want to say that the countability of M is merely relative — but I can't say why (6) would be false rather than merely inconvenient for my friend, and I can't tell what rejecting it commits me to. If (6) or (7) is the right thing to give up, does doing so really leave Cantor's theorem with a determinate subject matter, or does it only relocate the indeterminacy into the notions of the real ω and the genuine powerset? And what would it cost to reject the earlier steps instead — for instance by insisting that the intended semantics for set theory is full second-order semantics, that the argument only gets a grip because first-order logic is the wrong logic, and that the familiar complaints about full second-order semantics (that its consequence relation is not recursively axiomatizable and that it covertly presupposes the very powerset notion at issue) are prices worth paying?
