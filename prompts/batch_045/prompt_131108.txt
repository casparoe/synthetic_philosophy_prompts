I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I am finishing a long piece on chronic pain: specifically, on what the badness of pain is supposed to consist in once you give up the thought that it simply tracks tissue damage. Before I file it, and before I go on a podcast to defend it, I would like an audit of the position I have ended up in. I suspect it does not all hang together, but I cannot locate the strain, and I would rather find out now than on air.

Here is the package, as I would put it to a colleague. I have numbered the commitments only so that you can refer back to them.

(1) Pain asymbolia is a genuine dissociation, and it refutes the sensory theory of pain's badness. An asymbolic patient registers the location, intensity and quality of a noxious stimulus and reports feeling the pain, while the pain no longer bothers them. So whatever makes pain bad cannot be the sensory-discriminative content alone; the badness has to be located in the affective or motivational dimension.

(2) I do not want unpleasantness as a primitive. I want it cashed out structurally: what makes pain sting is that it issues a command rather than a description. It presents some region of the body as to be protected, and it presents this with the felt authority of an order, not the inertness of a piece of information. That structural claim is the main reason I find the so-called imperative view attractive.

(3) Pain's badness has nothing to do with whether that command can be obeyed, or whether there is damage for it to be about. Terminal pain in a patient whose injury is beyond any protective action is as bad as anything gets, and I want to say exactly the same about chronic pain in which no ongoing damage can be found: it is not less bad for being aimless. The whole point of the distinction between hurt and harm, as I use it, is that badness does not ride on the damage.

(4) I hold an unexciting intentionalism about pain's content: pain represents the body as damaged. Phantom limb pain and damage-free chronic pain do not threaten that, because representations can misrepresent, and a pain's being inaccurate is one thing, its being bad another.

(5) On evidence: I want to take first-person report seriously as evidence of what experience is like, and I think the asymbolia literature would be worthless if I did not. But I also think report on its own cannot establish that a dissociation exists; I want behavioural and imaging convergence before I build an argument on one.

(6) A verdict on a case. Consider the mirror image of asymbolia: a state, which I have been assuming is at least coherent and possibly real, in which the distress is fully intact but no localisable or describable sensory quality is left — full affect, no sensory content. Call it acryptalgia. I think acryptalgia is straightforwardly as bad as ordinary pain is.

(7) A second verdict. Suppose a drug that left the sensory-discriminative content and the withdrawal reflex exactly as they are but abolished the felt authority of the command: you would still be able to say that it is burning, in the left forearm, seven out of ten; you would still flinch; and it simply would not matter to you. I think such a drug removes everything that matters about pain, and I would take it.

(8) A third verdict. Patients who have lived with severe chronic pain for years and report that it no longer bothers them are, I think, still being harmed by it. I do not think adaptation settles whether a state is bad for the person in it.

What I want is an audit, not an introduction. I know the area well enough not to need the background, and I am not asking for a survey of positions or for any reading. Please treat (1) and any empirical backdrop as given, and argue from the commitments themselves: where two of them cannot both be true as I have stated them, say so, and say what each needs that the other denies.

Two further constraints. Do not rest anything on citations, sources or empirical findings beyond what I have given you. And where my view needs a distinction I have not drawn, supply it and give it a name.

Please structure your response as follows, in British English, using these section headings in this order:

1. Bottom line (around 80 words). Does the package hold? If not, which single commitment is doing the damage.
2. Tensions, ranked by severity (around 450 words). The pairs or triples that genuinely pull against each other, worst first. For each: name the commitments involved, state precisely where the strain lies — what each commits me to that the other rules out — and then say what specifically would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me.
3. False alarms (around 200 words). The pairings that look inconsistent but survive contact, each with the move that dissolves the appearance.
4. Cheapest repair (around 200 words). The single revision that buys the most consistency for the least loss to the article I actually want to write, and what I would have to stop saying in print if I made it.
5. Interviewer's question (around 80 words). The one question a hostile interviewer could put to me that would expose the weakest joint in the set, and the shortest honest answer available to me.
