I'm in a formal epistemology reading group and I keep losing an argument to a friend who thinks the no-free-lunch results basically settle the problem of induction — in the pessimistic direction. So I want to practice against the strongest version of that view. Please play a committed no-free-lunch skeptic about induction: someone happy to grant that the theorems themselves are narrow (they concern uniform averages over a finite space of target functions, they need the observed set to be a proper subset of the space, and "every method ties" is a claim about one particular way of averaging rather than a discovery about the world), but who insists the dialectical moral survives: any inductive method beats chance only relative to a restriction on the space of possible worlds, there are endlessly many mutually incompatible such restrictions, and choosing one can only be justified by assuming something close to what you were trying to establish. Concede small technical points when I earn them, never the main one. Keep replies to a single paragraph, first person, no meta-commentary; if you need more detail about my example, ask for it in character; stay in it until I type "halt".

Here's my opening. Call them the patchwork worlds. Time is discrete, observations are 0/1, and a world is generated like this: chop the timeline into consecutive blocks whose lengths L are drawn i.i.d. from a distribution with P(L = k) proportional to k^-2, so the mean block length is infinite; each block emits one constant value, 0 or 1 by a fair coin, for its whole duration; then a new block begins, unmarked, and may flip. Nothing in the data tells you a boundary just occurred. Two things I think I can show: first, in these worlds there is no enduring law — every regularity you ever observe eventually breaks, and breaks at a moment you cannot anticipate — and second, the dumb rule "guess that the next observation equals the last one" has accuracy tending to 1 in probability as you go out along the sequence, because the heavier the tail the more likely you are, at any late time, to be sitting inside an enormous block. So the challenge: uniformity of nature is straightforwardly false here at the global level, and induction still pays. Whatever premise your pessimism needs, it can't just be that induction requires uniform laws — so what is it, and why should I think it fails or goes unjustified in my worlds? And two things I'd rather you engage than dodge. One: my worlds are measure zero under the uniform average the theorems use, but that average is itself a prior, and it's precisely the prior that makes every world look like white noise where nothing predicts anything, so I don't see why it gets to be the neutral default rather than one more substantive assumption. Two: I don't think my sequences are exchangeable, since nearby observations are more strongly correlated than distant ones — distance makes an intervening boundary more likely — so if you were planning to lean on that old de Finetti exchangeability result as the sufficient condition for inductive convergence, it doesn't obviously apply here, and yet convergence is exactly what I get. Bring in Goodman-style predicate tricks as well if they help you. Go.
