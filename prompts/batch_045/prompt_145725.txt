I work in RLHF and safety-constrained RL. My working vocabulary is Markov decision processes, reward models fit to pairwise human comparisons, constraints and chance constraints on catastrophic outcomes, CVaR and other risk-sensitive objectives, discounting, and the choice between adding several objectives into one scalar and keeping them ordered by priority instead. Philosophy, beyond what I have absorbed secondhand, I do not have.

The position I keep colliding with, in the version a friend defends, I will call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Stipulatively:

(1) Nothing is intrinsically good or bad except the valence of conscious experience.
(2) Pleasure and pain are not magnitudes on one scale. Once an episode of pain exceeds a fixed threshold of intensity and duration, no amount of pleasure — not any finite quantity, however large — compensates for it; above that threshold, pain is lexically prior to everything on the positive side.
(3) Below that threshold, painful episodes have zero disvalue. Not very little: none.
(4) So the ordering over outcomes is not a sum. It is a lexicographic one: assess worlds first for above-threshold pain, and consult pleasure only as a tie-break among worlds already tied on that first criterion.

My hunch is that this is engineering I already know: (2) is a hard constraint, or a veto on a set of trajectories; (3) is a clip or hinge at zero; and the "commensurability" dispute people make so much of is just the old argument about whether to collapse two objectives into one scalar or keep a priority ordering. But when I try to write (1)–(4) as an objective over trajectories plus a rule for aggregating across the population of users whose judgments I train on, three frictions appear, and I cannot tell whether they are artifacts of my vocabulary or real features of the view. Tentatively: a clip at zero has no gradient, so no quantity of sub-threshold pain ever registers even in astronomical numbers; a zero-tolerance constraint on catastrophe looks unsatisfiable under model uncertainty unless the policy has some exit action available, and I do not know whether recommending that exit is a feature of the view or a reductio of it; and valence is not in my state representation at all, so what my reward model sees is behaviour and reported judgment, not experience.

Please do the following.

First, translate (1)–(4) into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, mapping each clause onto something I can write down — trajectory, reward signal, constraint set, discounting, population aggregation — and saying explicitly where you are interpreting rather than translating.

Second, run the translation against three cases and report what it returns, not what it should return: (A) one system-managed agent in agony for decades versus 10^15 agents each receiving a small pleasure and a pinprick; (B) 10^20 pinpricks, no above-threshold pain, versus one episode of agony; (C) certainty of one agony versus a one-in-a-thousand chance of agony together with a large quantity of certain pleasure.

Third, the residue report: which clauses translate cleanly, which only with distortion you have to flag, and which have no counterpart in my framework at all — in particular whether the difference between a lexically prior objective and a scalar with a very large weight is even representable when everything is finite-precision, finite-sample and function-approximated; whether my reward signal is a proxy for pleasure or for something else, such as desire or approval; and whether aggregating over a fixed, already-existing set of annotators can express the view's answer to whose experience counts.

Close by saying, for each residue item, whether it indicts my vocabulary or the original position. Open with a single sentence on whether my hunch is right, wrong, or right about the mechanism and wrong about the significance. Structure the body as a numbered translation, then the three cases, then a three-column table (translates cleanly / translates with distortion / no counterpart). Total, table included, under 800 words.
