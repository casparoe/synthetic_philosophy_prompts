I audit a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind, and last week's session — Malcolm's *Dreaming* (1959) set against Dennett's paper asking whether dreams are experiences — broke down into an argument between two of the regulars, Ravi and Ines, that ran twenty minutes past the room booking. I was the only person in the room without a settled view, so they've asked me to referee, and I don't trust myself to do it: I kept agreeing with whoever had just spoken. I don't want a survey of the literature or suggestions for further reading — I can look those up myself. What I need is an argument-level adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened, which I've reconstructed below, slightly tidied and numbered for reference.

(1) Ravi opened with Malcolm's criterion point: the only criterion for the truth of I dreamt that p is the dreamer's own waking report, so the report does not describe an independent happening — in the relevant sense it constitutes the dream. That is why Malcolm's conclusion is not the absurd claim that dream reports are false, but the claim that there is nothing there for them to be true of; hence I am now dreaming is not the sort of thing that can be true or false, and dreaming is not undergoing an experience. (2) Ines replied that this conflates the evidential basis of our ascriptions with their truth-conditions: that reports are our only evidence does not make them constitutive. And the premise is false anyway — sleep laboratories can predict from physiology whether a subject woken at a given moment will report a dream, and can decode coarse content, so the report is not the only criterion. She also offered a time-lag analogy: an experience can occur, become inaccessible, and be recovered only when the subject is later cued; the lateness of the recovery does not date the experience at the moment of recovery. (3) Ravi answered that the decoding result gives a criterion for *reports*, not for experiences with content — the decoder is trained on reports, so physiology borrows its content from the very thing Malcolm says is criterial. The time-lag analogy fails as well: with an ordinary recovered memory you can check it against the world (there was a crash; you were there), whereas dream contents admit of no such check, which is precisely why Malcolm's conclusion is about dreams and not about memory in general. And the real claim, he said, is about the concept: experience-attributions are bound up with a practice in which the subject can avow or correct what is going on more or less as it goes on, and where that practice is absent the concept has nothing to latch onto. (4) Ines said that requirement is testable and apparently false: in lucid-dream experiments subjects execute prearranged tasks from inside REM sleep, signalling with deliberate eye-movement patterns, and because the instruction was fixed *before* sleep its content is specified independently of any later report — so there is a practice of concurrent avowal for at least some dreams. Besides, demanding that physiology supply content is a demand you could not meet for waking visual imagery either, and nobody concludes that imagery is not experiential; and the occurrence question is separable from the content question, so physiology can be a criterion of whether something is going on even if it cannot say what. (5) Ravi replied that the signalling argument is circular in a specific way: the correlation between the eye movements and the reported content is established only by pooling later reports, so you calibrate the signal with the reports and then certify the reports with the signal. The prearranged instruction fixes what the subject was to do, not whether anything was undergone in the doing; a purely dispositional story covers the behaviour. Worse for her, lucid dreams are a small, trained, unrepresentative subclass, so at best she has shown that *some* dreams are experiences. (6) Ines argued that the circularity is broken by design features rather than by the signal alone — naive subjects, novel instructions, cross-subject replication, task-sensitive sequences such as counting and then signalling, whose fine-grained structure no dispositional story predicts — and she conceded the representativeness worry. But then she changed ground: none of this, she said, is a conceptual question at all. It is an empirical question about which process-story best explains the data, and it may have no determinate answer: a story on which the narrative is composed all at once at the moment of awakening and laid down directly as a memory (the cassette picture) and a story on which it is improvised in real time through the night may fit every possible observation equally well. If that is right, Malcolm gains nothing, since his argument would have to show that the concept of experience settles the matter, and it does not.

At this point the instructor interrupted with a stipulation and told us to take it as true and argue from it. Call it the late-commit finding: (i) coarse dream content can be decoded well above chance from neural activity recorded roughly forty minutes before awakening; (ii) two to four seconds before every awakening that yields a report, there is a stereotyped hippocampal–cortical *commit event*; (iii) a drug that selectively abolishes the commit event abolishes dream reports entirely while leaving the earlier decoded patterns intact; (iv) the commit event occasionally fires spontaneously shortly before an unforced awakening, and is then followed by a report even though the earlier patterns were absent; (v) all of this holds for wakenings from NREM sleep too, where the reports are much shorter and more thought-like than the long REM narratives. (7) Ravi took this as the cassette picture with the scaffolding removed: the drug result shows the report being manufactured at the commit event, the earlier activity being only a disposition to manufacture it, so dream time is effectively zero. And he offered a dilemma: if the earlier activity really is an experience, then when the drug removes the commit event an experience is going on that is, by Malcolm's own criterion, not a dream — a dreamless experience — which is a worse position than the one Ines started from. (8) Ines replied that (iii) is exactly what you would expect if the commit event is a consolidation or retrieval gate, a necessary condition of later access rather than of the experience itself; and she pointed out the symmetry — for every experiment that makes the late event look like the cause of the report there is an equally good description of it as the last stage of access to something earlier, with no observable difference between the two. That, she said, is the indeterminacy claim made concrete instead of gestured at. In passing she added that she also wondered whether the short NREM reports in (v) are the same kind of thing as the REM narratives at all, but she did not pursue it. (9) Ravi said that if there is genuinely no observable difference, then the felt item is a gratuitous extra and we should say so: consider a heavily sedated patient who shows the full pattern and then dies without ever waking to report anything. On his view there is nothing to worry about on that patient's behalf beyond the neural disturbance and the effects on whatever waking life follows. If you want to say perhaps they suffered, you have no way of making that either true or false — and that inability just is Malcolm's conclusion. (10) Ines said this is not neutral bookkeeping: if dreams are not experiences, then a recurring nightmare is not something that happens *to* the dreamer but only something that happens afterwards, when they wake frightened and remember it, whereas clinical practice — treating trauma-related nightmares, sedating patients through nightmare-producing procedures — is premised on the nightmare itself being bad for the person undergoing it. A view that has to rewrite that has a cost, and Ravi has not paid it; he has offered a redescription and called it an economy.
