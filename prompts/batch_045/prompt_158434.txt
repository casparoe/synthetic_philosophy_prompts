Two friends of mine got into an argument at a barbeque and I've been thinking about it since. One of them works in a synthetic biology lab, the other does philosophy of biology; I do neither, so I got elected referee and I honestly dont know. Here's the case and roughly what they said.

The lab "printed" an organism — a cave salamander assembled from a genome written on a computer, no ancestors, no population, no evolutionary history at all. It's a molecular duplicate of a real cave-dwelling species that has a small light-sensitive patch on top of its skull wired into the brain. In the real species, that patch ancestrally triggered a seasonal migration; in the cave population that migration is long gone, and the patch now feeds into a circuit that keeps the animal out of warm surface water. Also relevant: nobody designed the printed salamander. The genome came out of an optimization run that searched millions of candidates and kept the ones whose simulated animals survived longest — nothing in the process ever represented "thermoregulation."

(1) Nadia: the printed animal's patch has a function — detecting light and steering it away from warm water. That's just what it does. History is irrelevant; nobody would say a carburetor built yesterday has no function because it lacks an ancestry.

(2) Wes: functions need a story about why the part is there — either selection or a designer's intention. Your carburetor gets its function from someone who intended it to mix fuel and air. The printed patch has neither: no selection history, and the file wasn't written with that job in mind. So it has effects but no function.

(3) Nadia: fine, then explain the real cave salamanders. Their patch was selected for triggering migration and can't do that anymore. On your account its function is something it no longer does, while the useful thing it actually does now isn't its function. No working biologist talks that way — they'd say it now functions as a thermal sensor.

(4) Wes: that's the co-option case, and it shows these are two different notions, not that mine is wrong. Mine explains why the part is there and handles malfunction: a heart that can't pump still has the function of pumping blood. Yours can't say that, and it over-generates — the heart also makes thumping noises; are those functions?

(5) Nadia: over-generation is handled: you only count contributions to the capacity you're analyzing, and nobody analyzing circulation cares about noises. And on malfunction — "supposed to pump" is exactly the normative bit your view is built to explain. You can't use it as evidence for your side; it's what we're arguing about.

(6) Nadia: worst case for you. Tomorrow a mutation lets some salamander's patch do thermoregulation slightly better. It's a real advantage right now, in this individual, and no selection has happened yet. On your account it has no function, and it acquires one only after a few generations of other animals doing better. Same organ, same effect, same advantage, flipping from "no function" to "function" because of what happens to its relatives. That can't be right.

(7) Wes: or it's just true, and functions are historical properties like being a scar. And your optimizer case cuts my way: the search criterion was survival, so at most the patch was selected for promoting survival, not for thermoregulation specifically. The fine-grained claim needs a history of selection for that specific thing.

Can you go through the seven moves and say, for each, whether it's basically right, partly right, or wrong, and why in a sentence or two — then give me a short bottom line on who's more right overall and what they're both missing? Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
