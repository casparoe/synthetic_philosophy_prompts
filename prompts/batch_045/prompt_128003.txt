For the next episode of *The Third Variable*, a podcast about how quantitative evidence makes its way into medicine and public policy, I'm recording an hour with Dr. Naomi Reinholt, a statistician whose recent essays argue that "Simpson's paradox" is a misnomer. On her view, no arithmetical reversal is ever paradoxical, because a reversal is a property of a table *together with* a question: until you fix a causal structure and a target population, "which table is right?" has no determinate answer. She also holds that the pooled figure is not a corrupted version of the truth but an estimate of a different and much less often wanted quantity, and that once you say what you want — an effect in which population, conditioning on what — the paradox evaporates and only estimation difficulty remains. In conversation she tends to fend off pressure with some variant of "that's a question about the estimand, not about the data," and I'd like to be ready for that move rather than bowled over by it.

I'll have one worked example on screen (we record video). A hospital network runs an optional post-discharge "Transitions" program; the outcome is readmission within thirty days. Program patients: 600 in total, 156 readmitted (26.0%) — comprising 120 "robust" patients with 12 readmissions (10.0%) and 480 "frail" patients with 144 readmissions (30.0%). Usual care: 1,100 in total, 202 readmitted (18.4%) — comprising 880 robust with 132 readmissions (15.0%) and 220 frail with 70 readmissions (31.8%). So the program looks better within each frailty stratum and worse overall. I want to run the example twice: once with the frailty score computed at admission, and once with the score partly determined by a variable recorded seven days after discharge that the program itself encourages.
