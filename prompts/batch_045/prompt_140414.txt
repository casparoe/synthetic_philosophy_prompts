I run a small reading group with a few grad students on AI welfare and machine consciousness, and we keep getting stuck on the same narrow thing: whether an RL agent's reward signal (or the temporal-difference error, or the value function, depending on who's talking) could literally be valence — whether suffering could just consist in being at, or tracking, the negative end of that signal. Half the group holds something like this: no reward-and-value machinery, no valence, full stop, and everything else we find impressive — pain behaviour, escape, tradeoffs, giving up — is engineers projecting. The other half holds that valence is a functional role and the reward signal is at best one implementation of it: a state counts as suffering if its produced by something like damage or threat to the system's integrity, grabs control of action selection, drives avoidance and escape learning, gets traded off against other things the system does, and generalises to novel situations. And then there's a third position I secretly find most attractive, which is that suffering is frustration of what the system effectively wants, where "what it wants" means whatever its policy is best interpreted as trying to bring about, not necessarily the reward.

What I want from you is a case, because Ive been failing to build one myself. I need a scenario where the first and third views both say the system does not suffer and the second view says it does — that's the direction I find hard, since every time I try, either I smuggle a reward signal in somewhere (and then the first view flips) or the behaviour stops looking aversive at all (and then the second view flips). Constraints: something you could actually build or train now, no sci fi — a warehouse robot arm, a quadruped, a game-playing agent, whatever, but no AGI, no uploads, no exotic physics, and no stipulating either "now imagine there is something it is like" or "obviously there's nothing its like". The scenario itself should be one paragraph. And I want the disagreement to turn on the mechanism rather than on the system being too simple or too dim: the pain-role criteria should genuinely be satisfied — threat or integrity detection, control hijack, avoidance learning, tradeoffs against other objectives, transfer to new contexts — while there is no scalar reward being maximised anywhere and no frustrated goal. If it helps as a starting point, the version I keep circling is some kind of homeostatic or self-model-based monitor that isn't part of any objective and was trained by imitation or self-supervision rather than by reward, but every version of that I write either turns the error signal into a reward with the serial numbers filed off or makes the thing look like it just doesn't care.

What I'd like back: the scenario, then a short walkthrough for each of the three views — what it says, and which clause of the case is giving it that verdict — then an honest note on which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the three verdicts if you remove it, and finally how someone holding the reward-as-valence view would attack the case (my guess is the "that's just a reward signal renamed" objection, but I want to see how strong it really is and whether it's fatal). This is for the reading group: the point is to find out whether the reward-as-valence person is committed to saying something implausible about ordinary control systems, or whether the functionalist is the one doing the relabelling, so please dont build a case that works by stipulating the verdicts or by making one side look stupid — and if the specs genuinely can't be met jointly, tell me that instead, and tell me which constraint has to give and why.
