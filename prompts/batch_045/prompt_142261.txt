I teach a second-year decision theory course — rational choice, expected utility, a bit about risk — about 18 students, one 75-minute session, in a room with tables that can be pushed into four clusters. We did the St. Petersburg game last week and it went exactly as well as these things ever go: they can all tell me the expectation is infinite, they can all tell me nobody would pay more than a few bucks for it, and then they can all recite the three or four standard moves (diminishing marginal utility of money, the house has to have a finite bankroll, people weight small probabilities nonlinearly, and one of them had read something about averaging over time instead of over possible players). So they have the answers but they have not felt the problem. What I want is an activity where the gap between what the number says and what they would actually do is something they run into with chips in their hands, before it gets named. No lecture up front, and please do not have the facilitator say the words expected value until everyone has already bid.

Here is roughly the shape I have in mind, but fix it if you have something better. Four tables, four versions of the doubling game, and students circulate with a fixed stake of play money whose only real cash value is two or three extra-credit points on the next problem set — small, but enough that they care. Currency is points, no real money changes hands. One version I would especially like built in: the four tables share a single visible vault, and every payout drains it, so a game played late in the session is riskier than the same game played early and the house can visibly default partway through — I want them to notice that the value of a play seems to depend on where you are in the sequence, not just on the rules of the game. I would also like at least one table where the house cap is real but hidden in a sealed envelope drawn from a distribution I show them, so they are bidding under ambiguity rather than risk, and at least one table where they commit to a policy (play every round for the rest of the session, no matter what) instead of choosing play by play. Elicit willingness to pay with sealed bids rather than a show of hands, or run it as an auction if that gets cleaner numbers. Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and what each table is for.

The debrief is the part I most need help with. I want it to map specific moments onto specific pieces of the dialectic — the moment when someone who bid high busts and someone who bid low gets lucky should not just be a funny anecdote, it should be the hook for asking whether the two of them made different mistakes. I want them to arrive at the point that each of the standard fixes dissolves the paradox partly by changing the question rather than answering it, and I want them to see the difference between the value of a single play and the value of a policy of playing, without me handing them that sentence. One of my students also claimed last week that the expectation of a game like this is not even well defined, because the sum depends on what order you add the terms up in, and I waved it off at the time and I am now not sure I was right to — if the activity can put some pressure on that, great. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: someone bids zero as a joke, someone tries to hedge across tables, the vault math takes too long, a coin lands on the floor, the whole thing collapses into a discussion about whether extra credit is really money. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce — I assume the actual tail is unstageable, since nobody is going to flip thirty heads in a row, and that the fake currency aggregates across a semester in a way that a life does not — and say what the facilitator will just have to say out loud at the end because no set of rules will produce it.
