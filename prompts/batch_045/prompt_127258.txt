I'm taking an advanced seminar on the ethics of consent this term, and I've hit a problem with the deception condition that I can't see my way past.

We're working with the familiar tripartite account: consent is valid only if the person is competent, not coerced, and not deceived about anything material to her decision. My professor fixes materiality by a reasonable-person standard rather than by the patient's own values, and she insists that the counterfactual question — whether the person would still have consented had she been told the truth — is simply irrelevant to validity.

I want to press the claim that it isn't irrelevant. Consider a patient in a hospital trust that has recently switched to a modular digital consent system: before a procedure she ticks boxes for each separable component — type of anaesthetic, whether tissue samples may be retained for research, whether a trainee may assist. Suppose she ticks the box declining trainee assistance, that a trainee in fact assists because of a rostering error which the surgical team notices and says nothing about, and — the part I keep coming back to — that when asked afterwards she says she would not have minded in the least and would have ticked the box the other way had she realised there was a choice about it. She received the operation she wanted, performed to the standard she expected, and she says she is glad it happened.

My worry is this. If her consent was invalid, then on the account we have been given the surgeon has done something in the same normative category as operating on someone who refused outright. That seems a wild verdict about a case in which the patient authorised the operation, was benefited by it, and does not object. So it looks to me as though validity has to track whether the deception actually cost her anything or deprived her of any control — which is just to say that the materiality requirement is the counterfactual test in disguise: deception vitiates consent exactly when the person's consent depended on the falsehood. Is that the right way to understand it? And if not, how am I supposed to avoid the conclusion that a lie about something she did not care about converts a beneficial operation into a battery?
