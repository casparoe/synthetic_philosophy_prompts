I have been asked to contribute a short position paper to a philosophy of mind reading group that has just finished working through the Malcolm/Dennett exchange on whether dreams are experiences, and I would like the position audited for coherence before I let anyone read it. I come to it late and from outside: thirty-odd years recording sleep in a physiology laboratory, and philosophy only in the last few years. Here is where I have landed.

(1) I reject the verificationist strand in this debate. Whether a subject is undergoing an experience at a given time is not settled by what anyone — the subject included — could in principle establish. There can be an experience that no one can confirm and no one can rule out.

(2) Accordingly, I hold directly that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep, and the evidence I would cite is not verbal: lucid subjects who agree in advance on a distinctive pattern of eye movements and whose signals appear in the record during unambiguous REM, and patients with dream-enactment disorder whose behaviour during REM corresponds to what they later describe.

(3) But I take the picture of a stored reel of imagery, replayed on waking, to be wrong. A night's mentation is not already divided into the discrete episodes that reports describe; the segmentation, and much of the detail, is contributed at waking.

(4) So I want a positive account of content, and this is the part I constructed myself rather than inherited. Call the content of a dream whatever is assigned by the best-fitting neural trajectory, where the fit is constrained jointly by the physiological record, the waking report, and the stability of reported content across nights, and where no one of these sources has priority in principle. I like it because it seems to let me say both that there is a fact of the matter about what was dreamt and that reports are not authoritative.

(5) Methodologically, then: first-person authority has no special standing. A report is one measurement among several and may be overruled by the other measurements.

(6) On that basis I hold that the subject who is awakened from unambiguous REM and reports nothing is generally someone who has forgotten a dream, not someone who had no experience; the mismatch between physiology and report shows report failure rather than absence.

(7) I also think that the word experience, as it functions in this dispute, is a theoretical posit of a science still being built, whose extension is fixed by the explanatory work it does rather than by the criteria governing ordinary talk of dreaming. I take this to be why the older argument has proved so stubborn: it has been conducted as though there were a settled ordinary concept for the science to test itself against.

(8) Relatedly, I want to say that Malcolm's grammatical point — that a remark such as "I dreamt that..." is not a report of an observation — is correct, and that it costs me nothing, since it is a claim about our language and not about consciousness.

(9) Finally, I do not think dream consciousness raises any difficulty beyond the general difficulty about consciousness. I am a physicalist and I am content to leave the matter there.

What I would like is an audit rather than a summary. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which are only apparently so? I am fairly confident that (2) and (3) do not conflict, but I cannot see my way through the rest, and I would rather be told which joints are load-bearing than be given a general opinion on the topic. Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equals; for each, say what exactly would have to be revised and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; note which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why; and name the single change that would repair the most. I have no settled view about which item to give up, and I should mention that (4) is my own construction, though I do not know whether that makes it more or less likely to be where the trouble is.
