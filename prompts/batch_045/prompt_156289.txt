I write distributed systems and large-scale agent-based simulations for a living, and I have no formal training in philosophy; the little social theory I know I picked up because my team models marketplaces and urban systems. There is a standing argument on that team I would like converted into a document.

One side (call them the micro-only camp) holds that a claim about a group, a market, or an institution is explanatory only if it can be cashed out as a claim about the states of individual people — their beliefs, incentives, and resources — together with the local interactions among them. On that view, a statement about market liquidity, institutional legitimacy, or a neighborhood's segregation pattern is at best a convenience: a compressed way of referring to a very large number of individual facts, useful for the same reason a database index is useful, but not itself a description of anything extra. The other side (call them the system-level camp) holds that some predicates attach only to the whole and do real causal work: the same individuals, with the same preferences and the same decision rules, will behave differently under a different pattern of interaction or a different institutional history, and no description restricted to individuals plus their pairwise relations can say why. They have been at this for two years and neither has moved, which makes me think they are partly answering different questions under one heading.

What I can contribute from my side of the building is a class of tests that field researchers mostly cannot run. In a simulation we can hold the individual decision rules fixed and swap only the interaction topology; hold the topology fixed and swap only the distribution of rules; replay a single run with one individual's parameters perturbed and measure how far the trajectory diverges; and, for any macro property M a modeler cares about, compare the best predictor that is forbidden to use M as an input against one that may, with unlimited data and compute. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that this last question is where the real content lies and that it is formal rather than sociological: either the evolution of the system is a function of individual states plus local interaction structure plus update rules, with no term ranging over the macro state itself, or it is not, and the gap is measurable. A second hypothesis: a good part of the rest is verbal, because "explanation" is being used in two senses — one on which a mere compression explains, since it lets you predict and intervene, and one on which only the generating mechanism explains. A third: some of the apparent dispute is about which cases count as data, since each camp keeps producing the kind of example its own account handles.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Please include: (1) a numbered section of claims both would accept, including about the case we actually argue about — a residential-segregation model of the familiar kind, where agents mildly prefer not to be in a small local minority — and about what my swap, replay, and ablation tests can and cannot establish; (2) a disagreements table, each row giving the disputed claim, the formulation of it that each camp would accept as their own position, your judgment whether that row is verbal or substantive, and the crux it turns on; (3) for each crux, the test, formal result, or case both camps would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and a note on what each would count as evidence against their own view; and (4) a short closing note on what would count as progress here, or an argument that none should be expected. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is ill-posed rather than hard, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over.
