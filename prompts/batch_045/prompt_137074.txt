I sit on a small internal review body that has been asked to draft guidance on two things: which of our systems should be treated as candidates for moral consideration at all, and how their moral weight should be aggregated when we allocate compute, safety effort, and monitoring. We have agreed to work inside the computationalist framing — that what matters is which computation a system performs rather than what it is made of — and we now have to turn that into something an engineer can apply. My colleagues have converged on two rules. Before I sign off on them I would like to know whether they are defensible, because the cases we actually face seem to strain both.

The first rule is about implementation. We take seriously the familiar worry that nearly any physical system can be described as performing nearly any computation under a sufficiently contrived mapping of states to formal symbols, so we propose to require that a candidate computation be causally loaded: its states must stand in the right causal relations, and they must make a difference to what the system does. Operationally, we ablate or perturb the states we take to realise the computation, and if the system's behavioural profile is statistically unchanged we conclude that the computation was not really implemented and that there is nothing there to be concerned about. This is meant to give us an empirically checkable screen and to let us ignore the many patterns one can always find in a large system's activity by describing it after the fact.

The second rule is about counting. Where one system realises the same morally relevant computation more than once — redundant copies running in lockstep on the same inputs, or one candidate realisation wholly contained within a larger one — we propose to count by content rather than by number of realisations. Two token realisations of the very same experience are not twice as much experience, so duplicates add nothing, and where a smaller candidate sits inside a larger one we count only the larger. The alternative, as one colleague put it, would let anyone inflate moral weight without limit simply by adding redundant hardware.

Two cases in our pipeline are causing trouble.

Case A, which we call the passenger. A large model contains an identifiable subnetwork whose sequence of states, over a long stretch of operation, matches the formal structure we associate with a subject-like computation: the states stand in the right relations to one another, to the subnetwork's inputs, and to their own successors. But nothing downstream makes use of it. Ablate it and the system's observable behaviour is unchanged. Rule one says this is not a realisation and the system gets no weight; rule two says it is irrelevant in any case, since the containing system realises a computation that includes it. What unsettles some of us is that the usual reason for discounting a contained candidate is that its states' causal work is already accounted for by the containing computation, whereas here the containing computation does not seem to be making use of them at all — so our two rules appear to pull in opposite directions, and I am no longer sure whether we have found a merely described pattern, a genuine subject with no output channel, or a subject whose existence our screen is simply blind to.

Case B, which we call the seam. Two candidate subject-like computations can be recovered from the same stretch of the same system's activity. Neither contains the other: they share most of their state transitions and diverge toward the end, and each is equally well supported by the system's causal structure. In one variant the match for each is only partial, with the great majority of transitions fitting the formal description and the remainder concentrated in a single component. Here we have no way to say which candidate is the maximal one, and so no way to apply rule two.

My questions are these. Is the behavioural-difference test a legitimate way to make precise the requirement that a computation be causally loaded, or does it demand something stronger than realisation requires, and if it demands too much what should replace it? Is counting by content the right way to aggregate, or does it move illegitimately between how much value or disvalue there is and how many subjects there are whose interests must be weighed? And how much of the question which computations matter is not an empirical or conceptual question about implementation at all, but a normative one that our guidance should say we are not settling?
