I spent most of my career as a fisheries geneticist working on stock identification in salmonids, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group, where I have got into an argument I cannot settle. It concerns what heritability does and does not tell you, and a younger philosopher in the group has been pushing a line of reasoning that I find persuasive step by step and incredible as a whole. I would like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I don't want references — I can find those myself.

Let me fix a toy example so that we are not trading on anyone's memories of IQ or height. In a laboratory stock of a flour beetle, consider a trait I will call frons width, and suppose its narrow-sense heritability, h² = V_A/V_P, is 0.9. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it (my own numbering). (1) h² is a ratio of two components of the phenotypic variance in one population living in one range of environments; it is a statistic about a population, not about the individuals in it and not about a trait as such. (2) Being a ratio, it is silent about the mean: my beetles could owe essentially all of their mean frons width to the lab diet, since if every larva gets the same diet the environment contributes nothing to V_P, and the additive genetic variance can then be 90 percent of whatever variance is left. Equally, if the stock were genetically uniform at the relevant loci, h² would be 0 even though the trait is as genetic as you please. (3) So h² = 0.9 licenses no prediction about what any intervention would do. A uniform dietary change that adds 0.4 mm to every beetle leaves V_A, V_P and hence h² exactly as they were, while every individual's frons width is thoroughly counterfactually dependent on the environment. Call the ordered list of population means this stock would display across a specified set of environments its intervention-profile; my colleague's point is that h² pins down no element of that profile. (4) Nor, for the same reason, does h² say anything about the cause of a difference between two populations: two stocks differing only in larval density could each have h² = 0.9 while all of the difference between their means is environmental. This is the step I most want to keep; I use it constantly against people who read group differences straight off heritability coefficients. (5) But — and here is where I lose my grip — if all the work is being done by the fact that h² is a variance ratio in a population, that fact does no less damage inside the population than across populations. The partition is a bookkeeping device; whether it reflects the real causes of differences among these beetles depends on the causal assumptions of the model (no genotype–environment correlation, no interaction folded into the wrong term, random mating, and so on), and those are precisely what is in dispute and precisely what the usual designs cannot secure by themselves. So the number licenses no causal conclusion even about the individual differences it was computed from. (6) Which cannot be right. Quantitative genetics was built to predict the response to selection, and the breeder's equation — response equals h² times the selection differential — works, and works better when h² is high; breeders have been cashing predictions like that for a century. If h² licensed no causal conclusion at all, not even in the population where it is measured, that success would be a miracle and the concept would be empty.

So the argument seems to prove too much: a deflation that begins by protecting us from bad inferences about interventions and about group differences ends by making the whole enterprise vacuous. My colleague proposes to escape by distinguishing h²-as-partition from h²-as-predictor and saying the two can come apart, but I cannot tell whether that dissolves the problem or merely relocates it. I would rather keep (1), (2) and (4) than give up (6). Tell me which step or which transition is the faulty one, why it looked all right when I wrote it down, and — briefly — what each of the other ways out would cost me. Please keep your answer under 400 words: one sentence naming the step, then the diagnosis, then the price of the alternatives.
