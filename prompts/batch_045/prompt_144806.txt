You are a professional philosopher of science who works on classification, natural kinds, and the metaphysics of the special sciences. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the full space of interpretations before narrowing to a list.

I'm a field biologist who now curates the data model for a large biodiversity monitoring network built mostly from volunteer observations. We are arguing about whether records should be organized around Linnaean taxa or around functional guilds like nocturnal nectarivore or canopy seed disperser. Guilds cross-cut phylogeny, group together lineages as distant as bats, hawkmoths and honeyeaters, and are what most of our users actually want to make predictions about. At the last meeting a senior colleague closed the discussion with: classification should carve nature at its joints — as though that settled the matter in favour of taxa. Fifteen years of comparable observations ride on how we decide, so I need to know what that sentence could really be claiming.

Give me a menu of the distinct precise theses carve nature at its joints might express, ordered from the thinnest to the boldest: the near-vacuous reading on which any grouping whose members share a property counts, cluster/density-of-similarity readings, causal or homeostatic-property-cluster readings, projectibility and law-support readings, reference-fixing or truth-tracking readings, convergence-of-ideal-inquiry readings, and readings on which the joints sit at one privileged level of structure. For each, tell me whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or false or empty; whether it requires kinds to form a partition (mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive) or can tolerate overlap, hierarchy and cross-cutting levels; and whether it implicitly privileges fundamental physics over the special sciences.

Two things I care about especially. First: which readings, if any, would actually support my colleague's inference to taxa rather than guilds — and which would license guilds, or both, or neither, once you notice that guild membership supports its own inductions about trait distributions and responses to habitat change? Second: I have started using a rough distinction of my own — a joint is a place where nature comes apart, a seam is a division we can draw and use that tracks no break in causal structure. On which readings is that distinction real and load-bearing, and on which does it collapse into a merely verbal one? Rank the readings by how much work each would do in that meeting, and flag any that have been insulated against disagreement by becoming purely verbal.
