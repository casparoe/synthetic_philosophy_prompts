I am writing up my position on decision-making under undefined expected value for a decision theory seminar, and before I present it I would like the package checked for coherence. The games I am working with, stated so we agree on them: in the Pasadena game a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads falls on toss n, you receive 2^n/n dollars if n is odd and lose 2^n/n dollars if n is even. Summing the probability-weighted payoffs in the order n = 1, 2, 3, ... gives the alternating harmonic series and hence ln 2 dollars, but the positive and negative parts of the series each diverge, so the expectation itself is undefined and the series can be rearranged to converge to any value you like. Pasadena-plus-a-dollar is the same game with one dollar added to the payoff in every state.

Here is where I have landed:

(a) Where expected utility is well defined and finite, I maximize it. For ordinary monetary stakes I use a mildly concave utility in money, but I do not think risk attitudes are where the interesting action is here.

(b) Total utility is bounded, above and below. This is my main defence against fanaticism: it is what gives the St. Petersburg game a finite value and what lets me refuse Pascal's mugger. I would rather give up unboundedness than learn to live with fanatical conclusions.

(c) Some gambles nonetheless have genuinely undefined expected utility, and this is a real problem for decision theory rather than a curiosity of idealization. The Pasadena game is my paradigm case.

(d) When expected utility is undefined I fall back on statewise dominance: offered Pasadena or Pasadena-plus-a-dollar at the same price, I take the latter, and I take this to be determinately correct rather than merely permissible.

(e) Whatever principle handles these cases must be invariant under redescription of the state space: relabelling the states, or listing them in another order, cannot change what I ought to do. So I do not accept the natural order of summation — or truncation at a large n, or discounted/Abel summation — as canonically privileged merely because each of them happens to return ln 2.

(f) For gambles that can be repeated I fall back on the long-run average per play. My understanding is that for independent Pasadena plays the running average payoff per play converges in probability to ln 2, and on that basis I would advise a fund able to buy thousands of such gambles at fifty cents each to buy them.

(g) I also have fairly determinate one-shot verdicts: I would pay roughly sixty-nine cents for a single play of the Pasadena game, and roughly a dollar sixty-nine for a single play of Pasadena-plus-a-dollar.

(h) I do not think one-shot choice should be governed by long-run considerations: that a bet is favourable in the long run is not by itself a reason to take it once.

(i) I do not want a fix that works only for money. The same structure seems to me to arise for any unbounded good — lives saved, planets colonized — so a money-specific dissolution would not satisfy me.

(j) Where a determinate verdict can be had honestly, I prefer it to a permission: I treat "rationality is silent here" as a last resort rather than an achievement.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through this set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, and do not treat them all as equally serious: rank the real tensions by severity. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give, what the cheapest available repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set — in particular, which of my anti-fanaticism or anti-indulgence ambitions it undermines. For the merely apparent tensions, say briefly why they dissolve. Then tell me whether there is one revision that cleans up more than the others, and whether any commitment here is doing no work at all. If any item rests on a mathematical claim that is false or only half true, say so plainly rather than working around it.
