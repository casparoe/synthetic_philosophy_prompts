I have just come away from a long argument with an old friend — she is a geriatric psychiatrist, I am not a philosopher — about whether my mother's advance directive should be honored now, and I want to understand why neither of us moved the other by so much as an inch.

The case, briefly. My mother signed a detailed directive at sixty-five, in the mild early stage of what turned out to be Alzheimer's, refusing artificial nutrition and hospitalization for infection. She is now seventy-four, severely demented, unable to recognize us, apparently content, and she eats with pleasure when she is fed. My friend and I agree about every fact that is usually treated as decisive: we both accept the reductionist account of personal identity, on which identity just consists in overlapping psychological continuity and continuity admits of degree; we agree that on that account she is continuous with the woman who signed; we agree she is not suffering; and we agree that she is not better off, by the standards she held at sixty-five, in the condition she now refuses to be kept in.

My friend holds that the earlier self's word has no authority beyond that of a well-placed default: the earlier self knew the patient's values at first hand, had no conflict of interest, and cannot be suspected of weakness of will, so her document is the best available evidence about what should be done — but it is evidence, and evidence can be overridden, and here, she says, it is overridden. I hold that the document binds anyway: that a person has a normative power to settle in advance what is to be done within the boundaries of her own life, and that the exercise of that power is not answerable to how things turn out for the self who later inherits it.

Two things came up that I think locate the stalemate. First, the conversion case: at thirty, a devout Jehovah's Witness signs a transfusion refusal; at sixty, after a documented and reflective deconversion, lucid and unchanged in every other psychological respect, he asks for blood. My friend says the sixty-year-old governs; to my surprise I agreed. So temporal priority as such is not what either of us cares about, and yet in my mother's case we still divide.

Second, a variant I made up on the spot, which I think is the real test. Suppose that at seventy, with her dementia moderate but her competence intact and her sense of herself unchanged, my mother had executed a second instrument revoking the first: Everything I wrote at sixty-five was written by a woman who was frightened of a future she could not picture. I have now lived two years inside that future. Set the earlier document aside. Assume the revocation is informed, uncoerced, and not in any clinically demonstrable way a symptom. Now one life has issued two instruments, each purporting to govern the same incompetent stage. My friend, I think, must say the later one governs because it is later. I want to say it governs too, but not because it is later — and I could not say, to her satisfaction, what the difference was.

So: diagnose. My own hypothesis, which I would rather have tested than confirmed, is that the fight looks like a fight about identity and welfare, both of which we have neutralized, and is really a fight about what sort of thing autonomy is — a capacity exercised by whichever self is present, or a power to bind the whole of a life, a power whose validity does not depend on its effects on its bearer. On that reading my friend is not denying autonomy at all; she is working with another conception of it, and the two conceptions coincide everywhere except here. But I also want to know whether a disguised empirical or institutional question is doing the real work: whether one of us is leaning on an unstated guess about how clinicians actually use directives — as a defense against overtreatment or as a license to undertreat — or about what honoring them does to other people's willingness to write them, and would shift if that guess were corrected.

Is this a verbal dispute, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, two different questions pursued under one heading, or an empirical disagreement in disguise? Which of my formulations is doing illegitimate work? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction to be drawn, a case to be described, a finding to be gathered, or the abandonment of the question as ill-posed?
