I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship, and the committee wants a proposal of no more than about a thousand words that a non-specialist panelist can follow: the question stated precisely, why the existing positions leave it open, what I would actually do with the year, and a candid account of what would count as the project failing. My dissertation is on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and the fellowship year would fund the first of three planned papers.

The half-formed idea I keep circling is this. The received view is that the thesis cannot be proved, because it relates an informal notion (effective computability, algorithm, mechanical procedure) to a formal one, and that the evidence for it is the extraordinary convergence of every serious proposed explication of the informal notion on one and the same class of functions. I think the convergence argument is weaker than it looks. The later formalisms were constructed by people who already had earlier ones in hand, and a candidate was certified as an adequate analysis of 'mechanical procedure' partly by checking that it delivered the class everyone expected. If that is right, the agreement of the explications may be an artifact of how each was calibrated against its predecessors rather than evidence that they are all tracking an independent subject matter. I want the proposal to say whether that worry can be made precise enough to be assessed at all, and what follows for the status of the thesis if it survives.

Please turn this into a proposal, and make sure it does the following.

First, disambiguate the target before arguing about it. I want at least four theses kept apart: the narrow one linking the informal notion to the formalism; the bold or physical one, that no physical process computes anything a Turing machine cannot; the efficiency or feasible-computability one; and a fourth, normative one that I suspect is where the real action is — the thesis that a proof of uncomputability gives a researcher conclusive reason to stop looking, so that calling a problem undecidable is treated as closing a question rather than as reporting something about our current conventions. Say which of these the calibration worry actually touches, and whether it touches them all equally.

Second, give me a method that does not collapse into archival history, which I have no training for, or into a survey of exotic computational models. My sense is that the conceptual work comes first: state, before consulting the record, what pattern we should expect if the convergence is genuine tracking and what pattern we should expect if it is calibration, and identify the signature that would discriminate them. That should include the treatment of near misses — relaxations of the intuitive constraints that yield a strictly larger class, such as allowing an oracle, allowing infinitely many steps completed, or allowing exact computation over real-valued parameters — and an account of whether the field's criteria for an adequate analysis rule such relaxations out for reasons independent of the class everyone already accepts. Only then should historical material enter, and only in a bounded, specified way. Explain why this ordering is better than the obvious alternatives: a straight historical study of how the formalisms arose, another round of the realism-versus-antirealism debate about computability, or a catalogue of hypercomputation proposals.

Third, lay out the possible conclusions if the worry survives — the thesis as discovery, as stipulation, as an explication assessed for fruitfulness and fit rather than for truth, as a constitutive convention of a research field, as a low-grade empirical hypothesis — and say what considerations would decide among them. Then address the practice question directly: if the best reading is deflationary, can the authority that uncomputability results enjoy in mathematical and computer-science practice be accounted for, or does a deflationary reading imply that the practice has been over-reading them?

Finally, be honest about scope. Tell me what would make this too large for a single year, and what would count as its failing rather than merely landing somewhere I did not expect — for instance, if the distinction between tracking and calibration turns out to have no discriminatory consequences for anything downstream. Do not supply citations or a bibliography; I will add those myself.
