I'm a retired plant physiologist — 35 years of nutrient-uptake work, and now I've got myself into philosophy via a reading group I run. About 13 of us, mostly ex-scientists and engineers, 90 minutes, one long table, a whiteboard, and whatever I can carry in a shopping bag: poker chips, dried beans, index cards, a big sheet of paper, envelopes. We've been picking through Berkeley's little book of questions on the Irish economy, and two of them have got under my skin: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" and the one where he asks "whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?"

What I want is a game, not a talk. The thing I want them to feel in their hands is that the same fat pile of tokens can be worth wildly different amounts depending on whether anybody actually grew anything — and that a token keeps working only because something stands behind it (he even asks whether "the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold"). And then the bit I can't get out of my head: if money is a ticket for reckoning, recording, and transferring, then what it's really doing is carrying a verifiable record between people who don't know each other and will never meet again. "Recording" is the load-bearing word there. I want them to run into that themselves, not hear it from me. No lecture up front, and I'm not allowed to say the word "ticket" until they've hit it.

So: rules, materials, a payoff scheme with no real money, and a timeline for the 90 minutes. What I've got so far is probably too crude — round one, hand everyone an identical big stack of chips and then auction off a tiny fixed stock of real goods with nothing produced beforehand, chips scoring zero at the end and only consumed goods counting; round two, identical chip endowment but a production phase, so prices collapse; round three, the ship sinks, chips are confiscated, and they have to design a medium themselves while each of them makes one bulky thing they don't want and wants a different one, with goods not allowed to move. I also wanted a version where half the room trades as villagers who know each other and meet again, and half as strangers who get reshuffled after every trade, never trade with the same person twice, have no clerk, and must settle on the spot — my bet is the strangers can't run on trust or a ledger and grab for objects, and I don't know whether that bet is right or whether it's rigged.

Give me the debrief too: which moment in each round maps onto which of Berkeley's claims, what will predictably go wrong (I've definately got one member who'll say "that's just the quantity equation" and kill the mood, and another who will try to corner the bean market, and someone who'll announce early that really money is debt), and be honest about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking — I'm thinking of the way real money is a claim on production that hasn't happened yet, and the fact that in my game nobody is actually afraid of the taxman. And tell me what to do if the group votes down the paper scrip, which I suspect they will. n=13 wouldn't get past a referee, I'm not publishing it, I just want them to feel it.
