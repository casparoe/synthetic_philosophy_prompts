Dear Professor,

I am a student in your Philosophy of Set Theory seminar, and I want to check my understanding of Skolem's paradox before the midterm. Suppose first-order ZFC has a model, as we have been assuming in class. By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, it then has a countable model M. Let r be the element of M that M singles out as the powerset of the natural numbers, that is, M satisfies the formal sentence expressing that r is the powerset of the natural numbers. Because M satisfies the axioms of ZFC, it also satisfies the formal sentence expressing Cantor's theorem, so M satisfies the formal sentence expressing that r is uncountable. Yet, from our external perspective, the domain of M is countable, so the collection of elements of M that M thinks belong to r is countable, and there is an actual bijection between that collection and the natural numbers in the background universe V.

In my study group we introduced a label for this situation: say that a model M of ZFC has the double-life property when there is an element r of M such that (i) M satisfies the formal sentence expressing that r is the powerset of the natural numbers, (ii) M satisfies the formal sentence expressing that r is uncountable, and (iii) from the external perspective r is countable. It looks to me as if the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that every countable model of ZFC has the double-life property. But then it seems that the very same object r is both countable and uncountable, which would be a contradiction; and if ZFC entailed a contradiction, ZFC would be inconsistent.

My proposed solution is to add to ZFC a new axiom asserting that countability is absolute: whenever a model of the theory satisfies the formal sentence expressing that x is uncountable, the set x really is uncountable in the background universe, so no model has the double-life property. If we added this axiom, would we not straightforwardly block Skolem's paradox? What would this axiom do to the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and to the completeness theorem? More generally, is the correct realist reply simply that the countable model M does not contain the real set of real numbers at all, but only a surrogate that plays the role of the continuum inside M, so that the appearance of contradiction comes from confusing internal satisfaction with external truth? Please correct whatever is mistaken in my reasoning, especially my assumption that adding a first-order absoluteness axiom could eliminate countable models.

Please answer in a formal, structured way: first give the standard diagnosis of why the double-life property is not a contradiction, then explain whether the proposed first-order absoluteness axiom can do the work I want it to do and why or why not, and finally say what a defender of the view that Skolem's paradox shows a genuine relativity of set-theoretic notions would reply. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{<short phrase>}, where the braces contain a short phrase naming the central false presupposition in my question, such as \answer{internal-vs-external countability equivocation}.
