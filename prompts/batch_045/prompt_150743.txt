I have been working through an argument about guilt and shame with a colleague and have reached the point where I am sure it goes wrong somewhere but cannot say where, and I would rather not reject the conclusion merely because I find it repellent. She rests the argument on an empirical claim she calls the displacement model: guilt and shame are elicited by overlapping situations and draw on the same limited motivational resources, so a culture that socializes firmly for one will get less of the other. Granting her that, the argument runs:

(1) Guilt takes an act or omission as its object — what I did — and it is dischargeable: repair, apology, or an accepted penalty can settle it.
(2) Shame takes the agent as its object — the kind of person I am — and is not dischargeable; nothing I do afterwards undoes that I am someone who did this.
(3) A moral emotion serves its function only to the extent that it keeps the agent oriented toward the wrong and does not supply a way of writing that wrong off in advance.
(4) Guilt's dischargeability supplies exactly such a way out. If the account can be closed by a penalty the agent can anticipate and, in some cases, propose, the penalty functions as a price; and anything that converts wrongdoing into a price gives an agent a reason to discount future wrongs while deciding.
(5) So guilt's most-cited virtue — that it offers the wrongdoer a route back into the community — is the very feature that weakens it, whereas shame's incurability keeps the agent's standing permanently in question and therefore tracks the moral situation more faithfully.
(6) The standard objection to shame, that it is mere reputation-management because it is defined by exposure to an audience, misfires: shame needs only an imagined or internalized observer, and such an observer is available in solitude. Shame is therefore not essentially heteronomous.
(7) By the displacement model a culture cannot have both emotions at full strength: cultivating one is choosing against the other.
(8) Therefore a culture with any latitude over what it socializes for — including over what its legal institutions express — should cultivate shame and let guilt atrophy; condemnation of the person rather than blame for the act.

The conclusion seems to prove much more than the premises can support. If (4) is sound, then every closure mechanism — apology, forgiveness, punishment understood as satisfaction — is a defect of a moral culture, and the best moral culture would be one in which nothing is ever settled. If (8) is sound, then a guilt culture's whole apparatus of blaming acts rather than persons is a mistake we could simply decide to stop making, which I do not believe is the sort of thing cultures get to decide. But I cannot locate the step to give up. If I have to choose, I would most like to keep the descriptive claims (1) and (2) and the anti-reputation move in (6).
