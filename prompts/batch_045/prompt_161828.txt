me and the group chat have been going around in circles on this for like two weeks and I want someone to actually reconstruct the position instead of just gesturing at it. Here's the practice I keep staring at. Preventive cardiology: you go in, they plug your age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking and diabetes status into a risk equation that was fit to some big cohort, it spits out a single number that's supposed to be your chance of a heart attack or stroke in the next decade, and then a guideline says: above the threshold, offer a statin; below it, don't. I'm in my late 30s, South Asian, vegetarian, cycle a lot, and have family history, and what puzzles me is which things the system is willing to count. It will happily slice the population by age band, sex, and a handful of measured variables. It will let someone near the threshold get a calcium scan as a kind of tie-breaker, but a scan isn't allowed to move someone who's clearly below it. It will not touch my sleep-tracker data or a polygenic score, even though the people running the system will admit in conversation that both carry real information — the line I keep getting is that they aren't validated, or that there isn't a cohort for them yet. Meanwhile, right next door in the same clinic, screening for other things runs on nothing but a crude age cutoff (colonoscopy at 45, mammograms starting somewhere around 40, lung scans for people with a big enough smoking history in the right age window), even though risk models for those exist too. So the same institution individualizes with a model in one room, uses a crude proxy class in the next, and refuses information it concedes is predictive until that information has been refracted through a group. I've started calling the first thing the granularity lottery — two people with the same true risk get different numbers depending on how the population got carved up, and nobody can tell me which carving is the correct one — and the second thing the cohort filter: evidence about you only counts once it's evidence about a class you belong to.

What I actually want is the implicit theory. What does this way of doing medicine presuppose — about probability, about evidence, about what it is for a fact about a group to bear on an individual? Someone on the forums said this is just the reference class problem, but when I went and read around a bit it seemed to be mostly a fight about what probability is, which isn't quite my question; I want to know what the practice is committed to, not what probability really is. Please separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, because I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined: e.g. is a practice like this strictly committed to some frequentist or otherwise objective story where the class frequency is the thing that gets transferred to me, or could a Bayesian who treats classes as exchangeability judgments and the equation as a convenient prior endorse every single part of it, in which case the practice tells us nothing about which philosophy of probability is true? Same question for the arbitrary-looking cutoffs: does the practice secretly presuppose that there is a determinate correct class and a determinate correct threshold, or does it get along fine with the view that the cutoffs are conventions chosen for legibility and liability reasons, and if so does anything in it break?

Two more things, since these are where the argument actually stalls. First, coherence: do the three bits hang together under one theory, or is the clinic running a compromise — model-based individualization here, crude class there, cohort filter everywhere — that only looks consistent because each piece was adopted for a different reason? My friend who does biostatistics says there's nothing philosophical going on, it's just a bias-variance tradeoff, you narrow the class until the estimate gets noisy. I don't think that's an answer to my question but I can't say exactly why; is he answering a different question, and if so what's the gap between the two? (I vaguely remember some dead philosopher — Reichenbach? — saying use the narrowest class you have decent statistics for, which sounds like my friend's point, but note that "decent" is itself a graded thing and the guidelines never say who picks it.) Second, replacement: if we dropped the cohort filter and let in any evidence we had good reason to think was predictive about the individual — scans, wearables, genetics, whatever — what would actually have to change in how a clinic runs, and would it collapse into something silly, like every boutique biomarker getting a hearing or the number becoming unauditable? I'm not asking for a reading list or for what some paper said; I want you to reconstruct the epistemology the practice is running on, tell me which rival views it quietly rules out, and say where it's just coasting on convention.
