The teacher who runs my school's philosophy seminar has been ruling on supertasks one case at a time, and I want to know how she will rule on a case she has not seen yet, and above all what rule she is applying when she does.

Her verdicts so far:

- A lamp whose switch is flipped at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... minutes: no answer; nothing in the description settles whether it is on or off at the minute.
- A lamp flipped at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... minutes, so that the flips pile up at the start rather than at the minute: unproblematic, and the state at one minute is perfectly determinate.
- The first lamp again, but with a bulb rated for a thousand flips, which burns out on the thousandth: determinate — off, and burnt out.
- The first lamp again, but with one extra flip scheduled at exactly the minute: still no answer, since that flip merely toggles a state that was never settled.
- Infinitely many killers, the nth set to strike at 1/2^n minutes before one o'clock if no earlier one has struck: impossible, and not in the same way — here the description rules out every outcome instead of failing to specify one.

The new case: a lamp flipped at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... minutes, wired to a cut-out that opens the circuit at exactly the minute if and only if the switch has by then been flipped infinitely many times.

Predict her verdict. Set out the two or three rules that fit her earlier verdicts about equally well, say what each of them predicts here, tell me which precedent is the closer analogue — the burnt-out bulb or the extra flip at the minute — and, if the precedents do not settle it, name a further case that would tell those rules apart and say what she would be committing to with each answer.
