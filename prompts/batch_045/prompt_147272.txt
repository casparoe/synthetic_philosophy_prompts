I am in the second year of a PPE degree, and I am taking an option this term on the ethics and politics of corruption. Less relevantly to my supervisor but more relevantly to my week, I chair a small registered student charity. We raise around £30,000 a year, employ one part-time administrator, have two academic trustees, and run a single programme: each summer we send six to ten student volunteers to a partner school in northern Ghana for five-week placements. Every year the same family of decision comes up — what we may give, to whom, when, and out of which budget line — and every year we improvise it in a committee meeting and then write something evasive in the minutes. I would like a procedure we can actually run instead.

Constraints on it. It has to be usable by a committee of students with no legal training and no budget for advice, in about twenty minutes, with only what we can get out of our local coordinator by email. Eight steps at most. It should work prospectively, before a payment is made, and also retrospectively, as a test of decisions we have already taken and cannot now undo. It should produce a record: one or two sentences suitable for the minutes, in a yes form and a no form. We are vaguely aware that UK law is stricter here than we had assumed — something about payments made to speed up a routine administrative act, and about the point being whether an organisation has adequate procedures rather than whether any particular payment was made — but I do not want the procedure to rest on the legal position, which none of us properly understands. Nor do I want it to collapse into whatever the local norm happens to be: the whole subject of the course is that when in Rome is not an answer, and neither, plainly, is treating every departure from an impartial, arm's-length, rule-governed bureaucracy as corruption.

Where I have got to in my own notes — please use it, repair it, or replace it, and tell me which you have done and why — is this. The wrong-making feature is not the transfer but a substitution: a consideration that is a reason for the recipient only because of a personal relationship is allowed to do the work that should be done by considerations internal to the recipient's role. On that way of putting it the question splits in two. First, does the recipient occupy a role that carries role-indexed reasons at all — a local, contestable question about what the institution actually is. Secondly, if so, is the transfer there in order to make a relation-indexed reason outweigh those, which does not look local at all. Two further thoughts I would like the procedure to respect. Most of the instruments we would instinctively reach for — receipts, value thresholds, declarations of interest — catch one-off, transaction-shaped cases and are close to useless against the slow, ongoing accumulation of an obligation, so the procedure needs something separate for that. And there is a difference between what we may ask our coordinator and what we must not: asking him directly whether something would be regarded as a bribe puts him in an impossible position, and we should not do it.

So please give me, in order: a two- or three-sentence statement of the account of corruption the procedure operationalises, including where you think legitimate cross-cultural variation actually lives; then the procedure itself, with the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, what to do when we cannot get the answer, at least one gate at which it stops with a no, and at least one point at which it hands the decision to a person — our senior trustee, say — on the ground that it is a question of values rather than fact. Then run it on three cases from last year.

One. Our coordinator tells us that it is customary for a visiting organisation to make a small cash payment, around £40, to mark the signing of the memorandum of understanding with the school; the head teacher has asked for it through him; he advises that refusing would cause serious offence and could put the partnership at risk. Our trustees would call this a bribe. Several of our volunteers think it is a courtesy and that we are being stiff-necked.

Two. At the end of a placement the committee votes to buy, from charity funds, a phone worth about £120 for the family that hosted a volunteer for five weeks. Nothing was asked for. The volunteer says the family was kind and poor. One trustee points out that word will get about, and that families may compete to host whichever volunteer is most generous.

Three. Over three years our coordinator has built a friendship with an official in the district education office. The official now returns his calls first and quietly tells him which paperwork will move and when; nothing has ever been asked for, and nothing has been given in return for any specific decision. Each December we send the official a hamper worth around £25 and pay his travel to a one-day workshop. Other organisations wait months for the same paperwork. The committee regards this as ordinary relationship-building of the kind any organisation does; I suspect my own procedure condemns it, and I would like to know whether it does, and whether that verdict is over-generating.

At least one of these, I suspect, will come out in a way our committee will dislike. If so, say plainly which, and argue us into accepting it. Finally, be honest about what the procedure systematically cannot see — and please do not cite anything, since I will add references myself and my supervisor has said the exercise is about the argument rather than the literature.
