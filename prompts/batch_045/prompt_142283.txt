I stupidly agreed to help two freinds work out how to divide what's left of a small business they ran together — one wrote the software, the other handled clients and sales. Instead of just telling them to split everything down the middle I tried to do it properly, and now I've argued myself into a corner. I'd like to know which step I should back out of.

Set-up: two people, a set of feasible deals, and a walk-away payoff for each of them if they can't agree. A "rule" is anything that picks one deal out of any situation like that. Here's the argument. It has to go wrong somewhere, because the conclusion is useless to me.

(1) Efficiency: never pick a deal when some other available deal is better for one person and no worse for the other.
(2) Symmetry: mirror-image situations get mirror-image deals; people's names shouldn't matter.
(3) Invariance: renumbering someone's satisfaction — multiplying it, or shifting where its zero sits — shouldn't change the deal.
(4) Taking options off the menu that wouldn't have been picked anyway can't change the answer: if the rule picks deal x from a big menu, and x is still available on a smaller menu, it should pick x again.
(5) If the menu changes so that, for every level of satisfaction I might end up with, the most you could get alongside that level goes up or stays put, then you shouldn't end up with less than before. Your options got better, so you shouldn't do worse.
(6) Given (1)-(3), there's exactly one rule that satisfies (4): maximize the product of the two people's gains over their walk-away payoffs (I'm told this is Nash's). And exactly one rule that satisfies (5): find the "ideal point" — for each person, the most they could get in any deal where the other still gets at least their walk-away payoff — draw a straight line from the walk-away point to it, and take the efficient deal furthest along that line (apparently this is Kalai-Smorodinsky).
(7) Those two rules disagree on perfectly ordinary-looking cases, and each of them is the *only* rule satisfying its own extra axiom on top of (1)-(3). So no rule satisfies (1) through (5) together.
(8) So there's no such thing as a principled split here. Any number I hand my friends is just me picking a favourite axiom and calling it fairness; "fair" is only what we call the outcome we already wanted, and the whole excercise is power with extra steps.

I have a feeling I cheat somewhere between (7) and (8), but I can't say where. (4) has always felt like a magic trick to me and (5) I find hard to argue with, so I may just be rationalising.

The one bit I'm confident about is a case I worked out by hand, because it seems to settle (4) against (5) directly. Walk-away payoffs are (0,0). The deals on the table have corners (0,0), (1,0), (0.5, 0.8) and (0,1), plus everything in between, since they can flip a coin between any two deals.

- The product rule picks (0.5, 0.8).
- Now one more deal becomes available: (0.9, 0.5). For every level of the first person's satisfaction, the most the second person can get alongside it is now at least what it was, and at some levels it's more. Neither walk-away payoff moved, and the ideal point is still (1,1).
- The product rule now picks roughly (0.783, 0.588). The second person loses more than a quarter of her share because the menu got better for her.
- The other rule gives about (0.615, 0.615) before and (0.671, 0.671) after — both up, exactly as (5) demands.

Check the arithmetic if you want; if I've botched it I'd rather find out now.

What I actually want: which step do you reject, and why did that step look fine? If you make me choose, I'd rather keep (5) than (4) — but tell me what keeping it costs me. One cost I can already see is that the second rule makes the split depend on the ideal point, which is fixed by the best deal I could get in a world where you get exactly your walk-away payoff, i.e. a deal nobody would ever sign. In one sketch, adding a single option that gives me more than anything previously on offer while leaving you only barely better off than walking away — an option that never gets picked — moved the split from (0.5, 0.5) to roughly (0.77, 0.51). Is that a feature or a bug?

And please be explicit about where your own answer is most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind: if you're blaming (4), what would convince you the real culprit is (5) instead, or that the move from (7) to (8) is fine after all?
