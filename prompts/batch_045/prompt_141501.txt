My editor Marisol has been reading my stuff for nine years and she has exactly one recurring note, which is that I write "ashamed" where the character should feel guilty and "guilty" where the character should feel ashamed. She's been right every single time and I have never managed to work out what her actual rule is. I've got one scene left before this book goes out and I'd rather not get the note again, so I want to guess it right the first time.

Her track record on my last collection, as near as I remember it:

- The Ledger: an accountant falsifies entries to cover a colleague's embezzlement, confesses a year later, repays it himself, the firm buries it, the colleague forgives him, and he still lies awake about it. She made me delete every instance of the word ashamed. Guilt.
- Sous Vide: a chef tears into a line cook on camera, keeps her restaurant, pays him, he tells her they're fine, and the clip is still up. Shame — and she said the apology scene has to make things worse, not better.
- Balcony: a lifeguard at a private club lets a kid flail a few seconds longer than she had to because the kid's father had been rude to her. Another parent films it from above, it does the rounds of the club, the kid is fine. She quietly funds swimming lessons for the summer. Shame, and she said the swimming lessons are the most shameful beat in the whole story.
- Nightshift: a caregiver gives a resident the wrong dose because the pharmacy labelled two bottles identically. Nobody is harmed, she reports it herself, nobody thinks less of her. Neither. She wrote: if you give her either one you are writing a different book.
- The Wall, which is set in the same archipelago as the novel I'm finishing now: a man is caught stealing from the communal store. The settlement's practice is that he sits facing the wall while everyone files past him, nobody says a word, and the next morning he goes back to his job with his standing exactly what it was. Guilt. She wouldn't explain this one beyond "obviously, that's the point of the wall."

Two other things she's said in margins, which may or may not be the same opinion: on one story, that guilt is the one you can do something with; on another, that shame is the only one telling the truth about a person.

The scene I have to write: Anet is the settlement's ice-reader, which there is a person you trust to read the ice, and being the sort of person who can do that is load-bearing for how everybody there talks about what a person is. On a solo crossing she realises something only she could know: she isn't reading it. She never could. She's been guessing for years and her guesses have been lucky, and she has kept taking people out onto the ice anyway. Nothing has gone wrong yet, no one has been hurt, and no one saw anything. The last chapter is the settlement's annual naming, where every adult says aloud the thing they are worst at, nobody responds, and standing is unchanged afterwards — I invented it in the first book and I genuinely don't know what I built.

So: what does Marisol say Anet is feeling in the boat, and what does she say happens to it at the naming? I'm less interested in one verdict than in the two or three rules that fit all five of those precedents equally well and then come apart on this one — name them, say what each predicts about the boat and about the naming, and give me your best guess with the reason for preferring it. Which precedent is the closest analog, and what single change to the scene would flip her from one verdict to the other?

And the bit I actually care about for the book: if the rituals are the culture choosing, then does Marisol's set of verdicts say a culture can choose which of the two emotions its people end up with, or only which situations set one off? Because the wall seems to be engineered to land on guilt and the naming looks to me like it's engineered to land on the other one, and if both can't be true I need to know which one I have to go back and rewrite in book one.
