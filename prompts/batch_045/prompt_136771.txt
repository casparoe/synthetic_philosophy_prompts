I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on induction, prediction, and learning from data: eighteen students, mixed preparation (roughly half have done a proof-based course, the rest have only statistics). I have one seventy-five-minute session to spend on the no-free-lunch theorems and their relation to the classical problem of induction, and I want to spend most of it on something the students do rather than something I say. I am happy to give a two-minute framing at the start and a substantial debrief at the end.

The reason I am asking for help is an argument I had with a friend last night that neither of us could settle. His opening claim was that the no-free-lunch theorems are just Hume's problem of induction written in better notation: no learning algorithm is better than any other once you average over all possible worlds, therefore induction has no principled advantage, and the fact that machine learning works at all is a piece of luck about our particular world rather than a vindication of induction. I replied that the theorems are claims about one very specific thing, a uniform averaging over an unstructured space of possible target functions, and that they are perfectly consistent with there being good reasons to prefer some predictors, and moreover that they cut both ways: if no algorithm is better on average, then the anti-inductive algorithm is not better either, so the theorems cannot be used to discredit induction in particular. He said that symmetry is exactly his point rather than a reply to it: what the symmetry shows is that nothing about induction is doing any work, and that the reasons I wanted to appeal to all smuggle in a preference for simple or stable worlds, which is the very thing that stands in need of justification. I said a prior over worlds is not the same as an inference and that he was asking for something no account of reasoning could deliver. He said that was a confession, not a defence. We went round this loop four or five times and ended where we began.

So I want an activity in which the class feels two things that pull against each other, and feels each of them before either is named.

The first is the real force of the negative result: over the space of all possible worlds, every prediction strategy ties. More specifically, I want each pair or triple to invent a prediction rule they like, then to construct for themselves the mirror rule that anti-predicts theirs, and then to discover that the two score identically when the score is taken over the whole space. I do not want that handed to them as a symmetry argument; I want them to build the mirror and then hit the tie.

The second is that the tie is a fact about a distribution over possible worlds and not a fact about reasoning, and that nobody in the room, including whoever plays the skeptic, actually assigns that distribution. Induction pays only relative to a prior on which most of the space is noise and the actual world is not noise; that prior is not delivered by the data and not delivered by the theorem, and it is the unresolved point my friend and I were stuck on. If the activity lets them notice that they were already betting as though simple worlds were likelier, and then notice that they cannot say where that came from, that is the session.

My half-formed idea is to give them a complete book of every possible world-history of some short fixed length, five or six binary steps, small enough that the whole space fits on a handout and the uniform distribution over the space is not a metaphor but something they can literally enumerate and score against in full. What I cannot work out is how they get from every rule ties over the full book to and yet you were all already betting as though the simple worlds were likelier without my simply announcing the transition. I had thought of a betting or market round in which they commit something before learning which book the world will be drawn from, so that their own stakes expose their prior, but I cannot get the mechanics to do the work rather than the handout.

One more thing I would like handled if it can be. The standard statements of the theorems assume a fixed target: a world that does not change its rule halfway through the sequence. Part of what my friend was reaching for, I suspect, was not about generalizing from a sample at all but about whether the rule stays put. If the last ten minutes can make that visible in what happens rather than in what I say, I would like that; if it cannot be done in the time, tell me plainly and I will supply it in the debrief.

Constraints: no real money and no stakes that cost anyone anything; scoring by simple hand arithmetic; materials limited to cards, coins or dice, and printed handouts; workable for eighteen people in pairs or triples; nothing that requires a computer. No lecture before the activity, and the class should not hear the labels, the theorem's name, or anyone's name until the debrief. Please do not design it so that my side of the argument wins by construction; if the honest upshot is that my friend was more right than I was, the activity should be able to produce that result too.

What I would like from you: the setup and materials; the rules and the payoff or scoring scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the argument above, including who was right about what; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure; and an honest statement of which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply myself.
