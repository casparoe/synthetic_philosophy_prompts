Please act as a professional philosopher of mind who works on the metaphysics of consciousness and who is also comfortable talking to a physicist; take your time, and before you write anything diagnostic, spend a few moments privately mapping the logical space of the positions in play (constitutive panpsychism, cosmopsychism, brute and non-brute emergentism, Russellian monism, illusionism or quietism about macro-subjects) and working out exactly which version of the combination problem each one is exposed to and which it escapes. I am a retired condensed-matter physicist, and I sat in on a graduate philosophy of mind seminar this past year and got thoroughly hooked; I now cannot tell whether the argument I keep having with the seminar's tutor is a real disagreement or a case of two people answering different questions under one heading. The shape of it is familiar to you, I assume: the panpsychist says that the emergentist has to swallow a brute, miraculous leap from wholly non-experiential matter to experience, which offends against the principle that the macroscopic must be grounded in the microscopic; the emergentist replies that the panpsychist has merely relocated the miracle, because nobody has the faintest idea how a plurality of micro-subjects could sum, fuse, or otherwise compose into the single unified subject each of us takes ourselves to be, and that this failure is qualitatively worse than the corresponding failure in theaterial case, since the composition of matter is paradigmatically intelligible while the composition of subjects seems not to be. Both sides restate this; neither moves; theRussellian monist gestures at intrinsick categorical grounds; the cosmopsychist insists the direction of explanation runs the other way and inherits a decomposition problem instead; and the illusionist denies the datum. Twenty-odd years of this and, as far as I can tell, nobody has changed camp because of an argument.

I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a verdict, and I want you to test a hypothesis of my own before you offer one of yours. My hypothesis is that the dispute is downstream of an unargued disagreement about what composition is and about whether there is a determinate, scale-independent fact of the matter how many subjects populate a given region — in other words, that the argument is not really about consciousness at all but about whether the world has objective joints, and that the panpsychist is tacitly helping themselves to a very strong realism about macroscopic unities that my own field gives me no reason to accept. Three things push me toward this. First, the superconductor case: when Cooper pairs condense, something on the order of ten-to-the-twenty-third particles comes to be described by a single macroscopic quantum state with a well-defined phase, and a working physicist will happily say that the condensate is, in a perfectly technical sense, one thing in a way an equal mass of warm copper is not; this looks like a gift to anyone who wants a candidate bonding relation, yet it also looks like a poisoned gift, because coherence here is a matter of degree, is destroyed by environmental decoherence on timescales we can compute, and is arguably a feature of the description we choose rather than a new entity added to the inventory. Second, the renormalization-group lesson that macroscopic behaviour is largely independent of microscopic detail: if universality is the normal situation, then the micro-phenomenal facts the panpsychist postulates seem to do no explanatory work in accounting for my experience, and the brute emergence the emergentist is accused of looks no more objectionable than the bruteness physicists learned to live with a long time ago. Third, and what I have privately started calling the census question: in this room there are, on the summing view, subjects corresponding to me, to my cortical neural assemblies, to my neurons, and perhaps to something smaller still, and I cannot see how a principle is supposed to select one level of the hierarchy as the level at which subjects are counted without smuggling in the answer.

So: is my scale-relativity diagnosis right, roughly right, or a physicists' category mistake — and if it is wrong, what in particular is wrong with it, and what does each side in the literature actually say that tells against it? More generally, tell me what kind of impasse this is: a verbal dispute about the terms subject or compose; a substantive dispute about grounding that happens to be very hard; a clash of standards about what would count as an explanation of one experience in terms of others (deductive derivation, lawlike correlation, conceptual unpacking, or something weaker); a hidden empirical question in disguise; or several of these stacked, with people arguing about whichever one is nearest to hand. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress — a formal result about the algebra of composition, a distinction between unity-of-consciousness and unity-of-subject, an empirical result about which physical systems have unified experience, a change of question — or give me your reasons for thinking that no progress should be expected here and that the sensible thing is to stop asking the question in this form. Where I am confused or am importing an assumption from physics without noticing, please say so bluntly rather than being polite.
