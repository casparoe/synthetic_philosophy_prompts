For the last three days I have been arguing with an old friend from graduate school about what I am allowed to say on Saturday afternoon, and I would like help settling the argument in a form I can actually stand up and read aloud. Selby — Dr. Marion Selby, my doctoral supervisor — died in her sleep in June at eighty-one, and she left instructions for 'a party, not a funeral,' so what is happening is a rented room at the volunteer fire hall on the lake, a table of food, and one five-minute talk that her family extracted from her as the price of getting their party. Her working life went into arguing against the picture Dennett named the Cartesian theater — the idea that somewhere in the brain there is a place where it all comes together and a single inner spectator watches the show — and in favour of something in the neighbourhood of his multiple-drafts model. Her own version of it, which she never managed to publish and which I have been trying to reconstruct from seminar notes, was that what makes a mental event conscious is not its arrival at a place but its acquisition of an 'editorial deadline': a point after which later processing can no longer revise it. Before that deadline, she thought, there is no fact of the matter which draft is the experience; a decision becomes yours not when an inner self signs off on it but when it is too late to take it back; and what we call a person's character is whatever pattern survives many passes of revision, not a hidden core. This mattered to her in a practical way after a stroke at sixty-eight that left her unable to recognize faces, including her own in a mirror: she would identify the person in front of her by gait or by the sound of a voice within a second or two — 'give me a step or two, I'll have you by your walk,' she'd say to the neighbours whose dock she re-stained every spring — and when I once stupidly said I was glad she was still in there, she said no, there is no in there to be in.

My friend's objection, delivered over four hours and a bottle of wine, is in two parts. The soft part is that ordinary grief-talk is not a theory: 'she's at peace,' 'she knew we loved her,' 'she's watching over us' are idioms the room uses the way it uses 'bless you,' and I am being precious if I treat them as metaphysical claims that commit anyone to a spectator in the head — and, worse, I am turning her death into a seminar to avoid grieving. The hard part is the one I can't shake: if the drafts picture is right, he says, then there was never a single continuing subject of the kind a eulogy needs, no one whose life the story I tell is *about*, and so my five minutes are either dishonest (smuggling back the ghost) or empty (a story with no subject). What I want from you is that five minutes: about seven hundred words, in a register the whole room can follow — a third of them are her former students and colleagues, several of whom think the whole programme was wrong-headed and one of whom is coming expressly to hear whether I say something disloyal; another third are her family, including her sister Lois, who is churchgoing and has told me she needs to hear that Selby is at peace and that she knew we loved her; the rest are lake neighbours who knew her as the woman with the dock and the bad dog. So: give the family something that does the emotional work of 'she's at peace' and 'she knew we loved her' without consoling them by picturing an inner witness whose lights went out, or by picturing an intact self sitting somewhere behind the damage; answer my friend's hard objection rather than talking around it, including the question of what the 'she' of a life-story could be on her own view; and keep it a party speech rather than a lecture — she would have walked out of a lecture, and unless you can find a sense of it she would have accepted, I don't think I can say 'she's still with us.'
