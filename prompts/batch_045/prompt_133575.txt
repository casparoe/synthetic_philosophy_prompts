THE QUESTION (25 marks):

(a) Set out Hempel's raven paradox. Your answer should use two principles: Nicod's criterion (a hypothesis of the form "all A are B" is confirmed by an object that is both A and B, disconfirmed by an object that is A and not-B, and unaffected by anything else) and the equivalence condition (whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms every logically equivalent hypothesis). Be explicit about which observations confirm, disconfirm, or are neutral with respect to "all ravens are black," and say what makes the result paradoxical. [10]

(b) Does an observation of a black non-raven -- say, a black shoe -- confirm "all ravens are black"? Explain. Then explain the standard Bayesian point about why a single non-black non-raven, although it does confirm, is said to confirm only to a negligible degree, and what any of this has to do with how the observed object was selected. [7]

(c) Now consider the hypothesis H*: everything that is either a raven or a crow is black. Assume that crows are very much more numerous than ravens. Which observations confirm H*, which disconfirm it, and which are neutral? Does the paradox carry over to H*, and does the imbalance in numbers between crows and ravens make any difference to the case? [8]

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER (verbatim, typos and all):

(a) The paradox starts from two very plausible claims. Nicod's criterion says that "all As are Bs" is confirmed by finding an A that is a B, disconfirmed by finding an A that isn't a B, and left alone by objects that aren't As at all. The equivalence condition says that if two hypotheses are logically equivalent, then any evidence that confirms one confirms the other -- which is hard to deny, since equivalent hypotheses have the same content. Now "all ravens are black" is equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens": each is true in exactly the same circumstances, because a non-black raven is the only way for either of them to fail. So take a white shoe. It is a non-black thing and it is not a raven, so by Nicod's criterion it confirms "all non-black things are non-ravens," and by the equivalence condition it therefore confirms "all ravens are black." That's absurd -- looking at a shoe in a cupboard tells us nothing about ravens. A black raven, meanwhile, confirms both formulations, and a non-black raven would falsify both at once; that's the one observation that settles the hypothesis outright. So what the paradox really shows is that Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition cannot both be right: they are jointly inconsistent, and any adequate theory of confirmation has to give up at least one of them.

(b) A black shoe doesn't confirm or disconfirm. It isn't a raven, so Nicod's criterion has nothing to say about it; and on the contrapositive formulation it isn't an instance either, since the contrapositive only cares about non-black things. So it's neutral, which is the right result: not every observation that fails to refute a hypothesis is evidence for it. As for the white shoe, the Bayesian point is that it does confirm, but only microscopically. Confirmation is a matter of how much the probability of the hypothesis goes up, and since non-black non-ravens are vastly more abundant than ravens, finding one more of them moves the probability far less than finding one more black raven does -- the confirming power of an observation is diluted by how common the kind of thing observed is. How much it moves also depends on how the object was selected: if you deliberately go and look at things already known not to be ravens, you learn nothing, whereas if you sample from a pool in which the object might have turned out to be a raven, the tiny confirmation is really there.

(c) H* is equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," so exactly the same argument goes through: any non-black non-raven confirms H*, by Nicod's criterion applied to the contrapositive plus the equivalence condition. Crows are just one kind of non-raven, so they don't change the structure of the case at all. The numbers do make a difference to the Bayesian part, though. Since crows vastly outnumber ravens, the class of non-black non-ravens is even bigger relative to the class of ravens than it was in the original case, so the confirmation supplied by any single white shoe is diluted even further -- weaker than in the original paradox. And because a randomly encountered black bird is much more likely to be a crow than a raven, the observations that would confirm H* are mostly observations of crows, which seems a bit odd given that H* was supposed to be a hypothesis about ravens as well.
