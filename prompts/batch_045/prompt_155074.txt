I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on distributive ethics and I am writing the final exam: closed book, ninety minutes, students answer one question out of three, twenty marks. I need one question on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism that cannot be answered by restating the objection, and I would like you to produce the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question has to force students apart along three lines.

First, it should make them separate two claims that the standard statement of the objection runs together: the claim that it is illegitimate to aggregate benefits and burdens across persons into a single total, and the claim that a correct principle must be sensitive to how benefits and burdens are distributed across persons. The point is that the second claim is one a utilitarian can happily grant, so a student who only has it has not yet said anything that bites.

Second, it should test whether they can escape a dilemma the objection appears to face: either it is weak enough that utilitarianism satisfies it, or strong enough to forbid imposing any trivial unconsented cost on one person in order to prevent severe harms to several others. Build the question around two cases of my own design, stated in the stem: one in which the only difference between the options is how a fixed burden is spread — a single severe burden on one stranger versus a million barely perceptible burdens on a million strangers, where the summed burden in the second option is stipulated to be larger — and one in which five people can be saved from severe harm only by imposing a trivial, unconsented cost on a sixth. In the first case the anti-aggregation reading seems to deliver the intuitive verdict; in the second it seems to overreach. A strong answer should say what minimal principle gets both verdicts right, and should name explicitly which attractive claim that principle surrenders.

Third, it should confront reductionism about personal identity — the view that what unites a person over time consists in physical and psychological continuities that admit of degrees, so that the boundary between persons is in that respect like the boundary between a nation or a club. Include a reductionist reply and require an answer to it. I also want the question to use a device I have been using in seminar, which I will call the Mirror Test: take whatever principle one is inclined to endorse for distributing benefits and burdens between persons, apply it to the distribution of benefits and burdens within a single life over time, and then either accept the intrapersonal result or explain what makes the interpersonal case relevantly different. Require students to run the Mirror Test on their own proposed principle and to identify the difference-maker. A student who answers that the difference is simply that the people are different people has not answered.

The stem must be self-contained: state the cases and the Mirror Test in full so that no student has to recall a particular article, and let nothing in it depend on quotation, citation, or knowledge of who said what.

Please deliver three parts. Begin with the student-facing question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with no introduction or commentary around it. Then a model answer of 850 to 950 words, which must open with a single sentence stating its bottom line — the minimal claim it will defend — and only then argue for it. Then a marking scheme of five items worth four marks each, totalling twenty, giving for each item the move that earns the marks and, in one line, the way candidates typically get it wrong; open the marking scheme with a single sentence naming the one discriminator that separates an A answer from a B answer, and add a short note on the tempting inference that should cost marks even though weaker candidates make it confidently — in particular, setting the objection up as a straight choice between trivial compatibility with utilitarianism and an absurd absolutist veto, without noticing the third option in between. No bibliography or further readings.
