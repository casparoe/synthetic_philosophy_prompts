Hi — I'm a reporter, I cover science and ideas for a magazine and occasionaly do a podcast, and I've been offered a shot at a nine-month fellowship (stipend, no teaching, office hours with some philosophers) to work on one book-length project of my own choosing. I want to spend it on causation by omission and I need roughly 1,000 words I can hack into the application. No bibliography or reading list please — I'll fill in names and citations myself, and half of them would be guesses anyway. What I need is the argumentative spine.

Here's where I'm coming from. Last year an editor killed a sentence of mine — "the agency's failure to act caused the deaths" — and I couldn't explain to her what was wrong with it, only that it felt like it was doing something different from "the flood caused the deaths." Around the same time a friend who covers climate was trying to write about attributing harms to countries that didn't cut emissions, and we went in circles about whether an omission can be a cause at all or whether that's just a way of sneaking blame in through the back door. My half-formed idea is that the whole fight treats "an omission" as one kind of thing and that this is the bug. There seem to be at least three: an omission where a specific action was already scheduled or decided on and then withheld (the nurse who was going to give the dose and didn't); an omission where the agent had the ability, the means and the occasion but never formed any intention at all (the passer-by who walks past a toddler face-down in a shallow pond without once considering stopping); and an omission where nobody was ever in a position to act, because the thing that would have helped was a policy or a mechanism that no individual had the standing to create. And then a second, weirder thought: when we ask what would have happened if someone had acted, the comparison case we reach for is different from the one we use for actions. For an action we compare against the normal course of events — roughly, what would have happened anyway. For an omission we seem to compare against what the agent should have done, or what a decent agent would have done, or what this agent could have managed given her actual psychology and skills, or just what people in her role usually do. I don't know which of those is doing the work, and I can't tell whether any of them is purely descriptive. So the question I want to fund is whether there is any baseline for an omission that does no normative work at all, and if there isn't, whether "her inaction caused the harm" is ever a causal claim rather than a causal-shaped moral one — and what follows for courts and for headlines.

For the proposal itself: state the question so that it has a determinate yes-or-no answer, say why the three standard positions (omissions are causes, omissions are never causes, and the pluralist shrug) each leave it open, and set out what I'd actually do across nine months with no empirical budget beyond my own Rolodex — I can sit down with working reporters, editors and maybe a judge for structured half-hour interviews about how they'd describe a fixed set of cases, and I'd like you to tell me honestly whether that's worth anything or whether it's just vibes-laundering. Tell me why that approach beats the obvious alternatives (armchair case-mongering, or bolting the question onto the existing empirical work on causal judgment). Say what the deliverable is — I'm imagining a one-page decision tree an editor could run before printing any sentence of the form 'X's failure to do Y caused Z' — and say plainly what would count as the project failing: if my three-way taxonomy turns out to be relabeling the old dispute, or if everything just floats on which baseline you pick and nothing pins the baseline down, I want the proposal to say that out loud. One more thing: include a short section on what a committed defender of the 'omissions are never real causes' line would concede counts against them, and what they'd dismiss as merely awkward — I'd rather know where they're exposed than where they're stubborn.
