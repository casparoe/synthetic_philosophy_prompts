I write software for a living and have no formal training in philosophy. I'd like an essay on a question I can't settle for myself: whether the high level of a running program ever does genuine causal work, or is only ever a convenient way of describing what the low level does anyway.

The case I keep coming back to comes from science fiction and uses two made-up ingredients. Careenium is a hypothetical material with exotic microphysics: a slab of it, left to itself and doing nothing but what its own physics dictates, evolves through a sequence of physical states that we can also read as the successive states of a running simulation. Inside that simulation there are simmballs — simulated balls that collide with one another and with simulated walls. Nobody programmed anything and there is no interpreter: the careenium simply does what careenium does, and reading it as a physics simulation is a mapping we impose on it.

So: when a simmball veers off after a collision and, correspondingly, a cluster of careenium particles shifts into a new state, who shoves whom? Do the simmballs push the careenium around, does the careenium push the simmballs around, or is neither of those the right picture?

Two complications that I want addressed rather than bracketed off. First, suppose the same slab, under a second equally good mapping, also implements a simulated market, so that one and the same physical transition is at once the simmball's collision deflecting it left and a price drop triggering a sell order. Is there any fact of the matter about which of these sim-level causes is the real one, and what does the answer tell us about sim-level causation generally? Second, suppose I want the simmball to go left. I can rearrange careenium particles directly, in a way that happens to keep the slab's states in step with the simulation; or I can edit the simulated world's variables, which of course is itself realized by some rearrangement of careenium particles. Is there a difference in kind between these two interventions, or only a difference in bookkeeping? And is a counterfactual like "if the ball had been hit harder, it would have travelled further" made true by anything over and above facts about careenium particles?

Please weigh the strongest case for both sides — including the worry that the micro-level does all the pushing and the sim-level story merely rides along, and the opposing thought that the sim-level story is the only thing that lets us predict, control, and explain what happens in that slab. Then commit to a verdict and defend it. Finally, say plainly what your verdict implies for ordinary talk about software: when I say that a null dereference crashed the server, am I citing a real cause, or speaking loosely about microphysics I'll never inspect?
