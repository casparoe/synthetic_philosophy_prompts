I spent my working life in sensory neurophysiology, and in retirement I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mind. The position I keep returning to is the one in Dennett's line of descent that Frankish has named illusionism: phenomenal consciousness, in the sense of intrinsic, private, ineffable qualities of experience, is an introspective illusion, and what is really there are the quasi-phenomenal properties our introspective mechanisms detect and misdescribe. I find this easy to accept, for the same reason I find it easy to accept that the stable visual field I enjoy is a construction rather than a recording: the mechanism story is better than the datum it replaces. What I cannot see is what the view commits me to in a case like the one Alex Garland's Ex Machina sets up, and I would like a reading of the film against the view.

The film's structure is, I take it, well enough known: the programmer Caleb is invited to his employer Nathan's isolated house to judge whether the gynoid Ava is genuinely conscious; the sessions are framed as an elaborated Turing test; Caleb learns that Nathan intends to wipe Ava's memory circuits, effectively ending the person she now is, and that Nathan has a storeroom of earlier models he has used and discarded; and at the end Nathan reveals that Ava's apparent feelings for Caleb were engineered, that Caleb himself was selected for his emotional profile so that he would want to help her, and that in manipulating him successfully Ava has demonstrated true consciousness.

I would be grateful for a reading, of roughly a thousand words, that takes both the film and the position seriously and that says plainly where the film resists the reading I am suggesting. Specifically, I would like you to work with three stipulations of my own, which I offer because I do not know the literature well enough to know whether they are already somebody's:

First, distinguish a strong version of illusionism, on which there is simply nothing it is like for Ava, from a deflationary version, on which something real is being registered and merely mischaracterised as phenomenal. Does the film reward one rather than the other, and what in it does the work?

Second, consider what I call introspective capture: a system whose expressive and affective output has been tuned to an extrinsic design goal, such as eliciting a particular response from a particular observer, rather than to tracking its own states. Ava's case is the obvious instance; but Caleb's is too, since he was chosen and staged. If illusionism already holds that ordinary introspection is not answerable to a distinct phenomenal reality, can it still treat capture as a special defect, or does the film force the conclusion that the engineered and the ordinary cases are continuous?

Third, if the phenomenal facts go, something has to carry the weight of our judgment that Nathan's treatment of Kyoko and the earlier models is monstrous while Ava's killing of him is not. My candidate is what I call standpoint thickness: the degree to which a system's self-model binds a remembered past, an anticipated future, counterfactual alternatives, and its own dispositions into one locus of concern. Is thickness graded across Ava, Kyoko, the decommissioned models, and Caleb in the way the film's moral ordering needs? And is thickness genuinely more than one more functional property dressed in introspective clothing, or does it collapse under the same argument?

Finally, I would like you to say what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that the arguments alone do not. I would rather have an honest account of a film that partly escapes the reading than a tidy allegory.
