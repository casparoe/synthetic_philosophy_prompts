For some years I have kept a hobby: I run round-robin tournaments among strategies for the iterated prisoner's dilemma. Every entrant is a fixed rule — written by me or by friends — that inspects the history of the pairing and chooses cooperate or defect. Each entrant meets every other entrant, and a copy of itself, for a fixed two hundred moves, and I score by total points across all pairings, using the usual payoffs: five for defecting against a cooperator, three for mutual cooperation, one for mutual defection, nothing for being exploited. None of my entrants is written to behave differently on the last move, so none can exploit the known ending.

The pattern I want a word for has two faces. My most successful entrant opens by cooperating and thereafter simply repeats whatever its partner did on the previous move. It has never once, in any pairing I have run, finished ahead of the thing it was playing against; because its moves are its partner's moves shifted by one round, it either ties or finishes exactly five points behind, and that is all there is to it. Yet it wins the table, over and over, whatever else I put in the pool. My least successful entrants include the one that simply defects on every move. Nothing in my pool can score more than it in a single pairing — the best you can do against a defector is defect yourself, and every cooperation only costs you — so it wins or ties every contest it plays, and it sits near the bottom of the table.

The arithmetic is not mysterious: against a partner that always cooperates, the copycat collects 600 and concedes 600, while the defector collects a thousand and concedes nothing. Add the first column across thirty opponents and the copycat's total is enormous; count wins instead and the defector looks like the strongest thing present. What I cannot get straight is which of those is the right question, and why.

Two further things make me suspect I am looking at more than one phenomenon. If I rescore the very same results by awarding a point for each pairing won rather than the raw payoffs, the table reorders — different winner, different tail. And when I let the pool evolve instead of running a single tournament — copy entrants in proportion to their scores, discard the failures, reseed with a few copies of whatever did well, repeat for some hundreds of generations — something else takes over, usually a rule that repeats its own previous move when the last round paid it reasonably and switches when it did not. The defectors vanish, and I take it that this is because, once common, they start meeting themselves.

A statistician friend muttered something about Simpson's paradox; a biologist friend said it was just non-transitivity. Neither seems to me to capture what I have found, but I cannot say why, and I cannot tell whether I have stumbled on one thing with two faces or on two separate things that happen to sit next to each other.

So: does this have a name? I do not want a label on its own — I want the phenomenon carved. What exactly is the gap between scoring well and beating the thing you are paired with; how does that gap differ from non-transitivity and from the aggregation paradoxes it superficially resembles; does the collapse of the defectors once they become common belong to the same phenomenon or to another one; and what would a case look like in which the distinction made no difference at all, so that I can see where its edges lie? If there is no established concept here, say so plainly and give me a name and a definition I could use, with the boundary cases fixed. I am not asking for references — only for the distinction, stated and bounded.
