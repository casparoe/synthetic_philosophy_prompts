I'm part of a small reading group working through George Berkeley's *The Querist* (the three-part edition of the book first published in 1735), and we've stalled on a short run of queries about money where I can't tell how literal to be. Here is the passage as our edition prints it:

Query 21: "Whether other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?"

Query 23: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"

Query 25: "Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?"

Query 26: "Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

What I want help with is threefold. First, the vocabulary: I've been assuming "ticket or counter" picks out something like the counters or tally-tokens used by a transfer bank, and that "exponent" is being used in something close to its algebraic or musical sense (a name for a ratio rather than a thing), but I can't tell whether that reading makes sense of Query 25's move from proportions to "crown, livre, pound sterling." Second, the strength of the claims: is Berkeley denying that money has any value at all beyond convention, or only that money *as such* — considered precisely as money — has no intrinsic value, which would still leave gold and silver valuable as stuff with other uses? And is Query 26 a genuine policy thesis about what a country short of specie could do, or just a thought experiment meant to establish something about what money essentially is?

Third, and this is what the group keeps arguing about: one member insists the whole passage is simply Berkeley's immaterialism — the "to be is to be perceived" view he is famous for — applied to economics, on the grounds that if value is not a sensible quality and the physical world is mind-dependent, then money can only be a sign, and "the true idea of money" must be an idea in the strict technical sense of his philosophy. That feels too quick to me, but I can't say where the inference breaks. Can you separate what in the passage is a metaphysical thesis from what is an independent thesis about money that someone who rejected his metaphysics could still have accepted — and tell me whether his general theory of signs, as opposed to immaterialism itself, is doing the real work here? I know he also wrote about credit, industry, and a projected national bank, so if those parts of the book are what make these queries intelligible, I'd like to understand how they connect. I'm reading from a place where I can't reach a library database or any secondary literature, so please don't send me off to look things up; I just want to know how to read these four queries, what Berkeley is and isn't committed to by them, and whether my group's metaphysical reading is fair to the text.
