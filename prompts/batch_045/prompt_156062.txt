You are a professional philosopher of mind who has actually worked on self-knowledge — not someone summarising a literature — and at the same time a poet who can carry metre without audible strain. Please work slowly and carefully. Before you write a line, do a short planning pass: name the three or four claims you intend to encode, say which lines will carry which, choose your rhyme scheme, and state where the turn falls. Then write the poem. Then revise it once with the constraints below open in front of you. Finally, give me a brief note — no more than about 150 words — mapping lines to content, so that I, who am not a philosopher, can check that the poem is right and not merely atmospheric.

The occasion. I spent a little over thirty years in a physiology department, most of them measuring things, and I now attend a continuing-education reading group on mind and brain at the local university. I have been asked to close the final session — a term spent, as it happens, on whether we can trust what we say about our own mental lives — with something short. Around thirty people, mostly retired, half of them scientists of one kind or another, none of them philosophers. The poem will be read aloud once, with no printed page and no explanation afterwards, so anything that needs a gloss to land should be cut, and any effect that depends on seeing the line break rather than hearing it should be avoided.

Form. A sonnet: fourteen lines of loose iambic pentameter, either English or Petrarchan, your choice, but you must declare which and why in your planning note, and the volta must fall where you say it falls. I want the turn to do real work: the octave should build the naive picture, in which a first-person report just is the state's own readout — a dedicated sense turned inward, immediate, privileged, not the sort of thing that can be wrong in the way a measurement can be wrong. The turn should be the moment that picture meets the objection that having a sensation and knowing one has it are separable: the imagined subject who is in pain, who winces and groans and could work out from its own wincing that it is in pain if someone pointed it out, but who has no direct awareness of being in pain at all.

Content, in the weeds. In the sestet I want at least three further things rendered, not named. First, the alternative picture, on which a report is produced by the same inferential machinery we use on other people, fed by behaviour, situation and expectation — which is why confident first-person explanations of one's own choices can be false (I am thinking of the finding's shape rather than its citation: identical items in a row, a consistent preference for the ones on the right, and a fluent story about texture, which the subject goes on defending after the effect is shown to them). Second, the problem I have started calling the calibration problem: there is no independent standard against which to check the instrument, because every check either goes through the instrument itself or through some autonomic or neural proxy whose correlation with the experience had to be established in the first place by asking people. Third, that the instrument perturbs what it measures — attending to the breath changes the breath — and that this cuts both ways, since sometimes the machine is the one that is wrong and the subject was right; I spent a career discounting what subjects told me and was wrong about half of those times.

The couplet should say what has to be given up, and it should not say that we are strangers to ourselves. What has to go is the idea of the report as a readout with a known transfer function; what survives is the report as one instrument among several, uncalibrated, still indispensable. Leave open whether there is a determinate state the report can miss or whether the report partly makes what it reports. I have read enough verse about the abyss of the inner life; I want the stricter and less comfortable conclusion, which is that the instrument works perfectly well and we do not know what it measures.

Constraints on the verse. Do not use the words introspection, qualia, confabulation, phenomenal, epistemic, or incorrigible anywhere in the poem: I want the ideas carried by concrete language, not by labels. (You may gesture at the literature — the self-blind subject, the opacity-of-mind sort of view, the doubts about naive reports of emotional experience — in your planning note if it helps you be accurate, but no names belong in the poem.) Carry this image if you can use it: in my lab there was a chart recorder with two pens writing onto one moving drum, the subject's own rating on one trace and the machine's on the other, the two lines wandering apart. There was never a third pen, and no calibration certificate was ever issued for either. Use the image; do not narrate the anecdote.

If a line cannot carry both metre and content, keep the content and let the metre flex rather than the reverse. I would rather have one poem that a philosopher of mind would not wince at than three that merely sound well.
