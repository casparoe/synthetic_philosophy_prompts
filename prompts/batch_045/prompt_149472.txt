I am finishing a novel about a small town that maintains an annual ceremony no one, speaking privately, any longer wants, and I have been rereading Shirley Jackson's 1948 story The Lottery as a study in how such practices hold together. I would like a reading of that story through the epistemology of coordination: common knowledge, mutual belief of merely finite depth, and what it actually takes for a convention to come apart when each household's willingness to stop depends on what it believes the other households believe.

Three things.

First, the reading. Does the lottery persist because no one in that village has common knowledge of a general reluctance — everyone knowing that everyone knows, and so on — or does the text give us no reason to posit any general reluctance at all? The square is cheerful, the talk before the drawing is easy, Tessie objects only once the marked slip is in her hand, and the one villager who defends the rite does so in the language of antiquity rather than of conviction. Be honest about which reading the story supports and where the pluralistic-ignorance reading is imposed on it. Then tell me what would have to happen in that square for the ceremony to end: is it enough that most households would privately welcome its end, or must something public occur, and what kind of public thing — a show of hands, a household standing aside, the box left unrepaired and unpainted?

Second, a device of my own. In my draft a household may cut a notch into the church door at night; the notches are counted on the morning of the ceremony and are never attributed to anyone; the rite is suspended only if the count reaches every household. I believe this fails, and I want to know why in the theory's own terms. Does such a tally ever generate common knowledge, or only a finite tower of beliefs about beliefs about beliefs, and is that difference enough to explain why my village would go on stoning people even in a year when nearly every household has cut its notch? If a small change to the device would make it work — something irrevocable, something witnessed, something that forces a reaction to a reaction — tell me what it is, and what it would cost me in plausibility.

Third, what the story gives back to the theory. The models tend to picture agents who share a description of the game, know each other to be rational, and then reason; Jackson gives us a village in which no one states a belief and the ceremony is carried by bodies standing in a field with stones in their hands. Does the story suggest that this picture misdescribes how real conventions hold — that the theory has mistaken articulated belief for habit, or the absence of dissent for the presence of consent?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
