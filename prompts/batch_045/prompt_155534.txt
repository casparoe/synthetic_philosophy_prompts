Last night a friend and I spent three hours on the doomsday argument and got exactly nowhere: every move either of us made depended on some prior commitment the other refused to grant, and by the end we were just trading accusations of ad hocness. So before I read another defence or refutation, I want the specification I wish we had had on the table between us.

Write the desiderata that any theory of self-locating or anthropic probability must satisfy in order to be usable for deciding whether the doomsday argument should shift a person's credence in near-term human extinction at all — by any margin that could matter to action, down to a single percentage point. That is the purpose the theory has to be good enough for; I don't want a theory of self-locating belief in general. For each requirement, give in a sentence or two the case or consideration that motivates it.

Cover at least the following, but add any requirement I have not thought of if it belongs on the list, and drop any of mine that you think is really a demand of one camp rather than a genuine desideratum — saying which and why:

1. The re-description constraint: the verdict must not change when the indexical information is redescribed without new content — being the nth member of your species, versus being the nth member of your species and also one of the first to have read a formulation of this argument, versus being the mth member of the set of beings who will ever have entertained such an argument. My friend's view handled the first two descriptions and fell apart on the third; mine handled the third and gave different numbers for the first two.
2. The no-bespoke-rescue constraint: any resource invoked to defeat the argument — current growth rates, the calendar date, the fact that you are reading this sentence at all — must be statable as a general updating rule that a being who had never heard of the argument could apply, and must be fixed in advance rather than fitted to the conclusion you wanted.
3. The unit problem: the answer should not depend on whether the reference class is counted in persons, person-years, or observer-moments; if it does, the theory must say which unit is privileged and why.
4. The forgetting case: a future in which humanity persists for millions of years but in which no one ever again formulates an anthropic argument — call it the Alexandria future. The theory must say whether that counts as doom for the argument's purposes, and must give the same answer for a species in which no one ever formulates such an argument at all.
5. The retrospective check: the theory must be able to say, in advance, what it would have expected us to observe in a world where we later learn that we were in a simulation, or that our species did in fact colonize — that is, it must leave itself checkable in principle, without taking as input the very quantity it is meant to estimate.
6. Compatibility with ordinary updating on non-anthropic evidence: it should not let anthropic reasoning license ignoring census data, or, if it does, that has to be marked as a cost rather than hidden.

Then: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments one camp makes that its opponent need not share; flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied (I suspect the re-description constraint and non-triviality are in tension); and name the single requirement that rules out the largest number of popular responses. Finally, in no more than one sentence each, score the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and the "we simply have independent evidence about population sizes" response against your list.

For each requirement, state how confident you are that it is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice of one side, and single out the requirement you are least confident about. I'm on a train with no signal, so don't point me to any reading and don't attribute anything to an author or a title: argue it out. Keep the whole thing under 1,000 words, with the requirements taking up most of it.
