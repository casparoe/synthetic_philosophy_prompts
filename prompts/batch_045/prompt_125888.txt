I chair the Graduate Academic Standing Panel at my university — five of us, mostly faculty, and in six years we have seen little beyond ordinary plagiarism and the occasional ghostwriting case. The appeal in front of us next week fits neither pattern, and my colleagues are split down the middle, so I want to circulate a written opinion before we meet. The case: a doctoral candidate in public policy submitted a dissertation whose two central chapters — a sustained argument for a specific regulatory scheme — were produced with a research tool called Trellis. Trellis is an amplification or factored-cognition system: it takes a hard question, splits it into sub-questions, routes those to human workers on a crowdsourcing platform who answer in isolation without seeing the parent question or any rationale, and then has other workers recombine the sub-answers into answers to the parent questions, recursing until it returns a final output with a transcript of the whole tree. The candidate designed the top-level question, the decomposition scheme, and the recombination rule; she then read the transcript for about a week, could not have reconstructed the reasoning herself, and signed the result as her own judgment. Two details are causing the split. First, the tree was not a clean hierarchy: several depth-two sub-questions referred to the very recommendation the chapter was supposed to reach ("given that the chapter will recommend prioritizing equity over aggregate welfare, does the evidence support intervention X?"), and Trellis resolved this by iterating the whole structure to a fixed point over three rounds, adjusting the recombination weights as it went. Second, no one anywhere in the process ever explicitly weighed equity against aggregate welfare in context; that trade-off is an artifact of how the recombination weights settled. There is no generative model involved, so our AI-use policy does not apply; the only relevant rule is the Graduate School's requirement that a dissertation "shall be the candidate's own work and shall represent the candidate's own judgment." We have previously allowed a mathematics candidate to rely on a proof assistant he could not have checked by hand, and allowed a dissertation containing two co-authored chapters; we have previously sustained a finding against a candidate who bought heavily edited prose from a commercial service.

Please draft the short opinion I will circulate — roughly 700 to 900 words, in American English, written as a document the panel could act on rather than as advice to me. It should recommend one of three dispositions and say why: uphold the department's finding that the requirement was not met; overturn it and permit the chapters with a disclosure requirement; or remand with conditions, specifying what she would have to do and by when. I want the opinion to take seriously the strongest case for permitting this — roughly, that the underlying picture of these systems is that careful deliberation just is the factoring of a hard judgment into many small tractable ones, so that designing the decomposition, auditing the transcript, and owning the output differs only in degree from directing research assistants, using statistical software, or consulting a dozen experts whose contributions one could not reproduce. It should also say honestly what we would be committing ourselves to by choosing either way, and which single further fact about how the fixed-point iteration behaved would most change the recommendation.
