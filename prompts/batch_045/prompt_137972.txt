I am putting together the first session of a graduate seminar on infinite ethics, and I would like to hand students a decision tree rather than a reading list. The design goal is that a student can enter the tree holding only one commitment — that we cannot rule out that the world contains, or will contain, infinitely many welfare-bearing lives or moments — and come out at a named position having answered only questions about concrete cases. I do not want author names, dates, or citations anywhere on the handout; labels for positions only.

Please construct the instrument as follows.

1. Build the logical space before naming any view. Set out the diagnostic questions, the answers each permits, and the combinations of answers that are therefore possible; only then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, say so and diagnose why in one clause: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

2. Six questions, each with two or three answer options, each phrased as a concrete case rather than as a slogan. No item may ask the student to self-classify along labelled lines. The cases I want used are:

(a) The extra life case: in a cosmos that already contains infinitely many good lives, an act adds one further good life and changes nothing else. Is the resulting outcome better, worse, equally good, or is the comparison malformed?

(b) The density case: two infinite worlds of the same large-scale shape, where in one of them the proportion of good to bad lives is higher in every sufficiently large region (equivalently: higher value per unit volume in the limit). Rank them, or decline to.

(c) The spatial/temporal split: does the answer to (a) change if the infinity is spread out in space while the agent can only ever affect a finite region, as opposed to being an endless future the agent can genuinely shape? I want the tree to separate views that dissolve the temporal problem by temporal discounting from views that dissolve the spatial problem by causal or epistemic locality.

(d) The tiny-probability case: one act offers a chance of one in a billion of producing infinite value; another certainly produces an enormous but finite amount. And then the mirror image with infinite disvalue, so that the student's attitude to small probabilities of infinite goods can come apart from their attitude to small probabilities of infinite evils.

(e) The undefined-expectation case: a lottery over finite payoffs whose expected value does not exist because the positive and negative parts both diverge. Is this a counterexample to expected-value maximisation, or a sign that the payoffs were misdescribed?

(f) The mixed case: a world containing both an infinite heaven and an infinite hell, to which one finite instance of suffering is added. Does the addition matter, and if not, does anything matter in such a world?

3. Leaves. The tree must be able to land a student on at least the following: (i) an approach that keeps aggregation but extends the number system so that infinite totals remain comparable, e.g. by infinitesimals or by a lexicographic ordering; (ii) a bounded-utility approach on which no outcome has infinite value and the problem therefore never arises; (iii) a density or average-in-the-limit approach; (iv) an indifference approach on which all outcomes of infinite value tie and finite differences wash out; (v) a truncation or practical-finitism approach on which we evaluate some finite surrogate, such as the accessible future or the reachable region, and let the remainder drop out; (vi) a person-affecting or otherwise restricted-aggregation approach on which infinite totals are not the relevant object at all; (vii) an approach that accepts fanaticism about small chances of infinite value; and (viii) an approach that abandons expected-value maximisation while retaining a ranking of outcomes. Give each leaf the label it normally carries where one exists and a descriptive label of your own otherwise, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

4. Two distinctions of my own that the tree should respect, because the standard treatments run them together:

- Ranking-paralysis versus action-paralysis. A view can fail to rank infinite outcomes and still guide action, through dominance reasoning or a surrogate target; and it can rank outcomes perfectly well and still fail to guide action, because the relevant expectation is undefined. Tag each leaf with which of these it suffers from, if either. Call a view accessible if a finite agent with finite evidence, located at a particular time and place, could in principle use it to select an act, and mark the leaves that are inaccessible and say in virtue of what.

- Sensitivity to the kind of infinity in play: an endless future, a spatially unbounded present, an infinitely divisible stretch of time, or a branching multiverse with infinitely many branches. If a leaf's answer flips between these, say so rather than reporting a single answer.

5. Flag the pairs of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate, where two positions agree on all six cases but diverge elsewhere, and specify what further case would separate them. Do not manufacture a question that merely presupposes the difference you are trying to detect.

Format: the six questions with their cases in full first, then the tree in text or ASCII, then a compact scoring key mapping answer-patterns onto leaves, then the unoccupied regions with their diagnoses, then the unseparable pairs. Aim for something I could photocopy as a two-page seminar handout.
