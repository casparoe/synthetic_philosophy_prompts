we got stuck on the exclusion argument in my phil of mind reading group, and one guy keeps defusing it with an analogy, and I cant tell whether the analogy is doing real work or just smuggling in the conclusion.

The argument as we were handed it: every physical effect that has a cause has a complete physical cause; mental properties arent identical to physical properties; causation doesnt systematically come in duplicate pairs; so mental properties dont cause anything physical — or they cause only in some thin sense where the physical cause does the work and the mental is a free rider on it.

His analogy: mental properties are to the brain as a program is to the hardware. The program is what makes the printer print the right page, but it doesnt add any energy, any forces, any extra motions beyond what the circuits are already doing. So "theres no causal room left for the mental" is confused — you dont need spare room in the physics for the software to matter. The exclusion worry, he says, is just a failure to see that a cause can be higher-level without being a second thing pushing.

Someone else came back with a different analogy: mental properties are like the average of a column of numbers. Averages are real and they explain things — the team won partly because of its average height — but there is nothing in the world over and above the players and their heights, so the causal talk is shorthand for a pattern in the lower-level facts, not a second cause. And then she added that you should notice how much the software picture has to import: a program only does anything because some engineer wrote it and some machine is executing it and interpreting it, syntax plus an interpreter plus somebody who assigned the semantics. Nothing like that is handed to us in the case of belief and desire, so whos the programmer and whos the interpreter?

My own attempt, which I think is a third thing: mental properties are like the rules of a game. The rules dont push the players around, but they fix which movements are available as options, which count as legal, and which count as a goal. Nothing happens except the players motions, but you also cant say what happened without the rules.

Can you audit these? I want the correspondence tables — for each one, what plays the role of the mental property, of the neural realizer, of the effect, of whatever makes the higher-level description true — then where each mapping breaks, and which of those disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion its being used to carry and which are just noise. Specifically: does the software analogy quietly assume the distinctness of mental and physical that the arguments second premise is supposed to be establishing, i.e. is it begging the question? Does the no-extra-energy feature even matter, given that closure is a claim about causal sufficiency rather than about energy? Does the averages analogy prove too much — would it drain the causal power of the program, or of a virus, or of the shape of a key? And is my rules analogy any better, or does it just change the subject from makes something happen to makes something count as something? Last thing: is there a better analogy for whatever is actually true here, or is the honest answer that no analogy can carry this and the work has to be done by saying what kind of causal claim is even in play?
