Here is my situation. I sit on the release-review committee at an AI lab. We have finished training a model that, on our internal uplift studies, measurably lowers the skill floor for a class of dual-use wet-lab tasks — not catastrophic in itself, but the sort of result that makes a committee nervous. We are deciding whether to publish the weights.

At our last meeting the head of platform security argued for full weight release and closed with two slogans in a row: "security through obscurity is no security at all," and then, immediately, "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow" (he called it Linus's law; I have no way to check that). His conclusion was that withholding the weights buys us no safety at all, that the only genuine safety measures are on the deployment side — monitoring, access control, hardening the downstream targets — and that since those work just as well once the weights are public, openness costs us nothing and buys us scrutiny.

I do not want a rebuttal; I want a disambiguation I can hand him. Three things make me think the slogans are doing less work than they appear to.

First, our own lab relies on secrecy everywhere and nobody treats it as illegitimate: we do not publish the training-data mix, the post-training recipe, our internal red-team results, or the design of access control on our own cluster. If the slogan condemns reliance on secrecy as such, it condemns all of that too. So it must be expressing something narrower, and I want to know what.

Second, a distinction I proposed in the meeting and that he waved away. In ordinary security engineering the secret is small and cheap to replace: a key, a credential, a certificate. You rotate it, revoke it, reissue it, and designing on the assumption that it will eventually leak is exactly right. What we would be keeping secret here is on the order of a trillion numbers, and its "leak" is not a discrete event you can respond to: once it is out it is mirrored, quantized, fine-tuned and re-uploaded, and there is nothing to reissue. So even granting the maxim about secrets, what we hold may not be the kind of thing the maxim is about. Call this the rotate-versus-release asymmetry, and tell me whether it is a genuine difference in kind or merely one of degree that the maxim already covers.

Third, a related worry about the second slogan. Source code is a text with localizable defects — a line, a function, a diff — and review scales, roughly, with the number of competent readers. Model weights are not readable in that sense; a dangerous capability is a diffuse disposition spread across the parameter set, which you do not find by reading but by probing, and probing can be done against a closed system through an API as well as against an open one. So the eyeballs reading may be committing a category error about what sort of artifact a model is.

There is also an ambiguity I suspect he is sliding over: the obscurity might be obscurity from users, from attackers, or from regulators, and those are different complaints. And there is a question about whether withholding weights is obscurity at all. A closed model served behind identity checks and rate limits is not merely concealed; it is access-controlled, and access control is normally counted as a real security mechanism rather than as obscurity. The slogan seems to get its force from contrasting concealment with a mechanism, so I want to know whether our choice really belongs on the concealment side of that contrast.
