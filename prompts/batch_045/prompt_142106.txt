I am a philosophy professor preparing graduate seminar notes on Newcomb-style decision problems, and I would like you to act as an expert decision theorist and professional philosopher. Please begin by brainstorming, in a few lines, the different ways the following scenario can be formalized, then adopt the most defensible formalization for the rest of your answer. Recall the symmetric Death in Damascus case: a traveller in Damascus is told that Death, a perfect predictor who has already recorded his prediction, is in whichever city the traveller will be in tomorrow; the traveller may stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. If the city she occupies matches Death's prediction she dies, if it mismatches she lives, and assume for the core case that the trip has no cost, so the only payoffs are life and death. Notice that as the traveller deliberates and becomes more confident that she will stay, staying comes to look fatal while fleeing looks lifesaving, and as she becomes more confident that she will flee, fleeing comes to look fatal while staying looks lifesaving; I will call this instability the Damascus flip. Compare evidential decision theory and causal decision theory on this case: for each, say whether the Damascus flip arises, whether the theory can issue a stable pure recommendation, a mixed recommendation, or no recommendation at all, and which theory gives the more plausible guidance about what the traveller has most reason to do, with your justification. Finally, since I want the notes to model good epistemic practice, say what would change your mind: identify the argument or refinement—perhaps a different way of linking the predictor's accuracy to the traveller's self-knowledge, or a challenge to the no-cost idealization—that would lead you to the opposite verdict, and note where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
