I'm an economist by training, currently doing methodological work for a health technology assessment body that has to rank interventions by their effect on people's lives, and over the last two years I've drifted into defending a fairly hard-line version of the Samuelsonian revealed-preference position: that utility is nothing over and above a numerical representation of an agent's choice dispositions (actual choices, or the choices the agent would make under suitably idealized conditions — full relevant information, no time pressure, no framing artifacts, stable attention, no strategic misrepresentation); that a preference no possible choice could ever disclose is not a preference but a metaphysical extra; and that consequently the utility numbers we feed into welfare calculations carry no normative weight until we bolt on a separate ethical premise saying that people's own choices are what should count. A philosopher who sits on the same committee tells me the view has been qualified into invulnerability: every candidate counterexample, she says, gets dissolved either by idealizing the choice conditions a bit further or by reclassifying the offending state as something other than a preference — a feeling, an urge, a judgement, an aspiration, a self-conception — so that nothing could in principle dislodge it. I want to know whether that is true, and if it is true, whether it is actually a defect in a view of this kind or just what a well-constructed research programme looks like.

So please act as a decision theorist and philosopher of economics who has thought hard about the choice-theoretic foundations of utility, think carefully, and brainstorm before committing to an answer: brute-force a wide list of candidate defeaters first and discard the ones that don't bite. Then give me the position's exposure map. Alongside the standard material — the gap between willingness-to-pay measures and self-reported well-being, adaptive preference formation under deprivation, intransitivity and stochastic choice, the fact that we need a bridge principle from choices to welfare anyway — I want you to take seriously two probes I've been turning over, and tell me whether each is a genuine test or a category mistake. Probe one, the transparent surrogate: suppose that within a decade we routinely delegate decisions to AI surrogates we can fully inspect, each with a learned internal value model that ranks options and a decision rule that systematically deviates from it (a safety regularizer, a risk constraint, a fine-tuning term pulling choices toward imitated human data). The surrogate's choices are stable and axiomatically well behaved; so is its internal value model; and the two diverge in a lawlike way, with the value model doing real predictive work — it predicts what the principal would choose if the constraint were lifted, and it predicts post-hoc satisfaction better than the choices do. The revealed-preference reading says the utility function is the one exposed by choices and the value model is a mere causal intermediary. What would have to be true for that to be the wrong read, and is it a fair question to put to a view that was only ever about what it means to ascribe utility to an agent? Probe two, choice-inert evaluative states: suppose a reproducible empirical dissociation in humans — a measurable value-tracking signal that responds to option features in a structurally clean way, drives learning and post-outcome experience, and is prevented from reaching the choice mechanism by an implementational bottleneck. Under my view that state simply is not a preference. Is that a principled application of the view or a retreat, and what would distinguish the two?
