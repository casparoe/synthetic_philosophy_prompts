I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I'm putting together a short internal talk about provenance: what we even mean by the real artifact when the thing in question can be copied without any loss at all. (Think reproducible builds: if anyone can re-run the build and get a byte-identical binary, in what sense is there an official binary?) The philosophical literature on fakes, forgeries and the value of originals looks like it is about exactly this, so what I need from you is a case that separates two accounts of it cleanly, built to order, plus a demonstration that it does what I asked.

The two accounts I want separated:

(H) The historical account: an object is the original, and a forgery lacks whatever the original has, in virtue of its causal-historical relation to the artist's making. It is the thing that came out of her act, or the appropriate descendant of that thing.

(P) The procedural account: an object is an instance of the work in virtue of satisfying the identity conditions the work itself lays down. The work is more like a score or a specification than like a particular chunk of matter: any correct execution is a genuine instance, and being the first execution does not by itself add anything.

The case I need:

A scenario, one paragraph and no more than 150 words, set in the ordinary present-day world (a museum, a collector, a digital conservation lab; no simulated universes, no uploads, no perfect predictors), involving a generative digital work — the artist writes a program and the piece is an output of it — such that H and P give different answers to the question of which of two objects is the original. Call the two objects A and B. At the end both must be on the table, both equally available and equally displayable, and there must be no relevant fact about them that either party is ignorant of: nobody lies, nobody is confused, and no certificate, contract, will, auction-house rule or market convention settles the question. The disagreement has to be purely about which account is correct.

Constraints, because I have watched discussions like this go wrong:

- B must not be a copy of A in the ordinary sense. It is produced by running the program again, not by reading off, duplicating or reconstructing A.
- The two objects must not differ in anything anyone can perceive, and neither may be degraded, incomplete, more fragile, or harder to access than the other.
- Nothing may turn on what the artist later said, intended, or would have wanted. The artist can be alive or dead, but she does not get to settle it. If you find yourself reaching for her testimony, the case is doing no work.
- Do not stipulate the verdicts. Each account has to reach its answer by applying its own principle to the facts you set up.

Then show me that it works:

1. For each account, state the verdict: which object is the original, what the other object is on that account (a fake, a copy, a second instance, something else), the one or two premises that generate the verdict, and in a single sentence what the account implies about whether a fully informed buyer who knew all the facts would be making a mistake to pay more for the one you called the original.
2. Name the single feature of the case that does the separating work, show what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and argue for it rather than deferring to me. My own guess is that it is something about determinism plus the fact that digital objects are identified by their content rather than by their matter, but I would rather be corrected than agreed with.
3. Give me the best attack on the case from whichever account comes out looking worse — not a strawman, the objection you would actually raise if you held that view.
4. Tell me plainly if you think the two accounts do not really come apart here. If the honest answer is that H and P agree once they are stated carefully, say so and explain why, instead of manufacturing a divergence.

Where I want you to be self-critical: mark the step in the argument where you are most likely to be wrong, and say what would change your mind — the consideration that would make you switch to the other account for this case, or abandon the case as a failure to separate them.
