I teach a first-year seminar on freedom and happiness, and I have put Dostoevsky's Legend of the Grand Inquisitor beside Huxley's Brave New World. My students will have read both, and I want a reading of the novel through the Legend that is faithful in both directions: neither the novel reduced to an illustration of a thesis, nor the Legend flattened into a slogan about bread and freedom.

Two distinctions I want held apart. First, the Inquisitor's case seems to me to have an empirical half — human beings cannot bear the gift of freedom and will hand it over for bread and for miracle, mystery, and authority — and a normative half, about what love of humanity permits: he secures them a modest, guiltless happiness, allows them to sin in secret, and takes their guilt upon himself. Second, the Inquisitor claims to leave the creature as it was made and to pay the price personally, whereas the World State reconstructs the creature — hatcheries, conditioning, sleep-teaching, soma — and its Controller, as far as I can see, pays nothing at all. I want to know which half of the argument the World State actually realizes, and whether its guiltless, managerially administered version is the completion of the Inquisitor's programme or its betrayal: is his self-sacrifice load-bearing for his self-conception, or is it self-flattery that a working version of his regime would expose?

I also want the resistance. Huxley's immediate targets are Fordism, behaviourism, distraction, engineered pleasure, and his citizens appear genuinely happy without any of the consolations the Inquisitor takes to be indispensable. So the novel may be answering the easier question — whether happiness can be manufactured — and sidestepping the harder one, whether freedom is an ineradicable burden for a creature that knows it possesses it. Say plainly whether the premise about human plasticity is asserted rather than argued, and what becomes of the debate if it is false.

Then the ending. John seems to be offered only two exits: collapse inside the World State, or exile among the primitives on an island, and after that death. Is that dilemma fair, or has the novel rigged it? What would a third way have to look like in order to be an answer to Mond rather than a retreat? And does John's destruction vindicate the Inquisitor's anthropology, or indict a world with no room for him? As I recall the scene, Mond tells John enough about his own past — a young man once near exile for unauthorized experiments who chose the Controllership instead — to present himself as a Savage who said yes; tell me if I have that wrong, and whether the reading helps or cheapens the exchange.

Finally, which scenes would you put in front of undergraduates, and what, if anything, does the novel manage to say about freedom and happiness that the Legend, being a monologue delivered to a silent Christ, cannot say?
