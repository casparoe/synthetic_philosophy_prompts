Hi — I host a monthly live ideas night in the back room of a bar: about sixty people, drinks, a flip chart, no projector, and a Q+A at the end where somebody always asks the question I should have answered during the talk. Next month I've got a twenty-minute slot and I want to do jumping out of the system, using Hofstadter's MU puzzle as the worked example. I read Gödel, Escher, Bach a long time ago and I don't want to just re-tell the chapter.

The setup, so we're on the same page: strings built out of M, I and U. You start with MI. Four rules — if a string ends in I you may add a U on the end; you may double everything after the initial M; three I's in a row may be swapped for a single U; two U's in a row may be deleted. The question on the board is whether MU can be produced from MI by repeated application of those rules. Audience: curious grown-ups, low math comfort but they can count; maybe a third of them are software engineers who've heard of Gödel and will be smug about it; nobody in the room will have seen the puzzle.

I don't need the talk written. I need a plan for how to build it.

Things I want you to settle:

The order. What has to be in place before the puzzle even lands — that the rules are mechanical and meaningless, that you don't get to aim at MU, the difference between applying a rule and saying something about every string the rules could ever produce — and in what sequence. Also: when do I say the phrase jumping out of the system out loud? I'm tempted to open with it as a tease, but I suspect I should only name it after they've done it once, and I don't know what to call it before that. Same question about vocabulary on the flip chart: do I put up axiom, theorem, derivation, rule, or do I just say start string and producible and keep it clean?

The carrying example. Does MU itself carry the whole twenty minutes, or does it need a physical warm-up first — a parity thing like the mutilated chessboard, or some switches puzzle — to give them the idea of something that never changes? My worry is that a parity warm-up makes them guess count something immediately and deflates the reveal, but without one they may have no grip on the idea at all. Pick one and say where it goes.

The misconceptions. Here's my list; tell me which ones I've missed and, more importantly, the exact beat where each should be killed:
- they'll try to solve it, fail, and conclude nobody's found it yet or that a computer will crack it eventually
- they'll guess the invariant is that the number of I's is always odd — doubling kills that, and I'm not sure whether to let them hit the wrong guess or head it off
- jumping out gets read as cheating, i.e. using a move you're not allowed, instead of reasoning at the level of the rules and what they can produce
- unprovable in the system collapses into false, or into something mystical about broken formal systems
- someone will say just add a fifth rule — which I think is actually the smartest thing anyone can say, and I want to place it deliberately rather than let it ambush me
- the smug engineer: so it's basically the halting problem

The transfer test. I knocked together this thing and I want to know whether it tests what I think it does, or whether it's broken: a bag with red and blue marbles, you start with one red and no blues. Legal moves: double the number of reds; remove exactly three reds; add or remove blue marbles freely. Three targets to rule on — zero reds and two blues; four reds and one blue; three reds and no blues. My thinking is that the reachable one stops them from just answering impossible to everything, and that the three-red one is the real trap because the obvious route is get to three, then remove three. Does it work? Is it too close to MU to be a real transfer test? What's the smallest change that fixes it if it's off? And should I run it as a hands-up vote before I reveal the MU argument — I like it as a genuine prediction, but then I've got two unsolved puzzles on the board at once.

The take-home line. One sentence about jumping out of the system that the room can repeat to a friend and that cannot collapse into think outside the box — that phrase must not appear in it.

And the Q+A landmine: give me a fifteen-second answer to so is this Gödel's theorem, and tell me whether to place it before or after the transfer test. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to explain in twenty minutes, with a one-line way of refusing that doesn't sound like I'm dodging.

Format: first a table, one row per beat, seven to nine rows, columns exactly — Beat | Minute | Move (what I do, what goes on the flip chart) | Why here (what it depends on, what it unlocks) | Misconception planted or killed. Then a JSON object with these fields: carrying_example (string, under 25 words), warm_up (name it, or the string none, plus a one-line reason), invariant_line (the exact sentence I write on the chart), joot_one_liner (string, under 25 words, no use of the words escape, cheat or outside), vocabulary (array of the terms I should actually put on the board), transfer_test (object with verdict, smallest_fix, and targets as an array of objects with state, reachable as true or false, and reason in one line), misconception_map (array of objects with misconception, beat_number, handling), engineer_line (string), godel_question (object with answer and placement, before or after, plus why), refusals (array of objects with topic and one_line_refusal), check (string: what I should hear from the room, other than applause, that tells me it landed), order_fixes (array of strings — anything in my assumed sequence that's in the wrong order, or that twenty minutes can't hold). One sentence per string field, booleans as true/false, no nesting beyond what I've listed.

If you think something in my plan is definately wrong, say so in order_fixes rather than quietly planning around it.
