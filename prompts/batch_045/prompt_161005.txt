My reconstruction of it, more or less: assertion is governed by a rule the way a game is, and the only question is which rule. Williamson's answer is that you may assert p only if you know p; the rivals are that the rule is just that you must assert only what's true, or only what it's reasonable for you to believe. But the truth rule and the knowledge rule are arguably the same view in different words, since knowledge is just true belief with the luck taken out, so a lot of the fighting is verbal. The knowledge rule does have one real advantage: it explains why saying it's raining and I don't know that it is sounds absurd, which the weaker rules apparently can't explain. It's still too demanding, though, because knowing something requires being certain of it, and nobody is certain of anything empirical — so on the knowledge rule almost every assertion we make violates the rule, which is why most people in the debate have moved to the reasonable-belief rule instead. And the knowledge rule gets false assertions wrong: if you assert something false but you had perfectly good evidence, the knowledge rule has to say you did nothing wrong, whereas the truth rule correctly says you did.

My own hunches, so you know where I'm coming from: the "true belief with the luck taken out" line has to be doing something sneaky, because I take it the truth rule and the knowledge rule come apart on lottery-type cases where the speaker asserts something that happens to be true but couldn't have known it. And the certainty bit smells like a strawman of a view that's supposed to be anti-skeptical — though maybe I'm wrong and it's a fair reading of what fallibilism about knowledge actually commits you to. I'm much less sure about the last bit, the one about false assertions, and I honestly don't know whether that claim about where the profession has moved is anywhere near right; it sounds like something a popularizer would assert to make the story feel like it has a direction.
