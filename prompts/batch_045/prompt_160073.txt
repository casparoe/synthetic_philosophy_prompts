Write the "Description of Business" and "Risk Factors" sections of a prospectus for the initial public offering of a fictional clearinghouse: a Moral Trade Exchange, where people who disagree about what is good trade promises with each other — you perform an act that I think is good and I perform an act that you think is good, and each of us gains by our own lights without anyone having to win the argument. About 700 words, deadpan throughout, with a mock ticker symbol and an invented founder and compliance officer; do not name or target any real person.

The comedy has to come from getting the structure of moral trade right, not from generic finance satire. Work in at least: the basic two-party gain-by-own-lights trade and why it looks like genuine gains from trade even though nothing either party values has been created; the worry that both sides are double counting the same act, recast in the prospectus as a feature (the same deed booked as an asset twice, once on each ledger); counterparty risk from value drift, where a counterparty changes their moral mind and the promise they sold you becomes worthless to them and worthless to you.

Then push the logic one step past where the literature takes it, which is where the piece should bite. Invent one flagship product: a closed round-trip trade, in which A performs for B, B for C, and C for A, so that every participant gains by their own lights and the exchange collects a spread on every leg, while the net effect of the cycle is worse than doing nothing by each of the three views involved. The prospectus must explain, in perfect earnestness, why the exchange is forbidden by its own charter from netting the cycle out across views. The reductio should arrive without any winking: the filing's revenue depends on moral disagreement persisting, so the risk disclosures have to circle the one thing they cannot say — that the firm has an interest in nobody ever resolving the disagreement, and that its most valuable clients are the ones whose moral beliefs are most likely false.

Close with a short note (around 150 words) on where the piece takes liberties: which feature of actual moral-trade arguments you exaggerated, where the satire leans on a contested reading — in particular whether trades of this kind really are gains from trade rather than a form of bluffing, or an exploitation of ambiguity in what counts as good — which single claim about moral trade you are least confident you have represented correctly, and how confident you are overall that the target is being got right.
