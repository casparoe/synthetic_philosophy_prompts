I am applying for a three-month summer research grant and need a two-page project description for a paper on the badness of death. I have a half-formed idea and would like help turning it into a proposal I can submit.

The background, as I understand it: the Epicurean argument holds that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, on the grounds that the time at which the person exists and the time at which death is present never coincide — there is no subject there to be harmed and no time at which the harm befalls anyone. Deprivationist accounts answer that death is bad because of what it prevents, and that a harm's being bad for someone does not require that person to exist at the time of the harm. That move then generates a second, apparently compulsory question: if death is bad for you, when is it bad for you? The live answers say, roughly, before your death, after your death, or at no time in particular, and each faces a familiar difficulty — the first seems to make the badness depend on anticipations a person may never have had, the second seems to require a subject who by then does not exist, and the third looks like it abandons the claim that the harm occurs at any time at all.

My half-formed thought is that all three share an assumption none of them argues for: that being bad for a subject is time-indexed in the first place. Call the alternative subject-holism: the misfortune of death is borne primarily by a life considered as a whole, and only derivatively by the person at times, so that the timing question is not hard but ill-formed, and the Epicurean argument fails by asking when something happens of something that has no time. I want the proposal tested against two cases I have been turning over: first, prenatal versus posthumous nonexistence, which the Epicurean treats alike but which we manifestly do not treat alike; second, an interrupted life — a person annihilated and reconstituted fifty years later, with a subject on both sides of the gap, no anticipation of it, and no psychological continuity across it — where the rival timing views seem to pull apart even though the Epicurean complaint about a missing subject does not get a grip.

Please write the proposal with: the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; an explanation of why the existing positions leave it open, including why subject-holism is not simply one more entry in the timing debate; the approach, month by month, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternative of arguing directly for one of the three timing views; the range of conclusions the project could reach and what would decide among them; an honest statement of what would count as the project failing; and a realistic assessment of scope for three months. Refer to positions by their content and do not supply citations, a bibliography, or named authors — I will fill those in myself.
