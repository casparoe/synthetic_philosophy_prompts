My friend Priya did her PhD in epistemology — lottery paradox, preface paradox, that whole corner of the literature — and now works as a data scientist, and she has a track record of verdicts that I've been collecting because I definately can't work out what rule she's running. All of these are her answers to "does the person know / is it rational for them to believe it":

- single lottery ticket, one in fourteen million, the draw has happened but the numbers aren't posted yet: you don't know you lost, and it isn't rational to believe you lost either.
- reading the departure board across the concourse in good light: yes, knows the train is at 11:04.
- a friend who's right about this kind of thing nine times out of ten texts that the 11:04 is cancelled: yes, knows.
- the preface case: a historian writes a twelve-chapter monograph, checks every chapter twice, and also writes in the preface that the book almost certainly contains at least one error somewhere. Priya says the historian knows each chapter's central claim and also knows the book contains an error, and that the apparent contradiction is the same shape as the lottery case.
- blue-bus style case: 90% of the buses in the city belong to one company, one of them hits a cyclist, no one saw which, the base rate is the only evidence. No: nobody knows it was a company bus.
- rare disease, 1 in 10,000, a test that's 99% accurate in both directions, positive result, doctor relays it. No: the patient doesn't know, and shouldn't start acting as if she does.

I pointed out that 90% is way lower than the lottery ticket's 99.99999%, and she said the cases aren't the same because in the lottery you know in advance that exactly one ticket wins and you have no evidence about which one, whereas the forecast is evidence about tomorrow specifically.
