I'm in my final year of secondary school and I run our school's philosophy society. We are doing a session on the question of what exactly ought to be equal — equal happiness, equal income and goods, equal real freedoms, equal standing above a decent threshold — and I would like to build a short diagnostic questionnaire rather than hold another reading discussion: eight questions, each a concrete case with three answer options, plus a scoring key that sorts people by the answers they give rather than by the label they would pick if asked outright. My classmates have no philosophy background, so every question must be answerable from the case itself, with no jargon and with no option phrased as a theory name, but each one should be a point at which the actual positions come apart.

Please build the questions around cases of roughly these kinds: two pupils given identical equipment where one has a disability that makes the same equipment achieve less; a pupil who has deliberately cultivated an expensive taste and is now miserable on an ordinary budget; a pupil in a school culture where girls have been taught to expect less and who reports being perfectly content with less; a pupil who is an extreme night owl and loses about two hours of effective learning time a day to a morning timetable that suits nearly everyone else; a case in which everyone is well above a decent minimum but some have vastly more; and a case in which the only way to make things equal is to take from the better-off without giving anything to anyone.

The leaves I need in the key are equality of welfare (with its subjective, desire-based, and objective-list variants split out wherever the questions allow), equality of resources understood so that handicaps are compensated but expensive tastes are not, the Rawlsian primary-goods answer, equality of capabilities, luck egalitarianism, and sufficientarianism. For each question, say which option each of those views is committed to; where two of them must give the same answer, flag that the questionnaire cannot separate them and say what further question would be needed to do so. Then list two or three combinations of answers that no named view occupies, saying briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unoccupied so far. The finished handout should let someone be told which view they hold because of what they said about cases, and should tell me which of their answers did the work.
