I'm finishing a novel whose narrator is a welfare economist seconded to a consumer-protection agency, and the scene I cannot get right is the one where she has to testify, in plain language, about whether the choices people actually make tell us anything about what is good for them. While researching the book I sat in on a first-year micro theory seminar in which the whole of preference seemed to get compressed into a board full of inequalities: the weak and strong axioms, the generalized axiom, and the theorem that a finite pile of observations is consistent with maximizing some utility function exactly if the pile satisfies the axiom — after which utility is only a labelling of an ordering, rescalable by any increasing transformation, and carries no information at all about how well off anyone is. My narrator needs something more than that and less than a shrug, and she has to say it in about a minute of dialogue in a hearing room while hostile counsel reads out cases. So I have been working backwards: I wrote down my own gut verdicts on the cases, hoping the principle behind them would show itself.

My verdicts, on the cases counsel keeps throwing at her. (a) The wine shelf: she picks the middle bottle from a shelf of three, picks the cheap one when the middle is sold out, and picks the expensive one again when a worse-but-pricier bottle is added beside it — I refuse to say her taste changed; the shelf is doing the work, not she. (b) Across three separate pairwise menus she takes x over y, y over z, and z over x — I want to say at least one of those three observations is not a readout of a preference, but I do not want to say she has no preferences. (c) The only dish on the menu: taking it reveals nothing, though I am uneasy, since she could have walked out. (d) She skips an excellent Sichuan restaurant because a friend told her it had closed — that is belief, not taste, and I want the preference to be the one she would have with the belief corrected. (e) Wallet to the man with the knife: obviously not a preference. (f) The bottle she buys every evening, would rather not buy, has tried not to buy, and would pay someone to prevent her buying — I still want to say this purchase reveals something about her, while denying it tells us she is better off for it; and that sits badly with (e), since the difference between a knife at her throat and a craving inside her skin is exactly the difference I am failing to state. (g) The lockbox, the deleted app, the car keys handed to a friend before the party: I want the choice to remove her own future options to count for more than the choice she makes in the moment, which sits badly with (f), and also with the separate fact that she sometimes pays extra to keep her options open — refundable fares, the bigger menu — and I take that to be a real preference too. (h) Two women who adapted: one lowered her sights so early and so thoroughly that she says, sincerely, she is content with the smaller share and would choose it again; the other, after a stroke, reorganised her whole life around things she never used to care about and is content as well. I discount the first and honour the second, and from the outside I cannot point to the difference. (i) The union grapes and the vote cast out of duty: she acts against her own preference, and I do not want to rescue the theory by saying she must have preferred the worse outcome after all — the choice expresses a commitment, and I want the account to have room for that. (j) The pension default: staying in at three per cent reveals almost nothing; the ones who actively raise their contribution to six reveal a great deal; and the ones who stay and would defend staying by saying they trust whoever set it, I cannot place. (k) The mug: she will not pay five dollars for one and will not sell hers for ten. I do not want to conclude she has no utility for mugs, and I do not want to pick either answer as the anomaly. What I keep coming back to as a principle is something like: a choice reveals a preference only if it would survive being made again with true beliefs, unhurried, with the alternatives vividly present, and with her later self not paying to get out of it — which I fear is circular, because it defines the preference as the one she would have after cognitive repair, and then nothing she actually does could ever contradict it.

I also need to know what the resulting view commits my narrator to about the meaning of utility: whether utility is a bookkeeping device that merely represents an ordering, a measure of how well off someone is, or something that splits the difference, and what breaks if she answers one way rather than the other.
