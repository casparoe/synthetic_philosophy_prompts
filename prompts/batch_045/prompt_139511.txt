I've been reading Anarchy, State and Utopia (1974) with two friends from my old polisci cohort — we meet on zoom, nobody does the reading, we argue for an hour anyway — and I've realised that the position I keep defending is really a pile of separate claims I've picked up in different arguments, and I have no idea whether they fit together. For context: I'm not a philosopher, I work in health data, and I got dragged into this because I said out loud that I found the Wilt Chamberlain argument persuasive and then had to start answering questions. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) The Chamberlain argument basically works. If D1 is just and a million people each voluntarily hand over a quarter of their ticket price to watch Chamberlain play, the resulting D2 is just as well, and the fact that it wrecks whatever distributional pattern you liked is not by itself an objection. Holding a pattern in place requires continuously blocking consensual transfers, and I don't see why the shape of the distribution should beat what people chose to do.

(2) But I read the Lockean proviso pretty hard. Appropriation is only permissible if no one is left worse off than under a suitable counterfactual baseline, and "suitable" doesn't mean just "nobody's existing stuff or bodily security got worse" — I mean something closer to a baseline where unowned resources are available to everyone on roughly equal terms. I'm also inclined to say the proviso has to be re-applied over time, not only checked once at the moment of first appropriation, since what a resource is worth changes with technology.

(3) I think you can run that same non-worseness test one level up, at the level of whole systems of rules: a tax-and-transfer scheme that leaves virtually everyone better off than the nearest feasible alternative scheme seems to pass it. This is why I've never been moved by Nozick's claim that taxing earnings is morally on a par with forced labour.

(4) Where a holding does violate the proviso, I think the holder owes compensation going forward, and I want the amount owed to track how much other people currently have — i.e. sensitive to the present distribution and not only to the original wrong and its direct descendants.

(5) A verdict I defend constantly on these calls: a billionaire who got rich purely through voluntary trades — no fraud, no state-granted monopoly, no theft anywhere in the chain — has acted permissibly and owes nobody anything, even in a world with brutal poverty. But I also think a state that fenced off what was once common owes its citizens something like a resource dividend out of general taxation.

(6) Methodological thing: I treat full-blown self-ownership as optional for this package. I think the real work is done by the transfer principle plus the proviso, and I'm not sure the stronger premise is doing anything independently.
