Hey — I'm a backend engineer (mostly Go and Python; no philosophy background beyond a couple of blog rabbit holes) and I run a lunchtime paper club at work. Usually 10–12 people, we get a 90 minute room but realistically 15 of that is people arriving with food, so call it 70–75 usable minutes. Whiteboard plus a projector, no budget. Most of the room writes backend or infra code. Two people poked at a proof assistant (Lean, Coq) for a weekend and forgot most of it. One guy actually has a math degree: he'll check out if I go slow and will probably try to *solve* the game instead of playing it.

We did a session a few weeks back on proofs-as-programs / the Curry-Howard thing and it went badly in one specific way: everyone kept saying "sure, but obviously either P holds or it doesn't — whats the big deal?" Nobody felt the pull of refusing that, and I ended up just asserting that some mathematicians decline to use it, which convinced no one.

So: design a game or structured activity for this group where they run into the wall themselves. Real rules, roles, materials, and some kind of scoring/payoff that doesn't need money. The shape I want: people are mid-argument, they reach for a case split on some proposition nobody has a decision procedure for (whether a program halts, whether a big number has some property — whatever actually works), and the rules make that move fail or cost them something. Afterwards "P or not-P is not free" should be something they bumped into, not something I told them. No lecture up front, and I'd rather none of them hear "intuitionistic", "constructive", or "excluded middle" until the debrief.

Two mechanics I want built in, which I haven't seen in any writeup:

1. Asserting a disjunction has to be a *commitment*, not a claim about the world. If you assert A or B, you're on the hook to say which one when someone asks, and "one of them must be, I'll find out later" is not an answer the rules accept. I'm half-aware this is roughly the BHK reading where an or is a tagged witness and a negation is a function from a witness to absurdity — that's the version I want baked in, but tell me if baking it in begs the question I'm trying to get them to feel.
2. One single structural rule that separates intuitionistic play from classical play, something concrete and point-at-able. My guess was an asymmetry about what you may come back to: classical players get to save and branch (case split = fork two branches, prove the target in each, merge), while intuitionistic players have to play one continuous run with no rewind. If that's a bad way to cash it out, use whatever single rule actually does the work — but it should be one rule people can point at afterwards and say "that's the whole difference, right there."

One worry, and it's the reason I'm asking instead of improvising: I don't want this to secretly teach that constructivists are just being careful about ignorance, i.e. that "P or not-P fails" really means "nobody knows which." If the game is just classical logic with a hidden bit the referee knows, it's teaching the wrong lesson. Tell me how the design avoids that — or tell me if it can't.

Deliverable: rules and materials, how to split the room into roles and what the referee does, a timeline with rough minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the actual logical points (including where the math-degree guy's shortcut would have landed them). Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the guy solving the game, the person who argues with the referee, the two who team up — and how I handle each without turning into a lecture.

Be honest about what the game can't reproduce. I suspect it's something about the gap between the rules of the game and whatever the semantics are supposed to be, but you tell me; I'd rather get "here's the bit only the facilitator can supply" than a tidy activity that overclaims.

And since I'm out of my depth: add a short note on which reading of constructivism the activity commits you to (the proof-as-construction one? the growing-possible-worlds one? something else?), where it takes liberties, and which parts are contested. Please push back plainly if the request is confused — if "make them feel it" can't be done without smuggling in the epistemic reading I said I didn't want, or if the two mechanics above are in tension with each other, say so instead of politely handing me something polished that teaches the wrong thing.
