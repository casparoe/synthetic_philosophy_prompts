Two colleagues of mine sit on an internal review panel for a regional health authority. The authority is about to adopt a protocol for triaging suspected sepsis in emergency departments, and the case for it rests on two observational studies reporting large mortality reductions. Both of them have read both studies. Ada thinks the protocol will save lives; Tomas thinks the effect is probably null and possibly negative. They are epidemiologists of comparable seniority with comparable track records on questions of this kind. Please treat the medical question as a placeholder: I have altered the details of the file, and what I want adjudicated is their argument about disagreement, not sepsis.

1. Ada: We are epistemic peers — same training, same evidence, comparable calibration on questions like this. The equal-weight view says that when peers disagree, each should move substantially toward the other. I have taken that seriously: I came in around 85 per cent confident that the protocol works and I am now near 55. You have not moved at all, which means either you have not understood what peerhood requires of you, or you are applying to me a standard you would not accept applied to yourself.

2. Tomas: Conciliationism cannot be the whole story, because it does not survive application to itself. If I must split the difference with any peer who disagrees with me, then I must split the difference with the colleagues who reject conciliationism — in which case I can never confidently apply it. Worse, it seems to tell a jury that has deliberated together for two days to abandon its verdict upon learning that one member still dissents. And in any case we are not peers here: I have spent three months on this file and you have spent a week. My evidence is better, not merely differently distributed.

3. Ada: Your second point is the dangerous one. Every reflective person who disagrees with someone believes they have thought about it more carefully, so if "I have looked harder" suffices to dissolve peerhood, then the equal-weight view never binds anyone who actually disagrees — it collapses into steadfastness for precisely the people it was meant to constrain. Notice too that on your account the judgement about who has looked harder is made by the person with an interest in making it.

4. Tomas: But my claim is not a self-assessment; it is a first-order claim about what your evidence covers. I can name what you have not examined: the pre-intervention trends in the neighbouring district, which run the other way, and the fact that the pilot was run in a teaching hospital with roughly twice the nursing staff of the hospitals that would adopt the protocol. I am not claiming to be cleverer than you; I am claiming that your evidence base has holes I can point at. There is a second problem with your peer premise as well. Our shared evidence is thinner than "we have read the same two studies" suggests, because the second study re-analyses the first study's data under a different specification. Some of what looks like independent agreement between us is the same input counted twice.

5. Ada: If the gap is that I have seen less, the right response is not to hold your position but to close the gap: show me the pre-trends and the staffing figures. If I still disagree after seeing them, that disagreement is far more informative than our present one, because it will no longer be explicable by my having missed something. And your point about duplicated evidence does not help you: if the shared base is thin, both of us should be dragged back toward the prior, and since I have already moved and you have not, you are the one who should end up nearer my side.

6. Tomas: Perhaps. But there is a difference between what we should believe and what we should do. The board votes on Thursday. Suppose we agree the chance the protocol works is fifty-fifty. That still does not tell us how to vote, because a protocol that backfires does not merely fail to help — it reallocates scarce nursing time away from patients who were being treated successfully. A shared fifty-fifty credence plus an asymmetric downside does not add up to abstention.

Please work through the exchange move by move: for each of the six, say what is right and what is wrong in it, and assign it a grade (clearly right / more right than wrong / more wrong than right / clearly mistaken) with a sentence or two of justification. Then give an overall verdict in two or three sentences, including anything both of them are missing.

Two further requests. First, a hard limit of 500 words in total, number the replies 1–6, and put the overall verdict last. Second, and more important to me: I would rather be told the truth than handed a tidy answer. Where a move rests on a distinction that does not really hold, or on a claim about the epistemology of disagreement that is genuinely contested, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over; and tell me briefly how much confidence your own verdict deserves and what would most readily dislodge it. If you think the dispute as I have staged it is confused, or that adjudicating it properly requires more knowledge of the first-order evidence than I have given you, say that instead of answering politely.
