I'm a PhD student in behavioural economics — my dissertation is on commitment devices and present-biased preferences — and I host a graduate interview series in which we spend an hour pressing a single visitor on one thesis rather than touring their career. Next month's guest is a philosopher whose current project defends what I'll call the policy-first account of rational irrationality. As I understand her position, it has four parts: (i) in Parfit's hitchhiker case nothing is irrational at the level of the act — the act of paying once you are safely in the desert town is at worst a symptom; (ii) the only genuine requirement on the agent was to be, before the ride, someone who would pay, and that requirement is satisfied by a disposition rather than by an act; (iii) the disposition does its normative work only if it is not fully revisable at the moment of action — call this the grip condition — so the agent in the town is not free to reconsider it from scratch; (iv) every other apparent case of rational irrationality (the cultivated optimism of a founder, the resolutions of someone trying to quit, the self-deception that makes a cooperative arrangement possible) has this same structure, so there is no case in which it is rational, at one and the same time, to do what you then have most reason not to do.

I want eight to ten questions, ordered, that stop her from restating (i)–(iv) and force her to show how the account behaves where it is thin. For each question give me: (a) the question as I would actually say it aloud — one or two sentences, plain enough for a general philosophy audience, no notation; (b) the answer she is most likely to give, reconstructed in her own vocabulary and as charitably as you can manage; (c) a follow-up keyed to that specific answer, pushing on its weakest joint, together with a note on what would count as a real concession as opposed to a restatement; (d) one line on whether the question is doing independent work or is redundant with another one on your list.

Please make sure the set covers, at minimum:

1. The transfer problem. Why the rationality of the disposition does or does not transfer to the act it produces, and what the account says about an agent whose disposition is rational but whose acts are, on every occasion, acts she has no reason to perform.
2. The acquisition and advice problem. What the account actually recommends to the person in the car, given that dispositions are not adopted by fiat; whether cultivate the right disposition is itself advice it can be rational to follow; and what happens if the only available route to the disposition is one the agent knows will make her act against the balance of reasons later.
3. A variant I have been using in my own work and would like her to address: the graded-detector case. The driver's read on hitchhikers is a continuous score — her true intention strength plus mean-zero noise of known variance — and she stops only if the score clears a threshold; at the town the hitchhiker may hand over any amount between nothing and the agreed hundred dollars, with no consequences either way. Ask her what intention strength rationality requires (if more is always better ex ante, what caps it?) and what content the intention must have: must it be an intention to pay the full amount, or does any content suffice provided the strength clears the threshold? Press on whether the second option lets rationality come apart entirely from what the agent ends up doing, and on whether the account therefore has a sorites problem about how much disposition is enough.
4. The individuation problem. How finely the required disposition is typed — a disposition to repay benefits, to keep promises, to be the sort of person who repays, or to be read a certain way by this particular driver — and whether the coarse version is over-inclusive in ways the account cannot absorb while the fine version reintroduces the acquisition problem from item 2.
5. The collapse worry. Once a policy is modelled as a plan with some probability of being executed, whether the policy-first account is distinguishable in substance from ordinary expected-value reasoning over policies, and whether the grip condition just relocates the original puzzle one level up: can it be rational to be unable to reconsider something you know you would do better to reconsider?
6. The doxastic analogue. Whether the account extends to beliefs, given that beliefs are not acquired the way dispositions to act are, and whether the extension helps the account or shows that the action cases were doing all the work.
7. One question that connects her view to my field: whether the observed demand for commitment devices, and the fact that people knowingly restrict their own future options, is evidence for the policy-first picture or is equally well explained by a view that keeps rational assessment at the level of acts.
8. A closing question about what would change her mind, framed so that a refinement of the distinction between policies and acts does not count as an answer.

At least two of the questions should be built so that her standard move — that this is just the familiar point about the locus of rational assessment being the policy rather than the act — either has to do real work or visibly fails; please flag which ones these are and why. Finally, tell me the order you would ask them in and what the ordering is meant to achieve, and which single question you would cut if we run five minutes short.
