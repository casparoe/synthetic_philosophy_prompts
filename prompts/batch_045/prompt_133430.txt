Here's the question we all answered:

In class we reconstructed Pascal's wager as a dominance argument, not as a piece of expected-value arithmetic: either God exists or he doesn't; wagering on God does at least as well as wagering against in every possibility and better in one; and this is supposed to work no matter how tiny the chance of God's existence is, so long as it isn't zero. Answer all three, a few sentences each.

(1) The many-gods objection: there are lots of possible gods, including ones who reward exactly the people who refused to wager and punish the people who wagered for prudential reasons. So (the objection goes) for every option there is some possibility in which it does terribly, nothing dominates, and the wager gives you no advice at all. Does this refute the wager as we reconstructed it, or only some weaker version of it? Be explicit about what the objection would have to show in order to work.

(2) Suppose your confidence that God exists isn't a single number but a range — you'd only say it falls somewhere between, say, 1% and 30%, with no sharp cutoff at either end (call this a fuzzy credence). Does that cause trouble for the wager? Be careful to say which version of the argument it actually touches, and why.

(3) Couldn't you just hedge — go through the motions in several religions at once and collect whichever payoff turns out to be on offer? Does hedging get you out of the argument's problems, or does it create new ones?

Here's the answer I'm marking:

(1) The many-gods objection doesn't do much damage. Adding a pile of other possible gods doesn't change the fact that wagering on God does at least as well as not wagering in every case: the case where God doesn't exist comes out the same either way, and the case where he does exist favors wagering. The extra gods are just extra possibilities stacked on the "God doesn't exist" side of the ledger, so they can't help the objector. And even if there are many gods, we can just compare them by probability and wager on the most likely one, which is still the Christian God, and then Pascal's reasoning applies to it as before. The wager also isn't really about infinite payoffs in a strict mathematical sense — Pascal's point is just that the reward is so large that any chance at all beats everything finite, which is common sense.

(2) The fuzzy credence point is where the argument really breaks. If your confidence is only a range instead of a number, you can't do the calculation at all — you can't multiply a range by an infinite reward and get any answer. And since Pascal's argument is essentially a cost-benefit calculation, anything that makes the arithmetic impossible is fatal to it. This is worse than the tiny-probability worry, because with a range you don't even have a number to plug in, so you can't recover the conclusion that betting on God is better by any amount.

(3) Hedging basically solves the rest of it. You can show up at services, do the rituals, and act the part in several religions at once, so whichever god turns out to be real you've done what was asked, and it costs you maybe an hour a week. Going through the motions is cheap and it covers every bet, so the dominance argument isn't even needed — you can just take out insurance on all the gods at once and only lose a bit of time if none of them exist. Even if you only end up pretending, pretending is what the wager asked for anyway, so the argument goes through either way.
