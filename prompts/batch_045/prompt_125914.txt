I spent a career in chemical kinetics — nothing philosophical about it, beyond a long habit of asking whether an equation being derived is a substantive claim about the world or a consistency requirement in disguise — and I have only in retirement begun sitting in on a reading group in philosophy. The group has been working through the family of arguments meant to establish that a rational agent must maximize expected utility: you exhibit a preference structure that violates one or another of the axioms — completeness, transitivity, independence, continuity — and then you show that an agent with that structure can be walked around a cycle and end up with less of what she wanted, or can be induced to pay, again and again, for the same thing. I now have to advise the board of a small foundation I sit on, which can fund roughly three one-year fellowships, or if we prefer one fellowship renewed for three years, in the normative philosophy of rational choice; and I have argued that the money should go to this cluster of coherence arguments rather than to, say, formal epistemology more broadly. That means I owe the board an agenda rather than a survey: which open questions here most deserve the three person-years, and in what order? Assume the fellows are philosophers working alone, with no new empirical data, and that the board's aims are, first, durable conceptual progress on whether and how coherence considerations can underwrite expected utility maximization, and, second and more faintly, the possibility that any of this eventually changes how economists or policy analysts model agents who fail to maximize. Please tell me if the ordering would differ depending on which of those two aims we weight.

I should also say where my own doubts lie, because I want to know which of them are live research questions and which are confusions I ought to be talked out of. First, a recurrence worry: the exploitation arguments all seem to require that the agent actually traverse the cycle, and in my old field I would have asked whether the relevant process is recurrent — if each of the choice situations involved arises once and never again, or rarely and unrepeatably, it is unclear to me what the pump extracts, so perhaps these arguments quietly presuppose that an agent's life is a long enough sequence for every kind of choice to recur, and the real question is whether there is a version of the argument that does without that presupposition. Second, a cheap-commitment worry: if the agent can hire a custodian to hold her assets and refuse all trades, or, more weakly, can adopt a standing policy of declining any trade that forms part of a cycle, then for a small fee the pump never starts; and if so, the argument looks to be about the costs of self-management rather than about the axioms, which is a rather different conclusion from the one it is advertised as supporting. Third, a bridge worry: what the pump removes is money, and to get from ending with less money to ending up worse off one needs a premise, and if that premise is that more money is always preferred, then one may have assumed a large part of what was to be proved — so I would like to know whether there is a genuine reformulation in terms of what I would call a utility pump, in which the thing extracted is something the agent herself concedes she values, and whether the availability of such a reformulation is itself among the open questions.

What I want back is a defended priority ordering, not a list. For each question: what makes it worth a year of someone's career; which other questions its answer is upstream of, such that settling it would settle or dissolve several others; which questions you would push down because their answers would not propagate, or because they need formal machinery or empirical input that a lone philosopher will not have in twelve months; and what kind of result, if somebody produced it, would move a question up or down the list. I would also like you to say plainly if any of the questions I have gestured at are pseudo-questions, or are questions only because of an ambiguity, and to be explicit about the criteria you are trading off — importance, tractability, neglectedness, upstreamness — and about where you are sacrificing one for another. I do not need the names of papers or people; I can chase those up myself, and in any case I am more interested in whether there is an argument to be had than in who has already had it.
