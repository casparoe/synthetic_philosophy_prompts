A philosopher of mind I work with, who specializes in invertebrate welfare, assigns welfare weights on a 0–1 scale (adult human = 1) one case at a time. Her verdicts so far: adult honeybee 0.05; jumping spider 0.04; shore crab 0.03; fruit fly 0.02; adult mosquito 0.01; octopus 0.6; zebrafish 0.3; the nematode C. elegans, a tick, and a slime mould 0, though she grants that the slime mould solves nutrient-routing problems no single cell of it could; a honeybee colony treated as a single system 0, though she grants that it solves problems no single bee could; a domestic thermostat 0; a tabular gridworld reinforcement learner trained on a real reward signal 0; a cortical organoid of roughly a hundred thousand human neurons with no sensory input and no motor output 0. She insists she is not grading intelligence, behavioural flexibility, or information processing, and she declines to grade current language models at all.

What interests me is that every verdict above concerns a single organism, or something she denies is one. Nothing in her track record forces her to say what happens when the number of subjects and the quantity of affect come apart, and the number matters enormously downstream: our group is about to score a pest-control intervention affecting on the order of ten to the eighth insects, and one small subject per insect is not remotely the same total as several affective streams per insect.

The two cases I would put to her:

1. Fission. A hawkmoth whose brain hemispheres have been disconnected. Each hemisphere receives its own sensory afferents, drives the limbs on its own side, and independently acquires an odour valence; the two learn opposite preferences and pull the animal in opposite directions. One moth, no commissural integration, two competing affective streams.
2. Fusion. Two hawkmoths whose ventral nerve cords have been joined so that nociceptive afferents from either body reach both brains, with the pair flying as one coordinated animal that learns odour aversions and trades a shock off against a food reward as a single agent. Two nervous systems, one globally broadcast input, one coordinated output.

Predict her number for each case. Then say what rule she is actually running. I can see at least three that fit every verdict above — a unified-subject or integration condition, graded by degree of unification; an affective condition, graded by how much valenced, trade-off-governed motivation the system contains; and a precautionary heuristic keyed to whole-organism similarity to animals we already treat as sentient, with the numbers doing no real work — and each has a story about why the gridworld learner gets zero. Tell me which of the three you favour and why, whether her weight is calibrated per subject or per organism, which of my two cases is the more probative and what feature of it does the work, and if the pair does not separate the leading candidates, what further case would. Finish with what a defender of whichever rule comes out worst here would say about my two cases.
