I spent rather more than thirty years as a field ecologist, mostly on grassland and wetland bird populations, and a good part of that time arguing — successfully, I thought — that protecting and restoring habitat was among the least controversial things a person could do with their working life. Since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and I have drifted into the small literature on wild-animal suffering, which has unsettled that assumption in a way I did not expect. The argument that did the work on me is demographic rather than sentimental: in many taxa, especially the short-lived, high-fecundity ones that make up most of the individuals in any ecosystem, the overwhelming majority of lives end very early and badly, so the total quantity of suffering in a habitat is dominated by juvenile mortality rather than by the welfare of the adults one actually sees. If populations are regulated near the ceiling set by their habitat, then what matters for the total is not the fate of particular animals but the size and composition of the population the habitat sustains. I now hold something like this: for a wide range of habitats, reducing carrying capacity plausibly reduces the total suffering the system contains, and conservationists who assume the sign of their work is morally favourable are making an inference that the demography does not obviously support. I have also tried to sharpen this in a way I have not seen spelled out, and I want it tested along with the rest. What should be counted, I take it, is not the standing stock of animals at an instant but the flow of suffering-days per unit time — births, deaths, and the duration and intensity of the bad states in between. And on that accounting the comfortable conclusion may reverse: habitat degradation and conversion typically shift community composition toward short-lived, fast-reproducing, high-mortality taxa, and often toward very large numbers of small invertebrates, so the standing biomass can fall while the rate at which lives begin and end goes up. If that is right, the demographic-reduction presumption holds only where destruction lowers the equilibrium throughput of suffering-days, and in many converted landscapes it may raise it instead.

What I want from you is a map of the exposure of this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me what would have to be true — in the field data, in the theory, or in the ethics — for the demographic-reduction presumption, and for my throughput refinement of it, to be wrong. Concretely, that means: which measurable quantities would actually settle the sign, and how near are we to measuring them (age-specific survival and fecundity schedules for the numerically dominant taxa; how much of the total is spent in states we would count as suffering; how far welfare capacity scales with body size, neural complexity, or longevity; how much of any effect leaks off-site through trophic cascades, migration, hydrology, or the climate); which theoretical considerations cut against it, including the possibility that populations are not at a regulated ceiling at all but are transient and disturbance-driven, the possibility that deaths are nearly fully compensatory so that removing animals does not reduce the equilibrium population, and the possibility that suffering in degraded habitats is more intense per animal even if there are more animals in intact ones; and which objections are conceptual rather than empirical, such as whether the net welfare of an ecosystem is a well-defined quantity at all, whether the counterfactual baseline is stable given that land not destroyed usually becomes something else, what time horizon the comparison should use given the pulse of mortality during destruction itself, and whether aggregating across species with arguably incommensurable welfare is coherent. I would also like you to distinguish, for each defeater, the ones a holder of this view should concede from the ones such a holder would reasonably dismiss, and to be explicit about where the two parts of my position come apart — a finding could in principle refute my throughput refinement while leaving the core presumption intact, or vice versa.

Two further things. First, I am uneasy that this position may have been insulated into unfalsifiability: almost every empirical challenge can be met by saying that we know too little about invertebrates, or about the subjective lives of animals generally, to say anything. I want your honest view on whether that is what is happening, whether the insulation is legitimate given the state of the evidence or instead a symptom of the argument having no real empirical content, and whether a retired field biologist should treat the whole question as one where the honest answer is that nobody knows the sign. I would personally be most moved by the kind of data I used to collect — mark-recapture survival estimates, nesting success, time budgets — so say plainly which of your proposed defeaters are the sort that a monitoring programme could in principle deliver within a career, and which are not. Second, be disciplined about epistemic status: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, do not present a contested empirical claim as the consensus just because it is frequently repeated, and mark the places where you are extrapolating beyond anything that has actually been studied rather than reporting a finding.
