I sit on my university's Committee on Accommodations and Student Health — we decide what the disability office will and won't provide, and we're also the ones who get handed the messy petitions about prescription stimulants and "leveling" that don't fit anywhere. Last night I had a long, fairly annoying argument with my friend Priya about it over dinner, and I want to get something on paper before our meeting, because I think I mostly won the argument but I can't quite say why. Her position was that the whole therapy-versus-enhancement distinction is a gerrymander: "normal functioning" is always measured against an environment somebody chose, so calling something therapy just means we've decided to approve of it, and calling it enhancement means we haven't. I kept saying the distinction is doing real work for us — we have finite money, a six-week backlog, and no way to independently verify the diagnoses students bring in — but she had a good line I can't shake: if we can't tell the difference in practice, then drawing the line is just a way of laundering disapproval of people who want to be better than they happened to be born.

Here's the actual docket, which is why I need the memo rather than a bull session. We have three petitions and one policy proposal, and our current rule says accommodations are for students whose documented condition substantially limits performance relative to the typical student in the same program. (1) A second-year law student diagnosed with ADHD at 28, after doing fine as an undergrad at a less brutal school — mild symptoms, high baseline ability, wants extended time and a stimulant. Priya says she's the clearest case of "we invented a disease because the room got harder," and I think that's unfair but I don't know how to say why. (2) A student with no diagnosis who sleeps five hours because he works two jobs and wants the same prescription; his deficit is environmental, not biological, and he argues that if we'd accommodate a brain that was born distractible we should accommodate a brain made distractible by rent. (3) The one that actually kept me up: a student who used stimulants off-label during last year's exams — which we don't sanction but also don't police — now has chronic insomnia and panic symptoms, and is asking us to cover the treatment and grant her reduced course load as an accommodation. Is cleaning up the downstream damage of an enhancement we tolerated itself therapy, or is it just an indirect subsidy for the enhancement? And then the policy proposal on the table from a subcommittee: scrap the diagnostic gate entirely and define eligibility by a cut at the 25th percentile of the current cohort on our own skills measures, on the theory that what we owe students is fair competitive opportunity rather than restored biology.

Please draft the decision memo I can circulate to the committee before we meet. It should give a concrete recommendation on each of the three petitions plus a yes/no on the percentile proposal, with a timeframe for implementation and a note on what the office should do in the interim, and it should be written so that a skeptical committee member and Priya herself could actually respond to it rather than just being told our existing rule. I'd also want it to take seriously the strongest version of her objection — I'd rather the memo say where our line is arbitrary than pretend it's clean — and to say something useful about the ratchet worry one of our members raised: if eligibility is defined against the cohort, and accommodations raise the cohort's performance, then more people fall under the line each year, and either we spend forever chasing that or we quietly decide the cutoff is really about how much we can afford. Oh, and please don't lean on citations or "the literature says" — we read those memos on our phones between classes and none of us can look anything up, so the argument has to stand on its own.
