Here's the area and the half-formed idea. The triviality results (Lewis's original argument and the later versions that got the conclusion from weaker assumptions) are standardly read as forcing a choice: either the conditional is a genuine proposition in the algebra you're assigning probabilities over, or the equation P(if A, then C) = P(C given A) fails. The Adams/Edgington-style way out is to say: fine, conditionals aren't truth-valued, the equation governs something like assertability or degree of acceptance, not the probability of a proposition, and then everyone argues about whether that's coherent.

What I want to look at is a third variable that I don't see discussed much: how much expressive resources the collapse arguments actually need. So the question: take a finite algebra with n atoms, allow conditionals only up to nesting depth k (flat ones are k = 1), require the equation only where it makes sense, and ask where the boundary is.
