The question (two parts, 30 marks: 8 for Q1, 6 for Q2(i), 8 for Q2(ii), 8 for Q2(iii)):

Q1. Set out the threefold distinction between God's natural knowledge, middle knowledge, and free knowledge. Be careful about the kind of priority involved: in exactly what sense is middle knowledge prior to the divine decree, and what would be lost if it were not? Then say what Molina thought this distinction buys — which two things he wanted to hold on to that his critics thought couldn't both be had.

Q2. The Case of Halvard's Ledger. Halvard is a merely possible creature. Let C be a fully specified circumstance: Halvard is treasurer of a poor-relief fund, his daughter is ill, the ledger is left unlocked, no one is watching, and he has had a particular upbringing, a particular offer of grace, and so on. Hold, per hypothesis, that were Halvard created in C he would freely take the money; and that in C* — the same circumstance except that a friend happens to knock at the door — he would freely leave it.

(i) Suppose God wants a world containing Halvard's free refusal. What would God have to do to get it, and can He do it? Use the contrast between strong and weak actualization, and say what it is about counterfactuals of freedom, if anything, that blocks the strong route.

(ii) Now add this: God knows that were Halvard placed in C and were Halvard to refuse, God himself would freely respond by sending an unexpected inheritance the following morning. Call that counterfactual D. Which of the three kinds of knowledge does D belong to — or none of them? Note that the standard formula speaks of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom; does that word creaturely do any real work here, or is it incidental? And is knowing D something God needs in order to bring about a world in which Halvard refuses and the inheritance arrives?

(iii) Finally, suppose it turns out that in every circumstance in which Halvard could be created and left free — C, C*, C**, and so on without end — he would freely take the money at least once. What follows about what God can actualize? And does that limitation, if it is one, tell us anything about whether middle knowledge is a genuine gain over just saying that God knows what happens because He decrees what happens?

The student's answer:

"God's knowledge divides into three. Natural knowledge is God's knowledge of all necessary truths and of everything that is possible — including everything any possible creature could do, though not what any creature would do. Free knowledge is God's knowledge of what He has actually decreed and of everything that will in fact happen as a result. Middle knowledge sits between them: it is God's knowledge of what any possible free creature would freely do in any fully specified circumstance. These counterfactuals are contingent — the creature might have done otherwise — but they are true independently of any act of God's will.

Middle knowledge is logically and temporally prior to the decree. This is the whole point of it: God does not first decide what to create and then find out what His creatures do; He antecedently sees what every possible creature would do in every possible situation, and then decrees. If middle knowledge came after the decree we would be back with the Dominicans and their physical premotion, where God has to determine the will in order to know what it does. Molina wanted to keep both divine providence in its full and specific detail — predestination, the distribution of grace, particular prayers answered — and libertarian freedom, according to which the agent could have done otherwise in exactly the circumstances he was in. His critics in the de auxiliis controversy thought you had to pick one. Middle knowledge is meant to show you don't: God's plan is as detailed as you like, because it is built on what creatures would freely do, and it is still up to the creature.

Q2(i). God cannot strongly actualize Halvard's refusal. To strongly actualize something is to causally determine it, and if God caused the refusal it wouldn't be free. What God can do is strongly actualize the circumstances C*: He arranges the world so that the friend knocks at the door, and, knowing antecedently that in C* Halvard would freely leave the money, He thereby weakly actualizes the refusal. The counterfactual truth is not up to God — He finds it, He doesn't make it — so Halvard's refusing is genuinely Halvard's, and God gets what He wanted without touching the will. Notice the two things God can't do: He can't make Halvard refuse, and He can't make it true that Halvard would refuse in C.

(ii) D also belongs to middle knowledge. After all, God's own response is a free act too, and God knows before He decides anything that were Halvard to refuse He would send the inheritance; so this is just one more counterfactual of freedom, known in the same way and at the same logical stage as the counterfactual about Halvard. The word creaturely in the standard formula does no real work — it's just a reminder that the freedom in question is libertarian rather than compatibilist. And God does need it: in order to compare candidate worlds He has to know, for each arrangement of circumstances, what everything that would happen in it would be, including what He himself would do. Otherwise His survey of the possibilities has a hole in it and He can't tell which world He is getting.

(iii) Then no world containing Halvard and no theft is available to God. Every feasible world in which Halvard exists contains at least one free theft; God cannot strongly actualize the not-stealing, and there is no circumstance He can put him in at which he would refrain. So God must either create Halvard knowing he will steal, or not create him. This is a limitation, but it is a limitation on what is possible, not on God's power, and it isn't a limitation He is stuck with by decree — it is fixed by what Halvard would freely do.

The main objection to all this is the grounding objection: how could God possibly know such things, given that nothing in the circumstances determines the outcome? But the Molinist answer is straightforward. God's knowledge is not inferential; He doesn't have to work these things out from evidence, the way we guess what a friend would do. He simply knows them. So the objection fails. And the payoff is large: because the counterfactuals are true independently of God and He cannot determine them, God is not responsible for the evil Halvard freely does. He merely actualizes the best possible world He can, and the evil in it is Halvard's doing, not His. That is why Molinism is the version of theism that finally defuses the logical problem of evil."
