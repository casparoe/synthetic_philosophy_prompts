I got put on propranolol before conference talks about eight months ago — I'm a materials science PhD student and I'd got to the point where I couldn't get through a slide deck. Now I take 10mg an hour beforehand and something odd happens: my hands are steady, my heart isn't going anywhere, and I can still think all the thoughts (they're bored, I've lost them, this is going badly) while feeling completely calm. My roommate, who did a philosophy minor, says the thoughts just are the fear and the pill only stripped off the body stuff. I say I'm not scared anymore. Then he asked what about the time I was icily furious at my old landlord about the deposit — no body stuff there either, no shaking, totally composed, I made a very calm and very aggressive phone call — and I had to admit I'd call that anger 100 percent. So now I'm stuck, and I've started collecting cases. Here's where my gut lands.

1. Jump scare in a horror film. Heart hammers, and by the time it's over I'm amused, not frightened. Verdict: not an emotion, just a reflex.

2. The stove. Already in the car, twenty minutes from home, a flash of did I leave the stove on, and my stomach drops before I've checked anything or come to believe anything. I go back, it's off, feeling evaporates. Verdict: that was real fear, twenty seconds of it, with no belief involved at any point.

3. House spider. Full-body flinch at a spider I know is harmless and even useful. Verdict: still fear, and I refuse to give this one up — it's fear precisely because my beliefs are sitting there saying otherwise.

4. Grief. My dad died in March. I'm about as firm an atheist as you meet, I fully believe he isn't suffering and that death isn't a harm to him, and I still get hit by waves of missing him. Verdict: definitely grief, definitely an emotion, even though the belief that would supposedly be its content is one I reject.

5. Ugly-crying at a film I know is fiction, about people who never existed. Verdict: emotion. But I can't say I judged anything to be the case.

6. Rage at my laptop for eating an unsaved draft. Verdict: I say I was angry, but not really at the laptop — a laptop can't wrong me. It was at myself, or at the situation, or at nothing. This one worries me, because I don't know what to say the anger was about.

7. The propranolol case. Verdict: not really afraid anymore. Anxious thoughts, calm body, no fear.

8. The landlord. Verdict: fully furious, zero feelings.

9. A week in February where I was snappy with everyone for no reason I could point to. Verdict: a mood, not an emotion — different animal. But maybe it's just anger with a broken object-detector, and I'm inventing a category to save a theory.

10. Cold swim in the lake in October. Twenty minutes of feeling amazing afterwards, a kind of exhilaration I'd otherwise call joy. Verdict: I don't want to call this an emotion, but I can't say why not.

11. Working myself up into outrage about a news story because everyone around me was furious and it seemed required. Verdict: I was angry, but thinner, less real than the landlord anger. I don't know what to do with thinner.

12. Everyone told me I was jealous of my roommate's new relationship. I argued with them. Two months later I saw it instantly. Verdict: I was jealous the whole time and couldn't tell.

My rough principle, which I know is probably wrong: something is an emotion when it's a felt, valenced push to do something or stop doing something, aimed at something, and it keeps being that emotion even when your beliefs contradict it — which is why the spider case is easy and why I want to say the feeling is the part you can't drop, so the propranolol case isn't fear. But 8 has no feeling and is clearly anger, and 2 has a feeling with no belief at all while 4 keeps going with the belief gone, and 10 has the feeling with nothing it's about.

So: is there one account — emotions as judgments, as perceptions of value, as feelings, or something split — that gets all twelve? My friend says the perception version handles 3 and 5 and 4 beautifully, emotions being like seeing colors, but then I don't see what it says about 7, where the perception seems to have been switched off and only the thought is left. Give me the principle you'd defend, with the case that motivates each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one I should drop, and tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed: nightmares, whether my sister's dog has emotions, whether a language model trained only to predict text could be afraid of something, and whether an emotion can be wrong in a way that isn't just being a false belief.
