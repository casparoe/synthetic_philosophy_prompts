I'm a graduate student in a history department, and I run a small reading group — two historically minded political scientists, an archaeologist, and me — that has spent most of a year on cliodynamics, mostly on secular cycles in agrarian states: the demographic-structural story in which population growth swells the pool of elite aspirants relative to the offices and revenues available to them, pushes elite incomes and popular welfare down together, ends in fiscal crisis and state breakdown, and then repeats on the order of two or three centuries. I expected that after a year of this we would at least know what we were arguing about, and instead we have frozen. The modellers in the group think the framework describes a genuine, noisy quantitative regularity that we ought to be trying to forecast with; the historians think it is a set of evocative labels retrofitted onto cases that were picked because the outcome had already happened. The same three exchanges keep recurring: the modellers say the historians refuse to be held to any standard of evidence, the historians say that the inputs — elite overproduction, popular immiseration, state fiscal distress — are not one quantity measured many times but different local stories wearing a single variable name, and whenever a forward forecast is produced (one member keeps citing a supposedly much-discussed call, made a decade and a half ago, about rising instability in the United States in the 2020s) the historians discount it as too vague to fail and the modellers treat it as the whole point. So I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on who is winning.

My own hypothesis is that two separable things are being run together. First, I suspect the sides are answering different questions under the word prediction: one wants well-calibrated probabilistic forecasts of coarse-grained outcome types — how many polities in a region cross some violence or state-capacity threshold within a decade — while the other wants a causal account of why this polity broke when it did, and that once these are pulled apart much of the heat goes away and the live question becomes how much variance in breakdown timing a two- or three-variable model can actually explain out of sample. Second, and this is the part I care about more, I suspect a real methodological question is doing the work unnamed: nearly everything we read aligns cases by their crisis date and then averages the preceding two centuries backwards, and the cycle length is estimated from the very episodes it is later used to anticipate. I want to know whether that practice is a version of selecting on the dependent variable — choose the polities that broke down, stack them at the breakdown, and you will find rising stress beforehand on almost any measure you like — or whether aligning on the outcome is legitimate once the theory predicts a specific ordering and lag structure among the indicators rather than their mere rise. Relatedly, is there a difference in evidential weight between a model that commits to absolute dates and one that commits only to a shape, with the crisis supplying the axis?

Tell me which of these, if either, is the actual fault line, and what else belongs on the list: a verbal dispute about law, explanation, or data; a fight over who gets to code the past, given that the datasets are themselves somebody's reading of the sources; bedrock intuitions about whether history has the kind of structure that supports generalizing at all; or a hidden formal question — how many independent cycles, on how fine a grid, before a periodic model is distinguishable from a trend plus noise fitted to the same series. Please engage the positions as people in them actually argue rather than surveying the field, and don't hand me a bibliography or reading list; I already have one. Where you can, say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — pre-registered forecasts with the cycle period fixed from held-out regions, leave-one-region-out validation, an identifiability or statistical power result, or a deliberate reframing of the question — or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why. Keep clear what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested, flag where you are speculating rather than reporting, and mark which parts of the diagnosis come from me rather than from anything the field actually says.
