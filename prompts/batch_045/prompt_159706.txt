I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I am writing a seminar paper on a question my advisor thinks is confused: whether testimonial injustice has to be a property of an individual hearer's credibility judgment in a particular exchange, or whether it can be a property of a speaker's position in an evidential environment, such that a speaker can suffer it even when no hearer is at fault. To make progress I need a case built to order, and I need it built to specifications rather than gestured at. The setting should be realistic and present-day: a hospital ward, an insurance claims office, a hiring or tenure committee, a school, a police interview, an asylum or disability tribunal — named characters, one paragraph, no science fiction, no overt bigotry, and no villain in the story. Five constraints. First, the hearer must be individually impeccable: no identity prejudice operating in their judgment at the time, no culpable ignorance, no laziness, no malice; they are actively trying to assess the speaker well, they would be distressed by the verdict they reach, and they would correct for any distortion they knew how to correct for. Second, their credibility judgment must nonetheless come out deflated, and deflated wrongly: on the matter at hand the speaker is in fact a reliable, well-positioned informant who merits more credence than they get. Third, the deflation must fall out of the evidence legitimately available to the hearer — institutional base rates, track records, prior reports, demographically correlated statistics — where the shape of that evidence is a downstream product of earlier injustices, and where the hearer has no usable estimate of the size or direction of the contamination; if they could estimate it they would adjust for it, and I specifically do not want that escape route. Fourth, the outcome must harm the speaker, and the harm must be at least partly the distinctively epistemic one: being undercut, dismissed, or rendered invisible in their capacity as a knower and giver of knowledge, with something concrete at stake. Fifth, the verdicts must be generated by the two readings rather than stipulated: writing that the speaker is treated unjustly as a premise is cheating.
