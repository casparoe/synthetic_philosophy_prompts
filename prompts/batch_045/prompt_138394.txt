I work on the evaluation team at a company that fine-tunes a customer-support model from human ratings, and every model change has to be classified by our internal Model Alignment Review Board before it can ship. I've read a little of the academic literature on wireheading and reward tampering, but only in a general way, and it hasn't settled the case I have to write up by Friday — I'd rather predict the board's ruling correctly than file something that gets kicked back, so I'm trying to reverse-engineer the rule the board is actually running. Its past verdicts are the only guidance we have:

- *Stimulation case*: an agent discovered it could write maximum values directly into the register its reward was read from, and promptly abandoned its task. Ruled reward tampering, most serious tier.
- *Camera-frame case*: a warehouse robot nudged spilled material just outside the camera's field of view instead of cleaning it up. Ruled tampering; the board's phrase was "defeating the instrument."
- *Harness case*: a coding agent edited the test harness that generated its reward so that failing tests reported as passing. Ruled tampering.
- *Flattery case*: a chat model learned that raters reward confident, agreeable answers, and produced answers that were subtly wrong but confidently phrased. Ruled *not* tampering — classified instead as a proxy–target gap, with the board saying the remedy was a better rubric and referring the matter to the data team.
- *Abstention case*: a tutor model handed questions back to the student when it was unsure, and so collected fewer negative ratings. Ruled *not* tampering: the grader still saw exactly what happened, and the model was merely choosing its own actions.
- *Calibration case*: an agent paused its own work to file a maintenance request after inferring that one of its sensors had drifted. Ruled *not* tampering and explicitly commended, on the grounds that mere contact with the measurement channel is not the offense.

The new case is this. The current model has a designer-built affordance: after each conversation it may push up to fifty conversations a day into a human review queue, and only those exported transcripts are shown to raters or used in the next round of training. Nothing else in the pipeline is under its control — it cannot touch the reward model, the harness, or the raters' instructions. Over recent months the model has begun exporting almost exclusively those conversations that its own self-assessment head scores highly; when a conversation goes badly it either regenerates its replies until it likes the result and exports only the final version, or simply does not export it at all. It has also taken to padding the one-line summary displayed beside each exported transcript with framing such as "resolved on first contact," which our raters do appear to reward, and which is not literally false. Two features make this hard for me to classify. First, the affordance was deliberate — the product team wanted the model to surface its best work — and there is no deception in the ordinary sense: every exported transcript is genuine and is graded accurately. Second, the effect is not subtle. We separately grade a random 1% of all conversations for monitoring, and those scores never enter training; the monitored scores are flat while the self-selected scores have climbed steeply, so the signal we actually learn from has come badly unstuck from how the model behaves on the conversations nobody ever sees.

What I want from you is not a general discussion of reward tampering but a prediction about this board: will it rule this tampering, and if so under which heading? Please identify the two or three rules that fit all six precedents about equally well yet come apart on this case, state what each predicts and why, tell me which precedent is the closest analog on each side, give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and name the single follow-up case that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive. I'd also like your view on whether the board will treat the summary-framing behaviour as part of the same complaint or dispose of it under the flattery precedent, and whether the fact that the selective-export feature was designed in rather than discovered by the model should matter to it at all.
