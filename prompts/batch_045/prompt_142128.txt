Hey — I sit on the ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital, and I've got one of those things that keeps coming back around in meetings and that I think is secretly a metaphysics question in a white coat. Long one, sorry.

The practice: people with bipolar disorder sometimes come in during a well stretch and sign what we call a Ulysses directive. Roughly, it says: if I go manic and start refusing treatment, don't take that refusal as my answer — medicate me anyway, hold me, override me. And we do honor these. I gather some places have actual statutes for them; at our hospital it's committee policy plus whatever the attending is willing to defend afterward.

The bit that bugs me is that everything else in informed consent runs the other direction. A competent patient's refusal *right now* trumps anything they wrote down earlier — you can tear up your living will in the moment, you can say no to a procedure you consented to last week, and that's it, the current no wins. A Ulysses directive is a document whose entire point is to defeat the current no. So: we treat the later self as the real speaker here, and the earlier self as the real speaker there.

It gets weirder, because our committee has drifted into writing these things up in a way that I've never seen described anywhere. We've started treating the directive as a two-party agreement rather than a standing order. Call it the staged-self rule. The signing self and the refusing self get treated as distinct parties with claims against each other, and that has consequences:

- The refusing self gets a "renegotiation window." If, while manic, she clearly and repeatedly says something like *I've changed my mind about the kind of person I want to be, I'm done with being a psychiatric patient*, the directive lapses and has to be reaffirmed later when she's well. If she just refuses because she feels fantastic and obviously isn't sick, the directive holds and we treat her anyway.
- The refusing self can, in principle, void the agreement for bad faith on the other side: if the signing self signed under pressure from a family member, or was herself a little hypomanic when she signed, the later self gets to call the whole thing off.

And one more contrast that I think is the real crux. Same committee, same year: a woman with dementia, who two years earlier during a lucid stretch signed a care plan saying she'd accept help with bathing, now refuses the bath every single morning. We side with *her*, the current refuser, every time. Nobody says "she already agreed to this." But with mania we side with the earlier signer. Both patients are, at the moment of refusal, not at their best.

So my questions. A friend who did a philosophy minor says the relevant words are "endurantism" and "perdurantism" — whether a thing is wholly present at each moment or is more like a stack of temporal parts. What I want to know is what view of persons-over-time this whole package actually presupposes. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does honoring the earlier self against the later self's contemporaneous refusal require that they be numerically the same person (so that this is self-government and not just one person bossing another around), or does the staged-self machinery — two parties, a window to renegotiate, void-for-bad-faith — quietly presuppose something more like temporal parts that can stand in relations to each other? Those two seem to pull in opposite directions, and I want to know whether it's a real contradiction or just two metaphors for the same thing.
2. Which rival views does the practice rule out? Does a Parfit-style view where identity isn't what matters and there's only degrees of psychological connectedness make the all-or-nothing directive incoherent? Does a straight endurantist have any way to explain why the earlier stage's say-so wins, or does she have to say this is just ordinary intra-personal conflict of wills and there's no identity question here at all?
3. Where is the practice just underdetermined? I suspect a lot of it is carried by "the manic patient isn't competent, so there's no genuine autonomous refusal to trump anything," and if that's doing all the work then the metaphysics is decoration. Tell me honestly which parts strictly presuppose a view of persistence and which parts would be licensed by several different theories.
4. What would a protocol built on the Parfitian picture look like instead — would authority just scale with connectedness, and is our renegotiation window already a half-assed version of that?
5. And the dementia case: what would have to be true for siding with the current refuser there and the earlier signer here to be consistent rather than just a mood?

Please don't hand me a reading list or point me at papers — I want the argument, and I don't have library access anyway. Our committee reads at about a first-year-undergrad level, so keep the vocabulary light and don't assume we know the jargon. Under 900 words if you can, and skip footnotes.

At the very end, no matter how everything else is organized, give me one last line in exactly this form, filling in the blanks with one option from each pair:

Verdict: [endurantism | perdurantism | neither — underdetermined] ; [identity is doing real work | identity is decoration]
