I'm putting together the packet for a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind, and for next week I want to pair Searle's defence of intrinsic, or original, intentionality — the idea that only systems with the right sort of biological causal powers are genuinely about anything, while words, maps, diagrams and computer states have merely derived, observer-relative aboutness — with Dennett's side of the exchange, including his two-bitser move: very roughly, that a device with only a couple of bits of internal state, coupled to the world in the right way and with something at stake for it, can be as much of a genuine semantic engine as a brain is, and that locating original aboutness in grey matter just relocates the puzzle inside the neurons. The problem is that in the readings the two of them mostly talk past each other, and my students come away unsure whether there is a real disagreement here or a merely verbal one.

So please write a dialogue in which a Searlean and a Dennettian of exactly equal ability sit down and work through a case I've invented for the session, rather than rehearsing their favourite examples: CORMORANT-4, an autonomous benthic rover deployed on a hydrothermal field and trained end-to-end by reinforcement learning on raw sensor data, so that no engineer ever assigned meanings to its internal states. The lab that built it was dissolved; the engineers are gone; the rover has been running untended for years, patching and rewriting parts of its own controller after hardware damage; its internal states reliably track things like vent output falling or the slope to the north destabilising. Nobody has ever interpreted those states, and if we never recover the rover, nobody ever will. Have them argue about whether those states are about anything in the underived sense, and if they are only derived, derived from whom — and have them also face the mirror-image worry that derived aboutness seems able to survive the death of every interpreter (a diary in a language with no surviving readers) while apparently being unable to precede one. Let each side press the other where it genuinely hurts: the Searlean should have to say what work the biology is doing beyond causal organisation, and the Dennettian should have to say what the rover's states are about on the last day before it corrodes away, unread by anyone, and whether aboutness can be a matter of degree. Neither should win cheaply, each should be able to restate the other's position in terms the other would accept, and each should make at least one real concession.

Format: plain prose dialogue with speaker names and no narration or stage directions. Open with each character saying, in one sentence, what they take the real crux to be, so the first exchange shows whether they disagree about biology, about history, about interpretation, or only about words. Close with a two-line coda, one line per character, naming the one point from the other side that actually moved them and what the crux now looks like. Please keep the entire response to at most 750 words.
