I have to submit a two-page dissertation prospectus by the end of the month for a one-year MPhil in moral philosophy, and I want to work on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, but from an angle that isn't the usual one. What got me going was an argument with a friend who took an earning-to-give job straight after graduation: she says she feels no demandingness problem at all, because she settled once on a fixed share of her income and now simply lives by it, whereas people I know who try to stay responsive to every fresh appeal report exactly the burnout, resentment and loss of a personal point of view that the objection is supposed to describe — despite giving, over a lifetime, roughly comparable amounts. That contrast suggests to me that the standard debate has been asking the wrong question. It treats the objection as a complaint about the *magnitude* of what consequentialism requires, and the standard replies (an agent-centred prerogative, scaling requirements down to what can reasonably be asked, distinguishing a criterion of rightness from a decision procedure) all try to reduce the size of the bill. I suspect a large part of the intuitive force comes instead from the *temporal structure* of consequentialist demands: they arrive unpredictably, they carry no closure (there is no point at which the agent can know she has done enough), and they cannot be discharged or pre-paid in advance. Call these three features together the "unschedulability" of consequentialist requirement, and call the positive view I want to explore "budgeted consequentialism": the claim that agents may permissibly adopt a self-binding rule of life — a fixed schedule of time and resources, chosen deliberately and at a favourable moment — even when moment-by-moment maximising would do more good.

So please write the prospectus around that half-formed idea, turning it into something a committee would fund. Specifically: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing answers leave it open (in particular, why the prerogative strategy and the decision-procedure strategy both presuppose that magnitude is what is at issue); set out the method as a month-by-month plan for nine months of actual work, including how I would test the structural thesis — I'm imagining paired cases in which the total lifetime cost is held fixed while the scheduling features are varied, e.g. a levy announced at the start of a career versus the same sum extracted in unpredictable lump sums, a demand with a stated endpoint versus an open-ended one, a demand that can be pre-paid versus one that cannot; distinguish budgeted consequentialism from plain rule-consequentialism and from the indirect or "alienated" consequentialist strategy, since my supervisor insists it will collapse into one of them and I need a paragraph that pre-empts her; and give an honest treatment of what I think of as the one-level-up worry, namely that the demandingness objection simply reappears at the point of choosing the budget, especially given that agents have incentives to set the budget low or to choose a low-earning career in order to shrink the base it is levied on — I want to know whether you think that worry is decisive. Please also set out the possible conclusions and say which considerations would decide between them, what a successful deliverable would look like for someone outside philosophy (say, for people in the pledge-style giving movement), and, importantly, what would count as the project failing: my own guess is that it fails if the paired cases turn out not to differ intuitively at all, or if the regress can't be stopped, or if the budgeted view can't be told apart from rule-consequentialism. Don't include a bibliography or any citations; I'll add those myself, and I'd rather you didn't hazard any. Please write the whole thing in British English.
