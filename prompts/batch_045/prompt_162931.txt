So I'm a novelist with a plot problem that turns out to be a philosophy problem, and I need you to write me a document.

The setup in my book: my protagonist, Nia, is offered a big sum of money by a creepy philanthropist who will pay her purely for *forming, at midnight tonight, the intention of drinking a vial the next afternoon*. Drinking it makes you violently ill for about a day and does nothing else, good or bad. She never actually has to drink it — he pays for the intention and nothing else, and he's an unnervingly good judge of whether the intention is real. A friend tells me this is straight out of a famous puzzle by someone called Kavka (I think?) and that people have been chewing on it for decades. Great — that's exactly the argument I want on the page.

Two philosophers have been reading drafts for me and its been going on in the margins for about a year, and I've realised I don't want either of their chapters. I want the thing they'd have signed together if they'd behaved. You're writing that.

Camp one: she can't do it. On their view intending something is tied to what you actually expect of yourself; you can't wholeheartedly intend to φ while knowing perfectly well you won't φ, and since everyone can see she'll have no reason left to drink once the money's in her account, the intention just isn't available to her. Camp two: she can. On their view an intention is a commitment or a plan, not a forecast — people intend to quit smoking knowing they probably won't, soldiers intend to hold positions they expect to lose, and there's nothing incoherent about Nia resolving to drink and then not drinking.

I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about what's true at the moment for action, about what the money is for, about whether cases like this are a real problem for a certain style of thinking about rationality or just a cute one-off, that sort of thing; (2) a table of what they still disagree about, where each row gives the disagreement in wording its own side would sign, not the wording the other side would pick for it; (3) the cruxes, with the concrete case or argument both camps agree would bear on each one, and each camp's prediction written down in advance; and (4) your read on which parts of their fight are only about words — I suspect "can" is doing about three different jobs here (psychologically able, coherent, rationally permitted) and I want that untangled if I'm right.

Two complications from my plot that I need them to actually deal with rather than wave away. First, in my version it isn't one night, it's thirty mornings in a row with a fresh vial each time, and by day twelve she's drunk it twice — she's impulsive, and there's a scene where she does it almost out of superstition. So her confidence that she won't drink is definately not certainty, it's maybe 85%. Does that change the answer, or is the certainty just an incidental detail of the original setup? Second, the philanthropist's assistant sits in the room, and Nia has to say the intention out loud and write it in a notebook the assistant keeps, which gets read aloud in a later scene. So I need to know whether saying or writing "I intend to drink it tomorrow" could be part of what the intending consists in, or whether it's only ever evidence of an inner state — and, separately, whether deliberately getting herself into a state where drinking is forced (drunk, hypnotised, handcuffed to the vial, whatever) is a way of collecting the money honestly or a way of cheating.

Please don't give me reading lists, citations, or names to look up — I can't use them, I'm writing fiction, and I don't care who got there first. I care about the exact shape of the disagreement, and I want sentences sharp enough to steal for dialogue. If the honest answer to either of my two complications is "it depends which distinction you draw," say that, and say what each camp says. And if you think one of the camps is simply wrong, keep it to yourself till the last section — I need the joint document first.
