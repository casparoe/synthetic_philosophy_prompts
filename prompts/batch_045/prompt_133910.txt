I work as a backend engineer at a mid-sized payments company, and for the last couple of years I've been on the on-call rotation and in the incident-review process that follows it. Like much of the industry, we run what gets called a 'blameless' review: the write-up is not allowed to name an individual as the cause, contributing factors have to be phrased as properties of the system or the process rather than as somebody's failure, and the remediation work is tracked as tickets rather than as anything a person can be sanctioned for. Two features of the practice keep striking me as odd. First, it isn't really blameless: our policy explicitly reserves discipline for what it calls knowing circumvention of a control — pushing a configuration change straight to production outside the deployment pipeline, self-approving a change that requires two-person review — and, as in the aviation and hospital-safety versions of this framework, that carve-out is meant to attach to the behaviour rather than to the harm, so quietly bypassing a check and getting away with it counts as more serious than an honest mistake that takes the platform down for an hour. Second, there's an asymmetry in what actually gets suspended: we're scrupulous about withholding blame and entirely unscrupulous about handing out credit — the engineer who diagnosed the failure at 3 a.m. is named in the summary, thanked in the all-hands, and rewarded at performance-review time, and I have never once heard anyone suggest that praising incident response might be as hostile to learning as blaming it is.

I started wondering what picture of responsibility this practice quietly commits us to, and a friend who did some philosophy pointed me toward Strawson's 1962 British Academy lecture, which I've only encountered through summaries. As I understand it, the idea is that holding someone responsible isn't a metaphysical claim about whether they could have done otherwise; it's a matter of occupying something called the participant stance, in which we react to what others' actions express about their attitude toward us with resentment, indignation, gratitude, or affection, and to ourselves with guilt. Excusing someone, or exempting them altogether, is then a step out of that stance into the objective one, where you stop addressing them as a fellow member of the moral community and instead regard them as something to be managed, handled, trained, or treated. If that's roughly right, my industry's practice looks like an institutionalised and only partial withdrawal of the reactive attitudes — but I can't tell how much of Strawson it actually needs, which parts of the practice are doing real philosophical work, and which are just instrumental habits that any theory of responsibility would recommend.

So my question is what the practice presupposes about moral responsibility, and where it is underdetermined. Some things I'm specifically unsure about. Does it need the Strawsonian claim that blame is constituted by our reactive attitudes, so that a change of stance genuinely dissolves it — or is it equally well licensed by a view on which blame tracks control, capacity, or quality of will, with the review process serving merely as a cheap way to buy honest reporting? I also suspect our slogan that the system caused the failure is ambiguous between a causal-selection claim (no individual contribution was a difference-maker worth singling out) and a moral claim (no participant was an apt target of reactive attitudes), and I can't tell which one the practice requires: if only the first, it may presuppose no theory of responsibility at all. There's a further wrinkle I find hard to shake: the system isn't exogenous to us, it's the sedimented output of our own past choices and it's maintained by us, so an engineer who says the deployment pipeline is to blame is partly blaming the team that built it, herself included — does that make the excuse self-undermining on the Strawsonian picture, or does it just show that blame has to be redistributed rather than withdrawn? Relatedly, I'd like to know whether the practice treats the engineer as excused on this occasion or as exempted from the ordinary interpersonal stance in that role, and what happens to the carve-out for knowing circumvention if it's the latter. I also notice that most of our actual safety investment goes into removing discretion rather than removing resentment — automated rollback, error budgets, progressive delivery, mandatory review — which looks more like a control-based compatibilist strategy than a Strawsonian one. And then there's the praise asymmetry: since the reactive attitudes on this view include positive ones like gratitude and moral approval, is suspending blame while amplifying praise actually inconsistent, or does it reveal that the practice's operative commitment is something thinner, like the claim that blame degrades information flow while praise doesn't? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, mark where several rival theories would license the same practice, and then tell me concretely what would have to change in how we run incident reviews if we dropped the Strawsonian reading for a control-based or desert-based one — including whether a fully consistent version would have to give up either the violation carve-out or the credit-giving. I'm not looking for references or a reading list; I want the conceptual structure.
