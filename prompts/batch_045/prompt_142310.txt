I run a tiny reading group at work on thursday lunchtimes — six or seven backend engineers, one PM, zero philosophy majors. Last week we got completely stuck on that old rescue case: a ship goes down, five strangers are in the water on your left, one stranger is in the water on your right, you can reach one group but not both, nothing else is at stake, nobody is your friend, nobody is especially young or brilliant or about to cure cancer. Do you flip a coin, or do you save the five?

I gather this comes out of some 1970s paper by a guy named Taurek, but literally nobody in the group has read it, so please don't assume we have — and don't give us a reading list, none of us have library access anyway.

What I actually want is a decision tree we can walk through out loud so each of us finds out where we already are. 8 branch questions-ish, ending in 7-8 named leaves. Ground rules:

- Every branch node has to be a concrete question about a case or a claim, phrased so someone who has never heard any of the labels can answer it. Nothing like "do numbers count?" or "are you a utilitarian?". Nobody in my group knows those words.
- Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of -isms: give the questions and the answer-combinations they allow first, then locate the real positions inside that space, and flag the combinations nobody occupies — and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- These three cases have to do work somewhere in the tree: (a) the five are a family, and all five shout that if you can't save all of them you should save the one on the other side instead, because they don't want to go on as a remnant; (b) you can only haul one person at a time, so you have to pick somebody first and the rest drown while you're doing it — does flipping a fresh coin at each step leave you somewhere different from one big coin flip up front?; (c) you don't know whether you're one of the five or the one.
- These leaves all have to show up: plain add-em-up save-the-many; equal chances / coin flip; weighted lottery with the odds proportional to headcount; numbers count only as a late tiebreak after each person's separate claim has been registered; something pairwise / each-counts-for-one flavoured; the agent-relative one (save your friend, save yourself, no dice needed); and the "don't count numbers in the individual case but do adopt a policy or rule that does" one.
- Tell us which pairs of leaves your tree can't actually seperate instead of pretending the questions carve them apart.

Give it back as a single JSON object — I want to paste it into a script that renders the tree, so please keep it valid JSON and nothing outside the object. Schema:

- "nodes": array of {"id", "question", "why_diagnostic" (one sentence on which positions it splits), "options": array of {"label", "leads_to": node or leaf id}}
- "leaves": array of {"id", "name", "one_liner", "most_awkward_commitment", "who_holds_it": name or "unnamed"}
- "empty_regions": array of {"answer_path": [ids], "diagnosis": "incoherent" | "unattractive" | "unexplored", "why"}
- "unseparable_pairs": array of {"pair": [leaf id, leaf id], "why", "question_that_would_separate_them_if_any"}
- "self_critique": {"weakest_node": id, "smuggled_assumption": string, "where_my_reasoning_is_most_likely_to_fail": string, "what_would_change_my_mind": string}

That last field matters to me: tell us which argument, or which answer to which node, would make you redraw the tree, and be specific about which step you'd expect a real philosopher to laugh at.
