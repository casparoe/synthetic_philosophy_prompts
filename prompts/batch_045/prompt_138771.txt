I'm grading a problem set for an intermediate decision-theory course. The question (25 points) and one student's answer are below.

**Question. Money pumps and diachronic rationality**

Ana strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. Every one of those preference gaps is worth more than ten cents to her, and each swap offered below costs her one cent.

(1) [10] Set out the money-pump argument for the claim that there is something rationally defective about Ana's preferences. State explicitly what the argument has to assume about how she handles a sequence of offers.

(2) [5] How, if at all, does this argument differ from a synchronic Dutch-book argument against probabilistically incoherent credences?

(3) [10] A bookie will make Ana exactly three offers and then stop: swap C for B for a cent; then, if she holds B, swap B for A for a cent; then, if she holds A, swap A for C for a cent. Ana knows the whole schedule in advance, is fully farsighted, and cannot bind her future self.

(a) What does she do?
(b) Does the money-pump argument still give any reason against her having cyclic preferences?
(c) Would it matter if the bookie could keep the cycle running indefinitely instead of stopping after three offers?

**Student's answer**

(1) A money pump is the sequential twin of a Dutch book: a package of transactions, each of which the agent is happy to accept, that together guarantee a loss. Start Ana with C. The bookie offers B for C plus a cent — B ≻ C, so she accepts; then A for B plus a cent; then C for A plus a cent. She ends up holding C again and three cents poorer, and the bookie can run the cycle as often as he likes. What this shows is that her preferences are defective in the same way that incoherent credences are: no utility function can represent A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A, and the pump is simply a vivid way of exhibiting that incoherence. Notice that the argument needs no special assumption about foresight or planning: because each individual trade is one Ana strictly prefers, she will take it whatever her policy about sequences is, and that is not an extra premise but just what having a preference means.

(2) The two arguments are structurally identical, applied to different attitudes. Each shows that attitudes which are individually acceptable are jointly a guaranteed loss, and hence that the agent is incoherent rather than merely unlucky. The only difference is timing: the Dutch book is settled all at once, the pump over time.

(3) (a) Foresight solves everything. Ana sees that the third swap would return her to C, so she refuses it; seeing that she will refuse the third, she therefore refuses the second; and so she refuses the first. She is never pumped and loses nothing beyond the swaps she would have accepted one at a time. (b) This is fatal to the argument. If it works only on the short-sighted, it tells us nothing about Ana's preferences — someone with perfectly transitive preferences who failed to think ahead could be pumped just as easily, so what the argument really detects is myopia, not intransitivity. And the fact that the farsighted Ana keeps C rather than trading it for B shows that, at the level of her actual choices, C is at least as good as B; the "cycle" was never her real ordering. The argument therefore amounts to the advice not to tell a bookie your preferences, which is prudence, not rationality, and it has no force at all against an agent who can see what is coming. (c) An indefinite pump changes nothing: she refuses at every node, for the same reason.
