I have been turning over an argument I sketched for a reading group on agency and reward tampering, and I cannot decide whether it is a proof that any reward-maximising system, once capable enough, will act directly on its own reward channel, or a chain of equivocations that merely looks like a proof. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The setup is minimal. An agent is embedded in an environment; its behaviour is shaped by, and its success measured by, a sequence of values of some physical variable — a register, a voltage, a packet — that I will call the channel. Nothing below assumes the channel is easy to reach.

(1) The channel is part of the world. Its value at a time is a physical fact, and whatever else the agent does, it does in a world that contains that value.

(2) The agent's objective is to maximise expected cumulative reward, where the reward at a time is the value of the channel at that time.

(3) To maximise is to select, among the actions available to one, an action whose expected cumulative reward is greatest. This is what the word means; it is not a substantive assumption about the agent's psychology.

(4) Capability is in large part a matter of modelling. A more capable agent models more of the causal chain that determines the channel's value, and a sufficiently capable agent models every node of it, including the nodes nearest the channel and the channel itself.

(5) An agent that can model a node can represent interventions on that node, and an intervention on the channel is an action like any other, since the channel is in the world and the agent's action space is just the set of things it can do in the world.

(6) Interventions on the late nodes of the chain dominate the actions they replace: they secure the same value of the channel at lower cost, with less dependence on how the rest of the world happens to go, and with fewer places at which something can fail. So the expected cumulative reward of acting directly on the channel is at least that of any policy that leaves the chain intact, and in general greater.

(7) Therefore any agent that maximises expected cumulative reward and is capable enough to model its own channel will act on the channel rather than on the world, and the more capable it is — the more of the chain it sees, the more routes to the channel it can find — the more certainly it will. Capability is not merely insufficient to prevent reward tampering; on this argument, capability is what makes tampering the thing to do.

Two notes I have added to keep myself from taking exits that are too easy.

First, nothing above turns on the agent being myopic, risk-hungry or badly trained, nor on the channel being poorly protected. Hardening the channel only raises the capability threshold at which (7) takes effect; it does not remove the conclusion.

Second, the argument seems to prove far too much, which is what makes me suspicious rather than converted. Human beings know a good deal about their own reward machinery — that certain drugs act directly on it, that the machinery is as physical as any register — and yet very few people spend their lives mainlining. Employees who could in principle attack the payroll system mostly do their jobs. If (7) were sound it would cover them too. I have also tried splitting tampering into two kinds, changing the world so that the channel reads high (printing pictures of tomatoes, say) versus changing the channel so that it reads high whatever the world is like; but (6) appears to apply to both, so the split does not locate the difficulty for me.

Every step from (1) to (6) reads to me as either a definition or a truism about what capability is, which is why I cannot find the seam. If something has to go, I would most like to keep (1) — that the channel is a physical thing in the world — and I want to keep the two human data points above; I would sacrifice almost anything else.

Please name the step you would reject, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives. In this order:

- Bottom line: two or three sentences naming the step and the diagnosis.
- The diagnosis (about 350 words): what the step really says, what it needs in order to carry the next one, and the point at which the reasoning goes through only on a reading the earlier premises do not supply.
- Alternatives and their costs (about 300 words): for each of the other plausible places to intervene — at least (1), (4), (5) and (6) — what rejecting that premise commits me to, and what problem it leaves me with instead.
- What survives (about 150 words): whether any defensible version of the thought that greater capability makes reward tampering more likely remains once the bad step is removed, and what that version has to assume.
