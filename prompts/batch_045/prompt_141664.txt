hi — I volunteer at a hospice that runs "planning ahead" afternoons, where we sit with people while they fill in advance care planning forms. None of us have any philosophy training and afterwards we always end up arguing about the same thing: when the person who signed the form isn't really the person in front of us any more, whose say counts? I want to build a one-page decision tree we could pin up in the volunteers' room, and I'd rather it was built from the cases up than from a list of -isms someone read about.

What I have in mind: branch nodes that are plain claims a volunteer could agree or disagree with without knowing where the branch leads, and leaves that are the actual positions on whether (and why) an earlier self has authority over a later one. Can you:

1. Start from the logical space, not the names. Give me the questions first, say how many answer-combinations they allow, and only then drop the real views into the cells they occupy.
2. Keep it to nine branch nodes or fewer — it has to fit on one side of A4.
3. Build it so it separates on these distinctions (if they're the right ones — tell me if any of mine does no work): whether the document ever has *authority* as opposed to just being good evidence about what the person now wants or what's good for them; if it does have authority, whether that comes from the earlier self's say-so being a continuing exercise of self-determination, from it being a binding act like a promise, from the earlier self speaking for the person's endorsed values rather than their moment-to-moment experience, or from the earlier self simply having been in a better position to judge; whether psychological continuity makes the authority fade gradually or switch off at a threshold; whether a *currently competent* later self who has changed their mind defeats the document; whether it matters that the earlier self actually anticipated the situation that came up; and whether refusing treatment and demanding treatment have to be treated the same way.
4. Use these cases as the material the questions are made of (add your own if a distinction needs one): a woman with advanced dementia who no longer recognises the document but eats eagerly by hand and seems content, whose form refused tube feeding; a psychiatric directive written during remission that refuses the one medication that has ever worked, with the person now in relapse and asking for it; someone who converts, after signing, to a religion that forbids the treatment the form asks for; a Ulysses-style clause where the person is competent at the time and demanding the very thing they previously refused in writing; a form that includes a sunset clause saying ignore this after ten years; and a form that instructs the team to disregard any later version of the person who seems at peace.
5. Every leaf needs a name if a real one exists, "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please don't invent authors or paper titles — if you're not confident a name or a view is real, leave the leaf unnamed and describe it.
6. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate, and say what would separate them.
7. Note the unoccupied combinations and say briefly why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored — and whether any of them is in fact what most clinicians quietly do.
8. Trace two of my volunteers through it as a test. One says "if she's happy now, the form is just a piece of paper." The other says "she signed it when she was still herself; happiness isn't the point." Which leaf does each land on, and does the tree give them the answer they'd expect?

Two things I don't want: nothing should turn on what the law actually says in any particular country (we're not lawyers, and the law keeps changing), and no node should just ask straight out whether the person in the bed is the same person as the one who signed — that's the bit we're trying to sort people on, so it has to come out of what they say about the cases. Answer in British English please.
