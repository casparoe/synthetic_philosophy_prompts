Working title: "What Can A Price Know?" Topic: no-trade theorems and the epistemology of markets. Notes below, roughly in the order the sections would go.

§1. Start from the familiar epistemic defense of markets (in the Hayekian spirit, though I don't want the essay to be an exegesis): the point of a market isn't just that it allocates stuff, it's that it compresses a huge amount of dispersed, local, partly inarticulable knowledge into one number that anyone can act on. Call this the epistemic defense, as opposed to the allocational defense (markets are good at moving goods to where they're most wanted).

§2. Sketch the no-trade setup: agents with a common prior, common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge of each other's posteriors, trade that's voluntary and (for the speculative bit) zero-sum in expectation. Result: no trade. The mechanism worth dwelling on: my willingness to trade is itself evidence against me — if you want to buy from me, you either have information I lack or you think I've made an error, and either way I should update toward your view before trading, at which point the trade is no longer mutually beneficial. The "I wouldn't want to trade with anyone who'd be willing to trade with me" move.

§3. The move I want to make: run that argument backwards as an epistemic audit. Given that a trade happened, at least one of the no-trade conditions failed. So observed volume is not merely "interest" or "liquidity"; it's evidence about which condition failed. Three escape hatches: (a) allocational gains — hedging, risk-sharing, liquidity needs, different endowments; (b) genuine prior divergence, disagreement not traceable back to a common prior plus differential information; (c) error — bounded rationality, miscalculation, noise trading. Call the decomposition of volume across (a)/(b)/(c) the "no-trade audit," and say a market's epistemic status depends on how its volume decomposes.

§4. Distinguish signal trades from insurance trades. Signal trades are ones where the motive is disagreement about the fundamental; insurance trades are ones where both sides agree about the fundamental and are trading risk. Claim: only signal trades do the epistemic work the epistemic defense needs. An insurance trade tells you something about the distribution of hedging needs in the population, which is not nothing, but it isn't knowledge about the asset or the world.

§5. Bring in the familiar impossibility result about fully informative prices: if prices fully impounded everyone's private information, nobody would have an incentive to pay to acquire information in the first place, so a market can't be perfectly informationally efficient — some residual noise has to be there to reward the information producers. So the market needs ignorance to function as a knowledge-generating machine.

§6. The dilemma I want the essay to turn on. The epistemic defense needs signal trades; signal trades require (b); (b) requires that rational agents can disagree while sharing an evidence base — i.e., some permissivism about priors, no uniquely rational prior. So the epistemic defense of markets is covertly committed to a substantive and contested epistemological thesis. But if you instead hold that there is a uniquely rational prior (call it objectivism, or whatever the right label is), then residual disagreement is error, prices aggregate error alongside knowledge, and the epistemic defense collapses into the allocational one: prices are at best a noisy poll of a population that is partly confused. Both horns are philosophically loaded and neither is obviously available to the market's defenders.

§7. A thing I rather like: read the bid-ask spread as an index of the market's self-doubt. The spread is the market maker's charge for the possibility that the other side knows something; it's the adverse-selection tax on disagreement. Epistemically: the spread is the price of the market's own ignorance about who is informed. Punchline — as the spread widens toward the point where nothing trades, the market becomes maximally honest about its ignorance and ceases to produce any knowledge at all. Full self-knowledge is the end of inquiry; the market has its own version of the paradox where the fully-informed inquirer has no reason to inquire. Is this cute or is it a pun dressed up as an argument?

§8. Dynamics: the sucker learns. Traders who systematically lose to better-informed counterparties learn they're on the wrong side and withdraw; adverse selection thins the pool; thinner pool means worse price discovery and wider spreads; repeat. Markets, on this story, die of their own epistemicity. I want this to be the closing image but I'm not sure it isn't just a just-so story.

§9. Objections I can already see coming: (i) most real trade is allocational, so no-trade results are beside the point — reply: granted, but then the price isn't telling you about the world and the epistemic defense shrinks; (ii) priors are subjective, so (b) isn't irrationality at all — reply: fine, but then "price aggregates knowledge" becomes "price aggregates opinion," and the epistemic defense becomes a defense of opinion pooling that inherits all the usual worries about independence and competence; (iii) errors cancel out in aggregate — reply: adverse selection and herding make noise systematically correlated precisely when it matters, so the independence assumption fails where you need it.
