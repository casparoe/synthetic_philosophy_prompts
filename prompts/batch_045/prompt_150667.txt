hey — I'm in a constitutional theory seminar this term (joint law/pol theory, about twelve people, two-hour session, I get maybe 20 minutes to present and then basically a firing squad) and on Thursday I'm presenting a paper on whether the Ulysses contract is actually a good model for constitutional precommitment. Here's roughly where I land:

The analogy is usually run as: the people tie themselves to the mast so they can't wreck themselves later in a moment of passion. I think that framing hides the thing that's doing all the normative work, which is the release procedure. In the story the lock is absolute — the crew has been told to ignore everything Ulysses says while he's tied up, so there's no way out for him however sober and well-informed he later becomes. Constitutional entrenchment basically never works that way: it's a costly-release device — supermajorities, two votes with an election in between, referenda — where every outcome stays reachable if enough people want it hard enough and long enough. So the real question isn't "may we bind ourselves?" but "what should it take to get out?"

My actual claim is that a precommitment is legitimate only if the release procedure is (i) self-contained, meaning the bound generation can complete it on its own without needing permission from any body it didn't elect, and (ii) sensitive to how strong *and how durable* the later demand for release is, not just how numerically big it is. That kills unamendable clauses and purely court-enforced substantive locks, and it vindicates the two-legislatures-with-an-election-in-between rule. But it also has a consequence I'm nervous about, which is that (ii) seems to imply the release procedure should sometimes be harder to trigger exactly when the demand for release is most sudden and intense, and I'm not sure I can say that out loud to this room without sounding like I've given up on majority rule.

Who's in the room: our prof is a legal theorist who's sympathetic to precommitment but thinks the people as a single agent is a fiction, and she'll press me on that. Four or five students are democratic-proceduralist types, hostile to judicial review, they think entrenchment is just the dead hand. Two are public-choice / rational-choice people who'll say every lock is really just whichever interest group happened to be in the room at the founding taxing its successors. There's one Hayek-ish guy who thinks the whole point of general rules is that they outlive us, so he's on my side for the wrong reasons. And there's one person who works on medical ethics and advance directives, and she's the one I'm actually most scared of, because she knows the clinical Ulysses-contract side of this and will ask who plays the crew — i.e. who decides whether the later majority is competent to be released — and I think that question turns my paper into an argument for judicial review, which I definately don't want.
