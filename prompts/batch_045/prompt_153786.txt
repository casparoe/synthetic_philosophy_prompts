Hey — I run a small journal club for epidemiology and public-health grad students, mostly people who use risk scores daily and have never done any philosophy of probability. I want a session where they sort themselves on the reference class problem before anyone gets to say which -ism they like, so I need a decision tree built out of cases rather than out of a list of schools.

Start everyone from the same concrete root: there is one particular patient in front of you, you have to give a number for their chance of a cardiac event in the next ten years, and you have several statistics you could cite — the rate in their country, the rate among people their age, the rate among people their age with their cholesterol and smoking history, the rate among people their age with their cholesterol, smoking history and a specific genotype. The tree then has to get them, by eight to ten branch questions, to a named position: some kind of frequentism on which the single-case claim is elliptical for a class frequency, some kind of propensity or single-case physical chance view, a classical/indifference-style view, a subjective Bayesian view, an objective-Bayesian or maximum-entropy style view on which the total evidence settles it, and a pluralist view on which every admissible class gives a genuine but different probability and there is no fact of the matter. Six to eight leaves, each with a one-sentence note on the most awkward thing it commits you to. If a leaf has no standard name, call it unnamed rather than inventing a label.

Three things matter to me beyond that. First, no question should mention a school by name or ask anything like do you think probability is in the world or in your head — every node has to be a case or a claim a student could answer cold. Please use at least these: a case where the individual is in two classes with different rates and no narrower class has usable data; a Simpson-style case where aggregation over one partition reverses a comparison that holds in every subgroup; a case where the relevant marker is real but the patient could not have known they had it at the time you had to decide; a case where the individual's risk partly depends on which class you file them under because they will change their behaviour in response to the number you give them; and a case where the statistics come from a model trained on one population and the patient is not from it. Second, I want you to work from the logical space: tell me which combinations of answers are empty and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied, and flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot really seperate instead of pretending they diverge. Third, there is a position my students actually hold in practice that I don't see discussed: the probability just is whatever a deployed model outputs, and the reference class is whatever the training data happened to be. Give that a leaf if it deserves one, or tell me it collapses into something else and which.

Commit to somehting: say which two or three of your branch questions are doing all the real sorting work and which are decoration, and if you think my root is the wrong place to start, say so and rebuild the tree from the root you'd pick instead. Then plant a flag — end by saying which leaf you would stand on yourself and why, in a couple of sentences. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables. I know a tree wants a diagram, so do it in sentences — at each node say what the question is, what each answer commits you to, and which node it sends you to next. No citations or further reading, I'm offline and a bibliography would just be noise.
