I'm giving the toast at my doctoral advisor's retirement dinner. She has spent her career arguing that Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur (Discourse on Happiness) offers the most honest recipe for happiness we have: to be happy, one must have freed oneself from prejudice, be virtuous, be in good health, have tastes and passions, and be susceptible to illusions; one must also labor to replace dangerous passions with calmer tastes, conserve the trustworthy illusions one is given rather than dismantle them, and never let the heart love someone who brings misfortune. She further maintains, with du Châtelet, that the passion for gambling is a happy resource only if it is moderated and reserved for the time of life when it will be needed—old age—and that, because fortune has placed each of us in a particular state, the road to happiness is not to try to change one's circumstances but to be content with one's state, advice that du Châtelet addresses to those who already have a settled fortune.

In retirement my advisor intends to do exactly this: she is finally going to allow herself the moderate indulgence of her longtime passion for high-stakes bridge, a passion she has always kept within bounds and which she now regards as du Châtelet's resource for old age, and she has refused the university's new wellbeing dashboard, an app that tracks and optimizes moods, on the ground that a fully quantified life destroys the illusions du Châtelet thought we must conserve. The room will be roughly half philosophers, many of whom know the Discours well, and half family members and university administrators who have never heard of du Châtelet.

Please write a five-minute toast that celebrates her, lands as affection rather than as a lecture, and does not say anything she would consider false. In particular, it must not wish her "many more years of passionate research" or praise her for "following her passion" into retirement, because du Châtelet holds that one should replace passions with tastes as one ages; it must not tell her she will "finally have time to love and be loved," because du Châtelet warns never to let the heart love someone who brings misfortune and treats love as a passion full of illusion and ardor; and it must not offer consolation about her living on in her students or watching over the department from somewhere, or any other secularized afterlife, since du Châtelet's naturalistic account locates happiness entirely in this life and gives no survival in memory or elsewhere. Instead, the toast should take one of du Châtelet's own distinctions—such as the distinction between a passion and a taste, or between a trustworthy illusion and a harmful one—and use it to toast her future, in language warm enough for the non-philosophers but exact enough that the philosophers present will recognize the constraint and not think the toast has betrayed her.
