I'm a fourth-year PhD candidate in philosophy working on the norms of diachronic practical reasoning, and I have been asked to serve on the small committee that drafts this year's written comprehensive exam in practical ethics. My assignment is one question for the 90-minute, closed-book, no-library segment, on the topic of advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. The scripts are graded anonymously by three readers, so the question has to come with a standard attached.

What I want to avoid is a question that can be answered by running through the dialectic everyone in the cohort will have prepared: precedent autonomy versus best interests, whether personal identity or psychological continuity is what could possibly ground the earlier self's authority, whether the later demented self is still there, and the epistemic worry that we cannot tell whether she is. I want a crux that the standard literature does not already hand an answer to.

The device I have been building the question around is what I have started calling a non-accommodation clause. Suppose that at 42, having watched a parent's dementia, M. executes an advance directive refusing life-prolonging treatment under conditions that are later precisely met. The directive also contains a clause along these lines: I expect that if this happens the later me will seem content, will express revised preferences in favour of continued life, and will say that I was wrong about what makes a life worth living; I direct that any such revision, however confident, consistent and apparently well-integrated, be treated as a product of the pathology and given no weight at all in interpreting what I have written here. At 71, with moderately severe Alzheimer's disease, M. is in the specified condition, presents as content, and asks, with reasons she can articulate, that the directive not be honoured. Her reasons are not mere thrashing about: she says that her earlier self was harsh about dependence and frightened of being a burden, and that she now thinks that was a mistake. In form, this is indistinguishable from the kind of reflective value revision any competent person undergoes.

Three things I want the question to force, and which I would like the marking scheme to track:

1. The non-accommodation clause appears to be self-undermining in a way the ordinary directive is not. An advance directive is standardly justified as extending competent self-government across time; a clause that instructs us to disregard the later person's testimony about her own values, including testimony about the directive itself, seems to purchase protection for one exercise of self-government at the price of disabling the only mechanism by which self-government could be corrected. I want candidates to say whether that is a genuine problem, or whether it is a feature that any robust precommitment device must have, and how the answer bears on the analogy with Ulysses contracts in psychiatry.

2. The clause can be iterated: the earlier self can anticipate that the later self will agree with the clause while claiming her own case is the exception it was not meant to catch, and can pre-empt that move too, and can pre-empt the pre-emption. Candidates should have to say whether indefinite iteration changes anything, and why or why not.

3. There is an evidential asymmetry I want exploited. Later agreement with the directive is normally treated as confirmation that the directive speaks for the person; later disagreement is treated, under the clause, as noise. Yet the two states of the person are evidentially on a par with respect to whatever the underlying pathology does. I want candidates to explain what, if anything, could justify the asymmetry, and then to say what a surrogate ought to do — and what an institutional rule ought to say — when the evidence about whether the later self's revision is genuine versus pathology-driven is irreducibly probabilistic.

A further sub-part should ask what conditions an earlier act must satisfy in order to bind at all: stability over time, information, freedom from transient affect and from manipulation, perhaps something like having survived serious reconsideration. The question should press on whether these conditions are additive constraints on an otherwise unitary notion of authority, or whether the authority of the earlier self is better understood as something graded and defeasible rather than as a switch that a directive flips.

What I need from you:

The question itself, as it will appear on the paper, with its sub-parts, written for a 90-minute closed-book answer, requiring no citations, no case law, no jurisdiction-specific doctrine and no library access.

A model answer of roughly 800 words.

A marking scheme built around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth out of 20, and, attached to each, one line on the characteristic failure mode I should expect to see — the way a well-prepared candidate gets that point wrong rather than merely omits it.

A short paragraph stating what separates an A script from a B script that recites the standard positions correctly, and a list of moves that should actively lose marks rather than merely fail to earn them — for instance, settling the case by stipulating that the earlier self's authority is absolute, or by collapsing it into the question of whether the later self is numerically the same person.

Finally, flag any point at which my case as described above is under-specified in a way that would let a candidate wriggle out of the crux, and tell me how to close the gap.
