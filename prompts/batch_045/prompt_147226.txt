I co-edit a small end-of-term zine for the social choice reading group I help run — half of us are from economics, half from political theory — and a friend who works in DAO governance tooling keeps sending me her industry's marketing copy, which has become a running joke: every platform on earth advertises itself, in one phrasing or another, as immune to strategic voting. For the last issue I want a piece of satire built out of that joke, and I want the joke to come from getting the result right rather than from name-dropping it.

The piece: a leaked internal compliance memo — 700 to 900 words, deadpan corporate register, memo header (To / From / Date / Re) and numbered sections — written by the compliance team of a fictional governance platform called Sincere ("Vote your true preferences. We handle the rest," as the letterhead has it), in response to a marketing landing page that already shipped the claim that the platform is provably strategy-proof. Assume the audience: the theorem in question is the one Allan Gibbard proved in 1973 and Mark Satterthwaite independently in 1975 — any deterministic rule that takes voters' ordinal rankings, returns a single winner, and can produce three or more different outcomes is manipulable unless it is dictatorial — together with the later extension to rules that may randomise, and the known fact that on single-peaked domains there is a well-behaved non-dictatorial option (the median rule). Also assume what the definition does not cover: only one voter at a time is imagined to misreport, only ordinal rankings are transmitted, and nothing in the guarantee speaks to coalitions, to intensity, or to how the alternatives got onto the ballot. Section 1 should explain, in compliance-speak, why marketing's sentence cannot ship as written; Section 2 should set out the three "Compliance Modes" the team has available — I'll leave the codenames to you, but I have in mind something like a delegated-outcome mode where one ballot is drawn and recorded as the result, an agenda-pairing mode where every proposal is resolved between two pre-selected options, and a domain-restriction mode where members must attest that their preferences are single-peaked along an official Axis the company publishes — each with proposed customer-facing language; Section 3 should recommend one of them and say why the others test badly in focus groups; Section 4 should be residual risks, filed as reputational rather than mathematical. Two constraints. First: nobody in the memo may use the word "dictator" in any text a customer could see — the comedy is in the euphemisms they invent instead ("founder's resolution authority," "single-wallet determination," and so on). Second: the memo's last sentence should be flat, procedural, and genuinely chilly — the moment the reader notices that a platform that can honestly call itself strategy-proof has quietly stopped selling voting. No real companies, no real named people, no footnotes, no winking at the reader.

After the memo, add a structured summary as a single JSON object with this schema: {"modes": [{"mode_name": the internal codename, "condition_relaxed": which premise of the result the mode gives up — a range of at least three possible outcomes, non-dictatorship, or deterministic single-winner resolution, "theorem_licensing_it": which result makes the mode genuinely strategy-proof — the 1973/1975 theorem, its extension to randomised rules, or the single-peaked median-voter results, "marketing_euphemism": the exact phrase the memo proposes, "what_a_customer_hears": one sentence, "what_it_actually_means": one sentence, blunt}], "silences_exploited": up to five short strings naming features of a real election that strategy-proofness as defined does not regulate — agenda control, coalitional misreports, cardinal intensity, where the preferences came from, whether the winner is any good, "recommended_mode": the codename of the mode Section 3 recommends, "bottom_line": one sentence on why an honestly strategy-proof system with three or more options is not obviously something anyone should want to buy}.
