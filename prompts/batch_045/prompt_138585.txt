I'm applying for a one-year sabbatical fellowship and need a project statement of about two pages, so the question has to be one I can actually answer in a year rather than a research program.

Here is the half-formed idea. Derivations of the Born rule in the Everettian picture divide, roughly, into decision-theoretic ones, which extract rational credence from branching structure plus constraints on preference, and measure-postulating ones, which argue that agents situated in high-measure branches are the ones who should expect frequencies matching the amplitudes. Both, as far as I can tell, take branching structure as input: a preferred decomposition into branches, a definite number of them, exact orthogonality. But decoherence is approximate, and how many branches an experiment produces seems to depend on how finely one resolves the environment, so branch count is not a robust quantity.

My suspicion is a trilemma, and I want the proposal built around testing it. If the significance measure over branches is required to be additive under refinement of the decomposition, symmetric, and continuous, the squared amplitude is forced almost immediately: divide a state into n equal-amplitude branches, note that each must carry weight 1/n, and the rest follows. But that step requires exact branch counting. Relax additivity so that it holds only up to the vagueness of branch individuation, and multiplicative measures of the amplitude to a power other than two survive, and I suspect no finite body of experimental statistics distinguishes them from the Born weights.

Write the proposal around this: state the question so that it has a determinate answer; say why the existing derivations leave it open; give the method month by month for a year; lay out the three possible outcomes and what would decide among them; and say plainly what would count as the project failing. Frame it so that it makes progress even if the standing dispute about whether the decision-theoretic derivations beg the question is left exactly where it is — I don't intend to spend the year there. Please also say, briefly, what difference the answer makes to someone who doubts that the Everettian was ever owed a probability rule in the first place. No citations; I'll add those myself.
