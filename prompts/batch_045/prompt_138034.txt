I've got a long flight coming up and finally listned to a philosophy podcast episode on dreams — the host has a PhD and teaches somewhere, so I assumed it was reliable, but a couple of things in it dont sit right with me. Here's his summary of the Malcolm / Dennett dispute, as close to his wording as I can manage:

"Malcolm and Dennett both argue that dreams are not experiences, and they get to basically the same place by different routes. Malcolm's argument is a priori: the only thing that can count as someone's having dreamt is what they say once they're awake, since there's no way to check, while a person is asleep, whether they're dreaming. So dreams aren't something that happens to you while you're asleep — strictly it doesn't even make sense to say of a sleeping person that they're dreaming right now. Dennett says the same thing in a more scientific idiom: the only evidence we have about dreams is waking reports, and those reports are confabulations spun on the spot when you wake up, so there's no dream there for an experience to be of. The upshot is that the sleep researcher watching your REM is looking for something that isn't there: the brain activity is real, but nothing is experienced, and it wouldn't help if scanners eventually let us read dream content off the brain, because for Malcolm the question is settled by the grammar of 'dream' and for Dennett the reports are all we ever had anyway. And there's a decisive objection to Dennett: people misremember their dreams constantly — you wake up sure you dreamt about a beach and then realize it was a supermarket — and if the dream just was the memory, there'd be nothing for the memory to get wrong. Since Malcolm and Dennett are in the same boat on this, both are refuted: dreams really are experiences, and sleep science has known it since REM was discovered in the 1950s."

Two things make me suspicious. It can't be this easy — Dennett has obviously heard of misremembering. And I read somewhere that people with REM sleep behaviour disorder physically act out their dreams, and that sleep labs have had lucid dreamers agree in advance to move their eyes in a coded pattern once they're in REM, with the movements showing up on the recording at roughly the time the subject later says they were signalling. That looks like exactly the during-sleep check the host says doesn't exist.

Can you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful defender of each view would accept, what they'd wince at, and where the host has quietly conflated two things the views keep apart? Specifically: is it right that they end up in the same place, or does the difference between a conceptual argument about criteria and an empirical argument about evidence change what each of them is actually committed to? Is "the reports are confabulations" fair to Dennett, or does that word smuggle in something he doesn't need? Is the misremembering point as decisive as it sounds, and does it touch Malcolm at all? And what should I do with my eye-signal example — does it hand Malcolm the criterion he says is missing, or would he say the concept of dreaming has merely been extended rather than shown to have applied all along? Does it count against Dennett, or is it neutral for him?

Last thing: if the summary's conclusion doesn't survive, is there a deflated version of "dreams are experiences" that does? And if the honest answer is that the dispute is partly verbal, tell me what the non-verbal bit is and what would settle it. I'd rather know which sentences to stop repeating than who wins.
