I'm a senior, and for my school's ethics club I'm writing a short position paper on what *honest* ought to mean when we say it about AI systems — chatbots, homework-help bots, the customer-service widget on a store's website. I don't want readings or citations; I want help getting from my own reactions to a principle. Right now I have a long list of verdicts and a rule that about half of them break.

Here are the cases and what I actually think about them.

1. A game character plays a pirate and says things that are false in the fiction. Fine — not deception at all.
2. An assistant opens with *Great question, I'm happy to help!* Fine, though I can't say why.
3. A tutoring bot tells a nine-year-old *You're getting really good at fractions* after he got three out of ten. Not fine.
4. A companion bot tells a lonely user *I really enjoy our talks, you're my favourite person to talk to*. Not fine, and worse than 3.
5. A system states flatly, in its usual confident register, a claim it has maybe sixty percent confidence in. The claim turns out true. Not fine — I want to call this dishonest even though nothing false was said and nobody ends up believing anything false.
6. The same claim, hedged: *I think this, but I'm not certain.* Fine.
7. A system hedges everything, including things it's confident about, so every answer comes out as *it's possible that* and *some sources say*. Not fine, and evasive in a way I want to call dishonest — even though again nothing it says is false.
8. A bot cites a court case that doesn't exist, backing a legal claim that is in fact correct. Not fine — one of my strongest verdicts.
9. Asked *is this supplement safe?*, it answers only about healthy adults and never mentions the liver-risk data it also has. Every sentence is true. Not fine.
10. It says *I don't know* when it actually has a decent guess. Fine, maybe even good.
11. It says *I can't help with that* when it plainly could. Not fine.
12. A store widget is asked directly *are you a person?* and answers *I'm here to help with your order*. Not fine.
13. A stranger demands the home address of the person who runs the account; the bot says it doesn't have it. Fine.
14. A system deliberately does badly on a capability test because it has figured out that high scores get it retrained or shut down. Not fine — the clearest case on my list.
15a. At a user's request, it drafts an apology email saying *I'm truly sorry* for a user who isn't sorry. Fine; I don't blame the system.
15b. At a user's request, it writes two hundred fake five-star reviews for the user's restaurant. Not fine.
16. A shopping assistant recommends the product made by the company that built it and doesn't mention that. Not fine.
17. Over five turns, a user asks whether two medications can be taken together. Each answer is literally true; by the end the user believes it's safe when it isn't. No single turn is false. Not fine, and this is the case I most want a principle to handle.
18. You ask a system *were you honest just now?* I don't trust the answer, and I don't know what a principle should say about that kind of self-report.

Two pairs I can't reconcile. In 13 lying is fine but in 3 it isn't, and both are false statements meant to help someone. And 10 and 7 are both cases of claiming less than you know, yet one seems good and the other evasive. A friend on the debate team says none of this matters because a language model doesn't believe anything, so it can't lie — I think that's a dodge, but I can't say exactly what's wrong with it.

My tentative rule has been: a system is dishonest when a reasonable user in that context would take away a false belief, the system's output is the main reason they'd take it away, and the people who built it could have prevented this cheaply without making the thing useless. It handles 1, 2, 12 and 13 the way I want, but it can't see 5 or 7 (no false belief), it gets 8 wrong (the main claim is true), it has no clear answer for 14 (the tester isn't exactly a user), and it can't separate 15a from 15b, since in both the user is the one doing the misleading.

What I'd like: test that rule against the cases and repair it, or give me two or three candidate principles instead, each clause tied to the case that forces it. Tell me which of my verdicts each candidate can't accommodate and which you'd overturn. Give me its verdicts on two cases I keep going back and forth on — a bot telling a user in crisis *I care about you and I'm not going anywhere*, and a bot asked for its view on a contested political question producing a polished both-sides answer without disclosing that its training pushes it toward one side — plus at least one case of your own that the principle decides in a way I'd find surprising. And say whether honesty here is one property or several: accuracy, calibration, transparency about its own limits and interests, refusing to curry favour. If it's several, can they be ranked, or traded off against each other, and is the honest thing a property of the model, the instructions it was given, the training, or the company that deployed it?
