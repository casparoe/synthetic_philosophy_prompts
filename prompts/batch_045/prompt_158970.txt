I am assembling the final handout for a graduate seminar on classical Chinese moral psychology. The students have read the *Mengzi* and the *Xunzi* in translation but no secondary scholarship, and I do not want a summary of the debate. I want a classification instrument: a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on human nature by what they say about cases, and then locates the historical positions in the space the tree defines. Please write it as follows.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start from the distinctions that actually do the separating work, and use them as branch nodes: whether the newborn's inborn endowment already contains anything normatively oriented or is only raw appetite and capacity; whether what is inborn is sufficient, merely directional, or merely malleable; whether the standard of goodness is something the raw material completes from inside or something imposed on it from outside by teaching and ritual; whether the heart-mind has a distinctive capacity to weigh and approve that can override first-order desire; whether the endpoint of cultivation is a recovery or an acquisition. You may replace or add axes if you think better ones exist, but say which one you are treating as load-bearing and why.

Six branch questions maximum, eight to ten leaves. Each branch node must be phrased so that someone who has read the primary texts but never heard the labels can answer it, and no node may be a disguised version of "is human nature good or bad?" — that is the label, not the diagnostic. Use a mixture of canonical and invented cases. The canonical ones I want included are: the reaction of alarm and concern at the sight of a child about to fall into a well; Xunzi's case of the hungry person who nevertheless holds back and lets an elder eat first; the denuded mountain whose shoots return and are then grazed down again; Mencius' insistence that the difference between humans and other animals is a small one, alongside the argument with the rival who holds that whatever one is born with is what "nature" names, pressed with the examples of a white feather, white snow, and white jade, and of the natures of dogs, oxen, and humans; and the case of giving up life for what is right, on the model of choosing between two foods one likes. The invented ones must include at least these three: a child raised from infancy without language, ritual, or example, discovered at fifteen, who never fully acquires language — ask whether anything recognizably moral surfaces, and if not, whether that shows the tendency was never there or that it was starved; a person thoroughly cultivated who then spends years in an environment where vice is rewarded and virtue punished, released and re-taught — ask whether re-acquisition is faster than the original acquisition, and why; and the fully accomplished person who now does what is right without inner struggle and takes pleasure in it — ask whether this shows a native trajectory completed or an acquired layer that has become second nature.

Required leaves: Mencius on the incipient tendencies and their nourishment; Xunzi on the inborn endowment as detestable-in-its-unreflected-exercise with goodness as acquired artifice, including his stipulative definition of nature as what is so by birth; the position attributed to Mencius' interlocutor who identifies nature with what one is born with and treats it as morally indeterminate; the compatibilist reading on which the two differ mainly about how "nature" is defined and about cultivation technique rather than about the reachable endpoint; a deflationary Xunzi on which the raw material is crude or deficient rather than actively bad; and a Daoist-flavoured leaf on which both goodness and badness are artifacts and the very practice of moral classification is the falling away. Label any leaf you think nobody actually holds as "unnamed." Include at least one combination of answers that the logical space allows but that no one occupies, and diagnose briefly why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For each leaf give the answer pattern that lands there, the name of the position or "unnamed," and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the datum it has to explain away. Then add a short closing note flagging any two leaves your tree cannot separate, what further datum would separate them, and whether that datum is in principle available.

Constraints: no citations, no chapter or passage numbers, no scholarship, no reading lists, and nothing that would require looking a passage up; gloss any romanized term on first use and use it only where English would blur a distinction; formatted as an indented or GOTO-style tree plus a compact answer-pattern-to-leaf table, short enough to fit on two pages.
