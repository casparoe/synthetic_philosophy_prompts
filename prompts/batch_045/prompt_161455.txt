Next Tuesday I have to open a special town meeting as the elected moderator, and the warrant has a single article on it: whether Westhollow should merge with the neighboring town of Kestrel into one municipality, and — because the merger would void our charter — who is entitled to vote on that question. I get four or five minutes of opening remarks before the floor opens. The difficulty is not mainly about the merger; it is that the boundary question has to be answered before the substantive one can be voted, and every available way of answering it is itself a substantive position. Our charter defines eligible voters as resident taxpayers, but the charter is precisely what the merger would replace, so using it to pick the electorate that picks the replacement is circular. A political theorist from the state university has made this point to the selectboard, and she is bringing her graduate seminar — they will be in the back row with notebooks, and she will notice immediately if I quietly settle the matter by adopting some principle in the framing. The rest of the room will not be theorists at all: the volunteer fire chief; a woman who has run the diner on the green for thirty years and who has never once cared about a jurisdictional question until this one; several tenants of the old mill conversion who pay rent but no property tax; a scattering of hospital commuters who are subject to our ordinances for nine hours a day and vote somewhere else; two households from the unincorporated hamlet upstream whose wells sit in the watershed the merged entity would control; and a reporter from the county paper with a recorder.

There are two evasions I have decided I cannot use. The first is "the law decides who votes," which collapses here because the law is what is on the table. The second is "everyone affected should have a say," which I now think is nearly empty as stated: affectedness comes in degrees, causal chains do not conveniently stop, and the phrase only becomes a rule once someone sets a threshold, while setting the threshold is exactly the decision I am not allowed to make for them. One of the selectmen has floated what he calls the reverse-mirror rule — that the electorate should mirror not who is here now but who will be governed by the new entity, which would enfranchise people who have signed leases beginning in July and the students arriving in September, and would arguably weaken the claim of the people most likely to move away if the merger passes. I find that proposal genuinely illuminating and also genuinely strange, and I would like the speech to make its strangeness visible without endorsing or burying it.

What I want from you is the opening remarks themselves: roughly six hundred words, plain enough that the diner owner follows every sentence, precise enough that the seminar in the back row cannot accuse me of begging the question, and phrased so that it works as an actual call to order rather than a miniature lecture. It should give the room a way to hold the difficulty — perhaps by making vivid that "who is governed," "who has a stake," and "who is bound by the outcome" come apart in this particular town, and that the gap between them is the thing they are voting about — while leaving every option genuinely open. It also has to be something I can stand behind afterward whichever way the vote goes, including with the people who lose and who will be looking for evidence that the framing was rigged against them. No named theorists, no citations, no allusions that only the back row will catch. After the speech, add a short paragraph of at most 150 words listing the two or three places where you deliberately declined to settle the boundary question, and what each of those refusals costs the speech.
