My brother is marrying on Saturday and I am giving the toast, and I need help writing it because two things are true at once and I cannot seem to get them into the same speech.

Eight months ago I married my own partner in a registrar's office on a Tuesday afternoon with two witnesses we had met that morning. My mother has since said more than once, within earshot of the bride's family, that when people really mean it they do it properly. On Saturday I will be standing in front of roughly two hundred people: about half of them the bride's family, who have paid for a wedding I gather cost more than I earn in a year; a large contingent of my own relatives who know exactly what my wedding cost; and a table of the couple's friends from an economics department who will hear instantly if I misuse an idea.

I have been reading about the handicap principle, and it is spoiling the toast for me. As I understand it, three consequences follow, and each of them takes a sentence away from me. First, a cost that is not borne by the signaler carries no information about the signaler: most of Saturday's expenditure is borne by the bride's parents, so on the theory's own terms it tells the room about them and not about my brother. I therefore cannot let the expense do any work as evidence of his or her commitment — but I also cannot be heard to sneer at it, or to imply that my cheap wedding was the more honest one. Second, what makes a signal reliable is differential cost, not absolute cost: the only costs that can carry information about these two are the ones they took on themselves — he sold the motorcycle he had spent four years rebuilding; she turned down a fellowship abroad to stay in the same city. I would like the toast to turn on one of those, but without converting it into a price, and without saying that anything is proved, since on this picture signals concern interests and resolve rather than affections.

There is a further wrinkle I keep turning over, and it removes my favourite line. A wedding seems to me to be addressed at least as much to the guests as to the bride: its function is to enrol the room as people who will later hold the couple to what they said. That makes the sentence every toast reaches for — that the two of them need no one else — actively cancel the mechanism the wedding exists to create, and I need something to put in its place. I would also like the toast to contain no joke at all about the cost, about debt, or about what the couple may owe the bride's parents: a signal whose cost damages the condition it advertises is self-undermining, and a joke of that kind is not neutral, it points straight at the thing that would make the whole display unreliable.

Please write me a four- to five-minute toast built around the couple's own renunciations rather than around the money, which lands as affection and not as a lecture, and which says nothing my mother can take as an apology and nothing the bride's family can take as an insult. Tell me as well which sentences the theory rules out and why. One condition: the theory must be invisible. The words signal, cost, handicap and game theory are not to appear in the toast — if the idea is doing its work it will show up only in what I decline to say. And give me one sentence near the end that my grandmother and the economists at table nine can both hear without wincing.
