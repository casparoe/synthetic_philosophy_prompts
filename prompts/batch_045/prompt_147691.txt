You are a professional philosopher who also designs and runs classroom simulations: someone who has taught action theory and practical reason at university level and who knows from hard experience which mechanics actually land with students and which look elegant on paper and then die in the room. Start with a short brainstorming paragraph in which you sketch three or four candidate mechanics and say, in a sentence each, why you have set them aside; then present the one you keep and justify the choice.

The setting: I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on action, intention and practical reason. Eighteen students, a single 80-minute session, a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards, and a bag of identical snack bars if you want them. No student laptops; I have one. The students have read standard material on reasons, weakness of will, and the belief-desire model of action explanation. They have not met this particular problem on the syllabus, and I would rather they did not hear its traditional name until the debrief.

The session is on choice without preference: an agent faces two or more options, has no reason whatsoever that favours any one over the others, and must nevertheless act. I want them to feel, rather than be told, that this is a genuine practical fix and not a pseudo-problem, and I want them to discover for themselves why the obvious exits fail.

Design me a runnable activity — a game, a staged deliberation, or a structured sequence of real choices — in which the difficulty arises out of the rules and incentives rather than out of anything the facilitator announces. Roughly in this order, I want students to run into:

1. Real suspension. Faced with options they genuinely cannot rank, most of them will stall: they deliberate, inspect, look for a distinguishing feature, ask for more information — and discover that information is not what they lack. Any mechanic that lets them resolve the choice by uncovering a hidden difference has missed the point, so the options must be built so that no amount of inspection will yield a tie-breaker. Tell me how you guarantee this.

2. The second-order regress. I want a round in which randomising devices — coins, dice, drawing lots — are explicitly forbidden, so that students are forced to produce a reason for taking one option. Whatever they produce should be exposed, in the moment, as itself arbitrary: left before right, alphabetical order, first seen, nearest the door — all tie-breaks, none a reason that favours the option selected. Then a round in which the choice is not between the original options but between competing tie-break procedures, which is itself an unranked choice. Let the rules make the regress visible without my naming it.

3. Arbitrary versus random versus authorised. I want them to hit the difference between an arbitrary selector (a fixed convention such as always take the one on the left), a randomiser (a chancy device), and what I will call an authorised selector: a procedure agreed in advance, before anyone knows which option it will favour. My hunch is that consenting to a procedure behind a small veil changes how the outcome feels and whether it seems rational; I want the activity to test that hunch rather than assert it.

4. Preference laundering. After the choice is finally made, I want some way of showing that agents typically manufacture a preference for whatever they ended up with and report having wanted it slightly more all along. If you can build measurement into the activity — an intensity or confidence rating taken before the choice and again afterwards, or a written prediction that can later be compared with a written report — please do.

5. The contrast cases. Include at least one round where the options are not equal but differ imperceptibly, so that the agent's epistemic position is unchanged even though the world differs; and one where the options differ in kind and arguably cannot be measured on a single scale at all. Students should come away able to say what sort of difficulty each is and why neither is simply the tie case again.
