Longtime reader here — fifteen years or so of the same blogs and the same Sunday meetups has left me with a reflex flinch at any sentence that closes an argument by sounding wise. I'd rather cash this one out than dismiss it.

At a meetup this weekend we got onto donors who give a large share of their income and whether it matters that many of them seem to be in it partly for the good feeling. One person said the warm-glow donor's action has no less moral worth than anyone else's, since the money does the same work either way. Someone else answered with: it's the thought that counts. Twenty minutes later the same person used the same line for a second job — to say that when we are assessing the *agent*, lives per dollar is beside the point, so an ineffective donor who meant well is not to be criticised. I could not tell whether those were the same claim, and neither could anyone else in the room.

Please disambiguate it. I want exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no closing paragraph: each a distinct precise thesis the sentence could be expressing, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical, with a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false, and a note on whether it supports the first use, the second use, both, or neither. No citations and no reading list — I'll do that part myself.

Three constraints, so the menu is actually testable. First, tell me which reading is in play for "counts": I think at least three scoreboards are being conflated — whether the *action* has moral worth, whether the *agent* is praiseworthy or admirable, and whether the rest of us should encourage the behaviour — and I want each item located with respect to them. Second, every item should give a verdict on two cases I care about: the donor who has the right reason available but where that reason plays no causal role in the giving and would evaporate if it were ever the only thing moving her, and the donor moved both by the right reason and by the warm glow, who would have given anyway had only the warm glow been present. Third, if there is a serious case that the disagreement is verbal — that "moral worth" does not pick out one thing and the line is a way of not noticing that — make one of the five that reading, and say what survives the dispute once the phrase is tabooed and we ask instead what we are doing when we hand out moral credit.
