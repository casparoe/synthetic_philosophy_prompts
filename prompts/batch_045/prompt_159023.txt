I'm writing the final paper for a seminar on formal epistemology, and my topic is the problem of logical omniscience for the relational (possible-worlds) semantics for knowledge. The thesis I want to defend is deliberately narrow: the problem is not merely a special case of bounded rationality. It is easy to produce a failure of closure by making the agent stupid, careless, tired, forgetful, distracted, or computationally exhausted, and it is equally easy for a defender of the semantics to say that it is simply idealizing those limits away. I need a case where that reply is genuinely unavailable, so that the idealization reading has to do real work.

Would you construct one for me? The specifications:

1. One paragraph, realistic setting. A clinic, a lab, a courtroom, an audit, a codebase, a factory floor: ordinary human agents, nothing exotic, no ideal or infinite reasoners, no science fiction. I should be able to read it to a friend with no background in philosophy and have them follow it.

2. There must be a genuine deductive entailment from propositions the agent already believes to a proposition Q that, intuitively, the agent does not know. The inference must be short and transparent: one or two steps, the sort of thing nobody would call hard (modus ponens, universal instantiation, conjunction introduction). Please state the premise or premises, the entailment, and Q explicitly, so that I can check that the step really is deductive rather than merely very strong evidential support.

3. The case must not be one of the familiar anti-closure cases from mainstream epistemology: Dretske's zebra-and-disguised-mule case, Nozick's brain-in-a-vat, lottery or fake-barn style cases. Those trade on empirical or probabilistic consequence, or on causally remote alternatives, and they are answerable by relevant-alternatives and tracking theorists in ways that are beside my point. If your case would work equally well as a relevant-alternatives case, it is not doing the work I need.

4. The intuition that the agent does not know Q must not be purchasable by making the agent defective. The agent should be competent in the domain, careful, unhurried, with no memory failure, no distraction, no time pressure, and no computational strain. Go through these excuses one by one and say why each is unavailable in your case, since foreclosing the charge of finitude is the whole point. Tell me what is doing the work instead.

5. The failure must be one agent's. You can set the case in a workplace with several people in it, but the knowledge failure has to be attributable to a single agent, so that an appeal to distributed knowledge is not available as an answer.

6. It should not beg the question. I would like the case to be describable in the vocabulary of more than one diagnosis, so that I can use it as a shared example rather than as a case that assumes my conclusion.

After the scenario, please give me:

(a) The case run through three treatments, with what each says about whether the agent knows Q and by what mechanism: plain relational semantics of the familiar S5 sort; an impossible-worlds semantics; and an awareness-based or otherwise structured or syntactic account (Fagin–Halpern-style awareness, or a step or deduction-model approach, whichever you think is cleaner here).

(b) The single feature of the case that is doing the work, and what happens if I remove it: ideally, show me the minimally different version in which the agent plausibly does know Q.

(c) The strongest reply available to someone who holds that logical omniscience is a harmless idealization: presumably that what my case shows concerns only implicit or virtual knowledge, or that the intuition I am trading on is really about a normative notion such as being in a position to assert, or about assertion, blame, or negligence rather than about knowledge. Say how much of that reply you think succeeds.

(d) Briefly, and honestly: if the case survives (c), does it actually support the thesis that the problem of logical omniscience is not just bounded rationality? I would rather be told that it does not than be handed a case that flatters my conclusion.
