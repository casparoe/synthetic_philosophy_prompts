so our reading group has totally stalled on the same stretch of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations — the language-games material, the remarks about 'game', the bit about not thinking but looking — and I'm less interested in who's right about it than in what kind of stuck this is.

Roughly two camps. Camp A: he's making a substantive claim about how a lot of our concepts actually work, that they hang together by criss-crossing similarities rather than by necessary and sufficient conditions, that this is the sort of thing you could be wrong about, and that the psychology of categorization later vindicated him (prototype models, exemplar models, similarity-based categorization). Camp B: that reading just reinstates the confusion he's trying to quiet; he isn't advancing a thesis about concepts at all, he's dissolving a compulsion to demand definitions, and anything Camp A produces as evidence is just more material for therapy.

What makes it feel stuck rather than merely hard is that Camp B can absorb every possible finding ('that's the craving again'), and Camp A can treat that as refusing to say anything at all. We tried a thought experiment to break the tie: imagine a community whose word covering games has a crisp rule, applied without error, that agrees with us on every clear case and disagrees only on the wierd ones. Half of us said that would refute Camp A; the other half said it wouldn't touch it, because the claim was never about any actual community's usage. That second disagreement felt more diagnostic than anything about games themselves.

My hunch, which I dont fully trust: three questions are wearing one coat — whether the extension of a term like 'game' admits of any non-gerrymandered definition; how the concept is represented in a mind that uses it; and whether there's a fact of the matter about borderline cases that our practices answer to. And I suspect the first one is formally near-empty, since for any finite bunch of labelled cases you can write down some disjunction that picks out exactly those, so 'no necessary and sufficient conditions' has to be a claim about something like projectibility, or else about psychology, rather than about extensions as such. If that's right, Camp A's thesis quietly turns into an empirical claim and Camp B will say that's beside the point, and it's two questions under one name.

But I can't tell whether that's the real diagnosis or whether the real axis is a difference about what a philosophical result is even supposed to look like. So: is this verbal, substantive, two different questions, or ill-posed? Argue for one, engaging what each side actually commits itself to — I don't want a survey of the secondary literature or a reading list, I've got that. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here: a formal result, some finding about categorization, a new distinction, a change of question — or should we expect none, and why? Please answer in American English.
