I am revising a paper on the Chinese Room argument and would be grateful for detailed comments on the argumentative sketch below. I am fairly confident about the framing and much less confident about the central move, so I would especially like pressure on sections 2 and 4.

A note on scope before the sketch: I am not interested in whether the systems reply, the robot reply, or the brain-simulator reply individually succeeds. My question is what does the work in the argument once those replies have been set aside, and whether the thing that seems to do the work can survive a change in how the rulebook is produced.

1. Two readings of the conclusion. On the constitutive reading, the argument shows that no system could understand Chinese merely in virtue of manipulating symbols in accordance with rules: implementing a program is not the kind of property that could constitute understanding, and syntax cannot suffice for semantics. On the epistemic reading, the argument shows only that we are given no reason to attribute understanding to the system: the one part we can inspect from the inside reports no understanding, and the rest of the machinery is opaque to us. The constitutive reading is the one with consequences; the epistemic reading, taken alone, is close to a general scepticism about other minds and gives a computationalist nothing to answer, since she can simply say that our reasons for attributing understanding to a system are behavioural and structural rather than introspective. My suspicion is that nearly all of the intuitive force of the thought experiment comes from the epistemic reading, that the argument is historically read as delivering the constitutive reading, and that the standard dialectic does not bridge the two.

2. The Provenance Move. The only consideration I can find that supports the constitutive reading without appealing to the occupant's introspection is this: we know independently where the semantic credit for the Room's performance lies. It lies with the programmers, who understood Chinese and who arranged the rules so that the outputs would be ones a Chinese speaker would produce. The internal states of the system mean what they mean only for us, in something like the way the sentences of a book mean what they mean for its readers; the system itself has at best borrowed or as-if intentionality, parasitic on minds outside it. Call this the Provenance Move. I want to argue that it is doing more work in the received reading of the argument than is usually noticed, and that it is unavailable.

3. Provenance parity. Consider a child acquiring Chinese. Her competence is also the product of an environment she did not author; it is shaped entirely by the dispositions of speakers who already understand. If transmission of credit from prior speakers were disqualifying, it would disqualify the child, who is our paradigm of genuine understanding. So provenance does no independent work. It looks as though it does only because in the original scenario the transmission is mediated by something explicit and surveyable, a handwritten code we can inspect and see to be semantically inert, whereas in the child's case it is mediated by years of exposure. But that is a difference in the mechanism of transmission, and whether the mechanism matters is precisely what is in dispute. The obvious retort is that the child, unlike the Room, has the right causal powers and so converts borrowed content into intrinsic content. I concede the retort and claim it as a result: the retort abandons provenance and relocates the entire argument in a claim about causal powers, which the thought experiment asserts but does nothing to establish.

4. The unauthored rulebook. Now change the provenance of the rules. Suppose no one who constructed the system understood Chinese: the rulebook is the output of a search procedure that adjusts a very large parameter set so as to predict the next symbol in a corpus of Chinese text, selecting for performance on held-out data. Nothing in the construction chain requires Chinese competence, the resulting rulebook is not surveyable by anyone, and there is no sentence in it that anyone wrote. The remaining forms of the Provenance Move are: (a) the credit traces back to the authors of the corpus, which is parity again, since the child's credit also traces back to her speech community; and (b) the system is a mimic rather than a learner, which is a claim about what the system does and not about where its rules came from. My claim is that the Provenance Move is at its most persuasive when we can point to the place where the credit is stored, and that the unauthored variant removes the place to point at.

5. A test case I am unsure what to do with. Contrast a system trained only to predict continuations of Chinese text with one trained under corrective feedback from a Chinese-speaking interlocutor, who says in Chinese that a response is wrong or not what she meant, and whose corrections reshape the parameters. Defenders of the argument will presumably say the verdict is unchanged. That indifference is itself interesting: an argument insensitive to whether the system is causally coupled to a speaker in a corrective loop is either extremely strong or is not tracking the property that discussions of grounding treat as decisive. But the feedback case also revives the Provenance Move in a new guise, since one can say that the interlocutor's understanding is what the performance is borrowed from, exactly as a teacher's is what a pupil's performance is borrowed from. I do not know whether these two thoughts are in tension, and I would like help deciding.

6. Tentative conclusion. With the Provenance Move unavailable, the constitutive reading must rest on the claim that symbol manipulation as such lacks the causal powers required for understanding. That claim is either an unsupported empirical claim about what brains do, or a restatement of the conclusion. What survives is a demand rather than a result: we are owed a positive account of what would have to be true of a system's internal causal organization for it to understand, and the Room dramatizes the demand vividly without supplying any of the answer.

Please give me your comments on the following, leading with your overall verdict on the argument in your first sentence and then giving your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my questions:

Is the Provenance Move one that defenders of the argument actually rely on, and is my grouping of considerations under it fair? If I have misdescribed the dialectic, say so plainly.

Where exactly does the parity argument in section 3 fail, if it fails? Is there a principled distinction between learning from a speech community and learning from a corpus of that community's productions, or between deliberate authorship and incidental transmission, that a defender can use without begging the question?

Does the unauthored-rulebook variant in section 4 remove anything at all? I take this to be my weakest point, since Searle's reply to the systems reply, in which the occupant internalizes the entire system and still reports no understanding, seems to apply however the rules were produced. Say whether that reply settles the matter or whether it merely reimports the epistemic reading.

Is the paper worth writing as framed, or should it be recast? In particular, is the more interesting version of this material about borrowed intentionality and learning, or about what thought experiments of this shape can establish?

Finally, I would like you to be explicit about your confidence. Attach a short note stating how strongly you hold your verdict, which single consideration would most likely lead you to abandon or substantially revise it, and where you think my own reasoning is most likely to break.
