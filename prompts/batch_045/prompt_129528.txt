My roommate (sociology minor, extremely smug about it) and I got into it last night about whether generations are actually real things or whether they're basically a journalism invention that got out of hand, and I think I won the argument but I also have a nagging feeling I pulled a fast one somewhere, so I want to check it. Here's how I put it. First: the cutoff years are obviously made up. Pew says Millennials are 1981 to 1996, but my cousin was born eleven months after me, same town, same school, basically the same childhood — and allegedly she's Gen Z and I'm a Millennial. If sliding a line by a year reshuffles who's in which box, there's no fact of the matter about who belongs where, so these can't be real kinds, just conveniant buckets we throw over what is really a smooth continuum. Second, and I think this is the stronger version: I was reading about the age/period/cohort problem in demography, and the point seems to be that if you know how old someone is and what year it is, you already know when they were born — cohort is just period minus age, an identity, not a separate variable at all. So no model can ever isolate a genuinely 'generational' effect; whatever shows up as a cohort effect has to really be age and period effects in disguise. Millennial traits are just what being 25 in 2008 looked like. Doesn't that more or less settle it — generations are epiphenomena riding on top of age and history?

Assuming that's right, my actual question is about cleanup. If these labels are purely conventional, then what's the most principled way to choose the cutoffs when you do need them in a paper? Is there a defensible procedure — clustering people on attitude or life-outcome data and putting the boundaries wherever they minimize the variation inside each group, or something like that — or is it just a matter of taste? Relatedly, I've been toying with a replacement scheme: instead of birth years, grade people by how old they were at some fixed historical event (say, 12 when smartphones became normal, or 20 in the first recession you lived through) and call those 'shadow cohorts'. That feels like it captures the intuitions people actually have about generations without the fake precision of a birth-year range. Would grading by exposure instead of birth date fix the problem I described above, or does it just reintroduce it somewhere else?
