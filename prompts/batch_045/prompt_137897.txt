I teach a final-year seminar on social ontology, and I am assembling the last section of a handout on whether generational cohorts (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z) are genuine kinds or merely convenient bins that demographers and journalists have grown fond of. The handout already has the standard illustrations and the usual complaints about arbitrary decade boundaries, so what I want at the end of it is a case built to order: a thought experiment engineered so that two accounts of what a generation is deliver opposite verdicts about a single cohort. I am presenting the two accounts roughly as follows. The historical-cause account holds that a birth cohort is a genuine generation only if its members' shared outlook was formed by their undergoing the same formative events together — the same war, the same crash, the same curriculum reform or technology arriving at the same point in the life course — so that a generation's boundaries ought to track real discontinuities in causal exposure rather than round numbers on a calendar. The prediction account holds that a generational kind earns its place purely by being a partition of birth years that lets a researcher predict attitudes, behaviour and life chances better than the alternatives actually available to her (age, survey year, social class, education), with boundaries drawn wherever predictive power is maximised and redrawn whenever something better comes along; nothing about shared experience is required.

The direction I need is the one I keep failing to build: the prediction account says yes, this is a generation, and the historical-cause account says no, this is not one. My own attempts collapse in one of two ways — either I smuggle in a shared event after all, in which case both accounts say yes, or I produce something so gerrymandered that both say no. Constraints on the case: it should be realistic and self-contained, describable in a single paragraph, set in an institutional or demographic setting a sociologist could actually go and study (a school-entry cutoff, a pension reform, a hiring practice, a longitudinal survey, something of that kind), with no science fiction, no thought experiments about words, and no dependence on contested empirical facts about real Boomers or real Millennials that a reader would have to look up. The clustering you construct should be robust and genuinely predictive, and the causal story behind it should be perfectly ordinary — the trick has to be that the causes, however real and however many, are not causes that the cohort members underwent together as a shared history. Finally, the case must not stipulate its own verdicts: no saying "and the causes here are unrelated" or "and no one would call this a generation"; the mechanism has to be visible in the description, and both accounts must still give the intuitive answers about the familiar cohorts and about obviously bogus ones such as people born on a Tuesday. After the scenario, walk me through what each account says about it and why, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, tell me what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed or inverted, and give the strongest objection a defender of the historical-cause account could raise — the move she would make to claim the case as her own or to disqualify it as irrelevant.

Please lead with the scenario itself: one paragraph, no preamble and no restating my request. Begin the analysis that follows with a single sentence giving your bottom line, then give the reasoning. Write in British English.
