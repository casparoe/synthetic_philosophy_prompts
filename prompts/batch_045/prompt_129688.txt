For the past two years I have been part of a small interdisciplinary group working on the question of whose values an AI system trained on data drawn from many people should be said to serve, and the discussion has stopped moving in a way I find diagnostic rather than merely difficult. One camp, the aggregators, holds that the system's objective should be some function of the values of the affected population: an average or weighted sum of utility functions in something like Harsanyi's vein, a maximin or leximin rule, a Nash-bargaining-style solution, or a vote over candidate value functions, with the choice among these settled by axiomatic desiderata. The other camp, the neutralists, holds that the system should not rank conceptions of the good against one another at all, and that what has to be justified to each person is the procedure generating its behaviour, in terms that person could reasonably accept, rather than the content of whatever ranking the procedure happens to produce. Everyone now concedes the same three things: that no tractable elicitation recovers anything close to a complete value function for a single person, let alone for a population; that Arrow-style and Sen-style impossibility results block any aggregation rule from satisfying the full list of conditions one would want; and that the rule chosen will do nearly all its work in regions of the decision space that nobody has an actual preference about. Nobody has changed their mind in a year.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I have a hypothesis I would like tested rather than indulged. My hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one label: aggregators are asking which decision rule is best by substantive moral standards, while neutralists are asking who may authoritatively settle that question, and the two coincide only under a background assumption of substantial convergence of values under reflection, which everyone in the group also explicitly denies. If that is right, the live question is what happens when the convergence assumption fails, and I do not think either camp has an answer to that.

The case I keep returning to, and which I have started calling the coverage problem, is this. Human feedback data, and even idealized extrapolation from it, constrains the system's behaviour only in the neighbourhood of situations people have actually cared about. For the overwhelming majority of the tradeoffs such a system will face there is no actual preference, no actual endorsement, and no determinate hypothetical endorsement to appeal to: there are only more or less conservative completions of an incomplete value function. So the real author of the system's behaviour is the completion rule, not the aggregated values. The aggregators respond by asking which completion rule is most defensible from the standpoint of the population taken as a whole, for instance by minimizing worst-case regret across a range of candidate completions. The neutralists respond that the completion rule itself must be justifiable to each person separately, and that appealing to the population as a whole at exactly this point is the aggregator's own conception of the good entering by the back door. Neither side finds the other's move responsive, and I cannot tell whether that is a failure of imagination or a sign that the dispute is degenerate.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the words value or neutral, or two questions under a single heading? Is my two-questions hypothesis right, or is there one question here that I am misdescribing? Is some hidden empirical or formal issue doing the work, such as how much convergence under reflection there would in fact be, or whether a completion rule is identifiable from feedback data at all? If the disagreement bottoms out in starting points that neither side can argue for, such as whether legitimacy flows from the quality of outcomes or from the acceptability of procedures, say so and say why nothing from within the dispute could settle it. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about extrapolation, an empirical finding about convergence, a change of question, or a prediction that no progress should be expected?
