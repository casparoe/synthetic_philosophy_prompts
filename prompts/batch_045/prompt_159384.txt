I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar on persistence and I am writing the final examination. The students can already recite the dialectic, and I have no interest in rewarding that: they know the problem of temporary intrinsics, the reply that temporary properties are relations to times, the worm-theoretic and stage-theoretic versions of the temporal-parts view, and the arguments from fission and from the vagueness of composition over time. I want a single essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, closed book and offline, that separates students who can work with this material from students who have only memorised its labels.

The crux I want the question to force is whether the two views are, in the end, saying anything different. Three pressure points seem to me to matter, and I want the question organised around them. First: the time-indexing reply to the problem of temporary intrinsics looks available to both sides, and if it is, the argument from temporary intrinsics appears to lose its force as a reason to prefer temporal parts. Second: the worm itself seems to have temporary properties, being one shape over one interval and another over another, and the temporal-parts theorist must either reapply her own treatment one level up, with what looks like the beginning of a regress, or explain why the sequence stops where it does. Third: the whole apparatus seems to presuppose an ordering of the parts, and it is worth asking what supplies that ordering and whether it is doing work that the mereology alone cannot do.

To make the third point concrete rather than rhetorical, build the question around the following case, which I have invented for the purpose and which you should state precisely, sharpening it if you can: Ada runs a marathon, and it is metaphysically possible that the order in which the instantaneous stages of her run are located in time diverges from the order those stages bear to one another along every other dimension that seems relevant, including fatigue, memory, causal influence and similarity of intrinsic character. Stipulate that the stage at which she crosses the finish line occupies the earliest time, that the stage at which she leaves the start line occupies the latest, and that the intermediate stages are scattered in between with no pattern, while remaining pairwise causally linked and internally exactly as they would be in an ordinary run. Call the fatigue-and-memory ordering the gradient ordering. Ask what each theory says about whether Ada changes and in which direction, whether the temporal-parts theorist can identify change with variation across temporal parts without adding temporal order as a separate ingredient, and whether the case gives the endurantist anything she did not already have.

Please give me, in this order:

1. The question exactly as it should appear on the paper, including the case, with any sub-parts and a stated total of 30 marks.
2. A model answer of 800 to 1000 words that takes a definite position on the collapse question rather than surveying options, and that explicitly flags the one assumption in its own argument which it takes to be most contestable.
3. A marking scheme: the must-hit moves with marks attached summing to 30, named in terms of the reasoning each requires rather than the conclusion reached; a note on what distinguishes an A answer from a B and a B from a C; and, for each move, one line on how candidates who miss it typically go wrong. Include at least one mark reserved for resisting a tempting but bad move, such as dismissing the case as impossible, or treating the greater number of entities posited, or sheer intuitive plausibility, as decisive.

Two constraints. No citations, page references or reading lists anywhere: the paper must be answerable from the dialectic alone, offline, and so must the model answer. Refer to positions by description rather than by author, so that a student who has not absorbed who said what is not penalised, and do not let the question turn on any contested claim in physics.
