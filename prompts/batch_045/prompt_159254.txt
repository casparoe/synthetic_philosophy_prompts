For several years I have been writing a novel about a district nurse in a Derbyshire village in 1911, and the research has left me holding a set of moral verdicts I cannot get into any order. A friend who reads moral philosophy tells me the territory is called care ethics, but I would rather have the shape of my own intuitions straight before I go reading, so let me just report them. First: a stranger drowning in a shallow pond must obviously be pulled out, even at the cost of a ruined coat — yet when I consider sending the equivalent sum to a charity that will save a child's life instead of buying my daughter the bicycle she has asked me for all year, I think it is perfectly fine to buy the bicycle, and I don't think I am kidding myself. Second: my narrator, who spends an extra ten minutes with a patient she happens to warm to and ten fewer minutes spread thinly across four others, seems to me to be doing something good rather than something unfair — the warmth is not the sort of thing that can be divided evenly — although I start to balk the moment the favouritism begins to affect the clinical attention itself. Third: the same nurse leaving her own children with a neighbour three evenings a week to sit with dying patients I do not admire; something has gone wrong there, even though each patient's need is plainly greater than the children's. Fourth, and this is the pair I keep returning to: a daughter who hires an excellent, genuinely affectionate paid companion to visit her mother twice a week and who visits herself only at Christmas has, I feel, not done what she owes; and yet I also want to say that the companion's care — contractual, paid, ending at six o'clock — is care in exactly the same sense, not a lesser thing because money changes hands. Those two sit badly together, and I cannot see which of them I should abandon. Fifth: a woman caring for a mother with advanced dementia who no longer recognises her and sometimes strikes her seems to me to owe that care undiminished, indeed more insistently than she owes anything to a stranger; but a woman visiting the mother who abused her in childhood, purely out of guilt, I resist describing as caring at all, though I would not tell her to stop. Sixth: Mary, the real woman in the parish records my novel is built around, dead since 1913 — I feel I owe her a fidelity to the shape of her life, and it feels like an obligation of care rather than a rule about not falsifying one's sources; yet I feel almost nothing for the children I could save with a modest donation, and I am not convinced that is a defect in me.

The only thing I can extract from all this is something like the following: an obligation of care arises wherever I have taken another's vulnerability into my own attention, and its strength is set by how much of that person's life I actually carry in mind — call it narrative proximity — rather than by blood, contract, or even by whether the person is alive to receive anything. I would like that principle tested against the cases above and repaired where it fails, and I would like it stated precisely enough that I can use it, with a note of which case motivates each clause. Then I want to know what it says about the cases I have not settled: a living neighbour I have quietly built a minor character out of and never spoken to; a reader who writes to say the book got her through a year of illness; my ageing dog, for whom I would pay a great deal; a village or a parish or a language rather than a person; and whether the account collapses into mere favouritism toward whoever happens to be vivid to me. Please also say plainly which of my verdicts no single principle can accommodate, what it would cost me to give each of them up, and whether the Mary case means care is not really about the cared-for at all.
