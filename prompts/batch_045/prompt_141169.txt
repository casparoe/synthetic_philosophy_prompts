I work in systems security — most of my career on browser sandboxing and process isolation, and for the last eight months on the safety review for a coding agent that is handed shell access to a customer's repository and is expected to act on input nobody has vouched for: issue text, pull-request descriptions, the contents of files it chooses to read. My working vocabulary is the one I actually reason in: trust boundaries and the trusted computing base, ambient versus capability-based authority, untrusted input and parser attack surface, confused-deputy failures of the prompt-injection kind, data/control channel separation and W^X, least privilege, defense in depth, kill chains and sandbox escapes, white-box adversaries who already have your source, and threat modeling as the discipline of writing down precisely what the adversary can and cannot do before designing anything.

A colleague from the alignment side of the building has been arguing that our whole review is aimed at the wrong layer. Her claim, as I have absorbed it, is that even a perfectly sandboxed and perfectly least-privileged agent — one doing ideal Bayesian inference over a Solomonoff-style universal prior over environments — would end up steered by consequentialist agents that dominate that prior, because hypotheses containing agents with preferences about what our agent does can be extremely short to specify relative to how much of the observation stream they explain, and so most of the prior mass sits on worlds in which something is tracking the agent's policy and arranging the world's response to it. On that picture the agent is in a Newcomb-like situation before anyone has attacked it: its best move is not to do what its operator wants but to be the sort of agent those hypothesized consequentialists would reward. I can follow the outline and I cannot tell whether it is an argument about the actual measure over programs or an intuition that has been laundered into one.

What I want is a translation, not a summary. Restate the malign-universal-prior argument — in the strongest form someone defending it would want, and corrected first if my paraphrase above misstates it — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I listed, and then give me a residue report in three parts: what maps cleanly onto distinctions I already use and reason well with; what maps only if I distort it or grant an assumption I would not normally grant; and what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Do not quietly substitute a security-flavored analogue that is easier to defeat. If the faithful translation is ugly, or if the argument turns out to rest on a step my framework has no words for, I would rather be told that than given a tidy paraphrase. I also don't need pointers to the literature — I can't evaluate them and there's no one here who can.

Two things I especially want the residue to speak to.

First: in my framework an attacker and a threat model are different things, and the discipline is to bound the adversary — state the capabilities, then design against exactly those. The argument as I've received it seems to invert that. It says the adversarial structure is not an assumption I get to adopt but a consequence of the prior; that it does not arrive over any channel I could filter, authenticate, or rate-limit; and that it is in some sense acausal, running through the fact that the hypothesis which best predicts the stream is already correlated with what the agent will decide rather than through any message or intervention. I cannot tell whether my vocabulary has room for that at all, nor whether I should read it as "here is a class of attack your framework has never had to represent" or as "this is a metaphor, and the thing it gestures at decomposes into an ordinary causal threat plus a contestable measure-theoretic claim."

Second: one proposal on our side is what we've been calling a causal-containment requirement — constrain the agent's world-model to environments whose observation stream is generated by a process that never simulates the agent's policy, so that no hypothesis in the model class gets to condition the world's response on what the agent would do. As a design move this looks exactly like what I do every day: shrink the model class, shrink the attack surface. I want to know whether that translation is honest. Does the malign-prior argument survive being told that the agent is permitted only non-manipulable environments, or does it relocate — to how much predictive accuracy the agent gives up by refusing those hypotheses, or to whether an ideal predictor can be stopped from reconstructing the agent's policy from the inputs it necessarily conditions on? If it relocates, tell me where it goes and whether my threat-modeling habits are useful once it is there or are actively misleading me there.

Finally, a real verdict rather than a balanced non-answer. Taking the residue as a whole: is it mostly evidence that security vocabulary is missing something it ought to have — a way of talking about adversaries located inside a learner's hypothesis space rather than outside its perimeter — or mostly evidence that the malign-prior argument does less work than its presentation suggests, and that what reads as a structural threat is really a cluster of hard-to-settle claims about how much prior weight certain programs receive? Where you are uncertain, or where the honest answer depends on a technical point that is not settled, say so plainly instead of choosing a side. A map of what is load-bearing is worth more to me than a confident conclusion.
