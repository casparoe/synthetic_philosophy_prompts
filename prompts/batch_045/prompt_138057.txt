hey — I need to turn a half-baked idea into a two-page proposal for an internal seed grant. 9 months, one part-time RA, no teaching buyout, so please keep the scope honest. The topic is the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique and whether there's anything left of it.

My half-formed idea: the debate keeps stalling because everyone treats it as an empirical dispute about particular traits — is this thing an adaptation or is it a byproduct? — when what's actually load-bearing is a question about defaults. Which hypothesis is the null here, and who has to argue for their answer? And I think both sides can keep claiming the same cases because the question is secretly time-indexed: a feature can be a genuine byproduct at one point in its history and a genuine adaptation later, after it's been recruited for something and then refined by selection. The reptilian jaw bone that ends up as a mammalian middle-ear ossicle is the kind of case I mean — its origin story and its current-function story point in different directions, and each is true at its own time slice. Nobody in the argument seems to track which slice they're talking about, so a lot of the shouting is people answering different questions and calling each other wrong. I've been calling this "origin/maintenance slippage" and I want to build the project on it.

- explain why the standard framings leave it open — by which I mean the framing where adaptationism is a global thesis about how much of the phenotype selection actually explains, and the framing where it's just a heuristic for churning out hypotheses, plus whatever the usual move is for dissolving the whole thing into a debate about the word "function"
- the approach, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives. I really do not want to write another survey of case studies, and I don't want more armchair analysis of word meanings; say honestly why whatever you pick is better than those two
- what we'd hand a working biologist at the end. I'm imagining something like a principled way of setting the burden of proof locally — per trait, or per sub-system, or per kind of explanation — rather than settling it globally once and for all, but if that turns out to be a bad idea the proposal should be able to say so
