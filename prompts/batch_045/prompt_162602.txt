hi — I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology (bayesian-ish, mostly conditionals) and I've somehow become the person who keeps the climate-risk register at a small adaptation consultancy. Almost nothing that reaches me is a plain datum. It's a conditional sentence from an engineer or a modeller: if the pumping station loses mains power you'll see water in the substation within four hours; if winter rainfall is above the 90th percentile there's a real chance the embankment overtops; if the levee is breached anywhere upstream teh gauge at Marsh Road will read over three metres. I recieve thirty or forty of these a quarter, I have to turn each into revised probabilities in a spreadsheet, those numbers feed a ranking that decides which defences get funded next year, and my director — who is lovely but thinks a prior is a kind of prayer — signs off on the whole thing before it goes to the client.

The Judy Benjamin setup is the one I keep hitting, except in my job it's dressed up as engineering: the consequent is often about what *we* will later observe or be told, not about the world, and half the time the message only exists because some monitoring system with a fixed alert policy decided to send it. I need a procedure, not a tour of the options.

So: give me a decision procedure I can run in fifteen minutes per message, in a spreadsheet, without asking my colleagues to understand any formula. Numbered steps, in the order I should ask the questions, and for each one say what counts as a sufficient answer and what I do when the answer is "the expert didn't say". It has to do at least these things: sort the sentence into (a) a claim about the world, (b) a claim about what we will observe or be told, and (c) a directive that tells us how to *treat* something — and tell me what to do with (c), because our ops manual is basically a list of those. Where the conditional is the Judy Benjamin shape, say explicitly why naively taking it as a proposition and conditioning on it goes wrong, and what move to make instead given I only have a partition and no similarity metric. Handle hedged consequents where nobody will give me a number. Handle the alert-policy cases, where the fact that no message came is itself supposed to be evidence. Mark which steps stop with "log it and don't update" and which ones hand the decision to me or my director because it's a modelling choice rather than a fact.

Then run it on three: the Marsh Road gauge one; the hedged rainfall one with no number attached; and a case where I think the honest answer will annoy me — an alert-policy message, or one of the directives from the manual. I want to see the procedure refuse something I currently use.

Keep the whole reply under 900 words: the steps as a numbered list of eight or fewer, one small table mapping message-type to which update move applies versus when to refuse, the three worked cases in prose, then a short paragraph on what the procedure can't do. No citations, no literature review, no "van Fraassen says" — I've read all that, I need something my director can sign. Do flag which steps are actually forced by the theory and which are just sensible office conventions, because I'll have to defend the first kind to the client's technical reviewer and I can quietly adjust the second.
