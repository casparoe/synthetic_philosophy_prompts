I am in my final year at a large public high school, and I run our school's Philosophy Club. It meets on Thursdays for forty-five minutes in a library seminar room with a projector, a whiteboard, and whatever I carry in with me. Fourteen members, mostly underclassmen, and roughly three kinds of person: a few who do competitive programming and will want a decisive test for everything, a few from the creative writing and debate crowds who are comfortable with ambiguity but allergic to anything that sounds like a lab result, and a silent middle who will say nothing unless I ask them something specific. Our faculty advisor is a biology teacher who is kind but who thinks perception is a solved problem and that illusions are eyesight trivia.

Next week I want to run a session on the question of when, in time, a conscious experience happens, using the colour phi phenomenon as the way in: a dot of one colour appears at one place, then a dot of a second colour at another place, and viewers report a single dot that moves between the two and changes colour partway along the way — even though the second dot is what carries the second colour, and so the change seems to be experienced before the information needed for it has arrived. I want to get to the two competing stories about this, the one on which the experience really occurs as reported and is then quietly rewritten in memory afterwards, and the one on which nothing of the sort happens at the reported moment and the subject's account is composed later than it seems to be, so that the report is not a slightly corrupted transcript of an earlier event at all. I have seen these called the Orwellian and Stalinesque options.

What I need from you is not an explanation of this material for me, but a plan for how I should explain it to them, and I have some constraints that should shape the plan rather than decorate it.

First, I do not want the session to land as a debunking. The club has a standing rule against conclusions of the form you do not really see what you think you see, or consciousness is an illusion, and last term I lost two members to a session that ended that way. Whatever the arc is, it has to arrive somewhere that leaves first-person reports with some authority intact, or at least leaves it an open question, and I need to know which move in the sequence prevents the slide, not just a warning to be careful.

Second, I have built a small web animation of the effect with a slider for the gap between the two frames, and I have been planning a version where a loud tone plays shortly after the second frame, and where members mark on paper the instant at which the colour seemed to change. I would like you to tell me whether this should be the example that carries the whole session, what it would have to have and lack in order to carry it, and specifically whether the tone version smuggles in a mistake — my worry is that timing a button press or a pencil mark measures something other than the thing under discussion, and if so I would rather hear now than discover it in front of the room.

Third, I expect a predictable sequence of pushback, and I want the plan to absorb it in the order it will actually arrive: the biology teacher's claim that this is an optics curiosity rather than a question about experience; the programmers' demand to know what experiment, or what scanner, would settle which story is true, which I cannot answer and do not want to bluff my way through; and then someone concluding that if the two stories cannot be told apart from the inside, then there is no fact of the matter about when anything is experienced. Tell me at which point in the sequence each of these should be met, what has to already be on the board for the answer to stick, and what I should openly decline to settle in a forty-five minute session with people this age.

Finally, I have my own way of framing the upshot, which I have been calling the ledger rule: an experience belongs at the time at which the person can be held to it in the retelling, so that the interesting question is not which story is true but which difference between them would ever show up in anything anyone later does or says. I would be grateful if you told me whether this is a genuine third option or a confusion, whether it belongs in the session at all, and if it does, whether it should open or close.

Please also give me the order in which the components have to be introduced and what depends on what — in particular whether the demo must come before any vocabulary, and whether the two stories should be named before or after someone in the room has produced the puzzle in their own words — and one question I can put to the silent middle at the end that would let me tell whether they have understood the puzzle rather than only learned that perception is unreliable.
