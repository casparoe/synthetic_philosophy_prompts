Me and my friend Jonas spent about three hours last night arguing about German versus American folk morality and I still havent shaken it off, so I want to turn it into something.

His version: German rule-following is fear of the neighbours disapproval in a Kantian costume, and the American move of saying "I have to follow my conscience" is egotism wearing a halo. I pushed back on both halves — rules aren't mainly about obedience, they're a coordination technology that lets strangers trust each other without auditing each other's souls, and conscience isn't just a fancy word for preference, because a conscience that never once says no to a validly enacted rule isn't doing any work. He said fine, but who audits the auditors. We got nowhere, obviously.

What I want from you: the minutes of an extraordinary general meeting of a binational housing co-operative, German and American tenants roughly half and half, that has to agree on a single house rule — the Sunday quiet hours are the obvious candidate. Written in the deadpan voice of the minute-taker: agenda items, motions, amendments, vote counts, abstentions, and at the end an appendix giving the rule that was actually adopted. About 600 to 700 words.

The meeting should be argued by people who are each a bit right. Some things I want to be in the room:

- one tenant who keeps insisting that what is really being decided is who bears the cost of uncertainty — a rule lets you act without knowing what the other residents actually think, whereas a conscience-exemption is a way of correcting for the fallibility of whoever wrote the rule;
- the observation that every exception the house grants gets logged, numbered and cited as precedent the next time somebody asks, so that conscience-exceptions accrete into an unwritten common law that ends up fussier than the rule people were being exempted from;
- and a final adopted rule that is a straight-faced reductio of both cultures at once — something like a conscience-notice form that has to be filed 48 hours in advance, posted in the stairwell and indexed annually, with the index deemed to form part of the house rules.

Constraints: nobody in the piece is a caricature, and the German characters are not humourless robots any more than the American ones are narcissists. No laughing at long German nouns, and the Nazi card is off limits as a debating move — if the darkness of pure obedience shows up it has to show up in the mundane procedures of a stairwell, not in a history lecture. Dont let anyone announce the moral or sum the argument up; the reader should work out what is being ironised. By the end a reader who arrived on either side should feel the other side's case, and also feel that neither story is as clean as it sounds.
