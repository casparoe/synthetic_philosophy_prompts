I want a case analysis, not a tutorial on the theory and not a news recap. Take the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 and read it through the lens of informational cascades and rational herding; then turn it around and tell me what the episode does to the theory.

The facts I want you to work from, so that you don't have to reconstruct them: SVB was a Santa Clara–based lender whose depositor base was unusually concentrated in venture-backed technology and life-science companies and in the venture firms that funded them. Its deposits had roughly tripled during the 2020–21 boom, and it had parked much of them in long-dated Treasuries and agency mortgage-backed securities, about $91 billion of which were classified as held-to-maturity and therefore not marked to market; by the end of 2022 the unrealized losses on that portfolio exceeded $15 billion. Roughly 86–89 percent of its roughly $175 billion in deposits were above the FDIC insurance limit and so uninsured. On Wednesday, March 8, the bank announced that it had sold more than $21 billion of securities at a loss, had borrowed $15 billion, and would conduct an emergency offering of stock to raise about $2.25 billion. Prominent venture investors publicly advised portfolio companies to move their money. On Thursday, March 9, depositors attempted to withdraw roughly $42 billion, and another $100 billion in outflows was reportedly expected on Friday. The California regulator seized the bank on the morning of Friday, March 10; two days later the Treasury, the Federal Reserve, and the FDIC announced jointly that all depositors, including uninsured ones, would have full access to their funds, and the bank reopened on Monday as a bridge bank. Throughout, the coordination happened in group chats, on social media, and by wire, within a window of hours, and not in queues outside branch doors.

What I want from you:

First, a disciplined mapping. Who were the agents — founders and startup CFOs, venture firms advising portfolios, boards, the bank itself, the regulators — what exactly was each one's action set, what private signal did each plausibly hold (about the securities portfolio, about their own liquidity needs, about what other depositors were doing), and what were the payoffs, including the near-zero transaction cost of wiring a large balance elsewhere? Say plainly what the canonical sequential-choice models (Banerjee; Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch), Diamond–Dybvig-style run models, and reputational or "sharing-the-blame" herding would each call rational for those agents, and where they would disagree about what was rational.

Second, I want you to take up a distinction I have been toying with and either sharpen it or scrap it: what I'll call a belief cascade, in which agents withdraw because earlier withdrawals have swamped their private signal about the bank's condition, versus a positional cascade, in which what agents are herding on is not the state of the bank at all but their place in a withdrawal queue whose liquid assets are being drained in real time — so that withdrawal is preemptive self-defense against other withdrawals rather than a judgment about solvency. Which of these, if either, describes March 9, and does the distinction collapse into payoff complementarity on inspection?

Third, the places where the mapping fails, which I care about most. The standard models have agents observe an ordered history of predecessors' actions; SVB participants observed a noisy broadcast — the volume and urgency of chatter — plus privileged information flowing from investors with board seats. Does the cascade result survive that change in observation structure? More seriously: the state the cascade is supposedly about was not well defined independently of the run, since held-to-maturity losses crystallize only if the bank is forced to sell, and a run is precisely what forces the sale. If the underlying value is partly produced by the cascade, what becomes of the usual story that cascades can be wrong? And on the other end: did the March 12 announcement stop the run by changing beliefs or by changing payoffs, and which of those does the theory say a public signal is supposed to do?

Finally, the case's verdict on the theory. Is SVB a confirmation — a fast, networked, self-confirming panic that no private information could arrest — or an anomaly, a cascade that was arguably right about a genuinely impaired bank and early rather than mistaken? Or is it a case the framework never anticipated: a herding game with a strategic authority that decides, after the fact, whether to send the signal that breaks the herd? If agents rationally expect that signal to come, does the framework become degenerate, and does that tell us anything about whether cascades in modern banking are equilibria of the model or artifacts of a missing player? Where the theory is genuinely silent, say so rather than making it speak; I would rather have a marked silence than a forced application.

Please write about 1,200 to 1,500 words, organized under short headings, and argue in your own terms rather than pointing me to a literature — I have no way to look up references, so anything you cite is dead weight.
