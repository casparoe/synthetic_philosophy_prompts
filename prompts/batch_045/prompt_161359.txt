I work in health services research, mostly on screening instruments and quality measurement, and over the last few years I have kept noticing the same configuration in my own data and in other people's. I don't have a word for it, and I'd like to know whether one exists. Two cases, both somewhat anonymised, which I take to be instances of one thing.

The first: a brief questionnaire intended to detect eating-disorder risk in adolescent girls, administered in schools as a way of finding cases that would otherwise go unnoticed. Within a few years of its introduction, the way girls in those schools describe their own distress to counsellors has begun to track the item wording almost line for line — the vocabulary of the instrument has become the vocabulary of the complaint, and the shape of the experiences reported has narrowed toward the shape the questionnaire asks about. The research group that built the instrument treats the rising count as evidence of improved detection of a condition that was always there. A few clinicians in the schools think the opposite. What none of the participants report is being pushed into anything: the girls, when interviewed, describe the questionnaire as the first thing that had ever described them correctly, and say that being asked those questions was what finally let them recognise what had been wrong with them for years.

The second: a frailty score computed automatically from routine vitals and nursing notes and displayed on a ward board. After it is introduced, teams start describing certain patients as frail in a way they did not before, patients themselves start saying things like "I'm a frail old person now" and declining physiotherapy, and the behaviours in the notes that feed the score — short walks, unfinished meals — become more frequent. So the score's predictive accuracy for falls genuinely improves, year on year, and that improvement is then cited in the validation papers as evidence that the score measures something real. My own guess is that at least part of the improvement is the score's own doing, including through the route where the patients come to think of themselves as the kind of person the score says they are.

What I think is common to the two, stated as carefully as I can: a measure is introduced as a passive readout of something antecedent; the people measured change, both in what they do and in how they understand themselves, in the direction of the measure's categories; the change is then taken as confirmation that the measure was right all along, so that the more the measure is applied the more evidence there appears to be for it; and — this is the part I care about — the change is not experienced by anyone as compliance or gaming but as recognition, as being accurately described for the first time. It even seems to me that the configuration depends on that last feature rather than merely happening to have it. Where the pressure is visible, people resist and the pattern degrades; here it is stable precisely because nobody involved believes they are producing anything, only discovering it, and the discovery story is what keeps the instrument in place.

I have been pointed at a few candidate names and none of them quite fit, but I'd rather have someone who knows the literature check my reasoning than rely on it. A colleague mentioned something called a "looping effect" in the philosophy of classification; as I understand it that covers the changing of the classified and the revision of the classification, but says nothing about the change being taken up as self-knowledge, or about the invisibility of the loop being part of what makes the loop work. There is the economists' line about a measure ceasing to be a good measure once it becomes a target, which is close on the surface and wrong underneath: in my cases the measure gets better at its job, not worse. Self-fulfilling prophecy doesn't fit either, since there is no belief that starts out false and becomes true — there was no prior fact of the matter to be wrong about. Someone else suggested "interpellation," which as far as I can tell is about being addressed by an ideology and is too flat to carry the epistemic element, the part where the instrument's own outputs are what count as its credentials. I've also started reading some Foucault on the advice of a friend, on the grounds that "power/knowledge" is supposed to be about exactly this; but as I've encountered it the phrase seems to be doing something much more general, so that almost any institution would count, and I can't tell from it which cases are mine and which aren't.

So my question is whether there is an established concept — in the philosophy or social studies of classification and quantification, or in the Foucault-adjacent social theory where I have been told to look — that picks out this narrower thing: a classification that produces the property it claims only to detect, produces it specifically in the form of the classified persons' own account of who they are, and secures its credibility from that very production while disavowing it. If there is one, I want its name, the literature that uses it, and above all its boundary conditions: which cases fall under it and which merely resemble it. If there isn't one, I would like a term and a definition I could actually use in a paper, together with the near-miss cases it has to be distinguished from. A name to drop is not what I'm after; I need to be able to say what this is not, and in particular how it differs from the looping effect, from ordinary measurement reactivity, from target-degradation, and from self-fulfilling prophecy. Please reply in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write an email to a colleague.
