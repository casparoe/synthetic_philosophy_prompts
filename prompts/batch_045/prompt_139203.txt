hey, need a hand with the take-home for my advanced phil sci seminar (approx. 15 students, they've done Popper, Kuhn, Laudan, some realism/antirealism, and a bit of formal epistemology but no one's a logician). Topic of the last unit is truthlikeness, and I want one final question that can't be answered by reciting Popper's truth-content-minus-falsity-content definition, the Miller/Tichý result that sank it (no false theory can be closer to the truth than another, on that definition), and then hand-waving at the "similarity approach" and possible worlds. They do that in their sleep. So I've been building a toy example and I want you to turn it into a proper exam item.

The toy: a system with three binary features, so eight possible states, actual state = 111, and distance between states = number of features they differ on. Four candidate theories, each a set of states:

T1 = {111, 110}  — two of three features pinned down correctly, silent on the third
T2 = {110} — fully specific, wrong about one feature, excludes the truth
T3 = {111, 000} — a disjunction: either exactly right, or wrong about everything
T4 = {111, 110, 101, 100} — pins down one feature correctly, says nothing about the other two

Two competing measures, defined in the question itself so nobody has to recall the literature. Call the first one N: score a theory by how near its *nearest* state is to 111, and use informativeness (fewer states = better) only as a tie-break, so N leaves T1 and T3 tied at the top, then T4, then T2. Call the second one A: score by the *average* distance of the theory's states from 111, normalised, which gives T1 ≈ .83, T2 ≈ .67, T4 ≈ .67, T3 = .50. So they disagree: T3 is best-tied on N and worst on A, and N splits T2 and T4 while A ties them.

What I want from you:

1. The question as it would appear on the handout — self-contained (state the toy, define N and A, give the numbers so nobody burns twenty minutes on arithmetic), with a single clear thing to answer. 1,500 word limit, 48 hours, no notes, no library, so nothing that depends on looking anything up.

2. A model answer of about 1,000–1,300 words.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves, marks for each, and a one-line note on how people usually screw each one up.

The crux I'm after, in case it helps: the N/A disagreement isn't a technical glitch to be fixed by picking a better formula, it's a choice about what "closeness to the truth" is supposed to be (accuracy-first-then-informativeness vs. expected/risk-averse accuracy). And then the thing I really want them to confront: re-coordinatise the eight states by three different yes/no questions and T1's pair of states can be made to look exactly like T3's pair, and vice versa — since any old Boolean combination of the features counts as a yes/no question, unrestricted invariance under redescription collapses the likeness approach back into something purely content-based, which is exactly the dead end Miller and Tichý mapped out. So they have to either produce a principled, non-question-begging restriction on which redescriptions are admissible (with the obvious analogy: linear rescalings like Celsius to Fahrenheit preserve which of two values is nearest the truth, but a monotone non-linear regraduation can flip it, and it's not obvious why the non-linear one is illegitimate), or argue no such restriction is available and say what that costs.

Two more things the question should force. First: don't let them buy "the distance metric is just fixed by the natural/causal structure of the world" on the cheap — an A answer has to say what that commitment costs (what it commits you to about objective resemblance or natural properties, what it rules out). Second: even with a measure in hand we can't compute it, because we don't know which state is actual, so they need to separate the comparative question (which theory is closer) from the epistemic one (do we ever have reason to think science is getting closer), and say which of the two the realist's argument from success actually needs — and whether a merely regulative notion can do the explanatory work realism wants out of it.

Please make sure the wrong-answers notes catch the specific failure modes I keep seeing: reciting Popper/Miller–Tichý as if that settles the toy case; treating the N/A split as a mere technicality and picking a measure by gut feel without saying what's being traded; helping themselves to "natural structure fixes the metric" as a free move; and sliding from closeness-to-truth into better-evidentially-supported, which is a different question entirely.
