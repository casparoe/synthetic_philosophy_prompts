I'm 71, retired, and I spent most of my working life as a plant ecologist — pollination and seed dispersal, mostly. In the last three years I've been sitting in on a philosophy seminar at the university in town, which is the first time I've done anything like this. We spent two weeks on the therapy/enhancement distinction and I went home and started writing down what I actually think, case by case, and my list doesn't add up to anything I can state. I'm doing this at my kitchen table with no library access, so I'd rather have argument than pointers to papers I cant get hold of.

The principle I keep trying to make work is an ecologist's one: an organism is disordered when it doesn't fit the environment it actually has to live in, therapy is whatever repairs the fit, and enhancement is anything else that changes what the organism can do. It handles a surprising amount. But by the bottom of my list it seems to call things medicine that I think are doping, and to call things enhancement that I think are just medicine. Our seminar leader keeps saying that "function" is doing more work in my sentences than I think it is, and I suspect she's right.

Here are my verdicts.

1) Cochlear implant for a baby born profoundly deaf, hearing parents, will grow up oral: treatment, obviously. Though I notice my confidence drops if I imagine the same child in a household and a street where everyone signs and the child is thriving — and I don't think it should drop.
2) An implant that extends a normal-hearing adult's range up into the tens of kilohertz, so he can hear a failing bearing in a turbine by ear: not medicine. Fine for him to buy, my insurer should not pay.
3) A stimulant for a nine-year-old who can't sit still in a classroom, is failing, is starting to think he's stupid: treatment, give it.
4) The same stimulant, prescribed to a healthy 34-year-old warehouse picker so he can hold the rate the algorithm sets and keep his job: this makes me angry, and I don't think its medicine. My principle says it's the same case as 3, and I think that's a strike against my principle rather than against my anger.
5) Growth hormone for a boy whose pituitary isn't producing it: treatment, obviously.
6) Growth hormone for a healthy 12-year-old at the third percentile, short parents, no deficiency, tormented at school, miserable, begging his mother: I want to say give it to him. My wife says this treats his skeleton for what other children do to him, and I think she's right, and I still want to give it to him. And I don't see how 5 and 6 differ in the way that matters, given the misery is the same.
7) My own hip, replaced at 68 for ordinary wear and tear. If an account tells me that's enhancement because a 68-year-old joint is doing what selection left it doing after 68 years, I'll throw the account out.
8) A therapy for a 75-year-old that restores her immune response to roughly what it was at 25: treatment, and I'm fairly fierce about it — even though that's far above species-typical-for-age, which makes me think I don't understand how the "normal for your stage of life" version of the story is supposed to go.
9) A gene therapy that gives an ordinary healthy person a variant that already turns up in a small slice of the population with no downside — needing four hours' sleep and being fine on it, say, or naturally carrying very low cholesterol. I say enhancement, plainly, not something the health service should fund. But nothing here goes outside the species' normal range; it just moves one person from one normal setting to a better normal setting, which I think breaks any definition built on "beyond the normal range."
10) A drug given in the emergency room within hours of a violent assault that keeps the memory from consolidating into PTSD: treatment, plainly. The same drug given to a journalist twenty years on, to fade an old trauma she has already grieved and made sense of: I flip on this one weekly. Sometimes it reads as self-erasure, sometimes as the same medicine.
11) A wakefulness drug for a clever 19-year-old from a bad school, no quiet room, working nights, so she can sit her exam on something closer to the terms her tutored competitors sit it on: I think the state should pay, and I also don't think she's ill. The same drug for her wealthy classmate who just wants another two hours: no.

So: is there a principle that gets these, or nearly all, and where does it give up? I imagine the repair to mine is something like a threshold — enough to get along in the environment you're actually in, versus more than enough — but I can't state it so that it handles 4 without also losing 6, and I don't know what it says about 11, where the drug isn't restoring anything so much as evening something. Please tell me straight which of my verdicts I'd have to drop and why, and what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't put on the list: a cheap drug that lets everyone sleep three hours with no cost to health, and a deaf couple who, if they're going to use embryo selection at all, would prefer a deaf child, because their whole family and social world signs and they think a hearing child would be a stranger to them. I genuinely don't know what to say about either.
