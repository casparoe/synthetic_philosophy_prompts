I run a fortnightly logic-and-puzzles thing at the local library, mostly for teenagers, and for about a year now I've been hitting the same wall and I've started to suspect the wall has a name and I just don't have the word.

Here's the shape of it. I'll be showing that any number divisible by 6 is divisible by 3, and someone will happily accept my starting claim, and happily accept the step I take, and then ask: fine, but why should I accept that you're *allowed* to take that step? So I write the permission down as an extra line — if P, and if P then Q, then Q — and now exactly the same question gets asked about the new line, and we're off. I'm told this is an old puzzle involving a tortoise, and that the usual answer is something about mistaking a rule for a premise. I follow that, but it isn't the part I'm stuck on. The part I'm stuck on is what happens when I try to *answer* the question rather than diagnose it. Every answer I can think of is itself an argument of the very kind under dispute: if I say the rule has never led us astray, I'm using it to get from "never led us astray" to "probably fine"; if I say it can never take you from truths to a falsehood, I'm using it; even "you already rely on it everywhere else" looks like an instance of it. The same thing seems to crop up outside inference too — someone asks why they should trust the dictionary, and every answer is made of words whose meaning came from the dictionary.

And yet it doesn't feel like ordinary question-begging, because the claim I'm defending isn't sitting there among the premises; it's more that the *activity* of defending it is already an instance of it. My brother-in-law, who works on compilers, says this is just bootstrapping and obviously fine and I should stop fussing; a friend who reads more philosophy than me says it sounds like a known issue about whether a rule can be used to justify itself, but she couldnt remember what it's called and neither of us could put our finger on the word.

So: does this have a name? I don't just want the label — I want to know what the thing actually is, how it differs from plain circular arguing (where the conclusion is already in the premises) and from the tortoise version (where the trouble is an endless pile of extra premises), and whether it's a real problem or simply the shape any justification of something very basic has to have. If I'm actually mixing up two different things — one like "you can't come to accept it except by already relying on it" and another like "the justification can't do any work for someone who doesn't already accept it" — I'd like that pointed out too, and told what the difference is worth. Please don't just hand me a term; I want the edges of the thing.
