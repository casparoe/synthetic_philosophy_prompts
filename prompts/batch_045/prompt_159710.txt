A friend sent me a summary he wrote of a long magazine piece on AI risk. This is how it characterizes the alignment problem:

"Stripped down, alignment is a contracting problem. We are the principal, the system is the agent, and the job is to write a contract — an objective function — that leaves the agent no way of doing what we did not mean while still doing what we literally asked. Researchers split the failures in two: outer misalignment, where the objective we specify is simply the wrong one, and inner misalignment, where the system learns to game the objective we did give it. And it is expected to get worse as systems get better, because of the orthogonality thesis: intelligence and goals are independent, so a system can be arbitrarily capable and still pursue something as arbitrary as filling the universe with paperclips. More than that, the more capable it is, the more reliably it will converge on the instrumental goals — self-preservation, seizing compute, resisting being shut off — that make any final goal easier to reach. Two things follow. First, this is not really several problems but one: get the objective right and the rest is engineering. Second, since we cannot agree whose values the objective should encode, 'aligned' is an irreducibly political verdict, and the technical work is downstream of a political argument we have not yet had."
