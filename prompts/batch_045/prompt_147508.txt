I've been reading a chapter in one of those big-idea econ books — the kind where every topic gets four pages and a sidebar — and it summarizes revealed preference theory roughly like this:

'Before Samuelson, economists thought utility was a real quantity in a person's head, a kind of pleasure or happiness, and consumer theory was an attempted measurement of it in utils. Samuelson pointed out that this was circular, since the only evidence anyone has for how much utility a person gets from something is what they actually choose, so "utility" was just a fancy name for choice. He therefore redefined preference as whatever choice reveals: if you pick x when y was also available to you, you have revealed a preference for x over y. The point was that utility maximisation is empty as an empirical claim and survives only as a definition, so it isn't really falsifiable — whatever you do is by definition what you prefer. Later economists dressed this up with the so-called generalized axiom of revealed preference, which is only a bookkeeping device for checking whether a data set is consistent with some utility function, and since you can fit a clever enough utility function to almost any finite set of observations, it rarely bites. The upshot is that utility in economics just means observed choice, so it can never tell you whether someone is better or worse off, which is why welfare economics gave up on utility and fell back on money — willingness to pay, compensation tests, that sort of thing.'

I don't know what to make of it. Two things bug me. First, I've also heard the whole project described as an operationalist purge of unobservables, and this version makes it sound like a theory with real testable content, and those seem incompatable to me — though maybe I'm just wrong about what the operationalism was doing. Second, a friend who did a PhD in econ says the unfalsifiability line is a caricature, but when I asked him why, all he'd say was "budget sets, not menus," which meant nothing to me.

Where I really got stuck is a case we argued about at dinner. Suppose someone faces exactly the same prices and the same income on Monday and on Friday, and picks something different each time. If preference just is choice, then it seems like the theory either has to say his preferences changed between Monday and Friday, or say nothing at all — and neither feels like what the theory is supposed to be doing. Is that an actual problem for the view, or is it just me using "preference" in the everyday sense?

Can you go through the chapter's summary bit by bit — what a defender of the approach would accept as fair, what's a standard simplification, what's subtly off in a way that matters, and what the chapter would have to change to be accurate — and then tell me whether the conclusion about welfare economics really follows from the parts that are right?
