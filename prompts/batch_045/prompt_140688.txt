**Three Questions Inside One: a rigidity note on the hard problem**

**§1.** The framing I keep running into separates a family of tractable questions — how a system discriminates stimuli, integrates information, focuses attention, reports on its own states, controls action — from one residual question: given all of that, why is there something it is like to be the system at all? The tractable questions are supposed to be answerable in terms of mechanism, and the residual question is supposed to remain open even after every mechanism has been specified, because you could in principle specify every causal and functional fact and still be left asking why all that processing is not dark inside.

**§2.** My first claim is that the residual question is not one question but several, and that running them together distorts the debate:

- **Q1 (existence):** why is there experience at all rather than none?
- **Q2 (palette):** why these experiences — why does seeing red feel the way it does rather than the way hearing a low note does, or rather than some quality no human has ever had?
- **Q3 (structure):** why does experience have the shape it has — unified at a time, extended and apparently flowing across time, arranged in similarity spaces?
- **Q4 (report):** why are we so strongly and stubbornly inclined to say these things about ourselves?

**§3.** My second claim is about where the argumentative pressure actually comes from. Q1 is the question everyone quotes, but I think the two thought experiments that do the real work in the literature — the one about the researcher who knows every physical fact and then learns something new when she first sees a colour, and the one about a creature functionally identical to you with nothing it is like to be it — get their force from Q2. The second case is the limiting case of the first: it is the palette question with the palette emptied. The step from *all the function, different qualities* to *all the function, no qualities* feels natural only because the first step already felt available. So if the palette intuition could be defeated, the zombie case would be left with nothing supporting it.

**§4.** The part I think might be new. Consider the intuition that the qualities could be permuted while nothing functionally detectable changed: red and green traded, and every judgement of similarity, every emotional response, every memory, every disposition to act, adjusted in train, so that no behaviour, report, or functional relation distinguishes the result from the original. Call such a permutation a swap. A swap can exist only if the functional organisation has a symmetry: a relabelling of internal states that leaves every functional relation intact. The intuition pump therefore relies on a substantive claim, namely that the space of qualities has symmetries that the functional organisation does not.

But there is no reason to grant that. A sufficiently richly structured organisation may be rigid: the similarity structure, the valence structure, the way qualities interact with attention, memory, and action may pin each quality to a unique place, leaving no non-trivial relabelling at all. If the organisation is rigid, then there is no swap to perform, and the question *why this quality rather than that one?* collapses back into *why this total structure at all?* — which is Q1, not an additional brute fact stacked on top of it.

In terms I am more used to: an interface specifies behaviour, and for a thin interface there are many implementations that differ in ways the interface cannot see. But a rich enough interface admits only one implementation up to relabelling, and then the question *why this implementation rather than that one?* has nowhere to live. The swap intuition, I want to say, is an artefact of describing the quality space too thinly — as a bare manifold of anonymous somethings — and then being impressed that a bare manifold can be permuted.

**§5.** What I take this to establish, and what I take it not to establish. It does not touch Q1. Even granted rigidity, the fact — if it is one — that there is experience at all is exactly where it was. The interest is dialectical: much of the pressure toward adding extra brute psychophysical principles to the world comes from Q2, and if that pressure is removed, what remains is the bare conceivability of the functionally identical creature with no experience. That is a much thinner sort of support, and it seems to me that a functionalist who simply declines to be moved by it is not obviously being unreasonable.

**§6.** Where I know I am on thin ice. (i) Rigidity may be an empirical claim about creatures like us, or it may just be a restatement of the functionalist conclusion dressed in graph-theoretic language; I cannot tell which. (ii) The argument feels like it overgeneralises: the same style of reasoning would seem to show that any necessary connection whatsoever is merely a rigid structure, which sounds too cheap to be doing real work. (iii) Q4 may cut against me. If the reason we find Q1 compelling is itself a piece of cognitive machinery that mischaracterises its own states, that seems to support the view that the hard problem is an illusion generated by that machinery — which is not my view, and I am not sure my essay is compatible with it.
