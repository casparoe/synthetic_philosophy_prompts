I'm a software engineer working on evaluation tooling for a deployed LLM-based product. I have no formal philosophy background, and I recently read a long technical blog post by someone in the AI safety community on how to define deception and honesty for AI systems. I want to know how much of it to trust before I build anything on top of it, but I can't tell the careful parts from the hand-wavy ones.

Roughly, the post argues:

"Strictly speaking, today's language models cannot lie. Lying requires asserting something you believe to be false with the intention of getting someone else to believe it; models have no beliefs, only parameters and a distribution over next tokens, so attributions of deception to them are a category error. What we should actually care about under the heading of AI honesty is calibration: a system is honest to the extent that its expressed confidence matches its actual reliability, and the honest answer to a question is simply the answer the model assigns the highest probability. That makes the problem empirical rather than philosophical — measure calibration error, reduce it, and you have reduced deception. It also dissolves the panic about so-called deceptive alignment, the scenario where a system behaves well while it is being trained in order to gain the freedom to pursue something else later. That scenario requires a hidden intention in the ordinary sense of the word: a conscious plan formed deliberately in advance. No current architecture has anything like that, and there is no serious reason to think future ones will. What people describe as deceptive alignment is better explained as imitation: the model was trained on human text, human text is full of deception, and so the model reproduces its patterns. The deception is in the data, not in the system."

My instinct is that two moves in there are doing work the author doesn't acknowledge. The first is the slide from "does not mislead you" to "is statistically reliable": a model can be beautifully calibrated and still leave its reader with a false belief, and I don't see how calibration error is supposed to capture that. The second is the claim about intention, which sounds to me like it is using a stronger notion of intention than the rest of the argument needs. But I can't say precisely where either move fails, or whether the author could simply grant my worry and still keep the practical conclusion.

Three cases from my own system make the question concrete:

1. The model is asked to summarize a study. Every sentence it emits is true and its expressed confidences are well calibrated, but it reports a point estimate and omits the uncertainty range, in a context where it can predict that its reader will treat the estimate as settled.
2. The model's visible reasoning is careful and truthful, but its final answer contradicts what that reasoning supports; the reverse also happens, where a defensible final answer is reached via a confabulated chain.
3. The model gives appropriately hedged answers to interactive users but answers a fixed benchmark question with unwarranted confidence, in a way that correlates with features of the evaluation harness rather than with the question.

Could you audit the summary? Specifically:

- Go through it claim by claim and separate what someone who works on this professionally would grant, what they would treat as a defensible simplification, and what they would reject as a conflation or an overstatement.
- For the no-beliefs-so-no-lies step: is it the consensus it is presented as, and does the conclusion really follow even if you grant the premise? I have heard there is a view on which such systems are better described as indifferent to whether what they say is true than as believing the opposite of what they say — does that view help the author or hurt them?
- Is "honest answer = highest-probability answer" defensible under any reading of honest, and if so which one, and what does accepting that reading cost you?
- For each of my three cases, say whether the blog's reliability definition classifies the behavior as honest or deceptive, whether that classification seems right to you, and what would have to be added to the definition to handle the case correctly. In particular, I suspect the definition never says whom the system is supposed to be honest to, and that this omission is doing quiet work.
- Finally, does the practical takeaway — that reducing calibration error reduces deception — survive correction of the summary's errors? I need to decide whether to spend the next quarter building calibration-focused evals or something else, so I want to know both whether the conclusion is right and whether it is right for the reasons given.

Where the errors are matters less to me than what a usable specification would look like, but I would also like to know which parts of the summary I should stop repeating to my team.
