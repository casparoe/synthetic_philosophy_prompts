I'm in a grad reading group on epistemology (six of us plus one faculty member) and we got completely stuck on something last week that I keep turning over, so I want an actual number from you rather than another round of everyone restating their favourite view.

Setup in my own words first, so we don't talk past each other. By "ascription universality" I mean: when someone says "S knows that p", whether that ascription is correct doesn't depend on anything about the person making it — not their practical stakes, not their evidence, not the standards in play in their conversation. Two speakers with the same information about S who say opposite things are thereby disagreeing, not each saying something true-for-themselves. By "epistemic dominance" I mean the fallback people reach for when they want to keep universality: among all the perspectives in play (the ascriber's, the subject's, a later assessor's, the group's pooled evidence) exactly one is privileged, and its standards are the ones that fix the truth value; the others get no vote. My half-baked worry is that dominance is doing more work than anyone admits, for two reasons. First, nested cases: A says "B knows that p", and later C, in a very different context, says "what A said was wrong" — is the privileged perspective picked once for the whole embedding, or freshly at each level? The usual menu (plain invariantism, contextualism about the ascriber's standards, stakes-sensitive views about the subject's, assessment-sensitive ones) all seem to quietly assume there's an answer and that it's the same at both levels. Second, when perspectives differ along dimensions that don't rank against each other — A has high stakes, B has lots of evidence, C has neither but is the one doing the assessing — there may just be no dominant one. I've been playing with a weaker version, call it weak dominance: instead of a single privileged perspective, take the perspectives that aren't dominated by any other and count the ascription as correct only if it's correct by all of their standards. That buys you universality back, but it seems to render a lot of ordinary ascriptions neither true nor false, which might be worse than giving up universality.

So: what's your credence that ascription universality, in the sense above, is true of knowledge ascriptions — i.e. that there is a single ascriber-independent correctness standard and that ordinary cases don't quietly collapse into indeterminacy? I want a number and the decomposition, including at least these: that the question has a determinate answer at all rather than "knows" being indeterminate or plural between competing standards; that "knows" is semantically context-sensitive rather than only pragmatically shifty; conditional on that, your split over which parameter does the work (ascriber's context, subject's stakes, assessor's perspective, something else); that strong dominance survives incomparable perspectives; and that weak dominance rescues universality without trivialising it. A number and one line of justification for each, then how you combine them. I'm at about 0.45, the faculty member is at more like 0.2, and someone else in the group thinks the whole question is ill-posed — tell me which of those disagreements is the real one and what it's actually about. Then: which single sub-question would move your number most, and what would count as evidence for it? I don't mean citations, I can find the literature myself — I mean what kind of consideration would do the work: a new kind of case, cross-linguistic or corpus patterns, experimental results on ordinary ascriptions, or something about how the disagreement is reported. Also say plainly if you think my nested-ascription worry is a red herring, and why.
