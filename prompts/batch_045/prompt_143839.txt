I'm a novelist finishing a book that is partly set inside a small interpretability lab attached to a city transit authority, and I have written myself into a corner in one scene. Three characters — a junior researcher, a skeptical senior engineer, and an outside auditor — are standing around a workstation looking at the internals of a modest model, the kind a competent engineer could genuinely build and train this year, which was trained only to predict the next token in a corpus of subway maintenance logs and was never given a map, a coordinate, a line diagram, or a distance. The junior researcher says the model "represents the subway map"; the engineer says that is romantic fiction; the auditor has to decide whether that sentence can go into a report. I got into an argument about a version of this with a friend who designs subway signage, which is how I realized I don't actually know whether my three characters are disagreeing about the world or about a word — and my early readers include people who do this work for a living, so I need the case engineered rather than merely evocative. Please construct the scenario I can put in their mouths.

The specifications are these. First, realism: the setup must be something a skeptical ML engineer would concede is possible today — no superintelligence, no perfect or near-perfect predictors, no exotic physics, no sci-fi apparatus — and it must not collapse into the familiar story of a protected attribute smuggled in through a proxy feature; the feature at issue should be the map-like structure itself, not race, sex, or anything else loaded. Second, presentability: the case has to be statable in roughly two hundred and fifty words of dialogue with no equations and no diagrams, and still be recognizable to a smart non-specialist reader, so please give me the scenario first as prose and then again as about ten lines of dialogue my characters could actually speak without the structure being dumbed down. Third, and this is the part I care most about, the case must drive a wedge between three accounts of when an internal state counts as a representation, which I will define here so we are not trading on shared vocabulary: (A) the causal-historical account, on which a state represents something only if that thing, or a reliable proxy for it, was causally responsible for the state's being as it is and the training process in some sense selected for tracking it; (B) the structural account, on which a state represents a domain only if the relations among the system's internal states mirror the relations in that domain — distances, compositions, transformations — closely and systematically enough; and (C) the instrumental or "stance" account, on which content attributions are justified only relative to what they let us do, so that a state may be treated as representing something for purposes of prediction and intervention while nothing is gained, and perhaps something is lost, by saying so for purposes of explaining the system's actual behavior. The verdicts I need are: (A) says no, the model does not represent the map; (B) says yes, and says it determinately; (C) says yes for one specified purpose and no for another equally live purpose, and denies that there is any further fact of the matter once purposes are fixed. These verdicts have to fall out of the accounts plus the facts of the case — I don't want a scenario that simply stipulates who says what.

Two further constraints, because I can already hear the engineer's objections and the scene dies if they land. The structural evidence must be non-gerrymandered: the isomorphism has to be discovered by a procedure specified before anyone consults the actual map, and it has to support predictions of relations — which station sits between two others, which transfer is unexpectedly short — that are then independently checked, so that the obvious retort that any smooth state space can be made to look like anything does not win by default. And the state in question must be genuinely idle with respect to the model's ordinary outputs: gated, ablated-but-intact, or otherwise present in the internals while making no difference to what the model actually does, so that (A) has something real to bite on. After the scenario and the dialogue, please do the following: state each account's verdict and name the single feature of the case that is doing the work for it; identify which one feature, if altered, collapses the whole disagreement, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is changed incrementally — if the gate is opened a little, if the model is later fine-tuned on data that actually mentions geography; then tell me how a defender of whichever account comes out worst would attack the case, and whether the case as built survives that attack. Finally, two things I need for the book: tell me whether my characters are having a verbal dispute or a substantive one, and if it is substantive, say what observation would settle it; and tell me, briefly and honestly, what the auditor should write in the report. Please don't cite any papers or hand me a reading list — I want the argument itself, since I have no way to chase down references. Please write in American English.
