I serve on the accountability committee of a transnational research-funding institute with offices in Berlin and Boston. We regrant public and philanthropic money to locally run field programs, and our own staff work under a single Integrity Code that both offices negotiated and adopted together. We have two cases before us, and the committee has divided over them in a way that worries me less for what it says about the cases than for what it says about us.

Case one. Anke R., a program officer in the Berlin office, learned that a partner organization in her portfolio had charged roughly four thousand euros of staff taxi fares to a grant line that did not permit it. The misuse appears to have been routine, small, and, so far as anyone can tell, harmless in itself. The Code required her to report suspected misuse up a fixed escalation path. She did exactly that, through three levels, with no personal commentary and without requesting any proportionality review. The donor's auditors then did what the Code contemplates: the partner was suspended, a refugee-education program that had run for nine years was terminated, and forty-one locally hired teachers lost their jobs. There is no evidence that Anke wanted this or clearly foresaw it. There is evidence that several colleagues, including the Berlin office director, urged her informally to slow down, and that she answered that the Code does not permit her to slow down.

Case two. Marcus T., her Boston counterpart, found the same pattern of small, harmless-looking diversions across four partners in his region over several months. He did not report them. Instead he wrote a dated memorandum to himself explaining why: enforcement would dismantle functioning programs over bookkeeping violations that injured no one, and a rule whose predictable effect is that outcome is not one he was willing to be the instrument of. The memorandum sat unread until the donor's wider audit surfaced the diversions independently; Marcus then produced it. He disputes none of the facts. He maintains that he did the right thing and would defend doing it again.

We must dispose of both. The Boston members want Anke formally censured for what they call rule-fetishism: a professional who can watch forty-one people lose their livelihoods and answer that the rulebook left her no room has not exercised judgment at all, and a rule that leaves no one answerable for its outcome has become a machine for distributing responsibility until it dissolves. The Berlin members want Marcus suspended: an integrity system in which each officer privately redecides which rules bind him is not an integrity system; the trust of a donor and of forty partner organizations rests on exactly the predictability Marcus treated as optional; and a memorandum written to oneself and produced only after being caught is the signature of someone who wanted the standing of conscience without the cost of defending it at the time. The split does not map perfectly onto passports — two of our Berlin members are the most sympathetic to Marcus — but it maps closely enough that discussion keeps sliding off the cases and onto the two moral traditions they are heard as expressing.

I do not want a memo that merely splits the difference, and I do not want one that reads as a Rorschach test for national character. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate, with a To/From/Re header, a one-sentence recommendation at the top, and a body of roughly nine hundred words, doing the following.

First, dispose of each case with a specific, actionable outcome, including any time frame or condition, in language that Anke and Marcus could each recognize as addressed to them rather than to a type.

Second, give the committee a general standard it can apply to the next version of this case, since there will be one. In framing it, please engage two ideas I have not been able to get the committee to engage.

The first I have been calling the aggregation argument. A standing rule is not merely a constraint on me; it is compressed judgment, the residue of what the institution has already learned and the fixed point around which everyone else has arranged their expectations. On that picture, overriding a rule carries an epistemic burden and not only a moral one: the objector must be able to say what the rule's authors did not know, or could not weigh. Tell me how Marcus's position fares under that burden. Then tell me whether the burden makes sense at all when the disagreement concerns ends rather than facts. Aggregation seems to work for information; it is much less clear that it works for values, since there is no obvious unit in which the weight the Code assigns to bookkeeping and the weight Marcus assigns to forty-one teachers could be summed by anyone other than him.

The second is the publicity condition, as I have been putting it: an appeal of conscience is a claim made on other people; a claim made only in a private memorandum and revealed after the fact is unfalsifiable and gives the institution no opportunity to revise or to refuse; and Marcus's silence may therefore be a moral defect rather than merely a procedural one, because he may have withheld from the rule the very correction he believed it needed. Say whether you accept this, and say what it would demand of a future objector — what must be said, to whom, and when — for conscientious non-compliance to be something other than retroactive self-exculpation.

Third, take seriously the strongest version of the case against whichever side you come down nearer to. If you are inclined to excuse Marcus, meet the charge that the difference between a conscience and a pretext is precisely that a conscience is willing to be tested at the time, and that no institution can build a practice on untested private judgments. If you are inclined to censure him, meet the charge that a system with no room for proportionality will eventually do something obscene in the name of tidiness, and that the people who staff it will have been trained by the system itself not to see it coming. And if you are inclined to leave Anke alone, meet the parallel charge that following a rule while refusing to notice what it is doing is not fidelity but a way of being innocent by arrangement.

Finally, close with three or four sentences addressed to the committee rather than to the parties, saying which of the two moral grammars — fidelity to the shared rule, or the individual's conscientious judgment about what the rule is for — should be the working default for an organization like ours, and what the strongest thing is that can be said for the other. One of my Berlin colleagues has objected that the contrast is too crude, on the grounds that the tradition he is taken to represent also contains Kant's insistence that the moral law is something the agent gives herself, and that the postwar repudiation of the claim that an order is an order is itself a German achievement rather than an American import. Say what you make of that, if it bears on where you land.
