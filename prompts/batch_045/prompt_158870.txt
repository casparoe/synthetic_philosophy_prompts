Can you make a tortoise infer?

1. The set up. Achilles wants the Tortoise to accept Z. The Tortoise already accepts A and B. So Achilles offers a third proposition C: if A and B are true, then Z is true. The Tortoise happily accepts that too, and still wont accept Z. So Achilles offers D: if A and B and C are true, then Z is true. And so on without end. Everybody reads this as a puzzle about whether deduction can be justified.

2. What I think the regress actually shows. Look at any stage of the dialogue. The Tortoise has some stock of accepted sentences S and hasn't got Z. Achilles adds one more sentence saying that if everything in S is true then Z is true. Fine — the Tortoise accepts it. But nothing has changed about what he *does* with his stock. The new sentence is just one more thing he believes, and the gap between believing a conditional and drawing the conclusion is exactly the gap it was before. So the moral isn't that deduction can't be justified; it's that no quantity of extra propositional input settles how you are supposed to reason. Call this the conservation claim: adding premises never reduces the number of rules you have to apply without those rules themselves appearing as premises. At every single stage of Carroll's dialogue the leftover residue is the same — one rule, or one rule plus some conjunction shuffling — and it never gets licensed. So it isn't really an infinite regress at all, it's a loop, and the endlessly growing list of premises is a red herring that makes the story look scarier than it is.

3. Is this just the textbook point? The standard line is that a rule of inference is a licence to move while a premise is something you move *from*, so asking for a rule in the shape of a premise is a category mistake and the whole thing dissolves. I want to say my version is different from that. Grant the category point completely. Now hand the Tortoise the rule anyway — write it on a card, hard-wire it into him, whatever. There is still something left over, because he can now ask why he should *trust* the card. The demand reappears one level up: it becomes a demand about the acceptance of a rule rather than about the stock of premises. Call the first one the horizontal regress and the second the vertical one. My claim is that Carroll only actually constructs the horizontal one, and the vertical one is the one readers think they see in it. The vertical one is the interesting one: if it can't be stopped, then the problem really is about justification, and not about the silliness of trying to turn rules into premises.

4. The objection I'm most worried about. Someone will say that the Tortoise has to use modus ponens in order to demand a justification of modus ponens, so he is being inconsistent and that's the end of it. I want to reply that he doesn't have to use it at all: to produce the next conditional he only needs a syntactic recipe — take everything you accept, stick an if in front of it, put Z at the end — and following a recipe for writing a sentence down isn't inferring anything. So the objection as it's usually stated fails. What I think survives is weaker: the Tortoise is committed to Z in some sense even though he hasn't inferred it, and whether that matters for the epistemology is a seperate question.

5. Where I want to end up but am not sure about. I want to say the regress stops, if it stops anywhere, because accepting a conditional just *is* being disposed to make the corresponding inference — there's no extra step where you decide to apply a rule. On that picture the Tortoise's refusal isn't a refusal to accept some proposition, it's a failure to exercise a disposition he already has, and that's a psychological fact about him rather than a gap in the justification of deduction. So deduction isn't justified by an argument; it's built into what having beliefs is, and asking for a reason to accept it is like asking for a reason to have reasons. But I don't trust this move. It feels like I've explained why we *do* trust deduction instead of giving a reason to trust it, and those might be different things, and if they are then my paper quietly changes the subject somewhere in this paragraph.
