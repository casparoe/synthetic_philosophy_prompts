I need a hand with something before Thursday. I'm a PhD student in philosophy — I work on vagueness, which is the only reason I got pulled onto this — and I'm the grad rep on our Graduate School's Progress Review Committee. Every May we review about thirty fourth- and fifth-year students against the rule that to keep funding and stay enrolled a student must be "making substantial progress on the dissertation"; the ones who don't recieve a continuation get a "required to withdraw" letter. Twenty-odd of the files are obvious in both directions and five or six ruin everyone's week, and this year two committee members have dug in on what are, as far as I can tell, straight-up epistemicist and supervaluationist readings of what we are doing when we pass judgement on those six. Dana says: the phrase is vague but there is still a fact of the matter about whether someone is making substantial progress, our uncertainty is just ignorance, so we should spend more time on the hard files, pull in the students' own committees for more evidence, and write letters saying we judged carefully and got it right. Marcus says: there is no fact of the matter until we precisify, the line is something we choose and are answerable for rather than something we discover, so we should stipulate a precisification in the published policy, give our reasons for picking that one, and stop writing letters that read like reports of findings. My office mate, who is not a philosopher, thinks we should just do whatever generates the fewest appeals, and I have to admit that is a real consideration here.

The complication I can't get past is that "substantial progress" isn't vague along one dimension, it's vague along several at once — words on the page, chapters drafted, whether the prospectus was defended, whether there's a submitted article, whether data collection is finished — and the six of us on the committee are implicitly weighting these differently. So Marcus's "whatever holds across all admissible precisifications" move looks like it might leave almost nobody determinately qualifying, which can't definately be the right result for the four students who plainly are doing fine. And then whatever borderline category we invent will have fuzzy edges of its own, and I don't know whether that's a real problem for the policy or just philosophers' squeamishness. Someone floated resolving the borderline cases by a weighted lottery, the weight being the share of admissible precisifications on which the student counts as progressing; Dana's side said that's inventing a number and pretending to measure something, Marcus's side said it's acceptable as a tie-break but doesn't actually follow from his view. Separately: even granting Dana the semantics, I keep coming back to the point that near the boundary no judgement of ours is reliable in the way knowledge is supposed to be — there are always nearby cases where we'd have gone the other way — and I genuinely can't tell whether that pushes toward Marcus's picture or only toward some procedural fix that both pictures could live with.
