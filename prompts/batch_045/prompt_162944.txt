I co-teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of speech acts with a senior colleague — call him Halvorsen — who has unusually stable verdicts on assertion cases, and over perhaps fifteen years of reading groups and thesis defenses I have accumulated a fair record of them. He frames the question throughout as what it is permissible to say, distinguishes that from what is blameworthy, and is suspicious of the machinery some theorists build on that distinction. His verdicts, as I have them: (i) a lucky guess — someone with no evidence at all announces that the panel meets Thursday, and it does — is not in order, and its coming out true is beside the point; (ii) a meteorologist who asserts, on impeccable evidence, a forecast that turns out false is in order, and he resists the suggestion that the assertion was defective but blameless — nothing, he thinks, went wrong with the saying; (iii) fake-barn and garden-variety Gettier cases are not in order; (iv) telling a colleague that her lottery ticket lost, before the draw, on statistical grounds alone, is not in order; (v) a physician who asserts a sentence she fully knows to a patient who will predictably hear in it a stronger, false claim is not in order, even though she knew exactly what she said; (vi) a journalist who asserts a true claim she knows only from an ill-gotten confidential file is in order as far as asserting goes — the wrongness lies upstream of the saying; (vii) a language model that emits a true sentence on the strength of statistical regularities is not asserting at all, so the norms of assertion have nothing there to evaluate; (viii) an administrator whose claim that the college is solvent is partly made true by its being asserted is in order, downstream effects on hearers being irrelevant.

The case I want a prediction on is one my own committee faces next week, and I care less about the email itself than about what his answer would reveal about the rule he is running. Five of us have to send a single message, signed by all five and written in the first person plural, stating that the external review found no evidence of research misconduct in the unit. No one read the whole report: each of us read two chapters, the claim is supported by what we collectively read, and two of us — I am one — now have only a fading recollection of what the others reported, so our grounds are largely testimonial. The claim is true and uncontested. We also know that several recipients, including the dean, will read the sentence as each signatory's personal word rather than as a committee finding. Would Halvorsen say the message is in order as it stands, and if not, what is the smallest repair: a line making the division of labor explicit, the two of us re-reading the full report before signing, or dropping to three signatories who know?

So give me the map before I put it to him. Deliver it as a markdown table, one row per candidate rule, with these columns: rule_id; rule_statement (a single sentence, as exact as you can make it); fit_with_record (which of i–viii it predicts cleanly, which it must reinterpret or strain, and how it does so); prediction_on_new_case (in order, not in order, or in order only if repaired — naming the repair you expect him to want, from the three I listed); confidence (0–1); discriminating_variant (the single change to this case, or the smallest further case, whose verdict would separate this rule from whichever rule you take to be its closest rival). Four to six rows; include the truth norm only if you think (ii) and (iv) do not dispose of it; include a note under the table saying whether any rule fits the whole record without straining at least one verdict, and which verdict is the strain point. Then a short JSON object with these fields: best_guess (his verdict on the message as it stands), rule_relied_on (the rule_id you are betting on), confidence (0–1), smallest_repair (one of division_of_labour_note, reread_before_signing, fewer_signatories, none_needed), and question_for_halvorsen (one sentence: the thing I should ask him in the corridor that would most reduce my uncertainty).
