I'm writing a novel about two branches of one family, one in Wisconsin and one in a small town in Hesse, and I keep having the sam argument with my writing group about how the two sides raise their kids, and it never goes anywhere. The American characters treat "my conscience won't let me do this" as a full stop, and the German characters treat the rule as the full stop, and each side has a ready diagnosis of the other: one hears rule-fetishism and cowardice hiding behind procedure, the other hears selfishness dressed up as moral seriousness. I've started calling these two moves conscience laundering (exempting yourself from a shared procedure while still counting on everyone else to keep it, so the rule keeps doing its work without costing you anything) and procedure ventriloquism (letting the rule say the no so you never have to own the refusal). My hypothesis is that the fight isn't about obedience versus autonomy at all: it's about what a rule is for. One side thinks a rule's job is to make a shared, predictable space, and that this is worth having even when the rule is dumb in this particular case; the other thinks a rule is just a summary of reasons that you're always entitled to inspect and overrule. Is that the real thing, or is it more verbal than that — do the two sides mean different things by 'conscience' (a private verdict versus a faculty for applying rules) and by 'order' (predictability versus legitimacy)? Or is some hidden empirical claim doing the work, about which arrangement actually produces less cruelty over a lifetime? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of national character, and tell me honestly what would count as progress on this — a distinction, a kind of test case, a change of question — or whether it's one of those disputes that can't be settled in the terms it's posed in.
