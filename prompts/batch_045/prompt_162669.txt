I teach a joint seminar on personal identity and philosophy of physics, and one of my students has been coming to office hours for three weeks with a quantum-immortality argument that I think proves far too much, but I cant put my finger on where it breaks. He's sharp, he pre-empts every objection I raise, and I have to write comments on his final draft by Friday. The chain, more or less as he now has it:

(1) Everettian setup: every outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized somewhere in the branching structure, and conscious experience continues in a branch just in case there is a functioning, psychologically continuous successor of me there — so a branch in which the body dies hosts no experience of mine, and a branch in which it doesn't hosts one.
(2) What I should expect is settled by the branches that host experiences of mine. The dead ones host nothing for me, so they contribute nothing to my expectation; and since there is always at least one live branch, "I have some experience or other" is not a real restriction. So conditional on my experiencing anything at all, I am in a live branch.
(3) Now iterate. At each round, the branch in which the device fires hosts no experience of mine, so it drops out, and I should expect to experience surviving; by induction over rounds, I should expect to experience surviving any finite number of them.
(4) I thought I had him on a scope mistake — "for each round there is a branch that survives it" doesn't give you "a branch that survives every round" — but he points out that the branching structure is an infinite, finitely branching tree, and König's lemma hands you an infinite path through it, so there really is a worldline on which I survive every round. I looked up the lemma and he's right.
(5) I then raised the measure objection: the surviving cohort's share of total measure decays like (1/2)^n, so the branch I'm being told to expect is a measure-zero freak. He answers with what he calls the tapered machine — at round n, set the kill probability to 1/(4n²) rather than 1/2. Now the surviving cohort keeps ∏(1 − 1/(4n²)) of the measure, which converges to 2/π, a bit under two-thirds, and stays there forever. No bleed to zero.
(6) So, he concludes, I should expect to experience surviving arbitrarily many rounds: immortal from the inside.

The trouble is that this seems to buy me a lot more than immortality, and I cant tell which part of the machinery is printing the extra money. First: rewire the machine so it kills me unless a million fair quantum coins all land heads. Untapered, (2) and (3) still deliver certainty that I will experience the million-heads run, and tapering isn't available here without rigging the machine into harmlessness. So either (2) is doing the work, in which case I get to be certain of an absurd run, or the taper is doing the work, in which case the argument has collapsed into ordinary probability theory and a single-world theorist predicts exactly the same two-thirds, and nothing distinctively quantum has been shown. He won't say which it is. Second: wire the same trigger to a friend in the next room, and every branch containing a successor of me is a branch containing a successor of her, so I should expect her to survive every round too, which I have no independent reason to think and which doesn't come out symmetric once the devices are independent. Third: every cause of death I can think of bottoms out in quantum-level events, so the reasoning steps off the machine entirely — I should expect never to experience dying of anything, including old age, and should expect increasingly to find myself in the branch where everyone I know has died and I haven't — and it also seems to erase any self-interested reason to avoid a risk at all, since I survive either way from where I'm standing. Nobody who believes this acts as if that were true.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) than (2) if it comes to that — the physics doesn't care whether anyone is watching, and (2) is where all the philosophical work seems to be happening — but I want to know what I'm signing up for. Concretely: if the fix is to make first-person expectation track Born measure rather than track live branches, where does the measure get its authority over what I should expect (epistemic reading, decision-theoretic reading, something else), and what am I then supposed to say about the worldline from (4), the one that exists with probability 1 and carries measure zero and has a successor of me on it who never dies? Is "probability zero, but it's really there, and it's me" a coherent thing to think about my own future, or just a way of not saying anything? Price the other exits too: deny that survival survives branching at all and require uniqueness of the continuer, or else accept (6). If I require uniqueness, do ordinary first-person expectations under branching survive — do I still get to expect spin-up with probability |α|², or does that expectation collapse with this one? And tell me which of my three overreach cases is genuinely damning, if any is, and whether there's a reading of (2) that is true but too weak to get (3) off the ground. I'd rather not write "see me" in the margin a fourth time.
