I am finishing the last chapter of my dissertation, which defends a Dennett-style treatment of the self as a centre of narrative gravity — an abstractum rather than an illatum, in his vocabulary — against the phenomenological objection that such an account leaves out the primitive mineness of experience: the fact that my experiences show up as mine before I have told myself any story about them. My entire reply rests on the centre-of-gravity analogy. A body's centre of gravity is a determinate, locatable, prediction-indispensable point that is not an additional piece of matter; so, I argue, a self can be a real and indispensable posit of the intentional stance without being a further mental particular to be found alongside the narratives. I want that analogy audited before I lean on it in print.

Two cases make me suspect it misbehaves. First, any distribution of mass whatever, however arbitrary, has a centre of gravity, whereas I want selfhood to be something a system can lack. Consider a language model fine-tuned on a single person's complete diaries and correspondence, which emits fluent, internally consistent first-person narratives while possessing no behavioural organisation that those narratives predict, rationalise, or are answerable to; the formalism appears to hand it a self as unproblematically as it hands one to a stone. Second, the formalism has no analogue of negative mass. Some subjects sincerely and consistently avow that there is no continuing self at all, and their self-ascriptions appear to cancel rather than cumulate; a physics with only attractive mass cannot represent a repulsive narrative, so it is unclear whether the analogy returns the verdict no self here, a self located somewhere odd, or no verdict at all. I am also unclear what plays the role of the gravitational field — whether it is fixed by the subject's own productions or by an interpreter community's uptake — and what follows for selfhood if it is the latter.

Please give me an explicit correspondence table (mass distribution, field, the point itself, the point's lying inside or outside the body as in the Fosbury flop, its stability under perturbation of the distribution, the shift from centre of mass to centre of gravity in a non-uniform field); mark which disanalogies bear on the specific work I am making the analogy do and which are inert; say whether my deflationary conclusion — that there is no further fact about where the self is, or how many selves there are, beyond what narrative prediction requires — survives the two cases above, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time; and propose a better analogy if one exists, whether drawn from dynamical systems, eigendecomposition, or somewhere else.
