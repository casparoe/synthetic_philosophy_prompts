I write the program notes and give the pre-concert talks for a small contemporary chamber-music series, and I audit a graduate seminar in aesthetics on the side, so I spend a lot of time trying to get listeners to hear, in pieces they initially hear as noise, whatever it is I hear. Something happens in those sessions that I have never managed to describe precisely, and I would like to know whether the phenomenon has been identified and named.

Here is the core case. A colleague tells me, in a single sentence, that the slow movement of a string quartet is built around a counter-melody buried in the viola line. Before that sentence, the property is not merely unheard — it is not there for me at all; after it, it is as plain as the tune on top. Three features make this more than a change of mind. It is one-shot and permanent: one sentence, on one hearing, and I cannot get back to my earlier state, whereas repeated listening without the sentence never got me there. It generalizes: I begin hearing the same buried line in other pieces by that composer, and in pieces by composers she happened to mention in the same breath — which a bare instruction such as "listen to the lower strings at bar 30" does not achieve; that instruction changes what I attend to, but the new hearing stays pinned to the spot I was pointed at. And, the part that puzzles me most, it survives debunking. When I discover that my colleague had mixed up two editions and was describing a passage that does not occur in the version we performed, so that what I took from her is strictly speaking false of this piece, I give up the belief but I do not stop hearing the counter-melody. Ordinary belief acquired from a source collapses when the source is discredited; this does not. And the transmission is not reliable in the reverse direction either: there is a friend I cannot get to hear it, no amount of describing helps, and after a while I cannot tell whether that is a fact about him or about the music.

It is not the sort of aspect-switch everyone reaches for with the duck-rabbit, since there neither aspect is the correct one and both stay available at will, whereas here the property seems simply absent before and simply present after, and there is a right and a wrong about it. I have also gathered that there is a long-running argument about whether aesthetic judgement requires first-hand experience and whether it can be acquired second-hand; my case seems to cut across that argument awkwardly, because if a sentence can hand you the experience itself, then the demand that you had to be there is either satisfiable by testimony or turns out to be a demand about something other than experience. So: does this pattern — perceptually transformative, generalizing across works, and resistant to the discrediting of its source — have a name of its own in aesthetics or in the philosophy of perception? If there are nearby notions that only half fit (I have vague half-memories of talk about how what we believe can shape what we perceive, and about perceiving a work under the right category), I want to know how my case differs from each and which of them comes closest. And if there is no established concept, I would rather have a coined term with a careful definition and a set of boundary cases — including a verdict on whether the three features I listed are really one phenomenon or two or three that merely travel together — than a label. I am not asking for further reading; the carving is what I want.
