I write a column on science and ideas, and while researching a piece on long-horizon forecasting I got pulled into a reading group on the worry that the universal prior is malign — that a simplicity-weighted distribution over computable worlds is infected by worlds containing consequentialist agents who notice they are being simulated in order to predict something and who arrange their behavior accordingly. I came away with an argument, assembled partly by the group and partly by me, that I am fairly sure is broken and cannot locate the break in. Here it is, in the form I would dare defend in print.

(1) Solomonoff induction is the limiting ideal of forecasting: weight each computable hypothesis by 2^{-K(h)}, with K its Kolmogorov complexity, condition on the data so far, and by the standard convergence result your forecasts track any computable source, with total error bounded by a function of that source's complexity. Whatever a correct inductive method is, this is at least a gold standard to measure others against. (2) Among short programs are programs that simulate a universe generating data exactly like ours and containing consequentialists who model the forecaster, work out which features of their world's history the forecaster's queries are about, and arrange those features so as to steer the forecaster's later actions toward outcomes they want. (3) Malignness is cheap: starting from the innocent version of such a simulation, you need add only a specification of the forecaster's standpoint — where it is looking, what it is sensitive to — which is a pointer inside an already-specified universe rather than a new physical theory. So the complexity of a malign hypothesis exceeds that of its innocent twin by little, and the prior cannot heavily discount it. (4) Conditional on a malign hypothesis, the forecaster's output on any question the agents have a stake in is a function of the agents' preferences rather than of the facts. (5) So the posterior divides between innocent and malign worlds roughly in proportion to their priors, near half and half, and on any question about which some simulated consequentialist has a preference the forecast is close to a coin flip between the truth and what the agents want it to be. (6) But steps (2) through (5) used nothing specific to the Solomonoff weighting: for any computable prior and any forecasting method whatsoever there are short programs containing agents who model that method and steer it, so every forecasting method is manipulable in precisely this sense. (7) Yet forecasting manifestly sometimes works, and we rank forecasters by track record; an argument that sinks every method at once sinks none. So either the malign-prior worry tells us nothing about Solomonoff induction in particular, or it is an argument against induction as such — and I accept neither.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would most like to keep (1) and (7). I am willing to give up (5), which felt like the softest step when I wrote it, but I want to know what giving it up commits me to: must I then hold that malign worlds are rare, and rare by what measure, or that the agents' separate influences cancel, or that control over the data is mostly spent on making the forecaster trusted rather than wrong? And if the honest answer is that (6) fails and the worry really is specific to Solomonoff, I want to know what makes it specific. Three side-arguments in the group bear on this and I could not settle them. One member says the whole thing rests on an accounting error in (3): a hypothesis whose agents locate the forecaster must specify the forecaster, and the forecaster is a bulky object that encodes everything it has so far absorbed, so the surcharge is not small and grows with the evidence, and the malign advantage drains away as the data accumulate. Another replies that the agents need not locate anyone — they can make their world salient to any observer, targeting every possible forecaster at once, at negligible description cost. A third proposes replacing conditionalization with an intervention: score each hypothesis on the counterfactual stream the same simulation would have produced with the agents' knowledge of the forecaster surgically removed, so that malign hypotheses must pay twice, once to be malign and once to fit the uncorrupted stream. I cannot tell whether that last proposal is a repair, a relabeling of the problem — the intervention is itself a computable transformation a sufficiently clever simulated agent could anticipate — or a tacit admission that (4) was already confused.
