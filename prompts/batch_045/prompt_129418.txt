I'm drafting a long post on the badness of pain for readers who are entirely at home in the valence / hedonic-tone idiom — people happy to say that suffering is the thing that matters, and who have absorbed the standard passing reference to pain asymbolia. I keep watching the dialectic stall in the same place. Someone introduces asymbolia to show that a pain's badness isn't just its sensory intensity; someone else points out that the patient still winces, still withdraws, still rates it eight out of ten and still asks for analgesia, so nothing has really come apart; the first person then says that she doesn't mind it, which just stipulates the disputed point. I want a case that gets past this by construction, and the central constraint is that no sentence in the vignette may say or imply that the pain is not unpleasant, not suffered, or not something she minds.

What I need is a scenario in one paragraph of at most 150 words, set somewhere ordinary — a dental practice, a physiotherapy clinic, a long flight — with no fictional neurotechnology, no described lesion, no invented syndrome, and with the asymbolia-like profile built entirely out of behavior, intensity ratings, and small real trades (a fee, a wait, a minor inconvenience) rather than out of questionnaire answers, so that a revealed-preference skeptic can't wave the choices away and nothing in the setup gives her a reason to perform indifference. In it, a subject is in pain, and the following three views must each return their verdict from their own machinery rather than from a line in the case:

(1) A sensory-intensity view: badness for the subject supervenes on how intense and attention-commanding the sensation is, together with its sensory-discriminative character. Required verdict: the pain IS bad for her.
(2) A motivational / functional view: badness consists in the state's producing occurrent aversion and avoidance — the whatever-it-is-that-makes-you-want-it-to-stop view. Required verdict: the pain IS bad for her.
(3) An attitudinal or prospective-cost view: badness consists in the state's being the object of a standing negative attitude of the subject's, the kind of thing that shows up in what she'd pay to prevent it, what she'd choose for her future self, and what she counts as a cost when she totals up how her life has gone. Required verdict: the pain is NOT bad for her, or is bad only in a sense that doesn't register in her wellbeing.

Two ways of satisfying this are banned as cheating. She may not be globally apathetic: the case must make her averse to, and willing to pay to avoid, at least two other kinds of unpleasant experience, and she must otherwise be a reliable reporter and a normal agent. And the narration may not tell us she doesn't mind. My own guess is that what has to do the work is a gap between what she'll trade to end the pain now and what she'll trade to prevent it later — occurrent motivational force without prospective aversion — but I'd rather you build it and show that it works than take that on trust from me. The use I'll put the case to is the claim that valence, as this audience uses the word, is doing at least two different jobs, occurrent motivational force and prospective aggregative cost, and that conflating them is what makes the asymbolia exchange deadlock.

After the scenario, in at most 500 words: give each of the three verdicts with its derivation from the view rather than from the vignette; name the structural feature of the case that is doing the work and show what happens to the three verdicts if it is removed in each direction, first restoring the prospective aversion and then also removing the occurrent motivation or spreading the indifference to the other experiences; give the strongest available reply for someone who holds (2), phrased as I should expect to meet it, and say whether it lands; and say plainly which part of the argument the case cannot carry and what I'll have to argue for separately. Nothing else beyond the scenario and that apparatus; total response under 800 words.
