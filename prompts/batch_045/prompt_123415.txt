I'm in a third-year seminar on social norms — we did the usual story about conventions as solutions to coordination problems a few weeks back and I never really got how any of that is supposed to turn into an actual *moral* obligation rather than just "people will think you're rude" — and I've got 2000 words due Friday on whether everyday conventions are moral obligations and whether visitors have to follow local ones. I've been going through cases by gut and I want the principle behind my verdicts, or a better one plus a note about which verdict I should drop.

My verdicts (all stuff I've actually been in or watched happen):

- Sit-down restaurant in a US city where it's understood that servers are paid on the assumption that tips make up most of their income. Leaving nothing: not just rude, actually wrong, and it feels closer to withholding owed money than to bad manners.
- Tip jar at a coffee counter where the barista is on a proper wage. Putting in nothing: completely fine, no obligation at all.
- Cutting in front of five people at a bus stop in a city where queueing is the norm: wrong.
- Boarding a train in a city where there genuinely is no queue — everyone stands in a scrum and boards by jostling, and drivers and other passengers all know this. Jostling like everyone else: not wrong. But a visitor on their first day who shoves past a seventy-year-old and knocks them off balance: wrong.
- Crossing on a red pedestrian signal at 1am with the street completely empty, in a city where nobody waits: not wrong, and I don't even want to call it rude.
- Crossing on a red in a city where drivers do not expect pedestrians to do that, so a car swerves and nearly hits someone: wrong.
- Keeping your backpack on in a packed rush-hour carriage in a city where everyone takes theirs off: rude, not wrong.
- Tipping 20% every day for three months at the same small café in a city with no tipping norm, as a visiting student: mildly bad, because it ratchets up what the staff and other customers come to expect. Doing the same thing once as a one-off tourist: fine, harmless.
- A visitor who genuinely has no idea that tipping is expected and stiffs a server: not blameworthy, and I want to say not wrong either — but the server is out money and I feel shifty saying it's fine.
- Somebody cuts in front of me in the queue back home and I say something: fine, maybe good. Same person, same behaviour, standing at the scrum train door in the no-queue city and telling people off for not queueing: not fine, obnoxious and a bit imperialist.
- Country where everyone tips but there's an active campaign to abolish tipping and a lot of the workers themselves want it gone. Not tipping there: fine, maybe even mildly good.

Two I can't settle: (a) the office where everyone puts money in for the cleaner at Christmas, everyone privately thinks it's demeaning and would rather the whole thing stopped, and everyone assumes everybody else is keen — pluralistic ignorance, my seminar leader would say. Tipping or not? I go back and forth several times an hour. (b) A long-stay visitor who just avoids restaurants and taxis and crowded trains entirely so they never have to deal with the local conventions at all. Free pass, or are they dodging something?

The rule I've been trying out, which I'm calling the four-lock rule: a convention binds you morally just in case (i) people there follow it and expect others to, (ii) someone is made worse off — properly worse off, not just mildly irritated — if you don't, (iii) you could have found out about it without much cost before acting, and (iv) complying doesn't itself make things worse overall. It handles some of the list and I think it breaks on others, but I can't tell which clause to repair or whether the whole shape is wrong. I also can't tell whether I need separate principles for payment-ish conventions (tipping), coordination conventions (queueing) and risk conventions (crossing), or whether one thing covers all three. And I keep noticing that I want visitors to *comply* with local conventions but not to *export* their own — which sounds incoherent, or at least like two different principles wearing one coat.

3. My undecided cases — ~200 words. Verdicts on (a) and (b), with reasons.
4. Two cases you generate — ~200 words. Two situations I haven't listed that your principle gives an answer to, at least one of which should be the kind of case where you'd expect the answer to be surprising.
5. What I have to give up — ~150 words. Which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate, and which one you'd overturn.
6. How confident you are — ~100 words. Which clause you'd defend hardest, which is the weakest joint, and what kind of case would make you change it.
