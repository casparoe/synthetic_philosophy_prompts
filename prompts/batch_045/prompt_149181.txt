I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy or math training beyond what the job happens to require, and I run a small fortnightly reading group at work — four of us, mostly engineers — where we slowly work through things none of us is qualified on. We recently spent two evenings on the axiom of choice, after a colleague used it as a punchline in a code review: someone had written a helper that picked a 'canonical' element out of every bucket in a collection, my colleague said you can't always do that, and none of us could say exactly what he meant. We got as far as the shoes-and-socks illustration — with infinitely many pairs of shoes you can get by on a rule (take the left of each pair), whereas with infinitely many pairs of genuinely indistinguishable socks there's nothing in the setup to pick by — and we understood that this only bites when the pairs never stop coming, since for any finite number of pairs you can always simply go on picking. One person also insisted that the socks case needs only the choice principle for a countable family of two-element sets, which is weaker than the axiom in its full generality; I'd rather not overclaim, so please keep that straight.

What I'd like is a villanelle — nineteen lines, two refrains, five tercets and a closing quatrain in the usual ABA pattern — written from inside that setup: in the voice of whoever is doing the picking, or in the voice of one of the socks, whichever you think works better. The constraints that matter to me are these. (1) The two refrains must be genuinely interchangeable: swapping them everywhere should change nothing the poem asserts, and nothing anywhere in the poem may break the symmetry of a pair — no left or right, no first or second, no colour or size or 'the one nearer the door', no quietly smuggled rule like 'the first my hand touched'. (2) The poem should keep telling us that a sock was taken from each pair, and never once tell us which: that non-constructiveness is the whole point, and a reader holding the finished poem should still have no way to reconstruct the selection the poem claims was made. (3) It should be honest about the finite/infinite cut — somewhere it needs to register that any finite stretch of this is easy, that the poem can always be finished, and that the trouble arrives only with the endlessness. (4) The closing quatrain, where the two refrains meet for the first and only time, should land as the realisation that their meeting hasn't selected anything. (5) No technical vocabulary anywhere in the verse — no 'axiom', 'choice function', 'well-ordering', 'Zorn', or anything of that kind — because I want to read it aloud to teammates who've never heard any of this and have it work as a poem on its own, while still being accurate enough that a set theorist wouldn't wince at it.

After the poem, add three or four sentences of prose — no more — telling me which line is doing which of the jobs above, so I can check I haven't been handed something that only sounds right.
