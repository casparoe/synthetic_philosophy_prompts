I do a podcast about ideas that wreck people's intuitions — I'm a science journalist, not a philosopher, so assume I already know the pop-level stuff (Pascal's mugging, the EA habit of multiplying huge stakes by tiny probabilities, the St Petersburg flavor of worry) and skip the 101.

Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends expected value fanaticism straight up: no discounting small probabilities, no cap on utility, no threshold below which you're allowed to ignore a payoff — if the expected value is positive at any nonzero probability, you take it. I've read enough to know his two stock moves: the arbitrariness jab (pick any cutoff and I'll ask why *there*, and there's no non-arbitrary answer) and the step-by-step argument where each individual trade looks obviously fine but the endpoint is handing your wallet to a mugger. Assume he's already made both, and don't let him just restate them.

Give me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. At least one on each of these:

- The audit problem. At the probabilities that do the work here, the numbers come out of models with no track record — so the view seems to relocate all the action into the probability estimate rather than the value. Bit of scaffolding for you: a fictional Office of Long Shots that funds whatever tops the speculative-EV league table, staffed by people who know they'll never live to learn whether they were right. Ask him what its budget should be, and how it audits models it can never validate.
- The portfolio point. One one-in-a-million shot at an astronomical payoff feels insane; ten thousand of them, pooled, looks like venture capital or a research portfolio and starts to feel sane. Does fanaticism get more plausible in basket form, and if so does that make the single-shot mugging intuition untrustworthy as evidence *against* the view?
- Negative fanaticism. Is it symmetric? A tiny chance of astronomically bad outcomes — does the same reasoning that drags you toward the upside also let a micro-probability of catastrophe dominate every ordinary choice and freeze us, or is there an asymmetry he can defend?
- The branch you actually land in. Take the long shot every time and you lose essentially always. What does the view say about the value of the 99.99999% world you burned the money in — does it commit him to a kind of indifference to what actually happens, and is that a bullet he bites or a cost he's been hiding?

Close with one question about what would count as evidence against the view — not "what if the mugger is lying," I mean what actual empirical or theoretical finding would make him give it up.
