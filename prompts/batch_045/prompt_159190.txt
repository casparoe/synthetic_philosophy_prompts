I am a senior at a large public high school, and I write the monthly back-page satire feature for our student newspaper. The issue after spring break follows our civics unit on distributive justice, and I would like to run a piece on the veil of ignorance and the difference principle. I would be grateful for a draft I can revise.

The form I have in mind is a press release, datelined a few years from now, announcing the launch of a business-software product: a platform that lets a company's leadership set pay and promotion policy "from behind the veil of ignorance," so that nobody has to know which job they will end up with. Headline and dateline, three or four paragraphs of launch copy, then five to seven frequently-asked-questions entries in the same voice. Roughly 700 words for the whole piece. The venue is my school paper, so keep it clean, and use an invented company, invented executives, and an invented beta customer rather than real people.

I would like the jokes to come from the actual machinery of the argument rather than from the phrase "veil of ignorance" itself. Some features I have sketched, which you should feel free to sharpen or replace:

An "appetite" setting, shipped locked at zero tolerance for upside risk, which the copy presents as a purely technical default;
A choice of comparison currency, with a preset for income and wealth and another for self-reported happiness, plus a footnote that auditors rarely accept the second one;
A population-scope toggle, where the base tier covers current full-time employees and the paid tiers add contractors, supply-chain workers, and hires not yet born;
A nightly recomputation of the reference group, which clients discover they can game by adding one extremely low-paid intern — the "Anchor" — so that every subsequent executive raise can be shown to raise the floor as well, by a few cents;
A "commitment guarantee" allowing anyone who lands in the bottom tenth to walk away, voided if the appetite setting was ever moved off zero.

The Anchor feature is the one I care most about. My understanding is that the difference principle is genuinely ambiguous between a reading on which each increment of inequality has to benefit the least advantaged and a reading on which the arrangement as a whole only has to beat the alternatives, and I want the launch copy to quietly rely on the second reading while insisting it is doing something rigorous. Please keep the piece deadpan throughout: no winking at the reader, no character who steps in to explain why any of this is objectionable, and no definitions of the theory. A classmate who has not read any of this should still enjoy it; someone who has should find it fair. Please also avoid direct quotations from any philosophical text — paraphrase instead, so that nothing can be misattributed.

After the piece, and clearly set off from it as an editor's note of about 200 words in my own voice rather than the company's, please do the following: separate what is standard doctrine from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material. In the settled column I would expect things like the veil itself, the use of a maximin-style decision rule behind it, the permission to have inequalities only on condition that they work to the benefit of the least advantaged, and the use of income-and-wealth-style goods rather than happiness as the yardstick. In the contested column I would expect at least the question whether the setup really generates the difference principle or quietly assumes it, the two readings of the benefit condition I mentioned, whether the yardstick is the right one and what it does to people with disabilities or expensive tastes, and whether the scheme's scope is a single society or everyone alive plus future generations. Then say plainly where the parody exaggerates or leans on a reading that not everyone accepts — I would rather the note admit this than let a classmate think the joke settled something. If I have got any of the doctrine wrong in my sketch above, correct me in the note rather than building the piece on my mistake.
