Hey, I'm hoping you can help me sort out something I've been going back and forth on for months: when is it actually ok to take someone else's word for a moral question instead of working it out for yourself? Some philosopher I half-remember thinks there's something specifically bad about moral deference as opposed to, say, deferring to a mechanic about your car, and I feel the pull of that, but my gut reactions on actual cases are all over the place. I'll list them and you tell me whether there's a principle here or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

1. My friend Priya has thought about animals for fifteen years and tells me eating meat is wrong. I can't reconstruct her argument, I just trust her. I stop eating meat. Verdict: I'd do it, but I feel like I'm missing something — I'd be embarassed to say out loud "I think it's wrong because Priya thinks so."

2. I used my roommate's bike without asking. She says "honestly, totally fine, I don't mind at all." Her saying it seems to *settle* it — not just give me evidence that it was fine, but make it fine. Verdict: deference here isn't second-best at all, it's authoritative.

3. Same bike, except she's away and her best friend tells me "she really wouldn't mind, I know her." Verdict: that's just evidence, it doesn't settle anything, I'd still owe her an apology. Which is odd, because the friend probably predicts her reaction about as well as she does herself.

4. A hospice nurse I work with tells me I'm weighting things wrong: in end-of-life cases the family just being in the room matters much more than I think it does, and less turns on the clinical facts than I assume. I defer. Verdict: totally fine, and after a month on the ward I start seeing it myself — the deference taught me to see something.

5. My brother's partner cheated on him. A chatbot gives me a sensible answer to "should I tell him?" (no — it's his relationship, you'll be cast as the villain, you're protecting yourself not him). A friend gives me word-for-word the same reasoning. Verdict: taking the friend's answer is fine; taking the chatbot's feels cheap and sort of fake, even though the chatbot is arguably drawing on way more moral experience than one friend has, and even though I'd be fine reading the identical sentence in an advice column by someone with a track record.

6. A well-run study convinces me that people in my demographic systematically underrate how much a certain kind of comment stings. So I now follow a rubric at work instead of my own read of each individual case, even when the rubric disagrees with what I'd have said. Verdict: not merely fine — refusing to do this feels like arrogance. Note this is pure bottom-line verdict-following with zero understanding attached, and it seems mandatory.

7. There's a custom in a community I've joined about who's allowed to speak at meetings, tied to seniority I don't really grasp. I just follow it. Verdict: fine, and insisting on arriving at my own verdict first would be a kind of arrogance. Again: bare verdict, no understanding, no problem.

8. My mentor says: "stop asking me — you have to work out for yourself whether to quit." Verdict: I should listen to her about this, and it doesn't feel self-undermining, but I can't tell whether that's a coherent response or whether I've just been told to stop doing something by someone I defer to.

9. I've deferred to my partner on basically every household fairness question for years. Each individual deference seemed fine at the time. But now I notice I have no grip on the topic at all, and that feels like a real loss that I never agreed to. Verdict: something went wrong here even though nothing went wrong on any particular occasion.

10. Deferring to what I told myself years ago about how to handle something (a promise I made to a relative who was dying) feels different in kind from deferring to a friend's advice: more binding, and barely like deference at all. Verdict: fine, and I suspect it isn't really the same phenomenon.

My best attempt at a principle: it's ok to defer about *how much* things matter, and it's ok to defer when it's the other person's own claim being waived, but it's not ok to defer on the bottom-line verdict of what to do. Cases 6 and 7 make that look dead on arrival, and case 1 sort of does too. So: is there a principle that gets all ten, or do I have to give one up — and if you have to choose between a messy principle that saves every verdict and a clean one that overturns a couple, which do you prefer and why?

Who, if anyone, should I defer to, and what does your principle say about *picking* someone to defer to when the candidates disagree?
