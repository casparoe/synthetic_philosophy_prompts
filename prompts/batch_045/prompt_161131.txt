I'm auditing a graduate reading group on personal identity and death (I'm not enrolled, I just sit in, and I write up a two-page response for each session) and we just did Epicurus on why death is nothing to us — the symmetry argument, the one the Roman poet Lucretius gives in his long poem explaining Epicurean philosophy: look back at the endless stretch of time before you were born, it caused you no distress, so why should the mirror-image stretch after you die? One of the PhD students in the group keeps leaning on this as an analogy, and not just as a rhetorical flourish: death is, he says, *just like* the time before you were born, and since nobody lies awake worrying about that, there is nothing here to worry about and nothing here to be bad for anybody. He uses it to close down the whole question, not just the fear.

I pushed back with two cases and now I'm not sure they are fair tests, so I want them stress-tested. (1) The gap in the middle: a woman is destroyed at 40 by a machine that scans her perfectly, and rebuilt from the scan at 70, with no experience in between but with her memories, her unfinished novel, her job to go back to. Her nonexistence is sandwiched between two stretches of existence. Is that gap like the prenatal gap, like the posthumous gap, like both, or like neither — and does the answer change if she knew about the gap in advance and consented to it? (2) The shift case: compare being created ten years earlier than you actually were with dying ten years earlier than you actually will. Everyone in the room said the first isn't a benefit to anyone and the second is a harm, but on the symmetry argument both comparisons look equally empty, since in each case there is no subject at the relevant time. My flatmate (who is not in the group and heard this over dinner) said the shift case just smuggles in the answer by assuming there is someone there to be benefited in the dying case and not in the being-created case — and I think she might be right, but I also think that might kind of be the point rather than a bug.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the subject, of the stretch of time, of the thing that is supposed to be bad, of the person whose attitude is supposed to settle whether it's bad, and of the mirror in the mirror-image claim. Then where the mapping breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for whether death can be bad for the person who dies from the ones that are artifacts of how we happened to set up the comparison. I especially want to know what the prenatal case quietly imports: that the gap is finite and ends in existence, that it isn't preceded by a life with projects and expectations, that there was nothing yet that could be frustrated or disappointed or owed anything. Does the conclusion survive the audit — does it's just like before you were born really carry the weight he is putting on it, or was the analogy doing the arguing the whole time? If it doesn't carry it, is there a better comparison to put in its place (dreamless sleep? a project you abandon? being unconscious through something you desperately wanted to attend?) or is arguing by analogy the wrong move here definately? I'll fill in any references myself, so please don't bother with citations — I want the machinery, not the reading list.
