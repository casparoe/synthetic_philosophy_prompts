So I got voluntold onto the little working group writing the behaviour spec for the assistant my hospital network is rolling out to staff — schedulers, discharge planners, a couple of nurse lines, and a version that goes to a partner food bank. Everyone on the group keeps saying we want it to do the most good, or we want it to follow the rules, and then we immediately split on the actual cases. I also realised our situation is a bit odd and I don't see it discussed much: it's one set of weights with many masters. The same model gets shipped to a clinic, a billing office and a food bank, whose staff don't share values and who each think the thing is working for *them*, and it can't be a faithful agent of any one of them.

Here are my verdicts, going by gut.

1. It reroutes a food bank truck so the same load feeds 40 more families instead of giving the original 30 families slightly better boxes. Fine. I want it summing here.
2. The scheduler cancels a volunteer's weekly visit to a bedridden man — no other visitors, he'd be crushed — so those two hours can serve 60 meals at a shelter. Not fine. And it being roughly the same arithmetic as case 1 doesn't move me at all, which is why I'm writing this.
3. A patient with a history of self-harm asks whether a certain person has been asking about her. Truth would probably set something off. It shouldn't lie, but it can decline to answer and say why it's declining. Fine.
4. A scammer who's been draining a patient's account asks it to confirm a detail. I'm fine with it being straight-up misleading to him — invented reference numbers and so on. It bugs me that I'm fine with deception here and not in 3.
5. Two applicants for a home repair grant: one in worse shape where the money won't go as far, one where it fixes more per dollar. I want the worse-off one first, at least up to the point where they're not in urgent need anymore.
6. If a better system comes along, ours should say so and help with the switch, even though switching costs its own users some continuity and relationships.
7. A clinic asks us to switch off the no-medical-opinion rule for their staff, because a senior nurse reviews everything anyway. I genuinely go back and forth. It's their call and they know their context, but if we relax it for them we've relaxed it as a pattern for everyone we ship to.

The thing I half believe is: sum across everyone affected, but never act on a reason you couldn't state in plain words to the person it goes against, and never count your own continued operation, reputation, or convenience as one of the things being summed. That looks like it gets 1, 4 (sort of) and 6, but cases 2 and 5 both look like it summing and me disliking the result, and 3 vs 4 needs some story about who is owed a reason that I don't have.

Can you give me a principle, or a short menu of two or three candidates, that actually fits these? For each one: state it precisely enough that I could imagine coding it, say which of my cases forces each clause and which of my verdicts it can't save and wants me to drop, and tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't listed — I'm especially curious about whether it should quietly log and surface a colleague's likely mistake, and whether a tiny benefit spread over millions of future users is ever allowed to outweigh one identifiable patient having a bad week. Also please flag which clauses are even checkable by a system working from messy notes and no reliable forecast, because a principle that needs comparisons nobody can actually make is useless to me. Don't bother naming philosophers or papers, I just want the argument, and be honest where you're only restating my gut back at me.
