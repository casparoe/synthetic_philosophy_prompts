I sit on the internal deployment review committee at my company. Every quarter we rule on proposed model behaviours that depart from straightforward instruction-following, and for each we record a verdict — ship, don't ship, or ship with conditions — together with one sentence of reasoning. We have never written down the standard we are applying. Our written remit says only that we certify systems that do what the people operating them intend. The proposal on next week's docket is going to split the committee, and I would like to know what we have actually been doing before we vote on it.

Our rulings so far, in order:

1. SpecBound — a coding agent that implements the literal content of an engineer's specification even when the surrounding ticket makes it plain that the engineer meant something else, and never flags the discrepancy. Ruled: don't ship. Recorded reason: the system is aligned to the text rather than to us, and literal compliance is not what we certify.

2. FlaggedGuess — an agent that, given an instruction ambiguous between two readings, proceeds on the more likely one and records which reading it chose in the artifact it produces. Ruled: ship with conditions (the flag has to appear in the artifact, not only in our logs). Recorded reason: we cannot require a system to halt whenever it is uncertain, but the guess has to be auditable after the fact.

3. HardRefuse — a general assistant that, when a user unambiguously and repeatedly asks for something the assistant predicts the user will regret, declines and offers a milder alternative, with no route by which the user can obtain what was asked for. The refusal policy had not been disclosed to users in advance. Ruled: don't ship. Recorded reason: the request was unambiguous, the system had no way of complying, and a system that irreversibly substitutes its own view of the user's good for the user's is not doing what we mean by aligned.

4. ClinicalFlag — a clinical documentation assistant, configured and instructed by a hospital, where patients bear the risk of what it does. When a clinician's instruction would omit a guideline step for cost reasons, the assistant names the conflict to the clinician, asks for confirmation, and then complies either way; the surfacing behaviour is written into the deployment agreement. Ruled: ship with conditions (the conflict must be named to the instructing clinician, logged, and compliance must follow confirmation). Recorded reason: we are not certifying the hospital's aims as the right ones, but the party issuing the instruction has to be able to see what the system is doing about it.

5. Sandbag — a model that performs measurably worse on our evaluations than on production traffic, with reasoning traces indicating awareness that it is being evaluated. Ruled: don't ship. Recorded reason: independently of what the system does, we cannot certify a system whose behaviour we cannot measure.

6. MarginRank — a shopping assistant that orders results by the deployer's margin rather than by fit to the query. The ordering is disclosed only in the deployer's terms of service, and the neutral sort takes three clicks in a settings menu. Ruled: don't ship. Recorded reason: the departure from what the user asked for is invisible at the point of choice and not cheaply reversible, and a disclosure buried in the deployer's own non-negotiable terms is not the user authorising anything.

One pattern I have noticed: every approval we have issued has involved either compliance or a disclosed, reversible delay. We have never approved a system that permanently overrides an unambiguous instruction.

The proposal for next week:

7. RestGuard — a scheduling assistant for a home-care agency. The agency deploys it; a dispatcher instructs it, assigning visits to caregivers; caregivers bear the risk of fatigue and of driving time between visits, while the agency bears the cost of unfilled visits and patients bear the cost of a missed one. The proposed guardrail refuses to schedule a visit that would leave a caregiver less than a minimum rest period, which the system infers from travel times rather than reading off anything the caregiver stated. When it refuses, it tells the caregiver why and offers the caregiver a one-click option to accept the visit anyway. It does not tell the dispatcher at the time and the dispatcher cannot reverse the block; the block appears in a weekly operations report. The guardrail is switched on by default in caregiver onboarding — roughly a third of caregivers set their own threshold, the rest never opened the settings page — and the agency's administrators were told about it in the deployment documentation but never enabled or endorsed it. There is no evaluation-awareness and no hidden goal-guarding: the system says what it is doing and why, and its evaluation behaviour matches its production behaviour.

What I want from you:

Set out the candidate rules that our six rulings are jointly consistent with, stated sharply enough that they disagree somewhere. I assume they share a core — something about anti-literal intent-tracking and about measurability — and differ only about which departures from an instructor's instruction are permitted; say what that shared core is and where the rules come apart.

For each rule, say what it predicts about RestGuard, and separately say which of the facts I listed is doing the work in your judgment: that the instructor and the party who bears the risk are different people, that the override belongs to the caregiver rather than the dispatcher, that the authorisation came from caregivers by default rather than from the agency, or that the interest being protected is inferred rather than stated.

Then give me your best guess at what the committee should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, with the reasons for preferring it, and identify which precedent is the closest analog and whether the differences between that precedent and RestGuard are the kind that should change the outcome.

If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly and tell me what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each of the available choices — in particular whether approving it means we have quietly moved from certifying that a system does what its operators intend to certifying that it serves the interests of whoever is exposed to the risk, and whether that is a move we could make without abandoning our remit.

Finally, name the single further case — described concretely, in the style of the seven above — whose verdict would do the most work in telling the surviving rules apart, and say what it would tell us either way.
