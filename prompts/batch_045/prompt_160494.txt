So I basically have the ring right now and it's messing with my head. I run billing for a small company, and a few months ago I noticed that one particular kind of refund credit never gets reconciled against anything. I could skim a few hundred a month for years and nobody would ever check, and honestly I dont think I'd feel much either way afterwards. I went back to theRepublic book 2 material I read in college, and my roommate (philosophy PhD, insufferable about it) built me the argument below, which I think is meant as a proof that Glaucon is right.

(1) A consideration is a real reason for me to do something only if it would still be a reason for me to do it in the worst case for doing it — when doing it costs me everything I care about and gets me nothing anyone can see.

(2) The ring is exactly that worst case for justice: full impunity, reputation held fixed, and by the terms of the setup the unjust life is the one that goes better for the person living it.

(3) So if anything is a real reason for me to be just, it is a reason that still holds when being just costs me everything and pays me nothing.

(4) Nothing can be that kind of reason. The usual candidates all fail the test: that being just pays off, that it makes people trust you, that you would agree to it from behind a veil of ignorance, that it is what any rational agent could will universally, that it is what God commands. The first two are payoffs. The rest are either bare assertions or facts about what I want or would want, and facts about what I want are not reasons.

(5) So there is no real reason for me to be just. The only honest answer to why be moral is: when it pays, and not otherwise. Which is Glaucon's conclusion, and exactly what my bank account wants to hear.

(6) But now run (1) on reasons for belief. I have a real reason to believe what my evidence supports only if that is still a reason when believing the truth would ruin my life. The same candidates die the same way. So there are no real epistemic reasons either — no reason to believe anything except when believing it pays. And that is crazy; nobody actually thinks that.

(7) So the thing proves too much and it breaks somewhere, but I can't see where.

Of the claims in it, the one I'd most want to keep is the thought that I can have a real reason to do something even in a case where ignoring it would go better for me — that seems like the whole point of having reasons at all. I'd much rather give up something else than that. But I also don't want to hand Glaucon the win if I can avoid it, and I'd rather you didn't just tell me Socrates has an answer about the soul and leave it there, because I have never understood that move.
