I'm part of a small reading group working through twentieth-century and postcolonial historiography — source criticism, the Annales school, narrative-constructivist and subaltern approaches — and one sentence keeps doing an enormous amount of unacknowledged work in our discussions: "history is written by the victors." I want to stop either brandishing it or batting it away, so I'd like a precisification menu. (I don't need its provenance: I've seen it pinned on half a dozen people and trust none of the attributions.)

The occasion was an argument about the Spanish conquest of central Mexico. I said something like: of course our picture of 1519–1521 is slanted, the Mexica lost and the surviving documentary base is overwhelmingly Spanish, so we should treat the received narrative as one side's account. Two things came back at me. First, that on that line of thought I was committed to there being no fact of the matter about what happened — a general relativism about the historical past — which I don't think I ever signed up for. Second, that as a generalization about who writes history the sentence is simply false: the losers of the American Civil War largely wrote the popular history of it for roughly the eighty years that followed, in schoolbooks, monuments, and fiction. Both replies felt aimed at a different sentence than the one I meant, but I can't say which reading each of them requires.

So: lay out the distinct precise claims the line could be making, arranged as a menu rather than a single favored gloss. I've been grouping them crudely as (i) claims about who produces, preserves, and destroys the evidence, (ii) claims about which story gets circulated, funded, and believed once several accounts exist, and (iii) claims about whether there is a determinate past that a story could get right. But I also suspect a fourth family: claims about power fixing which questions, categories, and contrasts count as historical in the first place — who decides what is an event, what is a cause, what counts as a serious alternative — and I want to know whether that is genuinely distinct or just (i) in different clothes.

For each reading, give me: a statement precise enough to be true or false; whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical claim that is defensible, a substantive claim that is probably false, or a philosophical thesis that is contested; what "victors" would have to mean for it to hold (the side that won the war, the side that ended up holding the archives and the schools, or something else); and, most important, whether that reading would actually license either move in my argument — my inference that the received account of the conquest is partisan and needs discounting, or my interlocutor's charge that I owe you a relativism about the past. If a reading licenses both, or neither, say so explicitly.

Two points I especially want addressed. First, whether the strongest version eats itself: if a reading says that every account of the past is just an expression of power, does anyone retain the ability to treat the fact that an account is the victors' as a reason to doubt it, or does debunking need a contrast case that the strong reading denies us? Second, the asymmetry of deployment: in practice the line seems to be aimed only at narratives that flatter the winners, and never used to flag the perspectives missing from a record the winners never bothered to keep. Is that an accident of usage, or is it built into some readings and not others?

Please keep the verdicts calibrated. Separate what is settled among people who actually work on this — historians and philosophers of history — from what is contested, and don't smooth a live dispute into a consensus. Where a reading turns on a contested empirical claim about one of my two cases, flag it rather than assert it, and mark anything that is your own inference rather than something the field has settled. A numbered list or table of readings is fine; I want the disambiguation, not an essay. And no reading lists or citations — I'm after the claims, not the literature.
