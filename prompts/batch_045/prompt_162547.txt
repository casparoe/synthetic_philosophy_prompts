Next month I am defending a chapter of my dissertation on slurs in a mixed philosophy of language workshop, and I would like a Q&A prep sheet written for that specific room rather than for philosophers of language in general.

The chapter's thesis concerns reclamation. I argue that reclamation is best modeled as a change in what I call a word's standing-norm rather than a change in its meaning, in its expressive or conventional-implicature content, or in its prohibition status. Roughly: a slur carries a target, the group it ranges over; a derogatory appraisal; and a norm fixing who is in a position to settle what the word does on a given occasion. Reclamation, on my account, is the target group's acquisition of the third component: the group comes to hold the authority to determine when the word may be used and to what effect, and the appraisal component becomes downstream of that authority rather than fixed independently of it. The payoff I advertise is an explanation of the case the standard views handle badly: an out-group speaker who uses a reclaimed slur warmly, accurately, and with no contempt at all, in a way the intended audience still treats as a serious offense but which no content-based account can classify as derogatory. I also lean on two cases I take to be underexplored: pejoratives whose force depends on a stereotype locally shared in the source community and which simply does not travel when the word is borrowed into another language; and terms whose target group no longer exists, where the offense seems to survive everyone who could conceivably have authorized the use.

The room: about fifteen people. Roughly half are formal semanticists and pragmaticists who default to treating derogation as a compositional or conventional-implicature phenomenon; two or three hold that slurs semantically encode a pejorative property; one or two are prohibitionists who think the correct story is that the word is banned and nothing more; two are sociolinguists working on stance, indexicality and enregisterment who will want to know what empirical work my notion of authority is doing; and there is one person working on speech-act approaches to silencing and autonomy who is sympathetic to the political upshot of my view but suspicious of anything that looks like meaning.

What I want:

1. Eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it rather than in neutral philosophical English. Where one underlying worry recurs across factions, I want to hear the pejorative-content theorist's version, then the prohibitionist's version, then the sociolinguist's version, so I can hear how the same objection changes shape.

2. For each: the reply I should give in about ninety seconds, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft.

3. What I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, so that I do not look like I am overclaiming.

4. The single reply that has to land for the chapter to survive at all.

5. The objection I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it concerns either bootstrapping — if acquiring standing is itself accomplished by using the word without standing, the account looks circular — or dissent within the target group, if the authority I appeal to has no agreed holder. Tell me which is worse, and give me the sharpest version of it.

6. The weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
