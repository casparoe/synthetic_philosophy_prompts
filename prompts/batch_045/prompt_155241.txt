I am writing a paper on whether the Born rule problem is a real cost for the many-worlds interpretation, and I have reconstructed an argument — partly mine, partly my supervisor's — that I have come to think overreaches. I would like a diagnosis of which step fails, why it looked sound, and what each way out costs.

Setup: a measurement on a superposition with amplitudes α₁, …, αₙ, repeated N times. The Born rule says that in a long run the relative frequencies approach |αᵢ|².

(1) In the many-worlds interpretation every sequence of outcomes permitted by the amplitudes is realized in some branch. So the Born rule cannot be the claim that only Born-typical sequences occur. It must be the claim that Born-typical sequences are the typical ones — that the branches whose statistics match |αᵢ|² carry almost all of whatever measure is doing the predictive work.

(2) For the Born measure this is true, and it is just mathematics. Amplitudes multiply across trials, so the Born weight of a length-N sequence is the product of the single-trial Born weights; by the strong law of large numbers, the set of sequences whose frequencies approach |αᵢ|² has Born measure tending to 1 as N grows.

(3) But nothing in (2) is specific to the Born measure. Take any single-trial weighting w and form the product measure over sequences. The same law says that typical sequences, by that measure, have frequencies w. Two rivals make the point concrete. First, the counting measure: treat all branches equally, and typical sequences show uniform frequencies 1/n. Second, the quartic measure: weight each branch by |αᵢ|⁴, renormalized; because amplitudes multiply this is again a product measure, and its typical sequences show |αᵢ|⁴-frequencies. An agent who tracked either rival would observe non-Born statistics, and nothing in the concentration story has any complaint to register.

(4) Therefore concentration of measure cannot be what singles out the Born measure. Whatever makes squared amplitude the right weights, it is not the fact that Born-typical branches swamp the rest by Born measure.

(5) And if the measure has to be imported rather than fixed by the physics of branching, then what the many-worlds interpretation actually predicts is that agents see w-statistics for whatever measure w they happen to track. That is compatible with any statistics whatsoever. The Born rule is then not derived but postulated in the form the measure that matters is the Born measure, and the Born rule problem is unsolved.

(6) Here is why I think the argument proves too much. Run it in any theory where every nomologically possible outcome is realized somewhere — a spatially infinite Boltzmannian universe, or eternal inflation. It says that typicality carries no predictive content until a measure over observers is supplied, and that nothing in the dynamics supplies one. That conclusion I accept; it is, as far as I can tell, just the cosmological measure problem, and a real problem. But the Everettian case looks structurally identical: a multiplicity of realized outcomes, and a measure needed before predictions can be extracted. So if the argument is sound in cosmology, it should be sound here, and the many-worlds interpretation inherits a cosmological-style measure problem of its own. I do not want to accept (5), both because I think the interpretation does better than that and because I cannot see what would be left of the Born-rule literature if (5) were true.

My supervisor says (3) quantifies over measures that were never candidates. Amplitudes combine Pythagorean-style: a branch of amplitude α that decomposes into orthogonal sub-branches of amplitudes β and γ satisfies |α|² = |β|² + |γ|². Any measure over branches that depends only on amplitude and is additive under such decompositions must be proportional to |α|² — the quartic rival violates additivity, the counting measure violates it flagrantly, and Gleason-type uniqueness theorems are the heavy version of the same point. I have two worries about this. The additivity requirement looks like a normative principle about rational concern, something like it should not matter how finely a branch is carved into sub-branches, and not like a physical fact; so the Born rule would be derived partly from a claim about rationality, and I do not know whether that is a cost or a virtue. And if additivity is the disanalogy with cosmology, it had better be true that cosmology has no analogue of the Pythagorean structure and no canonical decomposition to be indifferent among. I am not confident of that.

A third possibility was put to me: the decision-theoretic derivations are what close the gap left by (4). But those seem to deliver, at best, that a rational agent should weight branches Born-wise in deliberation, and I do not see why deliberative weights should also be the typicality measure that fixes what observers see, which is the work (1) needs done.

So: which step do you reject? Explain why the step looked fine, and price the alternatives — including the price of the option I am drawn to, which is to give up (1) and with it the identification of empirical content with typicality. If I have to choose, I would rather keep the cosmological half of (6) than keep (5), and I would rather give up (1) than give up (2) or (3), which look to me like straight mathematics. I would also like to know, if the correct move is the additivity move, whether the Everettian has thereby answered the Born rule problem or merely stated its solution in a new vocabulary.
