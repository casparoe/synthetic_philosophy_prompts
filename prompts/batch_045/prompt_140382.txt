I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas, mostly archaeology and the human sciences for the last decade or so — and on Saturday I'm giving the opening keynote at a small invitation-only workshop outside Oslo on inferring institutions and beliefs from objects. 40 minutes, 15 for questions, it's on the record and being podcasted, and I'm filing a piece about the conference afterwards, so I can't exactly torch the room. I also can't footnote anything (no slides, it's a talking keynote) and I won't have time to chase anything down before Saturday, so please: no reading lists, no citations, no "you should really engage with so-and-so's 2011 paper."

The room, from the participant list: two senior settlement archaeologists who work on household assemblages and craft production and think every inference should be bolted down to formation processes; one post-processual / symmetrical-archaeology type who thinks the whole vocabulary of "belief" is a modern, protestant interiorization; a museum curator and a repatriation liaison who sit together and will definitely say something; a quantitative person who maintains a database of tens of thousands of assemblages and will want to know how to test any of this; a materials scientist who will want to talk about soil chemistry; and a scattering of PhD students who are mostly terrified of the senior people. One of the senior people has definately already read something I wrote that annoyed them.

Here's the thesis I'm defending, in rough form. Two claims, both of which I arrived at from reporting rather than from the literature, and both of which I've given my own dumb names.

First, the contestation rule: the materialization of a belief tracks how contested the belief is, not how deeply it's held. You don't build, carve, deposit, or standardize something to signal what literally everyone around you already assumes; you do it when the assumption is under stress or being asserted against someone. So the surviving record is systematically biased toward anxious belief and away from confident belief — the most deeply held things in a community are close to invisible.

Second, the repair signature: institutions show up in the record through their maintenance and failure debris — mends, replacements, discarded broken versions, the boring stuff in the middens — not through their prestige objects. The good stuff is what gets deposited on purpose, which is exactly when it stops being evidence of ordinary practice.

I'll open with a composite case: three river-valley sites, first millennium BCE, thousands of small fired-clay cones and perforated discs, most of them broken in midden fill, a handful in doorway deposits. It's a composite of three sites I've reported on, so don't hold me to the details. Standard readings are accounting tokens, gaming pieces, loom weights, and offerings. My reading is that the doorway deposits are the anxious, assertive, contested fraction and the midden scatter is what practice looked like when nobody was watching — and that everyone's been reading the anxious 2% as the religion of the whole community.
