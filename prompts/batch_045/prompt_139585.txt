hey — I help put together the in-house magazine at a foreign-policy think tank and we're doing a humour issue, so I need one short satire piece. About 700 words.

Form: an internal bureaucratic document — something like "Form 27-B: Application for an Approved Historical Analogy," issued by an Office of Precedent Allocation to staff who have already decided what they want to do and now need a history-shaped justification for it. Numbered clauses, deadpan administrative voice, no winking, no narrator stepping in to explain the joke. End with a worked example: the Office processes one invented crisis (say, an island city under blockade by a much larger neighbour — please keep it fictional, I really don't want to litigate a real case) and issues it an analogy. The applicant should ask for one thing and get issued something subtly unhelpful.

The bit that matters is where the laughs come from. I don't want invented problems with analogies; I want the real ones, made visible by the paperwork:

- the catalogue is tiny — the Office only stocks a handful of approved precedents, so the menu of thinkable policies is about six items long and everyone pretends that's the whole space
- analogies get picked on surface similarity and then quietly treated as evidence about causal structure
- any single case can be made to yield the opposite lesson depending on which features you code as background — so the form should have a checkbox for "which parts of the precedent count as scenery"
- the reference class is contested and the form has to paper over it (is this one instance or forty, and who decides membership)
- the word "lesson" implies history instructs, when the cases aren't repeatable and nobody ran the control
- the structural kind — the rising-power/established-power one people call the Thucydides trap — quietly deletes agency, turning choices into consequences of relative position, so the Office should love it, because it makes the memo's recommendation look like a law of nature rather than a decision

Procedures I want in there, roughly: an Approved Precedent Catalogue of about six entries; a Lesson Extraction Ratio (units of policy per unit of history); a mandatory Disanalogy Disclosure Schedule that nobody reads and which is capped at one page; a "no double-analogy rule" allowing exactly one analogy per crisis, justified in the text as a consistency requirement but which in practice forbids noticing that the case is also like the opposite one; and — this is the part I care about most — a double surcharge for second-order analogies, where the Office has to process both "this is another [X]" and the automatic reply "they always say it's another [X]," and charges twice because both moves are drawn from the same small repertoire.

The last clause should be the one a historian would call fair: the Office admitting, in its own language, that analogies do no predictive work at all but are nonetheless mandatory, because a memo with no precedent in it reads as arbitrary. Funny if you've never thought about any of this, and not cheap if you have. No real quotes from anything, no footnotes, and don't name any real politician or living person.
