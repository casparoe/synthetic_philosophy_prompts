Hey — I'm in a Behavioral Decision Making course this term and the professor has let me run one 75-minute section on my own: 18 undergrads, mostly econ and psych majors, nobody with any formal decision theory. Topic is satisficing and bounded rationality. She said no lecture and no slides beyond a one-page handout, so I need an actual activity, not an explanation.

What I want them to feel rather than be told: that stopping early can be the *rational* move instead of a character flaw, and that whether it is depends on the shape of the environment, not on some trait of the agent. And — the bit I care most about — I want them to run into the gap between doing well by the scoreboard and feeling fine about it. My guess is the people who stop early will end up with more points and feel worse about it, and I want that to be something they notice on their own, not something I announce.

Some constraints I've been turning over, which maybe you can tell me are wrong:

- Two separate costs: one for finding a new option (search) and one for finding out how good an option actually is (inspection). I want inspection expensive enough that they sometimes have to settle for a cheap proxy signal — I think satisficing only gets interesting there, and I don't want that to get flattened out.
- Options should arrive over real time, one per round, so the deadline is something they feel in their gut rather than a number on a page.
- Different tables get different underlying distributions — one flat-ish, one where a couple of great options are buried in a lot of mediocre ones, one where the proxy correlates with real quality and one where it basically doesn't — and nobody knows which one they're sitting in.
- Everyone writes down an aspiration level before anything starts, and they can revise it during play but each revision costs something, so they can feel the pull of moving the goalposts when the search goes badly.

What I need: materials and the handout text, exact rules, a payoff scheme in points (no real money, index cards and a timer only), a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the satisficing story — setting the aspiration level, the stopping rule, search versus inspection cost, adaptation of the aspiration — and say what to do with the fact that different tables lived in different worlds. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone tries to game the proxy, or the whole room is done in 20 minutes, or one table just refuses to stop), how I should handle each, and — honestly — which part of bounded rationality this game can't stage at all and that I'll have to just say myself at the end. Also give me one thing I can look at or ask at the very end that tells me whether it landed.

One last thing: please don't just hand me the secretary problem in a different hat. I've seen that one and it's about optimal stopping when you know the arrival structure up front, which feels like almost the opposite of the point here.

Oh, and write it in American English — half my section is international students and I dont want them picking up my British spellings.
