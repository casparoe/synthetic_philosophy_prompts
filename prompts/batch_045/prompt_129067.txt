I'm writing the "state of the question" section of a dissertation prospectus on the special composition question — the question, in the form Peter van Inwagen gave it in Material Beings, of under what conditions a plurality of things composes something. I don't need another tour of the options; I need a diagnosis of why this debate has gone in circles for decades.

The three families of answer are, as I read them: composition occurs never, or almost never, for inanimate pluralities (there are elementary particles arranged chairwise, but no chairs — organisms being the usual exception); composition occurs always, so that any plurality whatsoever, however scattered or gerrymandered, has a fusion; and composition occurs under some nontrivial and non-vague condition — the xs are caught up in a single life, or bonded in the right kind of way, or it is simply a brute matter which pluralities happen to compose. The pressures on each are equally well rehearsed: the argument from vagueness, on which any restricted condition would generate borderline cases of composition and it cannot be vague whether something exists, seems to force a choice between always and never; each extreme then draws the charge of being unmotivated or incredible, which pushes back toward a moderate view; and the moderate view is charged with arbitrariness. Everyone on every side has known all of this for a long time, and nothing moves.

My hypothesis is that the standoff is held in place by an unmarked equivocation about what composition is supposed to be for. Call it the role-fragmentation hypothesis: the question appears to have one answer because it is asked in one vocabulary, but it is really three questions, and each family of answer is tuned to a different one. There is the bookkeeping role — supplying objects to be values of our variables, so that whatever plurality we can talk about can be summed over. There is the causal-unity role — marking where the world contains a genuinely unified center of causal power, a whole that can do something its parts cannot. And there is the descriptive-explanatory role — marking which pluralities our best theories and ordinary practices must treat as a single thing. My suspicion is that universalism is close to the right answer to the first question, the organism-style restriction close to the right answer to the second, and something pragmatic or brute close to the right answer to the third; and further, that no single relation can serve more than one of these roles, because a relation permissive enough for bookkeeping cannot be selective enough for causal unity, and one selective enough for causal unity will look unmotivated when asked to track explanatory practice. Call that the incompatibility conjecture.

Here is the test I would use on any diagnosis. Suppose our best fundamental physics turned out to be stated over something like a continuum or a global quantum state in which no decomposition of any region is privileged, every candidate decomposition is on a par, and no mereological structure appears anywhere in the formalism — call this mereological flatness. Would that settle anything, and what does your diagnosis predict about how each camp should react to it?

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and nothing after the fifth: (1) your verdict on what kind of disagreement this is; (2) the strongest single reason to think it is substantive rather than a clash over the word composition or over three different questions sharing a name; (3) your assessment of the role-fragmentation hypothesis and the incompatibility conjecture, including which role, if any, is doing the real work; (4) what mereological flatness would and would not settle, given your diagnosis; (5) what would count as progress here, or why nothing should be expected to.
