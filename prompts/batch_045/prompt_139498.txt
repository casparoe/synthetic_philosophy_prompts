I've been having this argument with a friend about privacy and I cant shake the feeling that we keep talking past each other, so I want the philosophical version of what's going on underneath three setups I've actually lived under.

Setup A: in the US, a surprising amount of what the state knows about you is just public — property records, campaign donations with your name and employer on them, court filings, mugshots, and in a lot of states the voter file with your home address and which elections you turned up to. The law's basic move is that once information has been handed to a third party (a bank, a phone company) you don't really have a privacy interest in it anymore. At the same time the US has never created a national ID card, and that's supposed to come from the same suspicion of government.

Setup B: in Germany the constitutional court killed the 1983 census and spun a right to informational self-determination out of it. Data protection there runs on purpose limitation — data collected for one named job can't be quietly reassigned to another job, registers are supposed to be deleted rather than accumulated, and there is a deep allergy to any central register of everyone.

Setup C: in China, real-name registration for phone and internet accounts, public blacklists of people who ignore court judgments with travel restrictions attached, and local schemes that score residents on behavior. The design assumption seems to be that being visible and rated is what makes people act decently.

What I want from you:

1. For each setup, what is it actually presupposing — about privacy, or about the citizen-state relation, or about what makes someone free? I know the menu of options (privacy as limited access, as control over information, as keeping information in its proper context, as dignity or status, as not being dominated), so don't just label them; say which one each practice needs, and where a practice only looks like it needs one but would run fine on two or three different theories.
2. Consistency check within setups and between them: how can a society that publishes its citizens' addresses and mugshots also be the one that refuses a national ID? And is there any principled line between setup A and setup C, or is the difference just scale, aggregation, and who gets to see the result?
3. Three cases I made up to test the theories, and I want them used, not skipped:
   - The benign file: the state holds a complete dossier on you, guaranteed never to leak, never to be used to deny you anything, used only to route benefits to you automatically. Does the German-style objection survive with all the risk stripped out? If it does, the right isn't about expected harm, so tell me what it is about; if it doesn't, say so plainly.
   - The prediction: the dossier contains things about you that you don't know about yourself — that you will get sick, reoffend, drop out. A control-based account looks strange here, since you can't control information you don't have. Does that sink control accounts, or does it show the real objection was never about control?
   - Three kinds of opacity I've been playing with: temporal (data expires), compartmental (data stays in its lane), and relational (the state can't know this even though your neighbors can, or the other way round). Which of the three is setup B really protecting? Can a system have two and not the third, and would that be good enough?
4. The asymmetry claim — that the state should be transparent to the citizen while the citizen stays opaque to the state. Is that coherent, or does it smuggle in a story about the state being the citizen's agent? Does publishing the scoring code count as satisfying the openness half? And concretely, what would each of the three setups have to change if it took the claim seriously?

Please don't give me a vibes-y three-country comparison. I want the implicit theory reconstructed, the strict presuppositions separated from the ones that merely fit, and the places where the practice is underdetermined flagged as underdetermined — including if my three-way distinction collapses or misses a fourth kind of opacity.
