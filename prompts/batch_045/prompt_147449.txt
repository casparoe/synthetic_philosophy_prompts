I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of induction, and I keep hitting the same wall in the last third of the term. My students can state the formal results perfectly: that if you average over a suitably symmetric class of possible worlds or objective functions, every learning method does exactly as well as every other one, including the method that predicts the exact opposite; that a predictor that has been right on every day so far has a licensed twin that has been wrong on every day so far, and that over the whole class of admissible futures the two records are mirror images. They can also state Hume's point about the uniformity of nature. And none of it moves them. They leave the room still treating the method that has worked as the method that is supported by its record, and treating the symmetry result as a fact about a measure rather than as something that has to be answered. I have tried more argument, more formalism, and the usual science-fiction-and-philosophy reading list; what I have not tried is making them occupy the situation from inside, so I would like a short story to assign.

The setting I have in mind is a fictional early-industrial commonwealth, the Commonwealth of Orenn, which maintains an Office of Weights and Regularities. The Office licenses forecasting methods — for harvests, floods, ore yields, epidemics — and its founding statute, chiselled above the door of its Hall of Ledgers, requires that for every licensed method it also license and maintain that method's complement, the procedure that issues the opposite prediction on every occasion. These complements are housed on the Antipodal Bench and kept in the same ledger, on the stated ground that over all admissible futures a method and its antipode are right equally often, so the Office has no warrant to prefer one to the other a priori. For something over two centuries, in an apparently orderly world, the Antipodal Bench has been wrong about nearly everything. The story should concern a junior auditor assigned to review whether the Bench should be de-licensed, on the recommendation due to the Board at the end of the week. I would rather the story not re-stage the standard classroom devices — no grue-coloured emeralds, no bread and billiard balls, no last-minute vindication of either side.

What I want the fiction to do is dramatise two distinctions my students collapse. First, the symmetry result and the problem of induction are not the same claim: one is a theorem about a measure over a class of worlds, the other is a doubt about whether this world is the kind the measure averages over, and the story should let a reader feel that the theorem is perfectly compatible with there being excellent practical reason to go on inducting. Second, and more importantly, the real dispute moves to the measure: tilt the ledger's weights toward simpler futures and the antipodes lose their standing at once, since induction dominates under such a weighting — but adopting that weighting is itself the bias that the problem was about, and the story should leave the reader with the sense that this is simultaneously a genuine answer and a circular one. Constraints: no character should discover or prove a theorem, nature should not spectacularly break, and neither the auditors nor the antipodal bench should be vindicated; if the recommendation is made, let it be made on institutional or economic grounds rather than epistemic ones. Keep the technical material inside the institution's own language — committee minutes, marginalia in the ledger, one short exchange at the Bench — rather than in a narrator's exposition, and keep the whole thing under roughly fourteen hundred words.
