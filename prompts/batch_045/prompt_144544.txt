I'm a backend engineer and somehow I've ended up being the person who has to write up my team's position on AI safety stuff, which is really not my background at all. I've maybe read one blog post on this "alignment tax" idea but I think I get the gist: making a model safer costs you something — capability, time, money, whatever — and if everyone is racing to ship, whoever is willing to pay less of that cost wins the race, so nobody pays it. That second bit is what people call the competitiveness constraint, I think.

The problem is our VP keeps saying the tax is basically zero now. Her version is that the safety work we actually do — the RLHF-ish tuning, refusal behavior, guardrail classifiers in front of the model — mostly makes the product *better*: fewer users churning after the model says something awful, fewer embarrassing screenshots, better enterprise sales. So on her view there's no real tradeoff to manage and the whole competitiveness thing is an old worry that got overtaken by the fact that safety and product quality turned out to be complements. I dont think she's obviously wrong, but I think she might be measuring the tax in the wrong place, and I can't tell whether that's a real disagreement or just us using one word for two things.

My own half-baked version, which you should feel free to hand to whichever camp it belongs to: I think the tax shows up as latency, per-request cost, and product-team friction rather than as benchmark scores. A safety layer that adds 400ms and a bunch of extra tokens per turn never shows up in any eval anyone publishes, but it's exactly the thing that gets quietly cut in a crunch. And relatedly, I suspect competitive pressure doesn't select for *zero* safety — it selects for the cheapest *legible* safety. Model cards, eval dashboards, red-team reports: cheap to produce, very visible to a regulator or a customer's procurement team. The controls that might actually matter — not shipping this quarter, not training on some capability, keeping a human in the loop — are expensive and almost invisible from outside. I've started calling that the compliance sink, which is probably a name someone else already used, whatever.

What I actually want from you: draft the joint statement that a genuine no-tax / safety-is-a-free-lunch person and a genuine tax-is-real-and-only-binding-coordination-helps person could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims they can both agree on, including about what the tax even is and who has to pay it; the exact disagreements, each one phrased so that side would accept the wording of its own position; the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would actually move somebody; and for each crux a concrete measurement, case, or experiment both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. My guesses at the cruxes are: where you measure the tax (benchmarks vs. latency and unit economics), whether it's a one-time cost or a recurring one you can defer and pay back later with interest, and whether the race selects for no safety or for cheap visible safety. Keep them if they're real cruxes, replace them if they're not. If part of the fight is just about words, say which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Please keep it in terms an engineer could sanity-check — we're a small team, nobody here has an ML research or philosophy background, and I'm writing this offline, so no papers to go read and no citations, I can't tell a real reference from a hallucinated one anyway. Just start with the document itself — no "here's a draft" preamble, no summary of what you're about to give me.
