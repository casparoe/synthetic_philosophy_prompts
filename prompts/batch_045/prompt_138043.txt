About eight months ago I came off my bike and broke my collarbone, and in the ambulance they gave me ketamine. I remember the next twenty minutes really clearly: I could feel the injury, I could point straight at it, if you had asked me to rate it out of ten I'd have said eight or nine, and it did not bother me in the slightest. I was chatting to the paramedic about the Tour de France. A couple of hours later, same injury, same-ish sensation as far as I can reconstruct it from the inside, and it was one of the worst ten minutes of my life. I have not stopped turning that over since.

Reading around, I found out there are people who are like my ketamine self permanently. Something goes wrong in the brain — injury, or one of those old psychiatric operations, or morphine, apparently — and afterwards they will flinch from a pin, tell you it's a burning pain in the left forearm, put it at nine out of ten, and then laugh, or say the words "it hurts but it doesn't bother me," and never ask for anything for it. Their families find this terrifying. If that cluster has a name I'd like to know it, but that's honestly not what I'm after — I'm after the principle, and I don't want reading lists or citations, I've got no journal access anyway.

Here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases.

1. Ketamine me. Nothing bad happening. Maybe a hair of something, essentially nothing.
2. The permanent version of that. Also nothing bad happening — and I hold onto that verdict even in the version where the person is walking around with an untreated broken arm and will not let anyone touch it. Something bad is happening to that person, but it's the arm.
3. My mate who runs hundred-milers. At mile sixty his legs are agony, he says they're agony, he wants to stop, and he doesn't stop. Definitely something bad. He chose it, he'd do it again, he's having the best day of his month — still bad.
4. Years ago I gashed my hand open on something in the middle of a really absorbing argument and genuinely did not notice until I saw the blood at bedtime. I want to say that hurt at the time and I just wasn't attending — but that's wierd, because "wasn't attending" is more or less what I want to say about the ketamine, and there I say it wasn't bad.
5. The deep burn in my thighs at the end of a hard set, or a brutal sports massage. I'm attending to it, I want it to stop, and I'd pay for the experience again. Not bad. And not "bad but worth it" either — I can't find the bad bit.
6. Every afternoon around four I get this low ache-and-nausea thing. If you don't ask me about it I don't notice it, I don't want anything for it, and I would pay a stupid amount of money for it to be gone. Bad.
7. Phantom limb pain in a man who knows perfectly well the leg is gone. Bad, obviously.
8. The kid born unable to feel pain at all, chewing through his tongue. Nothing bad is happening in his experience — but something terrible is happening, and it feels like he's missing out on something too, not just in danger.
9. A stroke patient who grins and jokes and rates his pain at ten out of ten because he was raised to never show weakness. Bad, obviously. Behaviour tells us nothing.

So here's the principle I keep trying out: what's bad about pain is just the bothersomeness — the wanting-it-to-stop, the can't-leave-it-alone part — and everything else about it, how strong it is, where it is, burning or stabbing or crushing, is only bad because normally it drags the bothersomeness along with it. That handles 1, 2, 3, 7, 9 cleanly and I think it handles 8. But 4 and 6 push against it in opposite directions: in 5 I've got attention and wanting-it-to-stop and no badness, in 6 I've got neither and plenty of badness. Which makes me think "bothersomeness" is several things bolted together and my cases are pulling them apart, and I don't know what the pieces are.

Can you give me a principle, or two or three candidates, that does better? I want to know which of my verdicts it can't save and what it costs to save them anyway, whether the thing in case 2 is genuinely lacking in badness or is someone who has stopped responding properly to something awful, and whether "bothersomeness" is one thing or a bundle. And then some cases I haven't given verdicts on: pain you're having while asleep that wakes you up — was it bad before it woke you? Pain in a dog or a newborn, where nobody can tell you whether it bothers them. Pain in a dream. And the way people talk about grief as pain — is that the same thing I'm asking about or just a loose way of talking?
