I spent thirty-five years at the bench in experimental physiology, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy on my own — no doubt badly, but seriously, and mostly in the nineteenth century. The reading has started to interfere with my committee work, which is the reason for this request.

I sit on the council of a learned society of roughly nine thousand members. Among the things the council oversees are two small venues in which members speak to one another: the letters column of our monthly bulletin, to which contributions must be signed, and an online discussion board, to which they need not be. The board is the older and the busier of the two, and for years nobody on the council thought about it at all.

Two events have changed that. In March a graduate student posted a cautious question about a statistical practice used in several widely cited papers in our field. Within two days the thread had run to several hundred replies, almost all of them anonymous. Many mocked her; several stated, correctly, where she worked and who supervised her, which means that some of the people writing knew exactly whom they were writing about; two implied that she was being paid by an instrument manufacturer. No individual post broke our rules. She withdrew the talk she was to give at the summer meeting and did not renew her membership.

The second event cuts the other way. The single most useful thread on that board in the past year was also anonymous: a long, plainly expert account of a contamination problem in a standard assay, posted by someone who wrote that putting her name to it would cost her her position. It saved a good many laboratories a good deal of time, and the council's technical note in September came out of it. Whatever we do to the board, that sort of person must still be able to speak.

I have been reading Kierkegaard on the press and the public, and I cannot get his claim out of my head: that the crowd, as such, is untruth, and that the public is not a collection of persons but a phantom a medium manufactures — an everybody that is nobody, in which no one has to answer for what is said and to which anyone may belong. Two features of the account seem to me to carry the weight, and I have been trying to state them so that a moderator could apply them in under a minute. First, answerability: what makes a discussion into a public rather than a set of conversations is that there is nobody in it who could be asked to answer for it — I have even toyed with measuring this as the number of posts divided by the number of persons who could in principle be held to them. Second, leveling: in such a discussion the tone is set downward, because the cheapest contribution is available to everyone and the most demanding is available to no one in particular.

But the more I read, the less I can tell what the claim licenses, and that is where I need help. Kierkegaard made these complaints from inside the medium he was complaining about, and he made them under invented names: he argues that a known author's name is a kind of coercion, that signing one's own name lets the reader lean on one's authority instead of judging for himself, and that writing pseudonymously returns the reader to his own judgment. He also invited a Copenhagen satirical weekly to attack him in 1845; when it obliged and kept at it for months, he wrote about what it is to be turned into a figure anyone may jeer at, a figure that belongs to nobody and therefore to everybody. So the man who says the crowd is untruth also used the crowd's medium, defended concealing his name on principle, and was eventually chewed up by the thing he had baited.

One distinction I keep returning to, which I suspect is mine rather than his, is between anonymity that withdraws the speaker's name and anonymity that withdraws the speaker's answerability. The whistleblower and the pseudonymous author do the first while remaining entirely able to answer for what they wrote. The poster who writes as we, on behalf of what everyone in the field supposedly knows, does the second — and does it whether or not his name is attached to the post. If that is right, then requiring real names fixes nothing that is broken and destroys the one thing that works. But I may be bending his distinction to reach a conclusion I already wanted, and I would rather be told so than accommodated.

I have to recommend something. The options the council has drafted are these:

A. Require every post to appear under the poster's verified name, with a confidential exemption for members who can show that being named would put them at risk.
B. Keep displayed pseudonyms, but require that a verified person stand behind every account — known to the Society, not to the public — so that each post has someone who can answer for it even if no one else can name them.
C. Close the open board and replace it with moderated, topic-gated questions put to a panel.
D. Leave the policy as it stands and add two moderators.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the council before the meeting: something the council could actually act on, with the recommendation in the first two sentences, the reasoning after it, and the strongest objection to that recommendation stated as strongly as it can honestly be stated. Because I am a scientist and distrust anything I cannot check, I also want a short set of criteria a moderator could apply to a real post or a real thread, and one or two things we should measure a year from now to find out whether the policy did what we hoped. If you think my two-feature reading of Kierkegaard is wrong, or that it points somewhere other than where I have taken it, say that in the memo rather than building on it. I do not want a reading list or citations — I want an argument, 800 to 1,000 words, in memo form (To, From, Re, and so on).
