I design and playtest small economic games for a games club I've run for about six years, and one of them has been bothering me for most of that time. Two players sit opposite a pot of counters. On your turn you either TAKE, which ends the game with roughly 60% of the pot to you and the rest to your opponent, or you PASS, which hands the turn over and grows the pot by about 40%. The number of turns is fixed and announced in advance; if nobody has taken by the last turn, the pot is split evenly. There is an argument about this game that I must have explained two hundred times: at the last turn you would rather take than split, so at the turn before that your opponent, foreseeing this, would take, so you should take there too, and the same reasoning repeats until it reaches the very first move. Almost everyone I explain it to concedes every step, and then almost nobody does what it says — they pass several times before taking. That gap stopped surprising me years ago. What still surprises me is what happened when I started altering the setup, because the alterations never changed what any individual move pays, and yet they completely changed the standing of the argument.

The first alteration was length. I ran the game at six turns and at twenty turns with roughly the same forty players over a couple of months. At six turns, the first TAKE usually came on the fourth turn. At twenty turns it usually came on the seventeenth or eighteenth. Nobody took on the first turn in either version. So the argument is not anchored to anything — it says take now, whatever the length — while the actual stopping point behaves as if it were pinned a fixed distance short of the end. Except that a third version makes me doubt even that description: when I removed the announced last turn entirely and simply ended the game after a number of turns I chose at random and did not disclose, the stopping point still landed around the sixteenth or seventeenth turn, more or less where it had been in the twenty-turn version, which suggests my players were not measuring the distance to a known last turn at all. The second alteration is the one that really unseated me. I had both players, before play began and without seeing each other's, write down the turn on which they would claim the pot; the earlier claim won and the pot was split if they named the same turn. Nothing about the payoffs of taking or passing at any turn changed. But if my opponent has written seventeen, then writing sixteen myself wins me the larger share of a pot that is 40% smaller, and writing eighteen hands them the pot while I take the smaller share — so matching them is best, and the higher the number we both write, the better we both do. Most of my players wrote a large number, and when I asked afterwards why they were not afraid of being undercut they gave roughly that reasoning. The backwards argument simply does not survive being collapsed into a single decision made in advance, and I cannot put my finger on what it was drawing its force from.

My brother-in-law, who took one economics course, says this is just the prisoner's dilemma repeated with the numbers fiddled. A friend says it reminds her of the puzzle about the judge who tells the prisoner he will be hanged on a morning he does not expect. I do not think the first is right, and I cannot say why the second is wrong. I am not asking for a reading list or for names to look up — I have no library access and a list would not help me — and I am not asking for a label for its own sake. I want to know whether my three observations are one phenomenon or several; what the nearest established concepts are and, for each, exactly where my case departs from it and why; which one fits best, if any; and if nothing established fits, I want a term coined for it, a definition tight enough to decide hard cases, and a list of nearby cases it does *not* cover — a version where passing shrinks the pot, say, or one where the players are allowed to talk first, or one where each pass sends the turn to a brand-new opponent.
