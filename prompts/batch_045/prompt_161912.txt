I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory working on a dissertation chapter about the politics of working time, and I keeping hitting the same mushy question: who actually has a right to work and a right to leisure, and what exactly are they owed? I've got the three standard frames in my head — the Weberian work-ethic story where idleness is a moral hazard and work is a calling, the statutory-hours story behind things like the French 35-hour week where leisure is a claim-right enforced against employers, and the karoshi story where the right exists on paper and then gets eaten by workplace norms and unrecorded overtime — so please don't summarize any of them back to me. What I want is to run one case through them. My advisor suggested Ford's announcement in January 1914 of the five-dollar day: roughly as I remember it, the minimum went from a bit over two dollars to five a day, the workday was cut from nine hours to eight, a third shift was added so the plant could run around the clock, thousands of people lined up in the cold for jobs, turnover collapsed, and the profit-sharing half of the deal was conditional on tenure and on approved personal conduct — sobriety, thrift, a decent family life — monitored by a Sociological Department whose investigators visited workers' homes. Take my facts as given whether or not they're exactly right; I don't need them checked or corrected, and I don't want citations or a reading list, I want the analysis.

Here's the wrinkle I want injected, because I don't think any of the usual frames distinguishes it. Call them four senses of having leisure: leisure-as-exemption (you cannot be required to work, full stop), leisure-as-capacity (you could really rest — enough money, not wrecked, not on call, not doing a second shift at home), leisure-as-synchrony (the people you would spend the time with are free at the same time, so weekends and holidays are collective goods rather than individual ones), and leisure-as-license (you may spend the time as you like without anyone inspecting how). Analyze the Ford episode through the right-to-leisure question using those: say which senses the plan actually delivered and which it only appeared to deliver, map the players and what each could do — Ford, the worker, the worker's wife whose unpaid domestic labor is part of what made his rest possible, the investigators, and the conspicuous absence of any union or statute — and then run it the other direction, on what the three standard frames explain, what they misdescribe, and what the case does to the theory. My own hunch is that a right one man can hand out and take back isn't a right at all, and that all three frames quietly assume an individual male claimant with somebody else absorbing his domestic work, but I'd rather you argue for or against that than agree with me.

Give me, in American English, five titled sections in this order: "The four senses" (~120 words), "Who had what in January 1914" (~300 words), "What each frame catches and what it misses" (~300 words), "What the case does to the theory" (~250 words), and "Where I should stop" (~100 words). Where the mapping has nothing to say, say so instead of stretching it.
