I've gotten myself into the position of defending this at a reading group next week — I'm the econ person, not the decision theory person, so please don't assume I know the jargon and spell out what a defender of it would actually say back. The view I've ended up with is that you don't need functional decision theory, or superrationality, or any my-decision-and-the-other-guy's-decision-are-outputs-of-the-same-algorithm machinery to get cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between total strangers; all you need is a big enough world. The argument as I'd give it: in a sufficiently large world there are lots of agents sitting in structurally identical decision situations, with no communication, no predictor, no one being simulated, no sci-fi copies of anybody — just many agents facing the same payoff matrix from roughly the same epistemic position. Still, what I decide is evidence about what they decide, because similar situations plus similar reasoning tend to produce similar choices, and in a big world even a modest correlation gets multiplied: if I cooperate, that's evidence that some large number of other agents cooperate too, and the aggregate payoff across all those cooperators can swamp my individual cost of cooperating. So an evidential decision theorist run in a large world starts behaving like a functional decision theorist, and the acausal machinery was never doing the real work. Someone in the group called this evidential cooperation in large worlds, no idea if that's a real label for it.

What I want is an honest map of how exposed this is — what would have to be true for it to fail, and what would count against it rather than just being inconvenient. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one tell me whether it's live or merely logically possible. Here are the ones I can half-see, but I'm sure I'm missing the good ones: (a) the correlation assumption looks like a substantive empirical claim about how similarity is distributed in the actual world, not a theorem — in a large but heterogenous world the evidential mass might be dominated by distant, only-loosely-analogous agents whose choices my own choice barely tracks, and then largeness alone buys nothing; (b) noise: if any agent has some small chance of implementing the wrong action, then in a huge world a huge number of agents do, and I want to know whether the arithmetic still comes out in favour of cooperating or whether the tremble term eats the whole effect; (c) I'm not obviously a random sample from the set of agents similar to me — there might be a selection effect where the agents who find themselves deliberating about this exact question are systematically the weird ones; (d) my friend's objection that similar reasoning produces similar choices is just the FDT claim about algorithmic similarity smuggled in under a probabilistic name, i.e. that this is functional decision theory in a trenchcoat; (e) uncertainty about which subpopulation I'm in, if the large world is partitioned into groups with different correlation structures; (f) something self-referential that I can't quite formulate, where my choosing to cooperate is also evidence about how strongly correlated I am with others, so the correlation estimate and the action are mutually dependent and might not have a stable fixed point; (g) the manage-the-news style worry that in a big world the payoff to just making myself believe the cheerful thing, or delegating the decision, or publicly pre-committing before I've thought about it, becomes huge.

Tell me which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against the view and which they'd dismiss as missing the point, and add the ones I haven't thought of. Two more things specifically: first, does the view make any prediction its rivals don't — I think it implies cooperation should be sensitive to how many relevantly-similar agents there are, so that in a small world the same agent should defect, and I can't tell whether that's a feature or a reductio; and second, is there a version of the move where the defender retreats to the limit of infinitely many exact duplicates, and does that retreat insulate the claim so thoroughly that it stops being an answer to the practical question it was supposed to answer — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind or just the normal fate of limit results? I'd personally be most moved by objections that work against the realistic, non-adversarial versions of the correlation assumption, and least moved by the standard medical-Newcomb-style jabs at evidential decision theory in general, since those feel like they're aimed at a different target.
