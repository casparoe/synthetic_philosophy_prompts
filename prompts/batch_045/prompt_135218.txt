Write an internal guidance document of roughly 800 words for the licensing division of a state bureau charged with administering the supererogatory — those acts that are morally good and praiseworthy to perform but not blameworthy to omit. Give it a title in the manner of "Guidance for Applicants and Auditors: Permits for Acts Beyond the Call of Duty," format it as numbered clauses with a short preamble, and write it in a deadpan civil-service register: defined terms, form numbers, cross-references, worked examples, an appendix. The narrator should be a competent and somewhat weary official who takes the whole apparatus entirely seriously; there should be no winking, no editorial asides, and no moment where the document admits it is absurd. It must be internally coherent on its own terms.

The comedy has to come from real features of the category the bureau is trying to administer, exaggerated rather than invented. Build the document around at least the following:

Authorization is necessarily retrospective. A permit issued in advance would turn the act into something the applicant was required to do, so the bureau can only certify acts that have already been performed, and applicants must fill in the form without knowing whether it will be granted.

Obligation depends on what everyone else is doing. An act is reclassified as mandatory once the participation rate within the relevant reference class crosses a compliance threshold, at which point the permit is revoked and non-participants are cited retroactively. The bureau therefore has to manage demand: it caps permits, runs allocation lotteries, and maintains waiting lists of people willing to donate a kidney or return a lost wallet, for the sole purpose of keeping those acts optional.

The field test for whether an act was ever really optional is the reaction of bystanders. Officers canvass neighbours and passers-by for resentment or indignation at the omission; where such reactive attitudes are found, the act is held to have been a duty all along, the permit is refused, and the file is transferred to Enforcement.

Optional good works are not transferable credit. A claimant may not set an act of rescue or extraordinary generosity against an unmet obligation — a missed support payment, an unfiled return — and the ledger of optional works cannot be drawn down against the ledger of requirements.

Applicants are disqualified if they acted in order to obtain a permit, or because permits exist. Auditors review diaries and messages for evidence of credit-seeking motivation. Include a dry statistical note that valid applications have fallen steadily since the bureau opened.

An internal objector, the Office of Maximizing Review, holds that since the best available act is always the one you ought to perform, the category is empty and every permit is void. The bureau's appendix should not refute this so much as observe the awkward consequence for its own staff.

The worked examples should be deliberately mundane rather than melodramatic: staying late, returning a wallet, giving blood, pulling someone out of a canal. Let the clauses generate their humour from the classification problems these raise, including the fact that whether an act counts as beyond duty varies with local rates of compliance.

Conclude with a "forward programme" section that follows the bureau's own logic to its straight-faced conclusion: if anything is to remain supererogatory, participation must be kept low, so the bureau proposes to stop publicizing the scheme, to withhold the names of permit-holders, and to pilot a quiet discouragement of the most generous acts. End on that proposal, unremarked.

Constraints: no named philosophers, no quotations from real texts, no citations or footnotes, no references to actual legal instruments. A reader with no philosophy should still understand what is being satirized; a reader who works on supererogation, the demandingness of morality, or moral offsetting should find the satire fair rather than cheap.
