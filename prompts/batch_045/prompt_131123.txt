I've just come from the last session of a value-theory reading group on intransitivity and vagueness, and then from a three-hour argument with my housemate Oskar that I couldn't finish. We built our own case rather than using the textbook ones: imagine a print room holding a thousand impressions pulled from the same copper plate, each one taken after the plate has worn a hair blunter and after an apprentice has reworked the line a hair more gracefully — so each impression loses a hair of sharpness and gains a hair of life. At no step is the next impression worse: it is a trade the Keeper, who may hold only two adjacent sheets at a time and whose discrimination threshold is one hair, is not in a position to resolve. Yet impression one is a crisp, stiff ghost of the design and impression one thousand is a soft, beautiful blur that has lost the image entirely, and we both agreed that one is plainly better than the other. Oskar then said the real lesson is that transitivity of *better than* is not what fails: strict betterness stays transitive and acyclic, and what breaks is the tolerance relation — "not worse than," or being on a par — and that if you let that fail you give up the thought that value runs on a single scale, that there is one number by which the sheets could be ranked, which he thinks is too much to pay. I said I was happy to pay it, and that he was conflating it with the far heavier commitment he kept accusing me of: genuine intransitivity of betterness, cycles, the staircase that closes on itself and the coins you'd pay forever to walk it. We went in circles on whether the thousandth sheet is worse because some step worsened it (in which case there's a cut no one can locate, and locating it at random is its own embarrassment), or because worsening needn't distribute over the links, or whether the whole idea of a fact of the matter at each step is what should go. Nobody won.

So: you are a professional philosopher whose area of specialisation is value theory and who also writes competent formal verse. Think carefully before you draft — first brainstorm for a few minutes: pick your six end-words and note what each will carry, decide where the turn falls, decide which horn the close will take, and only then write. I want a sestina — six sestets of unrhymed lines, then the three-line envoi — set in that print room, and I want the form to do the arguing rather than decorate it: the rotation of the end-words is a thousand tiny trades each of which is nothing and all of which arrive somewhere, and the envoi, which forces all six words back into three lines, is the moment the first sheet and the last are finally on the table together. Give me plain mid-century diction and concrete images of paper, ink, burin and lamplight, no poeticisms and no apparatus: no footnotes, no italicised glosses, no philosopher's name dropped into a line to signal that you know the literature. Suggested end-words, if you want them: grain, hair, par, chain, stair, end — though take better ones if better ones occur to you, so long as each names something load-bearing in the argument. Sestets one and two should establish the pairwise verdict: each adjacent pair judged not worse, and no one able to do better than the Keeper. Sestet three should make the threshold itself visible — what it means that a difference is real but below the grain — and should get right the distinction Oskar insisted on, that what is in trouble here is the relation that tolerates small differences, not strict betterness, and that a cycle is a different and stronger claim than Oskar's opponents want. The turn should land in sestets four and five, when the reader sees that the last sheet is plainly worse while no single step made it so: this is where the fork should open, on locating a cut that cannot be found, on letting "not worse than" fail to chain, or on denying that there is a fact of the matter at the steps. The envoi should commit to one horn — commit, I mean, not hedge into a shrug — while still granting the others enough that Oskar would have to answer rather than sneer. It should be accurate enough that someone who actually works on these arguments wouldn't wince, and it should still be a poem someone who has never heard of a spectrum argument would want to hear twice.

I'm going to read it aloud at the last meeting, so keep it to the thirty-nine lines and let it be speakable. After the poem, add a note of no more than five sentences saying which premise your envoi rejects and what that rejection costs — I want to be able to defend the choice when Oskar pushes back on it, and I'd rather know the price going in.
