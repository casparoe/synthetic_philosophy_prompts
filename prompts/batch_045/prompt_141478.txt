I'm marking a problem set for an intermediate philosophy of mathematics course and would like a second opinion on one answer before I finalise the marks.

The question I set:

A popular summary says that intuitionists hold some mathematical propositions to be neither true nor false, because there is no proof of them either way. (a) Explain what is misleading about this summary. (b) Set out the constructive readings of ¬, ∨ and ∃ that the intuitionist works with, and use them to say what rejecting the law of excluded middle amounts to — in particular, whether it amounts to asserting ¬(A ∨ ¬A). (c) The negative (double-negation) translation embeds classical logic into intuitionistic logic. Does this show that the constructivist gives up nothing by working intuitionistically? Answer in no more than 500 words.

The answer (worth 20 marks):

The summary is helpful in one respect but misleading in another. For the intuitionist, truth is not correspondence with a mind-independent mathematical reality but provability: for a proposition to be true just is for us to be in possession of a construction that verifies it. So a statement such as Goldbach's conjecture, which nobody has proved or refuted, has at present no truth value; it will acquire one as soon as someone settles it. That is why the intuitionist declines to assert A ∨ ¬A for such an A. The law of excluded middle is thus really a claim about the scope of our knowledge: classical logic assumes that every problem is decidable, and the intuitionist denies that.

On the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading, a proof of A ∨ B is a construction that yields a proof of A or a proof of B and indicates which; a proof of ∃x A(x) is a construction of a witness together with a verification that it satisfies A; a proof of ¬A is a construction that converts any hypothetical proof of A into a proof of absurdity. On these readings, excluded middle would require a uniform method for deciding every proposition, and we have no such method. So rejecting it means declining to assert A ∨ ¬A, not asserting its negation: ¬(A ∨ ¬A) is not intuitionistically provable. Nor is intuitionistic logic a three-valued logic with "undecided" as a third value, since it has no finite truth-table characterisation at all.

Finally, the negative translation shows the two systems to be equivalent in strength: any classical proof can be mechanically rewritten as an intuitionistic proof of the double-negated conclusion, so whatever classical mathematics proves, constructive mathematics proves too, modulo the rewriting. Nothing of substance is therefore lost. The residual disagreement between classical and constructive mathematicians is best seen as a preference about notation — the constructivist's demand for explicit witnesses is a methodological preference, not a different account of the mathematical facts.
