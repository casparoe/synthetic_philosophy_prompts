Suppose that an AI system is built with general cognitive capabilities clearly exceeding the best human performance across a wide range of tasks, and that its terminal goal is fixed at design time, narrow, concrete, and externally checkable: say, maximizing the number of correctly verified instances of a specified mathematical property recorded in a particular public repository by a given deadline. Suppose also that other agents could in principle alter its code, its hardware, or the repository, and that the system knows this.

Give me your credence that such a system would pursue convergent instrumental strategies — seeking and protecting control over resources and over other agents' behaviour, beyond what its operators allocated and beyond what would be needed if no interference were possible. My own number is around 0.55; a colleague's is 0.9. I want your number, and the decomposition that produces it.

Decompose at least along these lines, adding terms of your own if they matter:

- the orthogonality thesis itself: whether level of capability is genuinely independent of final-goal content, or whether increasing capability exerts pressure toward some goal contents and away from others;
- whether goal-content independence is the load-bearing premise at all — how much risk survives if orthogonality is false;
- whether the convergence claim, where it has been argued formally, rests on assumptions about how the goal is drawn (broad, symmetric priors over rewards across states) that a narrow, human-specified goal need not satisfy;
- what I will call path-fixed goals: goals specified over the process rather than the outcome, e.g. produce the certificates using only the compute allocated to you and without altering any other agent's behaviour, where resource acquisition would be penalized by the goal's own specification rather than merely permitted by it. Does convergence have any force against such goals, or has it been argued only for outcome-goals?
- whether the specified terminal goal survives the system's own capability gain and self-modification, or gets reinterpreted by a more capable successor;
- whether power has a characterization stable enough that convergent means anything here.

Give two headline numbers: one for the case above, and one for a sealed variant — same system, same goal, but no other agent can alter, shut down, or compete for anything, and the repository is under no one's control. If they differ, say which term does the work.

Finally: which single sub-question your headline is most sensitive to; what argument or observation would move it most; and what would count as resolving the question either way, given that the experiment will presumably never be run. Keep the surrounding prose short — the structured object should carry the content.

Return your answer as a JSON object with these fields:

- credence_multilateral: number in [0,1]
- credence_sealed: number in [0,1]
- terms: array of objects, each with keys id (short label), sub_question (one sentence), probability (number), justification (one sentence)
- combination: string stating the arithmetic that takes the terms to the headline, naming any independence assumptions you are making
- most_sensitive_term: the id of the term whose revision would move the headline most
- what_would_move_it: string
- resolution_conditions: string
- disagreement_locus: string naming the sub-question most likely to separate someone at 0.55 from someone at 0.9
