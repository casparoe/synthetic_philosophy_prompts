I spent my career in experimental plasma physics and have only in retirement begun sitting in on philosophy seminars, so I am formally the least qualified member of my hospital network's Clinical AI Review Committee — and, as luck would have it, the one asked to draft its decision memo. We must vote in three weeks on a five-year licence for an ICU scarce-resource allocator. The system maximises expected quality-adjusted life-years saved across the incoming cohort, subject to two hard side-constraints that a philosopher would call deontological: no patient whose predicted survival probability falls below a fixed floor may be deprioritised, and no patient already assigned a bed may be displaced by more than a set number of rank positions at any nightly re-run. Its novel feature is a pluralism module. Because reasonable people disagree about which normative theory is correct, the system draws one at random each night from a distribution over three — outcome-maximising, constraint-based, priority-to-the-worst-off — whose weights were fitted to a citizens' assembly the vendor convened, and then optimises within whichever theory was drawn. The vendor reports a substantial improvement in expected QALYs over our current first-come-within-severity-tier rule, but the licence bars us from inspecting either the weights or the survival floor; we may only query a sandboxed interface with synthetic cohorts.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should end in a recommendation the committee can actually vote on — adopt, reject, or adopt under stated conditions — and it should settle two questions I cannot. First, whether the pluralism module is a principled response to normative disagreement or merely converts disagreement into unaccountable noise: on the sampling scheme, whether a given patient receives a bed can depend on which theory the system happened to draw, which seems to leave each patient who loses out with a complaint that no one can answer. Second, whether the distinction I keep meeting between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure dissolves our question entirely — whether the right answer is that the allocator should be consequentialist in what it aims at and rule-governed in how it proceeds, for reasons of limited foresight, of bias in the survival model, and of what visible trading of lives does to institutional trust, rather than because either theory has been shown correct. Give the strongest available case for the option you do not recommend, since I expect to be argued with.
