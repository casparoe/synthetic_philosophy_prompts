Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors — call them Ines and Sam — who disagree about what the strategy-stealing argument for the board game Hex really establishes, and about whether a non-constructive result of that shape can give a bounded agent any reason to act.

Both characters accept the following background, and the dialogue should make it explicit enough to be followed by a reader who has not seen the argument before:

- Hex is played on an n×n rhombus of hexagonal cells. The players alternate placing a single stone of their own colour on an empty cell; passing is not allowed. One player must connect one pair of opposite sides of the rhombus, the other must connect the remaining pair, and the first player to complete such a chain wins immediately. The board admits a symmetry — reflection in one of its diagonals — that exchanges the two pairs of opposite sides, so the two roles are structurally the same game. A completely filled board always contains a winning chain for exactly one of the two players, so a drawn board is impossible.
- The strategy-stealing argument runs as follows. Suppose, for contradiction, that the second player has a strategy guaranteeing a win. The first player places a stone on any cell she likes and thereafter plays as if she were the second player: she treats her initial stone as an invisible ghost, reads her opponent's stones as the moves of a first player, and follows the stolen strategy's instructions; whenever the stolen strategy calls for the cell occupied by the ghost, she plays on any other empty cell instead and transfers the ghost label to that new stone. Her real position is never worse than the position the stolen strategy was designed for, because an extra stone of her own colour cannot damage her own connection and can only help to block her opponent's. Hence, if the second player could force a win, so could the first — impossible in a draw-free zero-sum game, where at most one side can have a winning strategy. So the second player has none, and since the game is finite, has perfect information and no draws, the first player has one.
- The argument exhibits neither a winning strategy nor even a single recommended opening move.

Ines defends the following view: the argument is a genuine piece of a priori knowledge, and it is not merely an existence claim of the sort one files and forgets. It identifies *which* player wins — something that determinacy or backward induction alone cannot do, since those only tell us that someone wins — and in doing so it refutes the standing intuition that the second player's extra information about the first move might compensate for moving second. She also holds that the result is not practically inert: it licenses the search for a winning strategy (one is guaranteed not to be hunting a chimera), and it gives a player who is choosing whether to take the first move a reason to take it, at least against an opponent she does not expect to outplay.

Sam defends the following view: the appearance that the argument establishes a first-mover advantage is produced entirely by one substantive premise — that an extra stone can never make a position worse. On his reading, the argument's real content is a conditional: *if* moves are never a liability, *then* the first player wins. Whether moves are a liability is itself a structural fact about a game, not a formality, and the argument says nothing about games where it fails. He adds a second, independent line: even granting that the first player has a winning strategy, a proof that a strategy exists is not automatically a reason for a particular agent to move first, because what such an agent should care about is the expected value of moving first *given the policy she will actually execute*. He illustrates this with a player whose entire competence consists in mirroring the opponent's moves across the board's diagonal symmetry: such a policy is well defined for the second player and undefined for the first, so this player may do strictly better by moving second in a game where the first player provably wins. He concedes that this shows something about the policy rather than the game, and he is willing to be pressed on what follows from that.

To keep the dispute from floating, have them test it on a variant they define together at the blackboard. Call it Surplus Hex with parameter m, where m is a fixed non-negative integer announced before play:

- Everything is as in ordinary Hex — same board, same alternating play with no passing, same winning condition, game ends the moment a chain is completed — except for the following scoring rule. When a player completes a chain, let her surplus be the number of her own stones on the board minus the number of her opponent's, counted immediately after the connecting move. Her chain counts as a win only if her surplus is at most m; if her surplus exceeds m, the game is scored a draw.

Have the two of them work out, carefully and by explicit reasoning rather than assertion, what the surplus can possibly be at the moment a chain is completed, given that the players strictly alternate and never pass, and then trace what this implies for the strategy-stealing argument in the two regimes m = 0 and m ≥ 1. Ines should press the point that the counterexample is a knife edge and that for every m ≥ 1 the rule never bites at all, so that the conclusion she cares about is robust across the whole non-degenerate range. Sam should press the point that the conclusion flips the instant an extra stone carries the smallest possible cost, that under m = 0 the first player cannot win at all while the second player's chains still count, and that this is exactly what one should expect if the no-cost-extra-move premise was doing all the work. Both of them should also be careful about the following trap: the failure of the stealing argument under m = 0 does not, by itself, hand a winning strategy to the second player, and if the correct verdict is that the variant's winner is left undetermined by their reasoning, they should say so rather than pretend otherwise.

Requirements for the dialogue:

- Write it as a scene between two people who respect each other and who have clearly argued this before: they anticipate each other's moves, they answer the objection that was actually made rather than a neighbouring one, and they are willing to repair or retreat from a formulation under pressure. Do not let either character become a straight man or a strawman, and do not resolve the disagreement by having one of them simply win.
- Both should state premises rather than assert conclusions, and both should distinguish clearly the three questions that get run together: which player has a winning strategy, whether a proof of existence is decision-relevant for an agent who cannot execute the strategy, and whether the no-cost-extra-move premise is a formality or the whole content of the argument.
- Nothing may be settled by appeal to the literature, to attributions, to computer analyses of particular board sizes, or to any claim about which specific opening moves are winning in ordinary Hex; every step should follow from the definitions given above.
- The exchange should close with each character having conceded at least one substantive point and with the disagreement relocated to a sharper question than the one they started with.
- Use a polished but natural register, attribute each speech by name, and aim for roughly ten to sixteen speeches, about 1,000 to 1,400 words in total.
