I work on a small team that builds and maintains a large simulator of commuter traffic in a metro area — a learned model of routing behaviour sitting on top of a hand-written demand model and a network model — and city agencies run it to score proposals (congestion pricing, a bus-lane conversion, a tolled bridge) before anyone implements them. We keep having the same argument. One colleague says the model's assumptions are obviously false: drivers do not have full information about every route, they do not all trade time for money at the same rate, they do not re-optimize every morning, and so any number the simulator produces about a policy nobody has tried is worthless. Another says that is naive — a false assumption is just an inductive bias, a model constrained to be true would never fit anything, and what matters is whether it predicts. When I went looking I found that the methodologists of economics have been having this fight for seventy years, starting with the 1953 essay by Milton Friedman whose notorious line says, roughly, that the more significant a theory is, the more unrealistic its assumptions will be — Paul Samuelson nicknamed that move the F-twist — and continuing with people who sort false assumptions into different kinds: ones that say a factor is negligible here, ones that restrict the domain a model is meant to cover, ones that are stand-ins for a mechanism nobody has worked out yet, and ones that are false but can be relaxed later by putting the omitted factor back in. I have no philosophy training, and I would like the whole dispute rendered into the vocabulary I actually think in.

So: translate it, as faithfully as you can, into machine-learning terms — training distribution and deployment distribution, distribution shift and intervention, spurious correlation versus invariant mechanism, inductive bias and architectural prior, capacity against data, regularization and the bias–variance tradeoff, model misspecification, identifiability, reduced-form fitting versus structural causal models, sim-to-real transfer, shortcut learning and being right for the wrong reasons, mechanistic interpretability. I am fluent in all of that and in Pearl-style interventions and invariance; I am not fluent in "realism," "verisimilitude," "capacities," or "explanation," and I want to see how much of the debate survives once those words are gone. Please do the translation case by case rather than in the abstract. Three cases: (1) firms modelled as if they solved a profit-maximization problem they plainly do not solve, used to predict how prices respond to a tax — the classic as-if case; (2) an idealization everyone thinks is harmless, like frictionless planes or an infinitely large randomly mating population, where the falsehood is in principle removable by adding the omitted factor back; (3) a model fitted to historical data that predicts well under the regime it was fitted on and then breaks when the policy it was used to choose is implemented, because the relation it learned was not invariant to that intervention — the sort of thing the Lucas critique is about, in my case a traffic model that has only ever seen the current toll schedule.

Then I want the residue report, and it is the part I care most about. For each of the three cases: what translated cleanly, what you had to distort or quietly help yourself to in order to make it fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Specifically, I want your view on three suspicions of mine. First, that the economists' notion of a successful prediction is doing something my vocabulary cannot do, because in my world there is always a held-out set with labels on it, whereas the quantities these policy models are used for — what happens to congestion if the toll is tripled — are counterfactuals that will never be observed, so "predictive success" cannot be scored the way I would want to score it, and I suspect Friedman's instrumentalism is much harder to cash out than it looks. Second, that the difference between a false assumption that is de-idealizable and a false assumption that is merely wrong maps onto something like the difference between an inductive bias that is a true symmetry of the data-generating process and one that merely helps optimization — and that this means realism of assumptions should be replaced by something like invariance under the interventions you intend to make, which would settle at least one of the three cases. Third, that some uses of these models — showing that a large-scale pattern could arise from small local preferences, which is what the model is asked for half the time — have no counterpart in my framework at all, since I have no notion of a model succeeding by opening a possibility rather than by producing a number. For each suspicion, say whether the residue is a real gap in the ML vocabulary, a place where the ML vocabulary is simply aimed at a different task, or a sign that the philosophical dispute is less substantive than it looks from the inside. Close with what our model-review checklist should actually say instead of "are the assumptions realistic," given your translation: two or three concrete questions to ask of a model before it is used to pick a policy, with one sentence each on why.
