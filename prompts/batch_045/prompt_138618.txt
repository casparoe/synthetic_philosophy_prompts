Hey — I run incident review for a mid-sized distributed system (few hundred services, on-call rotations, the usual), and for about a year I've been the annoying person in the postmortem channel insisting that nothing has a single root cause. A friend who does history shoved a short reader on explanation in history at me and said this is my argument, just sixty years early and with worse diagrams. I think she's half right and I also think historians are arguing about something subtly different from what we argue about, and I'd like you to translate it into the vocabulary I actually use before I make a fool of myself in front of both groups. The vocabulary: root cause vs contributing factor; necessary vs sufficient; fault trees with AND/OR gates and minimal cut sets (the smallest set of component failures sufficient to produce the outage); layered defenses where each layer would have stopped the incident if the others hadn't also failed; latent conditions vs triggering events; blast radius; time-to-detect and time-to-mitigate; would removing this have prevented it, which we sometimes actually get to test by replaying traffic or by fault injection in staging; and the fact that a postmortem has a fixed length and a fixed budget of action items, so which causes get written down is a resource-allocation decision and not just a factual one.

What I want translated is overdetermination as historians pose it: several factors each individually sufficient for the outcome — the war, the revolution, the crash happens with or without any one of them — so the plain but-for test returns no cause at all, which everyone agrees is the wrong answer. Restate that in my vocabulary, and restate the standard repair of moving from but-for to something like a necessary element of a sufficient set, which as far as I can tell is just minimal cut sets in a nicer hat; tell me whether that identification is right, mostly right, or a pun. Then the part I actually care about, the residue report, and I'll inject two ideas of my own so you can tell me whether they're new or whether the literature already has them under some name I don't know. (1) Call it counterfactual sensitivity grading: in our world deleting a factor wholesale is rarely the interesting experiment — we dial it instead (inject 200ms of latency rather than killing the service, shed 5% of capacity rather than 50%) and watch how the probability of the bad outcome moves. On a graded picture symmetric overdetermination stops looking like a paradox: two safeguards each sufficient on their own still get non-zero, partly overlapping, graded contributions once you perturb rather than delete. Does the historians' debate have any room for that move, or is it structurally committed to binary removal? And if it does have room, what does grading cost them — do they lose the ability to answer the question they were actually asking? (2) Call it leverage selection: we deliberately write up the cause we can cheaply and durably fix rather than the one that mattered most, so a misconfigured retry policy that any one of four other things would have masked still gets four action items. Is there anything in the philosophical debate corresponding to selecting causes by intervention leverage rather than by causal contribution, or does the debate quietly assume that what caused it and what we should say caused it can only come apart through ignorance or rhetorical bias?

Third, where I think translation fails outright, and I want you to be brutal about whether I'm right. First failure: we get to define the outcome — an incident is whatever our alerting says it is, and we can re-run history in staging. Historians inherit an outcome (the French Revolution, the collapse of some regime) whose boundaries are themselves contested, and their counterfactuals are unreplayable. Second failure: our causes are component states, not agents with reasons, and alot of historical explanation seems to be about what people took themselves to have reason to do, which has no slot in a fault tree. For each, say exactly what breaks and whether the break is a defect in my vocabulary or a sign that the historians' notion of a cause is doing work mine can't do. And then the reverse question, which is the one I'd actually use in the meeting: if the philosophers get something right here, is root cause a concept my team should retire, or does it survive as shorthand for something we could make precise if we wanted to? Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I'm on a laptop with no library access, and I'd rather have your own reconstruction than a bunch of names I can't look up.
