I'm writing the final paper for a graduate seminar on truth, and I've committed myself to a version of deflationism I've been calling transmission deflationism: the entire content of 'is true' is fixed by the equivalence schema plus a norm of deference, so that to apply 'true' to a proposition you cannot yourself state or evaluate — in blind assertion, in infinite or effectively undecidable generalizations like 'everything in the sealed archive is true,' in agreement with a past or merely possible self — is to inherit the endorsement of whoever or whatever supplied it. There is no substantive property for the predicate to express: not correspondence, not superassertibility, not ideal coherence.

What would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and split into those a committed transmission deflationist should concede as counting against them and those they can reasonably dismiss. The candidates I can already see coming: whether the schema is genuinely conservative over the base theory or secretly needs a substantive notion to license truth-involving inferences; whether truth does explanatory work in connecting belief to successful action that a device for generalizing over propositions cannot duplicate; whether the norm of believing truly is statable in the transmission vocabulary at all; whether a merely logical device can explain why the liar arises; and whether my deference norm can be formulated without already presupposing truth. Add the ones I'm not seeing.

Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if every role truth might play is met by a schema-level or norm-level clause, what is left that the view risks, and is that insulation a defect for a theory of this kind? Please answer in American English.
