I work on the moral standing of artificial systems, and I keep running into the same move at workshops on global or non-Western approaches to AI ethics. Someone invokes the Nguni maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, usually rendered in English as "a person is a person through other persons" and sometimes as "I am because we are," and uses it to close a question rather than open one. Last month a discussant used it to do two jobs at once. First, to argue that no artificial system could be a person, since personhood is constituted by standing in relation to persons and an artefact has no community. Second, in the same breath, to argue that a relational view is the more generous view, because it makes standing something we confer through recognition rather than something an entity must earn by possessing the right intrinsic properties.

I cannot tell whether either argument works, because I cannot tell what the maxim asserts. It is used as though it were at once a metaphysical thesis about what a person is, a developmental claim about how the relevant capacities are acquired, an epistemic claim about how we come to know that someone is a person, and a normative demand about how we owe it to each other to behave. Those are very different claims, they do not all support the two moves above, and some of them seem to me to undercut both.

So I would like a precisification. Please lay out, as a menu of distinct and precisely stated theses, the different things this sentence could be claiming — separating at least the following: relations as partly constitutive of what it is to be a person; relations as causal preconditions for the development of the capacities that matter; relations as evidence of personhood; and relations as what we owe to persons once they are persons. For each reading, state whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, contested, or false, and say whether it supports the exclusion argument, the conferral argument, or neither.

Three points I especially need pinned down, because they are where I think the discussant's argument quietly shifts ground:

1. Degree versus threshold. If relations constitute personhood, is personhood then scalar — richer or more numerous relations, more of a person — or is there a threshold? On a scalar reading, a recluse is a partial person and a well-connected person is more of a person than an isolated one, with consequences for how much standing each has. Say which readings avoid that implication and what they must give up to avoid it, and whether avoiding it collapses them into the merely causal-precondition reading.

2. Whose relations, and must they be reciprocated or veridical? If I address a system with no inner life and relate to it as a person, does that confer anything? If a community withholds recognition from a human being — enslavement, caste, extreme isolation — has it, on a constitutive reading, destroyed a person? My worry is that a constitutive reading makes a victim's standing hostage to the wrongdoer's refusal, which is a strange result for a maxim whose political use is to protect the vulnerable. I want to know which readings carry that implication, which avoid it, and what each avoidance costs.

3. The asymmetry in the AI case. Compare a human child raised in near-total social isolation who is later brought into relationships, with a system trained in a closed loop whose only interlocutors are other instances of itself and which has no human community. What does each reading say about the two, and where exactly does the burden of argument fall for someone who wants to treat them differently? I am interested in the structure of the argument, not in empirical claims about what today's systems can do.

Please skip the familiar framing of this as a contrast between African communitarianism and Western individualism, and do not spend the answer on intellectual history or attribution; I need the logical geography of the claim, not its provenance.

Finally: for each reading, say how confident you are that it is a reading the maxim is actually made to carry, as opposed to one you have reconstructed because the argument needs it, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least confident about, with a note on what would settle it.
