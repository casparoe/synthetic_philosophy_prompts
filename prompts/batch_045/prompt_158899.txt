I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am drafting a piece on digital herding — the way an endorsement count or a trending marker seems to make public judgment lock in behind whatever the earliest traffic favored. The reform I want to recommend is a commit-then-reveal rule, now being piloted in different forms by a large review platform and by a professional fact-checking network: each participant would have to record her own verdict before the running aggregate is shown to her, and the aggregate would be suppressed for a fixed window, so that nobody can follow anyone else and the accuracy of independent judgment reasserts itself. My working assumption is that the standard sequential-choice cascade models describe a failure of rationality — agents who discard evidence they hold because they would rather be with the crowd — so that taking away the option to follow should be close to a pure gain in accuracy, and so that a bad cascade should break as soon as someone with a sufficiently strong contrary signal shows up. Before I build the piece on that, I would like the reasoning checked: is what those models describe really a failure of individual rationality, or is it rationality with a defective social outcome? Does withholding earlier movers' signals from later movers reliably improve accuracy, or can it make accuracy worse, including in the two pilots I described? Is the claim about a strong contrary signal breaking a cascade true in the models, or does it depend on assumptions about signal structure that may not hold in the pilots? Please say where the answers differ depending on whether the mechanism at work is informational or reputational, and flag any place where the crowd-framing I am using is itself misleading. Finally, keep separate what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it: I would rather not print one camp's assumption as the field's consensus.
