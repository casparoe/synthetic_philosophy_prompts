hey — I'm in a phil of law seminar this term and we did the Dennett vs Caruso stuff on just deserts and punishment over the last couple weeks, and the paper is "apply the reading to an actual case." I want to do Norway and Breivik: 2011 attacks, Utøya and the government quarter, 77 killed, convicted in 2012, got the maximum civilian sentence of 21 years in preventive detention that can be extended indefinitely if he's still judged dangerous, and he's been suing for years about his conditions, especially isolation (partial win in 2016 that got overturned on appeal, later cases failed). I know the shape of the disagreement between the two views already, so please don't spend pages re-explaining it — go straight to the mapping.

What I want:

1. Set the case up the way each theory would have to. Who the relevant actors are (the court, the correctional service, the Norwegian public, the foreign press, Breivik himself), what the actual options on the table were, and what counts as evidence. Disciplined structure, not vibes.

2. The bit I keep getting stuck on, which I think is where the real interest is: the most contested part of the trial was whether Breivik was criminally responsible — first forensic team said paranoid schizophrenia, the second said no psychosis but narcissistic and antisocial personality disorders, and the court ended up treating him as sane. Breivik himself wanted to be found sane, since a psychosis verdict would have destroyed the political meaning of what he did. But on Caruso's quarantine model, shouldn't none of that matter to whether you confine someone and for how long? Dangerousness is dangerousness. So why does a system that has supposedly given up on desert spend its most contested days litigating responsibility? Is that dead weight — leftover retributive reflex that could be abolished without loss — or is it doing work the quarantine model can't do for itself?

3. A test I made up, tell me if it's a bad one: suppose that after the verdict new evidence came out — a tumor, a genetic finding, a much stronger causal story about his childhood — that made the acts look substantially less like his own doing. Caruso should say: nothing changes except any reassessment of future risk. Dennett should say what, exactly? If desert is an engineered, forward-looking practice whose point is to make people more responsible, does a discovered tumor change anything, and if it doesn't, what's left of the difference between him and Caruso here? If my test doesn't discriminate between them, say so and give me one that does.

4. The conditions question. Caruso is explicit that quarantine isn't supposed to be afflictive — you can make it comfortable, you just don't let the person out. But Breivik's actual regime involves a lot of isolation, which he has challenged repeatedly. Is severe social isolation in an otherwise decent cell a quarantine measure or desert smuggled back in? Notice the odd shape of it: on quarantine logic, the more dangerous you are the longer you're held, not the worse you're treated. So is there any non-punitive justification the model can actually offer for the isolation, or is it a case where the model is silent?

5. The direction everyone skips: what does the case do to the theory? Norway is about the closest thing there is to a real, functioning, low-retributive system, and it gets wheeled out as evidence that abolition is politically and practically workable. Does it actually vindicate Caruso, or does it confirm Dennett's suspicion that the retributive structure reappears in disguise — indefinite preventive detention keyed to predicted dangerousness looks a lot like life without parole under another name? Pick one. I want a verdict, not "both have a point."

6. Where the theory goes quiet. Two things bother me and I want to know if I'm wrong about either. First, risk prediction: for a case this extreme, a one-off political mass-casualty attack, dangerousness assessment is close to uncalibrated, and yet the quarantine model hands the whole decision to it. Second, the expressive function: a trial like this is partly an official statement about what happened to 77 people and to the country, and neither Dennett nor Caruso seems to have much to say about that except as deterrence or denunciation. Is that a real gap or am I smuggling in retributivism under a nicer label?

Be committed: give me definite answers on 2, 4 and 5 with reasons, and say what would change your mind about each. On a seperate note, flag anything in my setup that you think is factually off or a contested reading of either author — I'd rather hear it from you than in the comments from my TA.
