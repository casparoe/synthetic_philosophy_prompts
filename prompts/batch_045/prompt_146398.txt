I'm 71 and retired. I spent about 35 years in experimental condensed matter — scattering, phase transitions, a lot of coarse-grained modelling, and more time than I care to remember fitting models to data that underdetermined them. In the last year or so I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university, and I've got stuck on the zombie / conceivability argument. I think I roughly see what it's supposed to be: you can conceive of something physically identical to a human being with no experience going on at all, so the phenomenal facts don't follow from the physical facts, so physicalism is false. But I dont trust that I've got it, because every time I try to say it to myself in the vocabulary I actually think in, it either turns out trivially true or it vanishes, and I can't tell which of those is the argument's fault and which is mine.

So: restate the argument — the conceivability premise, the step from conceivability to possibility, and what the conclusion is even supposed to be — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of a working physicist, and then tell me what didn't survive the translation.

By vocabulary I mean things like: state spaces and points in them; observables and expectation values; an (effective) Hamiltonian plus initial conditions; coarse-graining and reduced descriptions; macrovariables as functions on microstates; symmetries, degeneracy, gauge freedom versus gauge-invariant quantities; decoupled or spectator sectors; relevant and irrelevant couplings in the renormalization-group sense; models fit to data where the data don't fix the model; and the fact that a "complete" description is always a description at some level and never the whole thing. Assume I'm fluent in all of that and don't explain it back to me.

Some specific things I want mapped, because they're where my own attempt keeps breaking:

1. Supervenience. I want to write it as: there's a map f from physical states to phenomenal states, no phenomenal difference without a physical difference means f is a well-defined function on the physical state space, and the zombie claim is that there's a physically identical world in which f sends every human-scale state to the null experience — i.e. that f could have been the trivial map. Is that fair? And if it is, where does the modal weight actually sit? In my world, once you fix the Hamiltonian and the state you've fixed everything, so the only ways to get the trivial map are to change something that isn't in the physical description — which just is dualism, and makes the argument look circular — or to claim the physical description was never complete in the relevant sense. I want to know which of those the argument is genuinely doing, or whether it's doing something third that my formulation can't see.

2. My hunch, which I suspect is too glib: I keep wanting to say the phenomenal is either a gauge freedom or a hidden variable. If it's gauge — no observable's expectation value differs anywhere — then by my own lights the zombie world isn't a distinct world at all, it's the same world in different coordinates, and the argument has no conclusion. If it's a hidden variable — an extra decoupled sector bolted on at zero coupling — then zombies are trivially possible but so is every other invisible addition, and the argument has proved too much. Where does that dichotomy go wrong? In particular: are "no difference to any observable" and "no causal work" the same condition here? In my vocabulary they come apart in ways I suspect they don't in the philosophers'.

3. The irrelevant-coupling version. This is the bit I made up and can't evaluate. Suppose we say: the phenomenal sector is decoupled, so it's RG-irrelevant in the strongest possible sense, and the zombie world is just the one where its value is set to zero. In physics, a decoupled empirically-inert sector is something I would delete, not something I would call real. But the other side says: it's there, it's simply not in the algebra of observables, and that's a fact about my theory rather than about the world. Is that a honest restatement of the disagreement, or have I quietly converted a metaphysical dispute into a methodological one about what counts as positing something?

4. Structure and intrinsic nature. I keep running into a move — Russellian monism? I may be mangling the name — that physics only ever specifies relations and dispositions, that the mathematics fixes the structure and the roles and never what the things are "in themselves," and that consciousness might be the intrinsic nature of whatever occupies those roles. In my vocabulary this smells like: the formalism specifies an algebra of observables and a flow, and is silent about what the underlying degrees of freedom are, which is precisely why the same equations will serve for phonons, spins or field amplitudes depending on what you hang on them. Same point or a different one? And does the move actually give the zombie arguer anything, or does it just relocate the gap one step sideways?

5. Conceivability to possibility. In my field "I can conceive of it" is worth roughly nothing. I can conceive of a world with the same Hamiltonian and different initial conditions; I cannot conceive of one with the same Hamiltonian, the same state, and different outcomes, unless the dynamics is ill-posed or indeterministic. So what is supposed to be doing the work in the conceivability premise — ideal reflection, consistency with the laws, or mere absence of contradiction in the description? Does my vocabulary contain anything corresponding to the difference between the last two? My feeling is that a lot of the argument's force comes from sliding between them, but I can't say that precisely yet.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

- Which claims carried over without loss?
- Which ones you had to bend to fit — and be explicit about where you smuggled in an assumption from my vocabulary (say, that to be real is to make a difference to something measurable, or that a theory is a device for getting from states to expectation values) rather than translating at all.
- What has no counterpart whatsoever on my side? My guess is something about what-it-is-like / the first-personal, and something about necessity being metaphysical rather than nomological, but tell me if I've guessed wrong.
- And the verdict: is the leftover a blind spot in my vocabulary, or a sign that the argument was never saying anything as sharp as it sounds? Or both — in which case the interesting question is which parts are which.

Two constraints, sorry to be a pain. Don't point me at papers or reading lists; I've no library access left and I'd much rather have you put it badly in your own words than attribute something I can't check. And do the translation honestly: if the argument looks silly once it's in my vocabulary, say so, and say equally clearly if the silliness is an artefact of the translation. I'd rather come away knowing which of those it is than come away reassured.
