Me and my old PhD housemate have been having literally the same argument for nine years (whether the housing market in this city is a bubble — doesn't matter, don't worry about the object level, it could be anything) and we've somehow agreed to give a joint talk about it next month to a rational-choice crowd, both of us on stage disagreeing in public. We want to close by reading a poem together, one voice each, and then the last three lines in unison. I was thinking a sestina: six stanzas of six, six end-words rotated in the proper pattern, three-line envoy at the end — because the rotation feels exactly like the protocol in the Aumann agreement result, the same few words coming round again and again but every time in a new order and in the other person's mouth, until nothing more can be said. Two voices, one roman and one italic, and I'd like the italics to stop partway through the envoy so you can see on the page that there's only one voice left. The six end-words should be the theory's own vocabulary — prior, cell, partition, odds, whatever — not rain and stone and sea.

On the philosophy: I don't want "rational people have to agree", which is just wrong. What I want to come through is that the disagreement is completely fine right up until one of us says the number out loud — the work is done by the shared prior plus the posteriors actually becoming common knowledge, and two people who never announce anything can sit at different numbers forever with nobody being irrational. Second: what travels between them is the number, not the reasons. He's explained his reasoning to me a hundred times over beer and it's never moved me an inch, and the result says that him telling me how sure he is does something his evidence couldn't. Third, and this is the bit I care most about: don't make the ending a compromise. Let them land somewhere that isn't between the two numbers they started at (start them around a third and four-fifths, say), and if you can pull it off, have the number they converge on be one that neither of them would get by simply pooling what they each actually know — trading numbers can leave them saying the same thing while each still holds something back, and at the limit the number they agree on can be the prior itself, the one either of them would have given before either of them looked at anything at all. I want that to land as a chill, not as a reconciliation. And please: the prior is not a real time before the evidence, no garden we were both in once and are now walking back into — it's a standpoint neither of us ever occupied, and I'd rather the poem feel that than fix it. Also, someone who actually knows this literature should be able to tell why it's a sestina and not a sonnet; don't just say "Bayesian" three times and call it a night, and don't put Aumann's name in the poem, we've already got him on the first slide.
