I work in the secretariat of the standing committee that holds together a merged regional health network. The merger joined two systems whose leadership operate with incompatible frameworks. I'll call them the Directive bloc: it holds that certain acts are wrong in themselves, that an institution can be implicated in them by what it does, pays for, or publicly affirms, and that its own involvement in an act matters more to it than the acts of others that it merely fails to stop. And the Choice bloc: it holds that what matters is what patients want and what improves their health, and that nothing is wrong apart from its effects on people's lives.

The committee exists to broker packages. Every package gives something to each bloc, and the alternative to any package is dissolution: each bloc keeps its own hospitals and loses shared purchasing, the joint capital fund, and the combined residency program. We have five precedents, and a sixth proposal to decide next month. I've kept the drafting details in my summaries, because our decisions have turned on them.

1. Approved. Directive-side sites do not provide sterilization or contraception. Their clinicians hand the patient a preprinted card with the phone number of a separately incorporated provider that leases space on the same campus; they do not schedule, transfer records, or discuss it. In exchange, the Choice bloc accepted a network-wide forty-eight-hour reflection period and a documented counseling session before any such procedure, including on its own sites.

2. Rejected. A single network-wide clinical protocol under which Directive-side pharmacists dispense contraceptives prescribed by Choice-side clinicians, logged in the shared record as external prescriptions. The Directive side's argument was that filling a prescription someone else wrote is not its act. In exchange, the Choice bloc would have funded a full-time ethics counselor at every Directive-side hospital and closed two jointly branded clinics.

3. Approved. The central capital budget — funded by both blocs' facilities in proportion to revenue, with no earmarking and no accounting of whose money paid for which rooms — pays for a new wing at a Choice-side hospital that will house the service. The joint public statement describes the project only as expanding access to comprehensive care for the communities we serve, and each bloc may explain its own reasons to its own constituency.

4. Rejected. A line item paying the separately incorporated provider a fixed fee per procedure for the first hundred and fifty procedures each year, after which network funding stops. In exchange, the Choice bloc accepted the Directive bloc's framework for a different service and dropped its push for a third.

5. Rejected. A package whose substantive terms were the same as precedent 1's, except that both blocs' leadership were required to sign a joint Statement of Shared Commitments saying that the network affirms each patient's right to make their own reproductive decisions. The minutes record that the terms themselves were acceptable; the statement was not.

The new proposal: every patient in the network, including at Directive-side sites, is seen by one intake clinician, who may belong to either bloc. Where the patient's request is for something the Directive side does not provide, the intake clinician enters a single standardized order into the shared record — no discussion, no discretion, no verbal encouragement — and the system schedules the patient at a Choice-side site. The entry is labelled a transfer of care to another network provider rather than a referral, and the board minute would give the rationale as administrative continuity. Neither bloc would have to sign anything about patients' rights, and each may post its own explanatory note to its own staff and patients. In exchange, the Choice bloc accepts the Directive bloc's triage protocol network-wide and a prohibition on the service after twenty-eight weeks except where the patient's life is at risk. The Directive bloc's own counsel has objected that an order raised under a Directive-side site's name makes the service part of what that site officially offers, which is not the same as knowing that another provider exists.

What I need from you:

Set out the rules our five verdicts are jointly consistent with. I can sketch three that seem to fit — one turning on the role the bloc occupies in bringing the act about, per-instance and traceable versus pooled and structural; one turning on whether each bloc comes out better off by its own lights than under dissolution, counting what it prevents against what it takes part in; and one turning on whether a bloc is made to affirm the other side's framing rather than merely accept the outcome. Say what each predicts here, and where they come apart.

Then give me your best guess at the decision consistency with ourselves requires, with your reasons, and identify the closest analog — the preprinted card, the dispensing pharmacist, the undifferentiated capital fund, or the per-procedure fee. Note that our record contains one case where we accepted a re-description of a bloc's involvement (no earmarking) and one where we refused one (external prescriptions), so part of what I need is an account of what makes a re-description legitimate, and whether the transfer-of-care labelling passes it.

If our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, tell me what principle we would be adopting with each choice and what that principle would commit us to in the cases that follow. Name the variant of this proposal that would separate the leading rules — for instance, what changes if the order is generated automatically from a box the patient ticks, if the intake is done by a third-party call centre belonging to neither bloc, or if the board minute's rationale had to be stated in the Choice bloc's terms. And if you think the honest reading of our track record is deflationary — that we have never been tracking the shape of compromise at all, only which bloc could most credibly walk away — say so, and say what that predicts here.
