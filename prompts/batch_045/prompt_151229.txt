I convene a fortnightly decision-theory reading group — mostly graduate students in philosophy and statistics, plus two quant friends who insist on turning every example into a spreadsheet. For about a year the group has been split into two camps over Kelly betting and geometric rationality, and instead of letting them argue past each other for a second year I want them to produce the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration would produce: the claims both sides can sign, the exact residual disagreement stated in wording each side would accept for its own view, the cruxes, and, for each crux, a concrete test or case both would agree bears on it with each side's prediction written down in advance. Call one camp Team Time-Average: it treats maximization of the long-run growth rate — equivalently, expected log wealth per round — as the normatively basic criterion for choice under multiplicative dynamics, and it reads fractional Kelly either as a hedge against model error or as a separate taste for smooth wealth paths. Call the other Team Utility: it treats expected-utility maximization over terminal wealth as basic, with the utility function a primitive that is not to be read off any long-run argument, and it reads the growth criterion as either a special case of its own theory (log utility) or an optional extra that a finitely-lived agent need not care about. Both camps accept the other's computations; the fight is about which criterion is basic and in what circumstances, and about how much of the dispute is really about words.

To keep it concrete, stipulate the house game the group keeps using: even-money bets on a coin that comes up heads with probability 0.6, offered once per round, stakes continuously divisible, no house limit and no minimum, full reinvestment, no outside income, so that the Kelly fraction is 0.2 and half-Kelly retains roughly three-quarters of the maximal growth rate while halving the variance of the log-wealth path. I want the document to work in that game and in four variants of it: (i) an unknown stopping rule — the number of rounds is drawn from a distribution the bettor does not control, say a constant hazard of the game being shut down, or a stopping time chosen by an adversary who watches the bettor's wealth; (ii) the pooled-copy protocol — M bettors play independent copies of the same game and their terminal wealths are added and divided evenly, rather than one bettor compounding alone; (iii) the target protocol — the bettor wants to reach a fixed multiple of initial wealth before going broke, with no time limit, which one member of the group insists is a setting in which arbitrarily small bets beat any fixed positive fraction and in which full Kelly maximizes nothing at all; and (iv) an additive component — a fixed income stream, or a margin or position constraint, so wealth is not purely multiplicative. Here are the cruxes I want covered, though please re-cut them and tell me plainly where my framing is wrong: whether the growth camp's appeal to almost-sure eventual dominance is a decision criterion at all when the crossing time can exceed any horizon anyone cares about, and what the finite-horizon distribution for Kelly versus more aggressive fractions actually looks like at, say, forty rounds and four hundred rounds; whether the stopping-rule robustness the growth camp likes to attribute to log — the claim that one fixed fraction is optimal however the process is stopped — is special to log or shared across the constant-relative-risk-aversion family, and what it establishes either way; whether model uncertainty in large samples can justify a first-order shade below full Kelly or only a second-order one, given that the growth rate is flat to first order at its maximum, so that habitual half-Kelly looks to one side like a relative risk aversion of about two and to the other like prudent hedging against parameter error; how the verdict in the pooled-copy variant depends on the number of copies and on the horizon, given that a lognormal mean is dominated by rare enormous outcomes and that convergence of the sample average can be uselessly slow; and what either camp has to say to an agent with a target rather than a growth objective. Where a crux is decidable by a calculation or a simulation both sides would accept, say so and specify it; where it is not decidable that way, say why.

Please return a single JSON object and nothing else, with exactly these fields. "preamble": one jointly signable paragraph of 80–120 words setting up the house game and saying what the document is and is not. "agreed_claims": an array of objects with keys id (A1, A2, …), claim, basis (one of mathematical, conceptual, methodological), and epistemic_status (one of theorem, folklore, numerically checked, asserted by one camp only). "disputes": an array of objects with keys id (D1, D2, …), topic, growth_camp_wording, utility_camp_wording, verbal_or_genuine, and diagnosis — I want formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and for each dispute that is merely verbal, the reformulation both sides could sign instead. "cruxes": an array of objects with keys id (C1, C2, …), crux, linked_disputes (list of dispute ids), test, decidable (true or false), growth_camp_prediction, utility_camp_prediction, growth_camp_defeater, utility_camp_defeater — predictions should be stated so they could be checked against a simulation, and the defeater fields should say what observation would genuinely move that side, not merely embarrass it. "scope_concessions": an array of objects with keys id, condition, who_concedes, and concession, covering the cases in which one side grants that the other's criterion is the right one to apply. "signatures": an array of two objects with keys camp and reservation, one line each, as if both parties were signing with a caveat. Do not cite, quote, or allude to any published source — the model I am running this on has no web access, and I want the arguments rather than the literature — and wherever you are not confident about a mathematical claim, flag it in the epistemic_status field rather than stating it flatly.
