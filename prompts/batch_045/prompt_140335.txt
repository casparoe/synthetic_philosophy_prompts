I teach a graduate seminar on naturalizing mental content, and we have just reached the point in the syllabus where the teleological family stops looking like one view and starts looking like two: a consumer-based version, on which the content of a state is fixed by what the mechanisms that consume that state need it to be about, under Normal conditions, for their own successful performance to be explainable, and a producer-based or indicator version, on which content is fixed by what the mechanism that produces the state has the etiological function of indicating. My students can recite the frog-and-flies case in their sleep, and they have already learned the standard absorptive move whereby an awkward example is defused by declaring content indeterminate between a distal and a proximal reading. What I want from you is a case built to order for a problem set: half the room will be assigned one version of the theory and half the other, and each group must derive a verdict from the mechanics of its own theory rather than read the verdict off my description. So nothing in the case may stipulate what a theory says.

The specifications are these. There is one organism, one internal state at one time — call it S — and two verdicts I want forced apart: the producer-side reading delivers content Q, the consumer-side reading delivers content P, and P and Q are genuinely distinct, not related as determinable to determinate, as whole to part, as conjunction to conjunct, or as distal to proximal; the disagreement must be about which property S is about, at a single level of grain and a single level of causal distance. Both readings must be veridical in the situation described: no deception, no mimicry, no error, and no malfunctioning mechanism. The producing mechanism is doing exactly what it was selected to do, the consuming mechanisms are doing exactly what they were selected to do, and the organism is succeeding at whatever it is doing; the point is not to hand teleosemantics its usual easy win about misrepresentation but to make the two teleological theories diverge while everything goes right. I also want the divergence to be invisible from outside the family: a plain causal-covariation or information-tracking account of content should come out indifferent between P and Q, or indeterminate between them, so that whatever does the work is specifically etiological — the gap between what S was selected to indicate and what S's consumers need it to indicate. On plausibility: no molecule-by-molecule duplicates, no swamp-creatures, no neurosurgery, no artifacts with stipulated purposes, nothing that requires science fiction. The selection history should be the sort of thing a working evolutionary biologist could tell without embarrassment, and it should be described in a paragraph or two at most, since students have to read it in a sitting.

Here is the structural trick I have been toying with, which you should feel free to discard if it cannot be made to work: cases in which the selection pressure that shaped the mechanism producing S came from a lineage other than the organism's own — manipulation by a parasite, brood parasitism, a domesticated or symbiotic species, a signal whose fitness effects land on a genome different from the one that builds the receiver — so that the question of whose selection history fixes the content of S becomes live rather than rhetorical. My hope is that this lets producer-function and consumer-need come apart cleanly without anyone's being deceived or broken. If it cannot, say why and build the case some other way; I would rather have a case that works than a case that uses my idea. What I want back: the scenario first, kept short; then, under headings, (i) the verdict each theory gives and the derivation of it, spelled out far enough that a student can check the steps rather than trust you; (ii) an honest statement of which single feature of the case is doing the work; (iii) what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed or varied — in particular whether the case then collapses into something the literature already has a name for, such as the distality problem, and if so which; (iv) the strongest reply available to whichever theory comes out looking worse, phrased as an actual defender of that theory would phrase it, together with your assessment of whether it is a local patch or a general liability; and (v) the minimal change to the case that flips which theory gets the more intuitive verdict, so I can run the two versions back to back and show the class that the intuition is tracking the theory and not the biology.
