Hey — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working up a short piece on why we happily pay to watch films and read novels that make us miserable, and I could use a second opionion before I commit to an angle.

My editor wants an actual verdict, so here's how I've got it framed. The puzzle is that we seek out an experience whose whole point is a feeling we otherwise run from, and there seem to be only two live ways out. Option (a): the painful feeling itself gets turned into pleasure by the artistry — the distress is real but gets converted, roughly Hume's story. Option (b): nothing gets converted. The distress stays plain distress, and it's simply outweighed by the pleasure of the craft, the music, the beautiful sentences, the faint prestige of having felt something deep.

I'm ruling out the whole "you don't really feel anything, you know it's fiction" line, because the body clearly reacts: people sob, heart rate climbs, skin conductance spikes. Whatever is going on, it isn't fake.

I lean (b), partly because of a pet idea I want to include: sad art is grief practice — a safe dose of a threat-response we need to keep calibrated, the way a vaccine is a safe dose of a pathogen. That's still a compensatory story, since the shot is unpleasant in itself.

So: which of (a) and (b) is really the more defensible, and does the practice/vaccine framing buy me anything the standard versions don't already have?
