I work as a backend engineer and have no formal philosophy background, so my instinct is to treat an argument the way I'd treat a specification: what are the inputs, what is the feedback loop, and where exactly does it break. There's a distinction I keep losing my grip on in the socialist calculation debate, and I think I'd get it faster by working through a concrete design than through summaries. So here is a design. It's hypothetical; I'm not asking whether anything like it would be politically achievable.

- All firms are state-owned. Capital goods — steel, machine tools, factory floor space, rail slots, electrical capacity — are never bought and sold between firms. A Central Planning Board allocates them.
- The Board runs what I can only describe as a simulation. It publishes a price vector; every firm reports how much of each input it would want and how much output it would produce at those prices; the Board nudges prices up where reported demand exceeds reported supply and down where the reverse holds, and iterates until the reports roughly reconcile. To me this looks like gradient descent on a mismatch penalty, or like a numerical solver finding a fixed point.
- Consumer goods really are sold to households for money, and households spend genuine wages on them.
- Managers receive bonuses proportional to the 'profit' their unit books at the Board's shadow prices.
- One wrinkle I want to insist on: those bonuses are paid in a voucher that can be spent only on consumer goods, cannot be saved, and cannot be lent or exchanged for money. Nobody can hold a claim on future production, and no interest rate emerges from any part of the system.
