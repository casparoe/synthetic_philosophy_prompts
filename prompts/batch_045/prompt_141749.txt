I write a fortnightly column for a general-interest magazine on the behavioural-science side of consumer tech — I got into it after an argument with my brother-in-law, who designs onboarding flows at a payments company and who insisted that every "exploitative" pattern I complain about is just a firm giving people what they actually want at the moment they want it. Since then I've started throwing "money pump" and "diachronic irrationality" around whenever I describe a product: streak mechanics plus a paid streak freeze in a language app, a delivery pass whose per-order discount resets if you let it lapse for a month, the cancel flow that offers 40% off and then quietly restores full price two cycles later, annual prepay versus pay-as-you-go. My editor has started pushing back. She says that at this point "money pump" in my copy is just a thesaurus entry for "trick", the standards desk gets nervous when we accuse named companies of things, and she's not definitly wrong.

So what I want is a decision procedure I can actually run in about twenty minutes, from screenshots and the signup/upgrade/cancel copy, before I file — the questions in order, what counts as a yes and what counts as a no at each, the point where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to me because what's left is a values question rather than a factual one, and a plain-English gloss of each step that my standards editor (who has maybe six minutes for me) can read without knowing any of the theory. Some things it has to handle and I don't know how: a genuine cycle versus an ordinary change of mind when someone learns something or their circumstances change; whether the firm merely found the cycle or built the schedule that creates it; whether it matters that the person ends up back in the state they started in but poorer, as opposed to having paid for something they did consume; whether a cheap exit or an available commitment device defeats the charge; and the version I keep getting stuck on, where the same machinery runs backwards — a deposit contract that charges you when you skip the gym, or a savings transfer that auto-escalates on every raise — and seems to pump money out of you using exactly the mechanism I'm criticising, but on behalf of the person who signed up rather than the firm. Also, the money doesn't always land with the firm: in two products I'm looking at the surplus is captured by advertisers or by other users, and I don't know whether "money pump" still names anything when there's no one doing the pumping.

Then run the whole thing on three cases. One: the streak-and-streak-freeze language app. Two: an annual gym contract sold to someone who'd be better off paying per visit, and, as a contrast, the deposit contract that fines them for not going. Three: a case where I'm not going to like the answer — my suggestion is plain auto-renewal, which I've attacked in print twice and which I half suspect isn't a pump at all, because the customer isn't cycling back to a state they were in before, they're just not paying attention. Be explicit about which steps are real tests and which are judgement calls dressed up as tests, and be straight about what the procedure can't tell me: in particular whether pumping someone's cycles is wrong if they seem content at every single moment, and whether using the concept at all commits me to a view about which time-slice of a person is the real one — because if it does, I'd rather know that's what I'm doing when I call a company exploitative in print.
