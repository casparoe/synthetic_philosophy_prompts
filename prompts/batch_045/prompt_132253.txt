Here is the question I keep failing to settle: can 'avoid side effects' be defined as a desideratum for an agent without something close to a full specification of value? The standard move is to define impact counterfactually — penalize divergence from a baseline, usually the inaction baseline of what would have happened had the agent done nothing — via something like state-distance or relative reachability, or via attainable-utility-preservation style measures that penalize change in the agent's ability to optimize a bank of auxiliary reward functions. My worry is that this is Goodhart-adjacent in a way the literature understates: the baseline, the horizon, the discount, and the choice of auxiliary set each look like a place where value gets smuggled back in, so that what is really being offered is not 'a way to avoid specifying values' but 'a way of buying conservatism under value uncertainty, plus a lot of unannounced value content.' If that is the honest description I can live with it; I would like to know whether it is, and what would show it is not.

I want to test this on a case rather than in the abstract. Take the release of cane toads into Queensland in the 1930s. Stipulated facts, which you should take as given; if you think one is wrong, flag it in a line and do not lean on it: in the mid-1930s a small imported population of toads was released into sugarcane country to control beetle pests whose larvae damage cane roots. The toads did not meaningfully control the beetles. They established, spread across a very large area of northern Australia over the following decades, and are toxic at every life stage to native predators with no evolutionary exposure to them, several of which declined as a result. Current management is mostly about slowing spread at the frontier rather than removal, and it is expensive. The decision was made deliberately, by people with institutional standing, on the object-level merits, and it was not obviously irrational by the standards of its time.

3. Engage with a distinction I have been using and tell me whether it survives. I have been separating three things that get lumped together as 'side effects': option loss, where the intervention reduces what the principal can still bring about later; externalized harm, where it damages things the principal would have counted as valuable had the question been raised; and corruption of the feedback channel, where it degrades the principal's ability to notice what is happening at all. My suspicion is that baseline-relative measures get real purchase on the first, essentially none on the second without value content in the auxiliary set, and are structurally blind to the third, since the third damages the measurement apparatus rather than the world. 4. Deal with what I think of as the release-action problem, which I have not seen handled. Releasing a self-replicating population is not an action with a bounded footprint; the actual world and the counterfactual world diverge without bound, and the damage is done by a process the deployer no longer controls. A penalty computed against an inaction baseline over any fixed horizon will systematically underestimate this, and perhaps underestimate it arbitrarily badly. Is there a natural repair — discounted infinite-horizon baselines, penalizing change in the dynamics or in the agent's own future reachability rather than in the state, a special category for irreversible release? Or is this the point at which the framework is revealed to be built for one-shot optimizers and quietly presupposes that agency stays where you put it?
