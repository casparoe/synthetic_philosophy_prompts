Longtime reader of the rationalist blogs here — since the old sequences days — which I mention only because it probably makes me over-generous to answers that are framework-first and hand-wavy about the actual texts, so I'd like a second opinion on this one.

We set the Dennett–Caruso exchange on just deserts and punishment, plus a position of my own construction that started life as a comment thread on one of the forums. Here's the question I handed out, and below it one student's answer.

THE STEM I GAVE THEM (they were told to work with my paraphrase rather than with whatever they half-remember from the book):

Caruso's free will scepticism: moral responsibility in the basic desert sense — the sense on which a wrongdoer could deserve to suffer simply in virtue of having done wrong, prior to and independently of any good that comes of making him suffer — requires a kind of ultimate authorship of one's own will that we don't have. So nobody deserves to be punished, and retributivism is false. But that isn't quietism: on his public health / quarantine model, the state may restrain, treat and reform people who have actually caused harm, on the analogy of quarantining a carrier. The justification is forward-looking (dangerousness, reform, deterrence) and is a rights-based justification of the self-defence / harm-prevention sort, not a consequentialist maximizing one; it is keyed to the risk someone poses rather than to what he deserves; and it does not license locking up people who are merely statistically dangerous but have done nothing, since that would be punishing them for who they are.

Dennett's side: the compatibilist thought, in his version, is that what responsibility actually requires is something we do have — being an agent who is responsive to reasons, who can be moved and improved by praise, blame and the standing expectation that he'll do better. He thinks Caruso inflates what responsibility would have to be, and that desert-talk and blame do real work in holding a moral community together, work the quarantine model either can't do or quietly helps itself to. He is nevertheless not signing up for payback retribution, and he wants a far less punitive system than the one we run.

THE VIEW I INVENTED, which I called the compression view, to be assumed true for the purposes of the question: when ordinary people and sentencing judges say that so-and-so deserves five years, the judgment doing the work is a lossy compression of four strands — (a) an implicit estimate of the offender's future dangerousness, (b) the general-deterrence value of a sanction of that size, (c) the denunciatory or expressive effect on the community, and (d) a residue that isn't forward-looking at all: a judgment about the quality of will the agent manifested in acting. It's lossy in the sense that the strands can't be recovered from the desert verdict, and they can come apart: someone whose will was rotten but who is now plainly harmless, or someone who is dangerous but whose will was barely culpable at all.

THE QUESTION (three parts, 6 / 6 / 8 marks):

(1) Does the compression view, if true, support Caruso, Dennett, or neither? Say why.
(2) Can strand (d), the judgment about quality of will, survive scepticism about basic desert? Is it itself a form of desert judgment, or is it something a sceptic can keep?
(3) What does the compression view imply about whether desert-talk should be kept at sentencing — i.e. is it in principle replaceable by explicit findings about (a), (b) and (c)?

THE ANSWER I'M GRADING:

"Caruso's target is basic desert: the idea that a person could deserve to suffer just because he did wrong, independent of any good consequences of making him suffer. That is a backward-looking property, and it's what the sceptic says you can't have without being the ultimate source of your own will. The quarantine model is what's left once you drop it, and it isn't the same thing as deterrence-maximising consequentialism: on Caruso's version you can only restrain someone who has actually caused harm, only for as long as he poses the risk, and the proportionality is to the danger rather than to desert, which is why the model doesn't permit preventive detention of the merely statistically dangerous. That matters, because otherwise Caruso would collapse into exactly the sort of consequentialism he's supposed to be offering as an alternative. (And of course, if determinism is true nobody could have done otherwise anyway, so the compatibilist must be talking about something other than what the incompatibilist means by responsibility.)

Where the compression view is concerned, I don't think it obviously helps either of them just on its face, because it's a claim about what our desert judgments are made of, not about whether anyone could ever really deserve anything. Dennett can accept the psychological claim and Caruso can accept it too. The interesting part is strand (d) versus strand (a), because those are the two that come apart in the cases the question gives: the person whose will was rotten but who is now harmless, and the person who is dangerous but whose will was barely culpable. Dennett's reasons-responsiveness criterion is arguably just a way of tracking (a) anyway, since being reasons-responsive is what makes someone predictable and educable.

I also think Dennett's actual position is best understood as fictionalist: he thinks desert ascriptions are, strictly speaking, false, but that we should go on making them because holding people responsible does useful social work — shaping agents, sustaining the moral community, and so on. If that's right then the real disagreement between him and Caruso isn't metaphysical at all; they agree that nobody has the sort of free will that would underwrite desert, and they differ only about what policy to adopt given that. Caruso wants to replace desert-talk with quarantine; Dennett wants to keep the talk because it works.

And if the compression view is true, I think that settles the practical question in favour of replacing the talk. Since a desert verdict is just a lossy compression of (a), (b) and (c), it is strictly false whenever those strands diverge from it, and the honest thing is to decompress: instead of saying that the offender deserves five years, a judge should hand down an explicit actuarial dangerousness estimate, an explicit estimate of the deterrence value of the sanction, and an explicit statement of what the sentence is meant to express. Nothing is lost in translation, because everything the desert verdict was tracking is now stated in the clear. That is basically the quarantine model done properly, with better epistemics."
