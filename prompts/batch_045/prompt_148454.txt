I spent thirty years as a crystallographer — solid-state NMR, mostly — so I'm used to objects that are exactly alike in every intrinsic respect and told apart only by the site they sit in, and lately I've been poking at some varients of Black's two-sphere argument against the Identity of Indiscernibles. Take the principle the way I want it: nothing can be exactly alike, where "alike" covers all the relations they stand in to everything else, not just their own intrinsic features. Rank these four set-ups by how much trouble they give someone trying to hold on to that, hardest first, one sentence of justification each:

(a) the plain case: two exactly similar spheres a couple of kilometres apart in an otherwise empty Euclidean space.

(b) the mirror pair: the same two spheres, but the rest of the universe is crowded with all sorts of different, mutually distinguishable objects in a thoroughly lopsided arrangement, and every one of them happens to sit exactly in the plane that perpendicularly bisects the line joining my two spheres.

(c) the triangle: three exactly similar spheres at the corners of an isosceles but not equilateral triangle, so two of them are in each other's shoes while the third is plainly different from both.

(d) the odd one out: three spheres again, two exactly similar to each other and the third different from both, but arranged so that the arrangement has no symmetry at all — no reflection, rotation or swap leaves every inter-sphere distance unchanged — with nothing else in the universe, no background space with marked places, nothing to anchor them.

If one of the four isn't really a case of indiscernibles at all, put it at the bottom and say what's wrong with it. And tell me whether my lab instinct — two atoms in equivalent sites are as numerically distinct as anything, and there's plainly two of them — is doing real work here or just helping itself to the answer; I cant quite tell.
