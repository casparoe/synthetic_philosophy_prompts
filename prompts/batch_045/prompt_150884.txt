You are a philosopher of mind who also works on collective agency and the metaphysics of composition. Please think carefully and at some length. Before you rule on anything, spend a moment explicitly working out, for yourself, which considerations you take to be genuinely relevant to whether a social insect colony could be a minded thing, and keep that list in view as you go. Do not cite authors, papers, or books, and do not suggest readings: reason from the case as described.

Last week at a dinner party, two friends of mine got into an argument about Hofstadter's Ant Fugue, the passage where he converses with an ant colony he calls Aunt Hillary and invites us to take seriously the idea that she might have a mind. I was there for the whole exchange and could not referee it, and I would rather not say which of them I instinctively lean toward, because I want your independent read. Here is roughly how it went.

(1) Nadia opened by saying yes, literally and not as a metaphor: the colony gets along in the world by tracking and acting on states of affairs that no single ant tracks — that there is food forty metres to the southwest, that the nest wall has been breached, that of the several candidate nest sites the one under the flat stone is the best. In house-hunting species, she pointed out, scouts visit candidate sites independently and the colony emigrates only once enough scouts have converged on the same one; no ant ever compares two sites, yet the colony reliably picks the better. She called this the granularity gap: the colony represents the world at finer grain and wider spatial span than any of its parts, which is exactly the relation a brain bears to its neurons. Refusing the colony the courtesy we extend to brains is, she said, chauvinism about skulls and wiring.

(2) Owen replied that brains and colonies differ in kind, not just in speed. Neurons are wired into a single dense, fast, richly recurrent network attached to one body whose fate is shared and whose reward signals are shared; ants coordinate mostly by leaving traces in the environment and bumping into one another, a fragment at a time, over minutes or hours. And nothing in the colony ever has the whole picture available to it — there is no place where the colony's take on things is present to the colony in the way a scene is present to you. Above all, he said, there is nothing it is like to be Aunt Hillary: no unified conscious experience. Take that away and talk of her mind is just a colourful way of describing behaviour.

(3) Nadia answered that Owen had quietly switched the question. Intentionality and consciousness, she said, come apart: we ascribe beliefs and goals to all sorts of systems with no glimmer of phenomenology, and a colony can genuinely represent and want while being dark inside; whether anything it is like to be her is a separate question she was happy to answer no. And integration, she added, is a matter of degree, and degree is not disqualifying. She then offered a hypothetical: imagine a species whose nests are strung across an archipelago, linked only by trails that take weeks to walk, so that any signal crossing the whole system takes weeks. If you observed such a system at the right scale you would see the same distributed, error-correcting dynamics, just slowed by orders of magnitude; only our impatience makes it look like an ecosystem rather than a slow mind.

(4) Owen's response was that the instrumental reading proves too much. On any merely predictive reading, the stock market believes that the central bank will hold rates and the gut microbiome wants more fibre; if having a mind just means being usefully describable in intentional terms, then Aunt Hillary can have a mind while there is nothing more to her than a clever feedback loop, and the original question — whether there is really a subject there — has gone unanswered. What is wanted, he said, is not more examples but a principled boundary between systems that merely behave as if they believe and systems that really do, and Nadia has offered a gradient where a boundary was promised.

(5) He pressed the same worry from another direction: the boundary problem in space and time. Some army ants reproduce by colony fission, splitting in two; if the colony was a mind, one mind has become two, and nothing in the story settles which of the two is the original or whether there were two minds all along. Conversely, in some species neighbouring nests are so mutually tolerant that workers and brood are shuffled between them continuously, so the colony has no sharp edge at all: over a thousand acres of such nests, how many minds are there, and where does each begin? If no one can say how many minds are present and what their boundaries are, he said, you are not identifying a subject, you are drawing a circle around your favourite causal pattern.

(6) Nadia bit the bullet. Yes, she said, a sprawling supercolony is a mind — a very slow, very stupid one whose parts know nothing of each other — and yes, fission and fusion are hard cases; but hard identity cases do not show that the thing does not exist, any more than the puzzles about human fission show that people do not exist. Nor does turnover tell against it: workers live a year or two and are continuously replaced while the colony persists for decades, which is the ship of Theseus plus the fact that your own cells are replaced too. Mentality plausibly comes in degrees, and brains are simply the fast, compact, densely wired end of a spectrum; we mistake the end we happen to occupy for the definition of the property.

(7) Owen said that last move was the tell. If mind now covers an archipelago of nests and a thousand-acre supercolony, it covers watersheds and ecosystems as well, and the thesis has inflated into a panpsychism nobody signed up for, with the word doing no classificatory work. And the personal-identity parallel, he argued, actually cuts against her: in our own case we have a reasonably settled sense of what is at stake in survival — memory, character, the continuation of a life we care about — whereas for the colony there is no analogous candidate for what would make a given stretch of ant activity one life rather than a causal tangle. His conclusion: the colony can perfectly well be a genuine agent, a system that pursues goals and corrects its errors, without there being any fact of the matter about how many subjects are present; and that is all the mind talk was ever entitled to claim.

(8) Nadia conceded that the counting question is real, but said it is a question about method rather than about the colony: counting minds, she suggested, is like counting eddies in a river — the answer is relative to a scale and to a set of interests — so the right conclusion is that one mind or many has no scale-free answer, not that the answer is zero.

They left it there. Please adjudicate.
