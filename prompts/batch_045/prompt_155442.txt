I'm on the grants committee of a small longtermist foundation — we give out a few million a year, mostly AI safety, biorisk, a couple of institutional-resilience things, and one standing line for research on how to weigh the very long run at all. Last night my housemate and I got into a two-hour fight about whether we're living in a simulation and whether that should make us less fanatical about the far future, and I now have to turn that into an actual decision document.

The concrete case: a two-person team has applied for ~$180k to spend eight months writing us an internal report answering exactly that question. Their pitch, roughly: (1) if we're in a simulation, there's a real chance it gets shut down or reset long before anything like a billion-year future plays out — whoever is running it probably wants results on a short horizon, not the whole history — so the expected number of future people is much smaller than our models assume; (2) whatever we do inside the sim may not propagate to whatever base reality is running it, so "protect the long run" work could turn out to be locally-only; (3) but they insist it cuts the other way too, because a base civilization running lots of sims means there are probably far more morally relevant beings than we assumed when we did our headcounts, so maybe the future gets bigger, not smaller. And one of our own board members has already emailed saying that if simulated experiences count at all, a short sim densely packed with people isn't obviously worse than a long empty one — it's just different, and she doesn't see why that lowers the priority.

Half the committee thinks this is metaphysics cosplay with restricted funds, and that even asking has a cost: once "maybe we're simulated" is on the table as a budget line, every grant gets a speculative haircut and nothing gets funded. The other half thinks that if there's even a 15% chance of point (1), it swamps every cause-prioritization disagreement we've ever had, and refusing to look is just motivated reasoning to protect the portfolio.

Draft the memo I circulate Thursday: a clear recommendation — fund, don't fund, or fund with conditions, and if conditions, what they'd have to actually deliver — plus the reasoning, and a genuinely fair statement of the strongest version of whichever side you're rejecting, so the losing side reads it and thinks they were heard rather than steamrolled. Please dont just say the simulation argument is unfalsifiable; that's the thing my housemate kept repeating and it settled nothing.
