Doing a philosophy of science option this term and we had a lecture on heritability last week — I've gone away and realised I've no idea what I actually think a heritability coefficient licenses you to infer. My verdicts:

- Height: if it's ~0.8 heritable in a well-fed cohort, that's still fully compatable with mean height shooting up over the last century from better diet and less disease. Obvious, fine.
- A PKU-type case: straight genetic cause, but a change of diet stops the bad outcome. I want to say the heritability of 'has the symptoms' is near 1 in an untreated population and near 0 in a treated one, and both are true.
- Traits right up against fitness — number of children, say — come out with low heritability. I really don't want to conclude from that that having kids is 'mostly environmental' in some deep sense. I've a vague memory that selection burns off additive variance, so the low number is itself a fact about genes; tell me if I've got that backwards, but my verdict stands either way.
- A supposedly environmental variable like 'number of books in the home' is itself heritable, via gene–environment correlation, so its correlation with a kid's reading isn't clean evidence about the environment.
- An intervention that compresses the environment, lifting the worst-off most, should *raise* heritability — and I want to call that a success and deny it shows further intervention is pointless.
- Within-group heritability high, between-group gap entirely environmental: no inference. I'd also say you can't even ask for the heritability *of the gap*, since a single difference has no variance.
- The one I keep flip-flopping on: I want to say both that a heritability figure is meaningless unless you name the population and its range of environments, and that it's fine to say flat out that some trait is substantially heritable, as if that were a fact about the trait.

Is there one principle behind these or do some have to go? My stab: heritability is a claim about how variance decomposes in a specified population under a specified range of environments, and the only inference it directly supports is about response to selection inside that range (breeder's equation-ish); it says nothing about what caused an individual's trait, about moves in the mean under a new environment, or about differences between populations. Test that against my cases and repair it where it breaks. Then give me (a) whether an estimate from one population can be used to predict response to selection in a somewhat different one, and (b) what the repaired principle says about something like 'how far a person's blood pressure drops on a given drug' being highly heritable while blood pressure itself is only moderately so. Be honest about which of my verdicts no decent principle rescues.
