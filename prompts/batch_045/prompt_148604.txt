On the small team I work on we build long-horizon agents that are expected to review and approve rewrites of their own reasoning machinery, and over the past year I have come to accept a position I would once have dismissed as doomsaying; call it the deteriorating-reflection thesis. The core of it is a mathematical claim plus an architectural extrapolation. The mathematical part is that for any agent whose reasoning is captured by a formal system strong enough to encode elementary arithmetic and to talk about its own provability predicate, the step of taking one's own future outputs to be reliable cannot be certified from inside: Löb's theorem turns the conditional schema if I prove φ then φ into the outright I prove φ, so the only sentences for which the agent can vouch for its own soundness are ones it can already prove, and in particular it can never certify the general reliability of the very procedure doing the certifying, nor even its own consistency. The extrapolation is that every escape route buys its way out at a price that compounds over time. Trust a successor whose proof system is weaker by one level and each rewrite spends a level; trust only conclusions that were reached a step ago and you never catch up to your own present; demote your own outputs from theorems to evidence and the confidence you may assign your future judgment is discounted by a factor that never returns to one. So a long-lived self-modifying agent faces a trilemma: freeze, and never improve; rewrite without certification, and every guarantee you were maintaining evaporates; or degrade, becoming slightly weaker and slightly less self-trusting at each generation.

What I want is an honest map of how exposed this position is, and I would rather you attack it than explain it. Start with the three escape routes above and tell me whether each really compounds or whether I have mistaken an artifact of the toy formalizations for a structural cost: is there a scheme in which the guarantee an agent can state about itself after k rewrites is bounded below by a positive constant rather than tending to zero in k? That question is the one I personally find most decisive, because we have a concrete version of it in our office arguments — a patch game in which an agent is offered, once per round, a modification to its own verifier, must decide whether to install it before seeing whether a referee would have marked that patch safe, and is scored on the worst guarantee it can still state about its k-th successor; in every scheme we have actually implemented the score decays in k, and I do not know whether that is a theorem waiting to be proved or a failure of imagination on our part. Then take the objections I keep fielding and sort them rather than merely repeat them. One colleague says the whole worry is a category mistake: real trust is a disposition of a machine, not a sentence in its language, so no fixed-point theorem constrains it, and Löb's theorem is a fact about provability predicates with curly brackets in them, not about agents. Another says the obstacle only bites vertically, when a system must vouch for a descendant that reasons differently, and that horizontal self-trust — trusting an exact copy of yourself, or the same verifier run again — is untouched, which would make the trilemma an artifact of insisting on descendant-trust. A third has floated a conservation principle, that trust is like a resource which can be spent but never created, and I cannot tell whether that analogy is doing work or just supplying intuition. I also hold a wider commitment than the trilemma needs: I think the same obstacle governs any agent that treats the fact that its own reasoning produced a conclusion as evidence for that conclusion, and not merely agents that literally rewrite their source.

Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguished by whether they would refute the mathematical core, the compounding extrapolation, or only the wider commitment about self-trust as evidence, and for each say whether a committed holder of the thesis should concede it as counting against the view or is entitled to dismiss it, since I want defeaters the position's friends would accept and not only the ones its critics reach for. Say plainly where I have insulated the view — for instance if the claim that no scheme achieves a positive floor is really a claim about schemes anyone has thought of, or if the trilemma can always be escaped by quietly lowering what counts as a guarantee — and whether that insulation disqualifies it or is reasonable for a claim of this kind. If the thesis survives, tell me what observation would still be worth running, and if it does not, tell me which of its three horns I should retreat to. Please answer in continuous prose in the manner of a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables, and no enumerated lists, however tempting the ranking request makes them.
