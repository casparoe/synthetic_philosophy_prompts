Hey — I'm presenting to my reading group next week on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I don't want to spend the session rehearsing the argument again (the one where a proposition looks like it has to be a complex of a thing, a property, and some relation that ties them together, and then that relation needs tying to them by a further relation, and so on forever, so nothing ever gets put together in the first place). What I want instead is a spec: what would a theory of propositional unity actually have to deliver to count as adequate, where adequate means good enough for two jobs at once — underwriting a compositional semantics for a natural language, and being the object of attitudes, i.e. the thing two people can believe, the thing that's already true before anyone entertains it, the thing that stays the same while you first believe it, then doubt it, then remember it. So: the full list of desiderata, each one with the case or consideration behind it, the thing that makes it a requirement rather than a taste.

Things I want on the list explicitly, but add the ones I'm missing, because I'm sure I'm missing some: it has to separate a unified proposition from the bare list or heap of its constituents, so that Socrates and wisdom sitting side by side isn't yet anything true or false; it has to get directionality right, so that combining a with F one way round isn't the same proposition as combining them the other way round, and it has to do that without bringing in one more element that then needs uniting in turn, which I take to be the actual nerve of the regress; it has to let false propositions be completely unified even though nothing out there corresponds to them; it has to scale past the tidy two-constituent case to arbitrarily large and arbitrarily embedded structure; and it has to leave the proposition available to minds that never happen to meet it. For each one, why it's a requirement and what it knocks out. Then the two parts I care most about: mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp (my guesses are that unity must be explained rather than accepted as primitive is a prejudice of people who like explanations, and that a proposition must contain exactly the items the sentence mentions is a prejudice of people who like structure — tell me if I've got that backwards), and tell me whether some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable. If it is, give me the minimal unsatisfiable subset and the argument for it, because I think the subset is the interesting result here and the list is just scaffolding. Also name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates.

One constraint I need you to respect, because my group keeps quietly violating it: whatever does the unifying, it can't be the activity of a thinker, and it can't be an extra constituent of the proposition either. My case for the first half is a speaker who hears a sentence in a language whose word order doesn't mark which noun is subject and which is object, and who understands it correctly on the first pass — the content has to be put together with the right direction before she does any putting together, and I can't see how an act of synthesis could have settled which way round it went. So: given both halves of that constraint, is there any way through, or does the enterprise collapse? And then, briefly — I mean really briefly, one paragraph or a small table, I'll do the arguing in the room — how the main options fare against the list: the Frege-style view where one constituent is unsaturated and does the holding together, the Russell-style view where the thinker's act of judging is what unifies, the set-of-possible-worlds view where there is arguably nothing to unify, and the Wittgensteinian move where the unity of the proposition can't be stated, only shown.
