For forty years I worked in experimental condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have been reading my way, slowly, into the philosophy of mind. I have come to rest on a claim I first met in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and then in his 2007 book on the self: that the sense of being an I arises from an abstract self-referential pattern rather than from the stuff that carries it, and that such a pattern is not confined to one skull — that each of our 'I's is to some degree spread over a number of brains. His occasion for pressing the point was the death of his wife, and he holds that something real of her is carried in him and in others who knew her. On that case my sympathies are with him. On how far the claim reaches I have no opinion worth having, so I did what I was trained to do and went at it case by case. My verdicts, on inspection, do not add up, and I would be grateful for help in finding out why.

Case 1. A child has an entire cerebral hemisphere removed to stop seizures, and grows up recognisably the same child, one hemisphere doing the work of two. Yes: the same I, thinner in what it knows and can do, but a single locus.

Case 2. A man is scanned cell by cell, the scan is transmitted, his body is destroyed, and a molecule-for-molecule duplicate is assembled on Mars. No. The duplicate is not him. He is dead, and what walks on Mars is his heir.

Case 3. The same end state reached by the other route: region by region, over two years, his neural tissue is replaced by prosthetic units with identical input-output behaviour; he remains awake and conversational throughout; at the end no original tissue remains. Yes: him at every intermediate stage and him at the end. I know that Cases 2 and 3 are uncomfortable neighbours and that most of the things one might say to distinguish them are things I do not believe. I do not want to give up either verdict.

Case 4. Hofstadter's case. A woman dies after forty years of marriage; in her husband there persists a large and detailed pattern that governs judgement, taste, and self-description, that finishes her sentences, that votes as she would have voted, and that sometimes blocks him with a disapproval he experiences as not quite his own. Yes: a real fraction of her I persists in him, and I resist the deflationary story on which this is only memory and influence wearing a costume.

Case 5. A biographer who has spent twenty years inside a living subject's complete diaries and correspondence, who can predict the subject's verdicts better than the subject can and can generate them on new questions, but in whom nothing pushes back and whose own motives remain entirely his own. No: that is a model, not a remnant.

Case 6. My brother, who has known me for sixty-five years and carries an unusually faithful version of my patterns. If I die before him, I am inclined to say, with Hofstadter, that a part of me goes on in him. While I am alive I am not inclined to say that I am partly located in his head; I am here, and what he carries is a representation of me. I suspect these two verdicts cannot both stand, since nothing about the pattern changes at my death except that the original locus goes dark.

The case I go back and forth on: a woman with advancing frontotemporal dementia, whose self-symbol is fragmenting — she no longer recognises her own history, her tastes have drifted, she misidentifies her husband — while her husband, after fifty years, now carries a more faithful version of her patterns of judgement, taste, and self-description than she does. Part of me wants to say that she has partly migrated into him; part of me wants to say that she is being lost and that he is, marginally, a better copy. I cannot settle it.

So my question. Is there one principle — a set of conditions under which a system counts as the locus of an I, or as carrying a genuine fraction of one, or as carrying only information about one — that returns my verdicts in all six cases? I have a first pass of my own which I would like tested rather than flattered: a carried pattern is part of a person rather than a model of a person only if it is executive in its host, that is, only if it can initiate, veto, or override action and not merely predict or describe. I am also unsure whether some further condition of reciprocal causal contact during the original's life belongs in the criterion, and whether 'how much of her persists' is a quantity with an answer at all or only a façon de parler.

What I would like from you: the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; a compact table of its verdicts against mine, with disagreements flagged; its verdict on cases I have not listed, among them a remnant split across three people rather than concentrated in one, and a machine trained on a dead woman's correspondence that her family swears is her; and a plain statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what, in your view, I should think about the one it overturns. If no single principle gets all six and the choice is between a baroque one that saves them and a simple one that sacrifices one, say which you would choose and why.
