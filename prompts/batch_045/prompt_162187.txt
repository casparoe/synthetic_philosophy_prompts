I'm in a small reading group working through Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning, and we got bogged down on the primary text this week — we're on the chapter on Instinct in the 1859 Origin, and I can't tell whether Darwin is actually doing the thing Dennett later calls competence without comprehension, or only something that looks like it from a distance. The passage we're stuck on: "I will not attempt any definition of instinct. It would be easy to show that several distinct mental actions are commonly embraced by this term; but every one understands what is meant, when it is said that instinct impels the cuckoo to migrate and to lay her eggs in other birds' nests. An action, which we ourselves should require experience to enable us to perform, when performed by an animal, more especially by a very young one, without any experience, and when performed by many individuals in the same way, without their knowing for what purpose it is performed, is usually said to be instinctive. But I could show that none of these characters of instinct are universal. A little dose, as Pierre Huber expresses it, of judgment or reason, often comes into play, even in animals very low in the scale of nature. Frederick Cuvier and several of the older metaphysicians have compared instinct with habit. This comparison gives, I think, a remarkably accurate notion of the frame of mind under which an instinctive action is performed, but not of its origin." (A couple of lines earlier he also says: "I must premise, that I have nothing to do with the origin of the primary mental powers, any more than I have with that of life itself. We are concerned only with the diversities of instinct and of the other mental qualities of animals within the same class.")

Here's roughly where I'm at, so you can tell me where I've gone wrong. I keep wanting to split competence without comprehension into two claims: a constitutive one about the machinery while it operates — the animal performs the action with no grasp of what it's for, no representation of the end state, nothing that would count as understanding — and an aetiological one about where the competence came from, namely a causal process with no foresight, no designer, no comprehension anywhere in the history. Darwin's habit remark looks like it's drawing exactly that line (habit gives you the right picture of the frame of mind but not of the origin), and I want to know whether that's really his distinction or whether I'm reading a 20th-century distinction back into him. Then there's the caterpillar case he takes from P. Huber, which is the bit I care most about: "so P. Huber found it was with a caterpillar, which makes a very complicated hammock; for if he took a caterpillar which had completed its hammock up to, say, the sixth stage of construction, and put it into a hammock completed up only to the third stage, the caterpillar simply re-performed the fourth, fifth, and sixth stages of construction. If, however, a caterpillar were taken out of a hammock made up, for instance, to the third stage, and were put into one finished up to the sixth stage, so that much of its work was already done for it, far from feeling the benefit of this, it was much embarrassed, and, in order to complete its hammock, seemed forced to start from the third stage, where it had left off, and thus tried to complete the already finished work." My gloss is that what this actually demonstrates is goal-blindness — the routine is keyed to the caterpillar's own last step rather than to the state of the hammock, so it can't notice the work is already done — and that this is a much narrower result than it's not comprehending anything. You can build an open-loop controller that fails in exactly this way, and its failure tells you nothing about whether the system has inner states at all, only that it has no usable model of the endpoint. Does that undersell what Darwin needs, or is goal-blindness the honest cash value of without their knowing for what purpose it is performed?

And then the bit that's actually bugging me. If the evidence only ever establishes goal-blindness, then the inversion looks like it has a missing rung: you get competences that nobody designed and nobody understands, and you get, as Darwin concedes, a little dose of judgment mixed in — but I don't see where genuine comprehension is supposed to get built out of that, and his disclaimer about not touching the origin of the primary mental powers suggests he didn't think he owed us that either. Turing's version seems to go at it differently: instead of trying to specify an adult mind outright, you build something crude and childlike and educate it, so the competence comes first and whatever understanding shows up later is supposed to be a product rather than a precondition. Does that move actually close the gap, or does it just relocate it — i.e., does Turing's version need the constitutive claim (no comprehension inside the learner) in a way that makes it vulnerable to exactly the same worry that the caterpillar only shows goal-blindness? One of the people in my group thinks Darwin's disclaimer is pure diplomatic hedging against being called a materialist and carries no argumentative weight; I think it's load-bearing and that its the reason the inversion as he states it stops short of explaining mind proper. Tell me who's closer to right, and please walk me through the sentences themselves rather than just the secondary literature — I can follow the modern framing fine, its the 1859 prose I keep losing my footing in.
