The episode is US Airways Flight 1549, 15 January 2009, the ditching in the Hudson. The facts as I have them: an Airbus A320, out of LaGuardia for Charlotte and on to Seattle, struck a flock of Canada geese about four and a half miles north-northwest of the field at roughly 2,800 feet during initial climb, and both engines lost thrust. Captain Chesley Sullenberger, an experienced glider pilot and aviation-safety specialist with something like 19,000 total flight hours, took control; First Officer Jeffrey Skiles, who had only a few dozen hours in the type and was flying it for the first time on the line, worked the engine-restart checklist. Air traffic control first cleared a return to a LaGuardia runway; Sullenberger said he could not make it. ATC then offered Teterboro in New Jersey; he initially accepted and then, seconds later, said they could not do it either, and that they were going into the Hudson. The time from the bird strike to the ditching was under four minutes. All 155 people aboard survived; around 100 were injured, a handful seriously, and nearby boats took them off. The official investigation later ran simulator tests indicating that a return to LaGuardia was possible if the turn back were begun immediately after the strike, while concluding that the simulations did not capture real-world considerations and that the ditching was what gave the highest chance of survival. I also believe the crew got the auxiliary power unit running early and so retained electrical power, though I am not sure which systems that preserved, so do not lean on it.

What I want:

1. The mapping. Say who the deciding agent is — the captain, the two-person crew, or the crew plus checklist plus ATC plus airframe as a single distributed system — and what turns on the choice. Say what the options actually were, in what order they surfaced, and what the search cost consisted of (seconds, altitude, energy, attention). Then say what satisficing predicts about how such a decision should go, and where the episode matches or departs from that prediction.

2. The aspiration ratchet. Simon's aspiration levels are supposed to adapt across a sequence of decisions as experience of what is attainable accumulates. Here the sequence collapses to a single irrevocable trial, and within it the threshold appears to move downward: from land at an airport, to land somewhere survivable, to land on water without power. Is that downward movement rational adaptation, or does a theory whose thresholds can drift with the options on offer become unfalsifiable? What resources does the framework have for one-shot cases?

3. Endogenous option arrival. Satisficing usually treats options as arriving from an exogenous search process. In this episode, considering and partially pursuing an option consumed the very resource — altitude and energy — that the alternatives required, so the option set was degraded by the search itself. Tell me what satisficing, and my ledger in item 4, should say about a stop that is only reasonable because the earlier exploration destroyed a better option. Does Simon's machinery accommodate this, or is it a case the theory never anticipated?

4. My invented device, which I want you to use and then attack. Call it the settling ledger: score a decision not by its outcome and not by the option set an ideal agent could have enumerated, but by whether the agent's stopping rule was reasonable given only the options that had actually surfaced by the time the agent stopped, where an option counts as surfaced only if it was represented in deliberation or in the agent's trained perceptual-motor repertoire before the stop. Three questions. First, apply it here: on the natural reading the runway return had surfaced and was explicitly rejected, so the ledger cannot wave away the simulator counterfactual as merely unconsidered — it has to judge the rejection, and I want to know what it would need in order to do that (a rough probability? a threshold? a counterfactual about turn-back timing?) and whether it can get it without quietly reintroducing optimization. Second, is the settling ledger anything more than satisficing plus an ex post bookkeeping rule? Third, is it self-undermining: if the aspiration level adapts to what surfaces, can a minimally competent agent always pass the ledger, and is that a defect of the device or the right conclusion?

5. The as-if challenge. Any threshold-following behaviour can be redescribed as optimizing expected survival subject to costs of information and deliberation. Does this episode break the tie between satisficing and that redescription — is there anything in it that one account predicts and the other does not — or is the disagreement here empirically empty and merely normative or psychological? If it is merely normative, say what the normative difference amounts to when the outcome was, by everyone's lights, the best available.

6. A competitor. Gary Klein's recognition-primed decision model says that expert practitioners in time pressure typically do not compare options at all: they recognize the situation as a type, run a single mental simulation, and act. Characterize that model and tell me whether it or satisficing better describes the four minutes in question, whether they are genuine rivals or descriptions at different levels, and what the episode would have to have looked like to discriminate between them.

7. What the case does to the theory. I am drawn to the thought that the explanatory weight here falls not on the agent's stopping rule but on the structure of the environment — a glide ratio, a wide straight river, a dense fleet of commuter ferries, a checklist — so that what deserves the credit is the fit between a heuristic and a world, rather than any individual-level notion of satisficing. Say whether that is right, whether it collapses into ecological-rationality talk or is a genuine consequence of Simon's own scissors metaphor, and whether satisficing survives as a claim about agents if it does.
