Setup, so we're on the same page. Finite set of possible worlds. Each agent has a partition of the worlds (their information: they know which cell they're in). Each agent has a posterior probability for each world they could be in. CPA says: there's one single prior P such that each agent's posterior, at any world, is just P conditioned on her own cell. Standard payoffs people cite for assuming this: agents who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors can't "agree to disagree" — the posteriors have to be equal — and, under the usual extra assumptions, agents won't want to trade with each other purely on the basis of their information.

Claim 1 (the emptiness worry). The way I read CPA, it's an existence claim: there is some prior that rationalizes the profile of posteriors. But once you let the prior be chosen after the fact, it looks nearly vacuous. Fix any finite profile of posteriors and you can usually go shopping for a prior that generates them; the only obstruction I can actually find is cases where the two agents disagree about which worlds are still live — one of them assigns zero to something the other is still putting positive probability on. So the bare existence claim in CPA bans almost nothing, and tells us almost nothing about whether a given disagreement is reasonable. Something else must be doing the work.

Claim 2 (hindsight priors). Call a prior retrofitted if it was picked after we saw how the disagreement came out — we look at their posteriors, then shop for a prior that makes them look like innocent updating. If CPA only rules out mismatched priors and not retrofitted ones, it's not a constraint on reasoning at all, it's a tautology with extra steps. So CPA needs a timing condition: the prior has to be the one the agents actually had before the private evidence arrived, and it has to stay put when you ask what they'd have believed if the evidence had come out the other way. Everything interesting, I claim, lives in that timing condition — not in the word "common."

Claim 3 (no-envy-swap, which is my constructive proposal). I want to say the real content of CPA was never about a shared starting point. It's this: ex ante, before anyone sees anything, no agent would pay a positive amount to trade places — to get the other agent's partition and the other agent's updating procedure instead of her own, whatever the world turns out to be. You don't look at your informational situation and think "I'd strictly rather have hers." Call it no-envy-swap. My claims about it: (a) it can hold when priors differ, and it can fail when priors are identical — so it's a genuinely different condition, not a rewording; (b) it's closer to what the agreement arguments actually use, because the step where differing common-knowledge posteriors are supposed to generate a contradiction is really a step where each agent is committed to treating the other's update as fine-just-differently-informed.

Claim 4 (why I think this reorients the dialectic). The fight about whether persistent disagreement can be rational keeps getting run as a fight about priors. But nobody disagrees about priors; priors aren't the thing in dispute, the world is. What's actually in dispute, on my reading, is likelihoods — each agent's mapping from evidence to belief. If A thinks B over-reads the evidence (B would go to 90% on a result that A thinks only supports 60%), then A's disagreement with B is stable in a way that no shared starting point fixes, and A isn't committed to thinking B is irrational or stupid — only that B is miscalibrated in how she responds to evidence. So: swap "common priors" for "common likelihoods" as the load-bearing assumption, and the agreement theorems turn into theorems about who you'd trust to read the evidence on your behalf. Which is an ordinary question instead of a metaphysical one.

Claim 5 (the bit I can't settle). If no-envy-swap is weaker than CPA, does it still deliver anything? My guess is it gets you no-trade — you can't expect to profit from trading with someone whose reading of the evidence you regard as fine — but it doesn't get you full agreement, and I genuinely can't tell whether losing the agreement result is a cost to my view or a feature of it.
