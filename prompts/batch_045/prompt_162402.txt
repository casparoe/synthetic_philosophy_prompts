hey — I run a small reading group in Bristol (mostly lapsed physics and maths people, two actual philosophers) and our next session is on whether the simulation argument should chill people out about the far future. You know the version I mean: the trilemma where either civilisations near-always die out before they can run big ancestor-simulations, or they can but near-always choose not to, or we're almost certainly inside one. A few people in the group work on longtermist stuff and have got properly spooked: their worry is that if we're probably in a sim, then the number of people in "our" future is probably small, or the sim just gets switched off at some arbitrary point, so the astronomical stakes that make fanaticism about the far future rational (I mean the thing where you take a one-in-a-million shot at an astronomically huge payoff because the expected value still swamps everything else) quietly evaporate. Others say it doesn't move them at all, because simulated people are people and there could be vastly more of them than there are of us.

What I want is a printed flowchart for the session — one A3 sheet, so something like five or six branch questions and seven to nine leaves, no more than that. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on anthropic reasoning and population ethics, and take your time: brainstorm the logical space first (what are the separate claims someone can combine here? which combinations does nobody actually hold?) before you draw a single branch. Build the tree from the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space — don't just start from a list of view-names you've seen before.

Design constraints:

- Every branch node has to be a question someone can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. So not "do you think simulated people matter?" as a yes/no about a label, but a concrete case with two or three options.
- Please use these three cases as the probes at three of the nodes: (1) the archival rerun — a sim that runs a century of subjective time, is then reset and re-run a billion times, byte-identical except that the simulators nudge one parameter at each reset; is the value of the whole setup one run's worth or a billion times that? (2) the leaky sim — the inhabitants can get effects out into the base layer, say the simulators read their outputs or the sim can crash the simulators' machine; does being in a sim still reduce what's at stake? (3) the capped-horizon sim — you find out your sim is scheduled to be switched off in two hundred years, because studying how a civilisation handles that particular century is what it's for; does that change what is worth doing inside it? Add your own case if you think a distinction is missing — the sims-within-sims regress is the obvious candidate.
- Leaves I want on the poster, under whatever names you think are right: someone who says sim credence deflates the stakes and we should pivot to nearer-term work; someone who says sims are where most of the value is and it's business as usual or more; someone who says only the base reality's future counts; someone who says the simulation argument is just scepticism in a costume and has no special bearing here; someone who treats a short sim as one more risk among the existential risks and hedges across horizons; someone who says if we're in a sim our grip on what's valuable is too unreliable to bank on. Each leaf gets one line on what it says, one line on its most awkward commitment, and one line on what you'd actually do on Monday morning.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate. I suspect the "sims are full of value" person and the plain vanilla longtermist end up giving identical advice about what to do next week, and I want to know whether your tree is honest about that or quietly fudges it.
- List the unoccupied cells: answer-patterns nobody defends, with one line on whether that's because the combination is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.

No citations, no reading lists, no "as so-and-so argues" — we can't look anything up in the session and half the room wouldn't know the literature anyway, so it'd just start an argument.

Format: give me one JSON object, with these fields exactly —
