Last night I got into a long argument with my friend Ruth about whether evolutionary psychology is a science or an elaborate machine for generating just-so stories. Our friend Emil joined in against her, though not entirely on my side, and after roughly an hour the three of us were going in circles. Below I have reconstructed the argument as a two-sided exchange between Ruth and Emil, since that is where the substance lay; I have left myself out of it, and I would rather you not try to infer my own position from the way I have written it up, because I do not have one I am confident in.

1. Ruth: The problem is not that evolutionary psychology makes claims about the past; it is that the hypothesis is generated by staring at the behavior to be explained and there is no independent record of the selection event. Give her any trait — disgust, gossip, morning sickness, men's preference for younger women — and she can produce two or three adaptive stories in an afternoon that fit it equally well. A story built to fit the data cannot then cite the fit as evidence for itself.

2. Emil: That describes a parody, not the literature. Working papers derive predictions about phenomena that were not part of the data that prompted the hypothesis: if disgust is a pathogen-avoidance system, it should track cues of transmissibility rather than toxicity, should calibrate to local parasite ecology, should weaken when the costs of avoidance are high, and should not simply be fear in another costume. Those are testable now. And the charge proves too much: by Ruth's standard, historical geology and cosmology are just-so stories, since nobody can rerun the Cambrian.

3. Ruth: She will concede that some hypotheses are testable in principle; her objection is about what the tests actually do. The predictions are usually directional and soft — more, faster, stronger — so almost any outcome can be narrated afterward as consistent, and the rival cultural-learning or domain-general-learning account generally predicts the same direction, so the result rarely discriminates. As for the analogy: geology has strata and isotope ratios, an independent record. Recurrence of a trait across cultures is itself part of the data to be explained, so it cannot also be the independent check on the explanation.

4. Emil: Recurrence is not simply the datum restated. If the same pattern shows up in societies with no plausible contact or shared ancestry, then a diffusion story has to explain why, and a "we learned it locally" story inherits a cost it did not have before. That is a genuine constraint from outside the hypothesis.

5. Ruth: Then the target moves. Evolutionary psychology slides among three different things it might be explaining: the observable behavior, the underlying psychological mechanism, and the universal capacity of which the behavior is one local expression. Whenever cross-cultural data go the wrong way, the retreat is always to "the mechanism is universal; its output depends on local ecology" — and no pattern of cultural variation can refute that, because variation was built into the claim. She called this target drift, and said it is the real reason the hypotheses feel unfalsifiable.

6. Emil: That is not evasion, that is how biology works. Conditional strategies and reaction norms are evolved outcomes, not excuses: a mechanism that implements a switch is a specific, testable claim, because you can go looking for the cue the switch is set to and test whether the output tracks it. The accusation of target drift confuses explaining the mechanism with explaining the variance in behavior, and those are different explanatory projects.

7. Ruth: Fine — let her stop complaining about falsifiability in the abstract and make it concrete. She grants that the mechanism claim is testable. Her point is that the field only ever puts the mechanism claim at risk, never the selection claim, and she proposed a test, which she called the two-track test: separate (i) the design claim — there exists a domain-specific system with such-and-such input criteria, computations, and outputs — from (ii) the selection-history claim — it was shaped by selection for function F in ancestral environments. Track (i) is testable now, but it does not entail (ii): the same mechanism is equally compatible with selection for a different function, with a by-product of something else that was selected, with exaptation, with selection operating at a different level, and with drift plus cultural canalization. Unless the non-adaptive alternatives are specified with equal care and given real prior weight, and unless the field publishes tests designed to discriminate among them, the science all lives in track (i) and the evolutionary story is ornament.

8. Emil: That is the spandrels objection wearing a new hat. By-product, exaptation, and constraint hypotheses are stock moves in the field, not suppressed ones. And inference to the best explanation across several independent lines is exactly the method historical science has; what Ruth wants is a discriminating experiment, which imports a standard from experimental science that no historical hypothesis could meet — you cannot rerun the Pleistocene. She should also say what her rival is: if domain-general learning explains these phenomena, that is a testable claim too, and it has been tested.

9. Ruth: The Cambrian analogy is the weak joint. In the cases that give historical inference its good name — the eye, echolocation, locomotion — there is an independent criterion of good design supplied by optics, acoustics, or mechanics, against which the structure can be checked; the fit is informative because the task is specified without reference to the organ. In human social psychology the adaptive problem is normally described in the same folk vocabulary as the behavior it is meant to explain, and the ancestral environment is often reconstructed partly from the trait itself. So the fit is much closer to circular than the analogy implies, and consilience among lines that share that vocabulary is weaker evidence than it looks.

10. Emil: Then treat the evolutionary story as a heuristic rather than as a finding. Its value is that it suggests mechanism-level hypotheses nobody would otherwise have thought to look for, and those are testable now; a story that turns out to be literally false can still have earned its keep by what it led someone to discover. She is also ignoring semi-independent constraints: formal models of optimal behavior under ancestral constraints, and comparative data from other primates.

11. Ruth: If that is the defense, it concedes what she actually cares about. Evolutionary psychology would then be a source of hypotheses about mechanisms, not an explanation of why people behave as they do — and the field's own summaries, popular ones and a good many published ones, present it as the latter. She is not asking anyone to stop telling stories; she is asking that the story be marked as a promissory note rather than cashed as a result.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each numbered turn, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why — I would rather have eleven verdicts than one overall judgment. In doing so, please keep separate: (a) whether evolutionary-psychology hypotheses are testable in principle; (b) whether the field's actual practice puts the selection claim at risk or only the mechanism claim; (c) what Ruth's two-track test would and would not establish if it were actually run; and (d) whether Emil's retreat to "heuristic, not finding" gives Ruth everything she asked for, or something less than she thinks. Then tell me who is more right overall — or tell me that question is ill-posed and why. Say also what both of them are missing.

On confidence: for each verdict, mark how confident you are and what kind of evidence or argument would move you off it; if your confidence differs a lot between the turns, I want to see that rather than a uniform tone. And if something in the way I have framed this rests on a mistake — if "just-so story" is doing two or three different jobs in their exchange, if the two-track test cannot bear the weight Ruth puts on it, if the strong form of her circularity charge would indict paleontology and archaeology too and so proves too much — say so plainly rather than answering politely. Being corrected is more useful to me than being agreed with. Please write in American English and take whatever space you need, but do not pad. And please do not hand me a reading list or citations: I am interested in the arguments, not in what to read next.
