I'm closing the final session of a graduate reading group on classical AI and its critics, and instead of a summary I want to end by reading a poem aloud — something that takes about three minutes to deliver.

The subject is the frame problem, and I want the form itself to do the philosophical work: a sestina. Six unrhymed six-line stanzas, the end-words rotating by the standard 6-1-5-2-4-3 permutation, then a three-line tornada in which all six end-words come back, two per line, one of them closing the line. Use exactly these six end-words: battery, wagon, bomb, timer, wall, door. Keep the lines at ten to twelve syllables with a discernible iambic pulse — the pulse may break where the robot does, but it shouldn't dissolve into shapeless free verse.

Set it in the familiar thought-experiment scene: a robot goes to fetch its own spare battery from a room in which a bomb is ticking. But give this robot an advantage the usual versions don't have. It has already solved the representational side of the frame problem: it holds a complete and correct ledger of which facts survive which actions, so it never has to write another frame axiom, and there is no consequence of what it does that it cannot in principle derive. It still comes to grief. The failure I want dramatized is what I've been calling the clock reading of the frame problem, as against the ledger reading: even with the ledger perfect, the question of which of the endlessly many persisting facts matters to the action has to be settled before the timer runs out, and settling it takes time — deliberation is itself an event in the room, and the timer runs while the robot reckons. Somewhere in the poem the robot's checking has to cost it something.

Constraints on the content, please:

- Keep the frame problem cleanly distinct from the qualification problem (the unstated preconditions of an action) and the ramification problem (its downstream effects). What is at issue here is only what does not change.
- The bomb must not arrive as a clever deduction that a better axiomatization would have handed over for free. If the poem reads as the robot was missing one more inference, it has missed the point entirely.
- Let wall keep returning attached to some consequence the robot can prove perfectly well and has no reason to care about. The rotation should feel like the holism of relevance, not like a formal stunt.
- The tornada is where the clock runs out. Don't let the ending offer reassurance — not robots lack common sense, not embodied creatures are fine. Leave it genuinely open whether this is an artifact of a representational scheme that a different architecture could dissolve, or a structural feature of any agent that must act on beliefs about a changing world before the world changes again.

It should survive two audiences: someone who works on the problem should recognize which distinction is being enacted and in which lines, and someone who has never heard of the frame problem should simply hear a robot alone in a room with a bomb.

Begin with the poem itself — no preface, no framing paragraph, no gloss before the first line. After the tornada, in no more than 120 words, note which lines carry which part of the distinction, and be honest about where you bent the form.
