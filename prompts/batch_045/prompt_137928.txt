I spent thirty-two years as a research hydrogeologist, most of them on groundwater–surface-water interaction, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of science and decision theory. It is my first sustained contact with the subject. Please gloss any term a well-read non-philosopher would not know, but do not condescend, and do not pad the document with background I can get from a textbook.

I also chair the Science and Engineering Subcommittee of the Kestrel Basin Water Authority, a small special district. The full board meets on 12 September and must decide whether to obligate 6 million dollars (about 3.5 for a stripped-down variant) on a two-year investigation before committing to a capital project. The money lapses on 30 September. I would like you to draft the memo I will table — from the subcommittee to the full board — and it will be read into the minutes of a public meeting, so every step of the reasoning has to be stateable out loud without embarrassment.

The decision. Three candidate projects, net present values in millions of dollars, staff estimates, capital costs already netted out. The open question is whether the paleochannel under our wellfield actually connects to the estuary: well connected (prior 0.30), partially connected (0.30), effectively isolated (0.40).

Option A, full-scale managed aquifer recharge: 120 / 30 / −60.
Option B, staged modular recharge: 60 / 55 / 20.
Option C, pipeline interconnection with treatment: 45 / 50 / 48.

At the prior, expected values are roughly 21, 42.5 and 47.7, so without further study we build C. That is the baseline, call it 48.

The investigation on the table is a tracer and borehole program that returns one of three findings. Roughly: a finding of connection (probability about 0.31) leaves us at (0.79, 0.15, 0.07) and both readings below pick A, worth about 95; an inconclusive finding (about 0.33) leaves us at (0.14, 0.50, 0.36); a finding against connection (about 0.37) leaves us at (0.04, 0.25, 0.71) and both readings pick C, worth about 48. Averaged over findings, the study is worth about 63 in expectation before its cost, so the gross value of the information is about 15 against a cost of 6.

Two complications that I think are where the philosophy actually bites, and where I would like the memo to be精确 rather than hand-wavy.

First, the statutory standard. Our enabling act requires the board to select the alternative favored by the weight of the available evidence. Counsel has never had to say what that means, and it will bear two readings. On what I call the expected-value reading it means: choose the alternative with the greatest expected net benefit given the evidence. On what I call the most-likely-world reading it means: identify the most probable state of the world and choose the alternative that would be best if that state obtained. These diverge in the inconclusive branch: the most-likely-world reading sends us to B (about 43), the expected-value reading to C (about 49), and the difference of roughly five and a half points is the whole of the gap between the two readings in this case. Someone in my reading group told me that for an expected-utility maximiser who remains free to ignore what it learns, information cannot be a losing purchase in expectation — I believe it is called Good's theorem — and I want the memo to explain, in a paragraph a board member can follow, what exactly in that result does the work, which of its assumptions our situation breaks, and how badly each break costs us. My own suspicion, which I cannot reconstruct properly, is that the most-likely-world reading breaks it because we would then be committed in advance to a rule that is not the expected-utility rule, whereas the expected-value reading does not break it at all. Say whether that is right and what follows.

Second, and I think this is the larger one: our results will be public records, and under the basin's curtailment rules any published finding short of a clean negative triggers a connection determination by the state engineer. Senior right holders downstream then come under an administration that costs our ratepayers, on staff's central estimate, about 40 million in present value, with a defensible range of 20 to 70. The probability of a triggering finding is about 0.64 for the three-outcome design and about 0.31 for the stripped-down design, which reports only connection indicated or connection not indicated. Staff also think the coarser design gives up essentially nothing in decision value, because the findings it pools would have led to the same project anyway. I find myself inclined to read that as an instance of something general — that once an agent is not free to respond optimally to what it learns, a less discriminating experiment can be strictly better than a more discriminating one, and that informativeness is a design variable rather than a good in itself — but I would like the memo to state that claim carefully, to say whether it applies here, and to notice that none of this is fixed by the board binding itself in advance to a response rule, since the party we cannot bind is the state engineer.

There are four live options, and I want the memo to pick one and to say why the others lose: commission the study as designed; re-scope it to the stripped-down design; decline to investigate and instead adopt the modular project B, learning from its performance as we go, on the understanding that staff did not price the option to expand it later; or decline to investigate and build C now. A colleague has also suggested commissioning the work through a university partner under a research permit so that nothing enters our public record until we choose. Tell me what to do with that suggestion; I am fairly sure it is a bad idea but I want the objection stated in a form the board can act on rather than a moral sigh.

Two further constraints. One is that the memo must be honest about the strongest argument on the other side, which an NGO representative on our panel has already put to me in writing: that selecting a study design partly because it is less likely to generate an inconvenient public finding is the deliberate manufacture of uncertainty, and that a scientific advisory body which does that has damaged something it cannot repair. I take this seriously, having spent a career arguing the opposite. Do not resolve it by assertion. The other is that the memo needs to be explicit about what the Authority is maximising, because the sign of the conclusion turns on it: our purpose clause names both reliable supply at least cost to ratepayers and the protection of instream beneficial uses, the board has never ranked them, and the curtailment is partly a transfer to irrigators and partly a real gain for the estuary.

Length: about 1,200 words, not more than 1,500. Open with a one-paragraph bottom line that states the recommendation, any condition attached to it, and the date by which something must happen; then the reasoning; then a short closing section headed something like What would have to be true for this to be the wrong call, covering the sensitivity to the curtailment estimate, to the objective function, and to counsel's eventual reading of the statutory standard. Use my numbers but tell me plainly if any of them is doing more work than it can bear. No citations, no bibliography, and no reading list: if a point has a standard name, name it in a parenthesis and move on, and I will chase the references myself.
