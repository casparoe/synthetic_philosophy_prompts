I work as a software engineer, and most of my career has been on ranking and recommendation systems — which is to say, I have spent a lot of time watching what happens when a team picks a scalar quantity, declares it the objective, and then optimizes against it. I now sit on a small internal review group that is supposed to judge product changes by their effect on the people using them, and I keep finding that we have no written spec for the thing we are claiming to protect or raise. So before I read any more theories of well-being, I want the spec written down and argued for.

The position that got me interested is the one I have seen called tranquilism: the claim that the only thing basically good for a person is the absence of craving and suffering, and that nothing else — not pleasure itself, not knowledge, not achievement, not love — is good for a person in its own right, so that a life free of craving and suffering lacks nothing worth having. Please write me the desiderata that any theory of what is good for a person would have to meet to be usable as an objective of that kind — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the structural features it needs to have — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a mark on each saying whether it is non-negotiable for my purpose or only something one camp happens to want.

Among the things I want you to decide the status of, with cases you construct:

1. A theory has to say what happens to episodes that involve no craving and no reportable pleasant feeling at the time but which the person later counts as the best hours of their life — absorbed work, a hard climb, a long debugging session. Is that a case the theory must accommodate, or a confusion it is entitled to dissolve?
2. It has to handle someone whose affect is blunted: few or no cravings, little or no suffering, and no interest in anything. If the theory's verdict is that this life is going as well as a life can, say so plainly and tell me whether that is a cost.
3. It has to handle suffering the person would not give up — grief, hard training, a project that hurts while it lasts — and craving the person endorses and would refuse a pill for: someone who wants to go on wanting things.
4. Since the good on offer is an absence, the theory needs a comparative structure: what makes one tranquil life better than another, and how episodes of absence combine across a life, if they combine at all. A quantity that is only ever zero seems to give me nothing to maximize — is that an objection to the view, or a misunderstanding of what absence talk is doing?
5. It has to say whether the good it names can be aimed at without self-defeat, and what it means for a theory if it cannot. I am thinking of the structure you get when the instruction is to stop wanting things: the wanting reappears in the trying.
6. It has to distinguish felt craving from something that plays the same role without being felt — a drug cue, a hunger signal, or, in the machine-learning sense I am used to, a reward-prediction error that drives behavior with no experience attached. Which of these count, and does the answer change if the only evidence available is behavioral?
7. For my group's actual use, it has to say whether the theory needs to put different people's conditions on a common scale, or whether interpersonal comparison is a separate module that can be bolted on afterwards.
8. It should be stable under a change in how the same stretch of life is described — per second, per episode, per project — in the way I would want an evaluation metric to be stable under a change of measurement granularity.

Tell me, for each of these, whether it is a genuine desideratum or an objection to one family of theories smuggled in disguised as a requirement; I would rather find out now that I have begged the question against some candidate than have the list quietly do that work for me. Then: which subset of the requirements, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want, since if the list is unsatisfiable the list itself is the result — and which single requirement excludes the most candidates.

Close with a few lines each scoring tranquilism, a sensory pleasure-and-pain hedonism, a desire-satisfaction view, and an objective-list view against the list. Keep the scoring short and spend your words on the requirements instead. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or suggestions for further reading: I have no library access and I would only be guessing at what is in the books. Define any term of art as you use it, and aim for something I can read in ten minutes.
