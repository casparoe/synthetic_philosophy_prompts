QUESTION: Historians often say that large events — the outbreak of the First World War, say — were "overdetermined". (a) Explain the difference between saying that an event had many causes and saying that it was overdetermined. (b) Does overdetermination make historical explanation easier or harder? Illustrate with an example of your own choosing.

ANSWER (candidate 4021): Two different things get bundled together when people say a historical event "had many causes". On the first reading, the event had a number of conditions that had to come together for it to occur: take any one of them away and the event would not have happened, or would have happened differently. This is the ordinary case in history. The assassination at Sarajevo, the July Crisis, the alliance system, competitive rearmament and the mobilisation timetables all fed into the outbreak of war in 1914, and it is doubtful whether any one of them would have been enough on its own. Overdetermination is something stronger. An outcome is overdetermined when there are two or more causes each of which would have been sufficient to bring it about by itself, so that removing any one of them would have made no difference to what actually happened. The textbook example is the firing squad: two marksmen fire at the same moment and either shot alone would have been fatal. Because both shots are fired, neither was necessary in the circumstances — but it would be absurd to conclude that neither of them caused the death. Overdetermination therefore shows that causation cannot simply be counterfactual dependence, since here each cause is a cause even though the outcome does not depend on it. Nor is this just a philosophers' curiosity. Plenty of historical causation has this shape. A second gunman waiting in the wings, ready to fire if the first one loses his nerve, is the same phenomenon: the archduke would have died anyway, so again the counterfactual test gives the wrong answer and no individual cause is necessary. It is in something like this sense that historians call 1914 overdetermined. Diplomatic, economic and domestic-political explanations can each look sufficient on their own, and it is tempting to conclude that the war was structurally bound to come. Which brings me to part (b): overdetermination makes explanation harder, not easier. Where an outcome is overdetermined, the historian cannot use counterfactuals to test her account — the question "would war have come anyway if Franz Ferdinand had not been shot?" has no determinate answer, because another route to the same outcome was available. Nor can she rank her causes, since each of them was by hypothesis enough. The consequence is that singular causal claims about history cannot really be checked against evidence in the way a causal claim in physics can be: what remains is a narrative assembled after the fact, shaped as much by what we find salient as by what actually produced the event. Overdetermination also tells us something about the event itself — an outcome with several routes to it is one that would have survived a fair amount of change in the background conditions, so it is robust rather than fragile, and safer to explain by long-run structural factors than by the decisions of individual agents.
