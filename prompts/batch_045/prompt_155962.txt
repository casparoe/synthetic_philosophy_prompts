I'm writing a short term paper for a seminar on explanation in biology and mind, and the move I want to make in it depends on getting a distinction right. My department's library had a recent popular book on Dennett's Darwin book — the chapter called something like the one on skyhooks and cranes — and before I rely on it I'd like to know how much of its summary I can trust. The passage that matters reads as follows: before Darwin the only answer to "why is there design?" was a designer, and Dennett recasts that whole history with a pair of images borrowed from construction: a crane is a machine that stands on the ground and lifts things up, and it can do that only because it was itself assembled by earlier cranes, whereas a skyhook hangs from the sky and lifts without any support of its own. Cranes are natural mechanisms like natural selection, which build complexity gradually out of simpler parts; skyhooks are supernatural interventions — God, a life force, a miracle — that drop complexity in from above. To ask for a skyhook is therefore just to ask for a miracle, and Dennett's real claim is that every apparent exception to Darwinism, from consciousness to language to morality, is at bottom a disguised request for one. His real target, though, is not creationism but greedy reductionism: the greedy reductionist thinks that since everything is made of physical stuff, only fundamental physics is really true, and talk of minds, meanings, and values is a temporary shorthand that a finished science will discard. Against this he recommends "good" reductionism — stay at the level of the pattern, treat higher-level explanations as autonomous, don't try to derive biology from chemistry or the rationale for a chess opening from quantum field theory. The upshot, the book says, is that Dennett is best read as an anti-reductionist who uses Darwin to defend the autonomy of the special sciences without buying into anything spooky.

Two things in that worry me, though I can't say exactly where it goes wrong. The first is the anti-reductionist framing: I've also heard Dennett described as someone who thinks reductionism has an undeservedly bad reputation, so "stay at the level of the pattern" may be a methodological point about where explanation has to begin rather than a metaphysical claim that higher-level facts float free of the lower ones — and if so, the book may have turned a modest caution about skipping levels into a full-blown autonomy thesis he wouldn't sign. The second is the slide from "you say there's no crane for this" to "you must be smuggling in a skyhook," which looks like it closes off a third option: a mechanism that is grounded and non-miraculous but not itself a product of cumulative selection, such as the sort of spontaneous self-organization or structural constraint people appeal to when they think selection only canalizes what physical law already makes likely. A friend who read the same chapter says the whole crane/skyhook business is just "natural versus supernatural with better PR," and I don't want to repeat that in a seminar if it's unfair either.

What I'd like is a clause-by-clause audit rather than a verdict: which sentences a specialist would accept as stated, which are fair simplifications of something messier, which are subtle misrepresentations (a distinction collapsed, a modest thesis overstated, a term doing more work than it can bear), and which are outright wrong. For each part I'd also like to know what a defender of the view — someone who tries to explain apparent design without skyhooks and without skipping levels — would say in reply, and in particular how they would answer two challenges: that the distinction is question-begging against a theist who says the designer is self-explaining rather than unexplained, and that "grounded but non-Darwinian" is a third category the dichotomy doesn't have room for. Finally, since my paper turns on it: if a theorist says consciousness arises from complex physical organization and that this arising is a brute, further irreducible fact about the world, what test should I apply to decide whether they have offered a crane or a skyhook — and what does the answer imply about how much work the distinction can really do in that debate? Please reason from the structure of the position rather than from sources I'd have to look up.
