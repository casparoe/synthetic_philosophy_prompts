I work in a small analytical unit inside my country's national statistics office, and I'm doing a part-time master's in public policy, so I keep meeting the same puzzle from two directions at once. My government has just written a well-being objective into its budget framework, openly modelled on the World Happiness Report. The headline number is the mean answer to the Cantril ladder question — picture a ladder on which the best possible life for you is ten and the worst is zero, and say where you stand today — averaged over three years of about a thousand respondents annually. The statutory target is not a level or a threshold but a rank: top twenty within ten years. Ministries now bid for funding by costing proposals in well-being-adjusted life years, one point of ladder score for one person for one year, and the treasury ranks bids by cost per unit, so a cheap intervention producing a small gain for a very large number of mildly dissatisfied people beats an expensive one that rescues a far smaller number of people in deep misery. My colleagues treat that trade-off as obvious arithmetic and I can't see why it is. Three further features strike me as odd. First, what gets measured is a reflective judgement about one's life rather than anything about how life feels from moment to moment, even though our survey also asks about yesterday's laughter and worry, and in pilots the two move in opposite directions. Second, the counted population is residents aged fifteen and over: a programme aimed at children is scored only through the answers of the adults around them, people who arrived last year count fully in the average, our own emigrants vanish from it, and nobody beyond the border is counted however much our trade or emissions policy affects them. Third, the same government's health service allocates using quality-adjusted life years with a severity weighting that gives extra weight to the worst off, so the two halves of the state seem to disagree about whether it is total welfare or the condition of the worst off that matters.

What I want is the implicit theory, reconstructed rather than asserted. What view of well-being does benchmarking a country on mean life evaluation presuppose — about whether well-being is a judgement, an experience, a state of the person, or something about the fit between a life and what its owner wants — and about whether such a thing is comparable across persons and across cultures on one zero-to-ten scale at all? What does targeting a *rank* presuppose that targeting a level does not, given that a rank can be gained by other countries falling and lost while everyone improves? What does cost-per-unit allocation presuppose about aggregation, about whether gains to different people simply add up, and about time and people not yet born? And what view of the political community is carried by the choice of resident adults as the counting unit? Alongside that, I'd like the negative part: which of these commitments does the practice strictly require, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it underdetermined, so that several quite different theories of well-being and of distribution would equally license it and adopting it commits us to less than it appears to? If, say, a hedonic, a desire-satisfaction and an objective-list theorist could all sign up to most of this apparatus, I'd like to know which features would separate them and which would not.

A few requests about how you answer. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Where your reconstruction rests on a contested claim in philosophy or in survey methodology, mark it as contested and tell me how confident you are and what would settle it, rather than presenting the tidy version as received wisdom; I'd also like to know which of my three puzzles you think is the deepest and which is really a measurement problem in disguise. And if the whole framing is confused — if what a practice presupposes isn't a well-defined question here, or if two of my puzzles are the same puzzle, or if the trade-off I find puzzling is genuinely just arithmetic — please say so plainly instead of working through each item politely in turn.
