I'm putting together the centrepiece handout for a six-week module on end-of-life decision-making that I teach inside a larger applied ethics course, and I'd rather it be a classification instrument than a survey: a decision tree that takes a student from answers about concrete cases to a named position on the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die cluster. The students have plenty of clinical exposure but no theory vocabulary, so each branch node has to be a concrete case or a plain claim they could answer without already knowing which view it pushes them toward — no node may simply ask whether killing is worse than letting die, and no node may turn on a label. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions that genuinely separate positions (is there a descriptive distinction here at all; is it pre-moral and causal or does classifying the case already require knowing what the agent owed; is it binary, scalar, or does it need a third category like enabling; does it carry moral weight in its own right or only as a proxy for counterfactual dependence, causal structure, intention versus foresight, rights and claims, agent-relative prerogatives, or facts about who was going to be responsible for the outcome anyway; does the asymmetry run only one way, or are some allowings worse than the matching doings), then locate the actual positions in that space, and note the combinations nobody occupies with a one-line diagnosis of whether they are incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

On cases: I want ten diagnostic questions, and I want the set to stress the distinction rather than confirm it. Please include at least one case where the agent both created the threat and now declines to remove it; one where not preventing requires a positive act (unplugging a ventilator, opening a valve, cancelling an automatic delivery); one where the harm is produced by an independent agent the subject merely fails to restrain; one where the omission is a breach of an institutional role; one genuine killing-by-letting (leaving someone exposed to cold, opening a door onto a hazard); one where the causal mechanism was set up long in advance and the agent merely declines to intervene now; one where the agent prevents a third party from rescuing; and one where the doing/allowing classification seems to shift depending on how fine-grained you make the description of the action — for instance whether refusing to continue a treatment is different in kind from never having started it, and whether the tree can actually tell those apart or whether it has to flag them as inseparable.

Deliverable format: name each leaf where a name exists and mark it "unnamed" where it doesn't, with one sentence on its most awkward commitment; mark which nodes are load-bearing (many leaves split there) versus fine-grained; and flag explicitly any pair of positions your tree cannot separate, such as a brute principle of doing and allowing versus one derived from victims' claims, or a counterfactual-dependence reduction versus a causal-process reduction. Finally, add a short scoring note for me: patterns of answers that indicate a student is tracking the distinction itself, versus patterns driven by something orthogonal that would make the handout misfire in seminar — outcome severity, legal blameworthiness, or simple squeamishness about certain mechanisms — since I'd rather drop a question than spend a class hour on a misdiagnosis.
