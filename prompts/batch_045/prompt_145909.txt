I need a speech and I'm a bit out of my depth, so I'm hoping you can draft one for me.

The occasion: my alma mater is opening a new building next month, the Center for Democratic Deliberation, and I've been roped into giving the 10-minute address at the dedication. The building exists because one guy — a hedge fund guy, his name's on the thing — gave the money for a reason. His reason is that Athens is the cautionary tale: a direct democracy murdered Socrates and lost the Sicilian Expedition, so what we need is a center for studying how good deliberation gets protected from popular pressure. He, his family, and a bunch of his business friends will be maybe a third of the room, and they'll be listening for whether the place is going to say what he paid for it to say.

The other two thirds are faculty, grad students, and some undergrads, and essentially none of them agree with him. Their view is that the three canonical Athenian failures aren't really about democracy at all — they're about an empire doing empire things, and the Assembly was just the room where imperial policy got ratified. So the two available speeches are both terrible: if I say the crowd was fickle and emotional, the faculty think I'm shilling for the donor; if I say it was all imperialism and the demos had nothing to do with it, the donor will know he's been had at his own ceremony. And honestly both of those are just the thing everybody already says.

Here's what I actually want to try out on them. I think the three cases group together around something else: in each one, somebody had a live chance to reverse course in public, and the institution made reversal expensive. Mytilene is the case where it happened — they voted to kill every man in the city and enslave everyone else, slept on it, reopened it the next day, and had to send a second ship to overtake the first one, and it barely got there in time. Sicily is the case where it didn't happen but you can see the shape of it: the way I read Nicias's second speech, he was trying to talk them out of the expedition by itemizing everything it would take, and instead of balking they voted him more of it. And the trial of Socrates is the case where the reversal was built into the procedure — there was a second vote, on the penalty — and Socrates used it to propose that the city reward him with maintenance in the Prytaneum, and only then a fine his friends would guarantee, rather than the one thing that would have let everyone climb down, which was exile. So: not emotion, not empire, but the price of changing your mind in front of 6,000 people.

Can you build the speech around that? What it needs to do:

Not tell either half of the room they're wrong, at their own building.
Be specific enough that my old professor hears something real. She's 88, she's in the front row, she's forgotten more about the Mytilene debate than I will ever know, and she will notice both any detail I get wrong and anything she's heard a hundred times before.
Land on an actual claim about what a Center for Democratic Deliberation ought to spend the next decade studying — something with teeth, not deliberation is good and we should have more of it.
Be a speech, not an essay at a podium: short sentences, something the undergrads can follow without the machinery showing, and nothing that makes the recieving line afterward weird.

Don't over-index on keeping both sides happy, actually. If the argument is worth ten minutes it'll annoy somebody, and I'd much rather annoy both camps slightly than bore both. I just don't want the donor's family to feel personally insulted at the ceremony for the thing they gave. And please don't pad it — ten minutes, and it should sound like a person talking, not like a TED talk.
