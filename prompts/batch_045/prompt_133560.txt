You are a professional philosopher working in moral and political philosophy, thoroughly familiar with Camus's abolitionist writings — his essays against the guillotine and his broader refusal of the role of executioner — including the several independent arguments they contain: that the penalty is irreversible and therefore cannot answer to fallible courts, that it is preceded by a wait that is arguably a worse punishment than the death itself, that its deterrent effect is unproven and in any case not why it is defended, and that it cannot be squared with any honest version of proportional return. Please think carefully before answering: consider at least two or three candidate principles and what each one costs before recommending one, and do not simply restate Camus's arguments back to me.

I am trying to work out what I actually think about killing by the state, and I trust my verdicts on cases more than my arguments, so treat the following as data.

- A police marksman shoots a hostage-taker in order to stop him killing his hostage: permissible.
- A soldier kills an enemy sniper in a defensive war, at a moment when the sniper poses no threat to anyone: permissible, or at any rate not something I am willing to call murder.
- A murderer whose guilt is certain — confession, video, no procedural defect — is executed after a scrupulously fair trial: not permissible.
- The same murderer sentenced to life without parole: permissible.
- A prisoner who will certainly go on arranging murders from any prison available to the state, and who cannot be isolated: I go back and forth on this one.
- A jurisdiction that executes by a means that is instantaneous, painless, and unknown to the prisoner in advance — he is told nothing and dies in his sleep: I still say not permissible, but I notice that this is precisely the case where I lose the argument from the cruelty of the wait.
- A condemned man who demands to die and waives every appeal: I feel this is worse, not better, and I cannot say why.

Is there a principle that captures this pattern, or close to it? State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case, name the case that motivates each of its clauses, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them you think I should therefore give up, and apply it to the two cases I have not settled: executing someone for a crime that killed no one, and executing a reformed man for a murder committed forty years ago after four decades of imprisonment. Finally, tell me which of Camus's own arguments my verdicts actually commit me to and which they undercut — I would rather find out that one of them is doing no work for me than go on repeating it. Keep the whole answer under seven hundred words.
