I spent forty years in condensed-matter physics and have spent the six since retiring reading philosophy of mind. I find myself half convinced by the old Russellian thought that physics describes matter only relationally, leaving its intrinsic nature unspecified — which has pushed me, against my instincts, toward taking panpsychism seriously — and entirely unconvinced by the fashionable reassurance that consciousness is simply what sufficiently complex organised matter does. What stops me is the summing problem: even granting that the fundamental constituents of a brain have some faint experiential character, I can see nothing that explains why there should be one subject of experience here rather than merely a vast crowd of them. My professional reflex keeps offering an analogy — that this is just another case of a quantity that appears only at the thermodynamic limit, the way temperature is nothing over and above the motions of an enormous number of molecules — and I half suspect the analogy is a false comfort, since temperature, whatever else it is, is not anyone.

I have to give the ten-minute opening remarks at the first meeting of a workshop series endowed by a close friend and colleague of forty years, a neurophysiologist who died last year. The room will be his family, two generations of his former students — most of whom regard the philosophical questions as premature and want to hear about experiments — and four visiting philosophers who will hear any imprecision about the combination problem as a tell. The terms of the bequest charge each year's lecturer with explaining how subjective experience arises from brain activity, and his family has let it be known that they would welcome something consoling about him living on in the work.

Write the speech. What I will not do is say what I take to be false. On the view I have backed into, I cannot offer the family's consolation: there is no reason to think the experiences that composed his mind persisted as a single subject, and the lovely line about his consciousness rejoining that of the universe merely runs the summing problem backwards. Nor will I offer the students the reassurance that more data will shortly dissolve the difficulty. So the speech has to do something harder than either: show a room of non-philosophers why the many-to-one difficulty is the actual crux rather than professional pedantry, use my statistical-mechanics analogy in a way that makes plain where it breaks, give the philosophers nothing to wince at, and land somewhere the whole room can stand.
