Last night me and a friend spent about three hours arguing about the dark forest hypothesis — the idea that the sky is quiet because any civilization that could talk is deliberately staying quiet, and staying quiet is rational because anything you reveal yourself to has a reason to destroy you before you grow into a threat. I ended up defending it, mostly because her counter felt too cheap: her whole case was that it's unfalsifiable, any amount of silence is consistent with it, and it's just the great filter in a trench coat. But I realised I don't actually know how much the view risks, and I want to before I keep sticking up for it.

So: what would count against it? I don't want a survey of Fermi paradox solutions, Ive read plenty of those and they all say the same handful of things. I want a map of the position's exposure, and I want it sorted by how decisive each item would be.

Things that would make the answer useful to me:

First, I think the view is three claims glued together with different exposure: (a) there are or were lots of technological civilizations; (b) the reason we see none is that they hide; (c) hiding is rational because detection is cheap and destruction is cheap enough that the expected value of making contact is negative. A defeater for (a) is not a defeater for (c). Tell me which defeaters kill the distinctive mechanism and which just leave the whole scenario unemployed — if life is simply rare, or the filter is ahead of us rather than behind, or we are just first and the grabby ones haven't arrived, then the dark forest hasn't been refuted so much as made irrelevant — and I want your verdict on whether that second kind should count as counting against it at all, or only against the package deal.

Second, my own candidate for the strongest defeater, which my friend shrugged off and I want a real verdict on. If the galaxy has been around for over ten billion years and civilizations are common, plenty of them should have been within a few hundred light years of us at some point. Earth has been advertising a chemically weird atmosphere — oxygen together with methane, hard to explain without life — for hundreds of millions of years at least, maybe billions. And at, say, one percent of lightspeed, a hundred light years is ten thousand years, which is nothing. A civilization scared enough of future rivals to shoot on sight has an obvious cheaper move than waiting around for someone to shout: hit promising cradles while they are still defenceless. We are sitting on one, visibly, with no sign anybody ever bothered. Doesn't that hurt more than the usual "we've been leaking radio for a century and nobody has shot us" point, which seems to me basically worthless because the reply is just that we'd have to wait a few thousand years for the answer? And if it is strong, is there a version of the dark forest that survives it — one where detecting a biosphere is much harder than detecting a deliberate signal, or where sterilizing planets isn't actually cheaper than simply outrunning them?

Third, here's what I take to be the load-bearing assumptions; tell me which really carry weight and which are decoration: that you cannot reveal that you exist without revealing where you are; that you cannot make a credible promise not to attack; that hiding is cheaper than being found but more expensive than just talking; that a civilization is a single agent with stable preferences across the millennia any of this takes to play out; and that every civilization reasons roughly the same way about the risk, so nobody defects. My suspicion is that the last two are where it is genuinely fragile — the whole thing seems to need a convergence that nothing about evolution or culture guarantees — but I can't tell whether that is fatal or just a reason to say the forest is patchy rather than dark.

Fourth, what would people who actually hold the view concede as counting against it, as opposed to what its critics say counts against it? And of everything you list, which defeaters are live rather than merely logically possible?

Last: is my friend right that it has been qualified into unfalsifiability? Does it predict anything we could in principle notice besides more silence — something about what old stars and old galaxies should look like, about what we should or shouldn't find floating around in our own solar system, about whether anyone has ever seeded it with quiet listening hardware? If it is well insulated, say so, and then say whether that is actually a problem for this kind of claim or just what you'd expect from a hypothesis about what other agents choose to do. For what it's worth, I'd be moved most by a positive detection of something loud and old — galaxy-scale engineering, waste heat where it shouldn't be — and almost not at all by another argument that life is just rare, because that feels like changing the subject rather than refuting anything.
