I have been thinking on my own about the old argument from hallucination: if a hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a genuine perception, then it seems that what you are aware of in the good case must be something the bad case also contains — some inner ingredient shared by both episodes — so that seeing the world is really just a matter of how that ingredient happens to have been caused. I can make out two quite different ways of resisting that last step, and I would like you to compare them.

The first holds that the two episodes are simply different kinds of conscious state: in the good case your experience is at least partly made up of the perceived object itself, so there is no shared ingredient to be found, and the fact that the two feel alike from the inside is only a fact about how they feel, not a clue to what they are. The second grants that the two episodes may be exactly the same kind of conscious state, and instead claims that only the good one can give you knowledge or justification, since knowing how things are requires that they actually be that way.

Which of the two is really what the argument from hallucination is pressing against, and which of them, if it turned out to be right, would change our thinking about perception more? Please also take up a third case that I find harder: illusion proper, where you really are perceiving something — a coin that looks elliptical, a stick that looks bent — but it appears other than it is. Does that case embarrass one of the two views more than the other, and why?

One last pressure point: suppose a future neuroscientist could produce in you a neural event of exactly the sort that occurs when you genuinely see a red apple. Would the first view then be forced to say that you are not conscious of anything at all in that moment, and if so, how much of a cost is that?
