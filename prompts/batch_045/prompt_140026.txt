I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and my editor has asked me to run a ninety-minute session for our science desk — about a dozen working reporters, no history-of-science training beyond whatever they absorbed as undergraduates — on writing about new findings without writing Whig history: the habit of narrating the past as a march toward the present. The desk covers AI and biomedicine, which is exactly where the pull is strongest, because our house style rewards the breakthrough, the first, the long-awaited, and because our sources — papers, press releases, lab PIs — hand us the teleology pre-packaged. Most of the room will arrive a little defensive: they know the term, they suspect it is an accusation of naivety aimed at people who write about science for a living, and they will want to know what is left of their job if one is not allowed to say that a field has finally cracked something. I do not need Whig history explained to me; I need a plan for teaching it. And please do not give me a reading list or a bibliography — I am assembling this against a deadline from what I can think through myself.

What I want, concretely: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what (my assumption is that the room has to feel the pull of the narrative before I name it, and that the claim that science really does accumulate reliable knowledge has to be conceded explicitly before I can attack the claim that the route to us was a route to us, without anyone concluding that the alternative is relativism); the one case that should carry the whole session, what it must have and what it must lack, and why not the obvious candidates — my worry is that anything canonical arrives with a pre-digested narrative already attached, and the reporters will rehearse that narrative rather than analyze it; and the misconceptions in the order they will surface, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Here is the list I am expecting, so that you can correct it rather than reproduce it: that it is a ban on saying past science was wrong; that it is only about heroes and hero-worship; that the remedy is to add more obstacles and context to an otherwise unchanged story; and — the one I most want handled — that the anti-Whig story, the vindication plot in which the neglected figure turns out to have been right all along, is the cure rather than the same disease with the valence flipped. I have also been sketching a scheme of four failure modes to organize the session around: the anticipatory move, in which X is said to have paved the way for Y; the vindication plot; the maturity metaphor, in which fields come of age or are not yet mature; and a fourth I have been calling the retrospective unit — writing as though today's categories (biology, AI safety, peer review) were the natural joints in the past, so that history reads as a slow assembling of our own furniture. Tell me whether that four-way scheme should be the spine of the session or whether my fourth simply collapses into something I have already named.

Two more things. Say plainly what I should refuse to cover in ninety minutes and announce as out of scope, and give me one question I can put to the room at the end that would show me they have hold of the distinction rather than the vocabulary. Then tell me how confident you are in each part of the plan, and where your recommendations rest on a contested reading of what the term covers — I would rather have that flagged than smoothed over. And if the premise is broken — if ninety minutes cannot do this, if my four-way scheme is muddled, or if what I have described is really a request for a style guide dressed up as historiography — say so directly rather than handing me a polite plan for a workshop that should not be run.
