me and my friend Sam got into a massive argument last night about cranes and skyhooks and I've been turning it over since. I've basically come to accept the crane side: anything that looks like it needs a top-down, designed-in, irreducible explanation can in principle be rebuilt bottom-up out of dumber parts, and the reason this sounds like a cheat to people is that they hear a crane story as a "nothing but" story — the thing people call greedy reductionism is bad because of the rhetorical move, not because bottom-up explanation is in trouble. Sam's counter, which got under my skin, was that this is unfalsifiable: any apparent skyhook can always be declared a crane we haven't finished building yet, so the position makes no prediction anyone could in principle fail, and it's really just a research heuristic dressed up as metaphysics.

So I want a straight answer to: what would actually count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and split them into what a committed crane-person should concede counts against them versus what they can legitimately shrug off — not just what critics say counts. Two things I want you to deal with head on. First, my own made-up category: "smuggler's cranes," where the bottom-up story quietly reimports the very thing it was supposed to explain (representation, function, normativity, aboutness, meaning) into its base machinery, so the explanation only looks complete because you stopped asking what the base parts are doing. If most of the famous crane stories are smuggler's cranes, is that a defeater or just a to-do list? Second, a case I invented to test myself: imagine neuroscientists find a mechanism whose causal powers can only be specified by reference to what the organism's states are about, and every purely structural description of it loses predictive power — call it the Rosetta case — versus a variant where the purely structural description predicts everything perfectly and the semantic description is just bookkeeping. Which of those should move me, and is either of them even the right shape of evidence, or is the position insulated against empirical findings altogether and only vulnerable to a conceptual collapse (say, an argument that every crane story has to presuppose intentionality or normativity somewhere, so crane-ism quietly becomes the view it was meant to replace)?

The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a stall audit rather than a thought experiment: for a given phenomenon, say in advance what a completed crane story would look like and what observable difference there'd be between one that's progressing and one that's stalled — then run that on three areas, roughly: how matter comes to be about anything, conscious experience, and normativity or moral fact. I'd like you to say where, honestly, each of those looks like it's progressing, where it looks stalled, and which of the three is the one where a crane-person should be most worried. Please don't give me reading lists or citations — I can't look anything up right now, so argue it out from the structure of the view itself. And end with a single last line in this form: Verdict: [one of: falsifiable / partly exposed / insulated / unfalsifiable] — [the defeater class you'd put money on, in a few words].
