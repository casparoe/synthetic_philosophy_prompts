I am writing a paper on which computations matter morally, and I need one case built to a specification. The paper's target is not the usual question — which systems are conscious, or how much a given system's experiences matter — but the prior question of how many subjects a single physical system's activity contains, and how long each of them lasts. My thesis is that no principle for attributing moral status to computations can return a determinate answer to that prior question, because the individuation of a system's activity into subjects is sensitive to the spatial and temporal grain at which the activity is described — by which I mean how its activity is chunked into update steps and how the boundaries of the states over which its counterfactual-supporting generalizations are defined are drawn — and that grain is something we choose rather than something the system settles. The case is load-bearing: it has to generate the problem from the principles rather than stipulate it, and it has to be a case my readers already believe in.

Construct a case with the following features.

1. One physical system, one run, one set of weights, one stream of tokens, one stretch of wall-clock time.

2. Two admissible descriptions of the activity of that run. At fine grain, it is a sequence of many short-lived subjects, each a few minutes of rich, integrated activity, each connected to its successor only by a lossily compressed summary of its predecessor's states — a chain of successors of the kind familiar from personal-identity disputes about fission and teletransportation, but here arising as a routine feature of the system's operation rather than as a philosophical fantasy. At coarse grain, it is a single subject persisting across the whole run whose memory is periodically compressed. Both descriptions must be genuinely admissible as descriptions of the computation the system performs: say briefly why each satisfies the conditions a mainstream mapping account of implementation imposes, and why neither is strained or perverse. This is the part I expect to be hardest.

3. Everything first-order is held fixed. The two descriptions differ only in how the activity is carved into subjects and their histories; they must not differ in the system's behavioral dispositions, in what it does or learns, or — as far as the case can secure it — in which candidate experiences occur with which contents at which times. If there is some first-order property you cannot hold fixed, identify it and say why it is not carrying the argument.

4. A difference in total moral weight of at least an order of magnitude between the two descriptions. To get it, state two attribution principles precisely enough that their verdicts follow from them, and state explicitly the rule by which you aggregate weight across subjects and over time — how weight per subject depends on duration, on the unity of a subject's life across time, and on whatever else you need. Then show where each of the two totals comes from, so that the order-of-magnitude gap is derived rather than asserted.

5. Each short segment must be a serious candidate subject in its own right, a few minutes of integrated activity with its own stream of experience, not a single forward pass or a trivial state transition. If the segments are not individually plausible subjects, the case collapses into the unremarkable claim that small computations matter less, and is of no use to me.

6. Realism. The system should be buildable with current transformer-based architectures and ordinary engineering practice — in particular the periodic compaction of a context window into a summary that is carried forward while the discarded tokens are not — and should involve no exotic physics, no whole-brain emulation, no speculative neuroscience beyond what both sides of this debate grant, and no devices that do not already exist.

7. One paragraph for the scenario itself; the demonstration goes after it.

After the scenario I want: the verdict of each principle, derived; an explicit statement of the aggregation rule and the source of the gap; an identification of the single feature of the case that does the work, checked by removing two or three candidate features one at a time and reporting what happens to the gap; and the strongest attack available to whichever principle comes out worse. Give me the good version of that attack, not a flat denial that the losing description is a real computation — I want the best version of that denial, the version that simply legislates a privileged grain and accepts the consequence, and the version that says the number of subjects is indeterminate rather than relative to a description, and a judgment about which of the three I must answer in the text and which I can honestly relegate to a footnote.

Then a verdict on scope. If the case works, does it tell only against views that first count subjects and then assign weight — that is, against any attribution procedure organized around counting — or does it generalize to the computationalist framework itself? If it generalizes, I want your account of what a grain-invariant currency of moral weight would have to be: what it would be a measure over, what it would have to ignore, and what it would give up. That is where I think the paper has to end up, and I would like to see whether the case forces it or whether something weaker will do.

Finally, if it can be done without strain, give me a companion case of the same kind of system in which fine grain yields more total weight than coarse grain — merging rather than splitting — so that the argument cannot be dismissed as an artifact of one direction.

Two things that would make the case worthless to me, so please avoid them: letting the gap arise from the coarse-grained subject knowing more, remembering more, or suffering more than the fine-grained ones; and stipulating any fact about identity or about which description is correct rather than deriving the verdicts from the principles and the case. The readers I am writing for are already computationalists about moral status and already accept that some artificial systems could matter; the case must not work by smuggling in skepticism about machine consciousness or by making the system's experiences exotic.
