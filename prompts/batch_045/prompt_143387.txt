I direct a state-level interfaith advocacy coalition — about ninety congregations across Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Sikh, and Buddhist traditions — that prepares lay witnesses to testify before legislative committees. We send witnesses to forty to sixty hearings per session. They are almost always volunteers with no training in philosophy or law, they get two minutes each, and my three staff members review and sign off on every testimony in advance. We currently handle religious content case by case, and it is failing in two opposite directions at once: some witnesses testify in a thick theological register that legislators hear as a demand to defer to an authority they do not accept, and other witnesses — told loosely by us to make it secular — end up reciting arguments they do not believe and cannot defend under questioning, which is worse than the original problem.

I want the public-reason tradition turned into a procedure my staff can actually run. The raw material is familiar enough: the thought that coercive law should be justifiable to the people bound by it; the idea that a reason whose force depends on accepting a revelation, a scripture, or a comprehensive doctrine is not available to all citizens in the way a shared reason is supposed to be; the milder proviso version, on which religious reasons are admissible provided that public reasons are also given, in due course; and the standard reply that this asks religious citizens to split themselves in two and silently favors whichever comprehensive views are already culturally dominant. I do not need the debate rehearsed. I need it operationalized: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a named person because it turns on values rather than on whether the rule was followed.

Six things keep going wrong, and I want you to decide which of them are real distinctions that must survive the simplification and which are confusions to discard:

Translation versus ventriloquism. A staffer can usually produce a secular argument that supports the witness's position. The test I have been using is to ask the witness whether, if that argument were decisively refuted, they would still hold the position: if yes, there is a reason left over that has not been made public, and the testimony is ventriloquism. Is that the right test, and is ventriloquism actually a failure here or an acceptable bit of rhetoric?

The proviso's deferral. The permission to supply public reasons in due course is unenforceable in a two-minute oral testimony that is never followed up. Should the procedure require the public-reason counterpart in the same venue, and what does it do with the witness who has none?

Three audiences in one utterance. The witness is speaking at once to the committee, which will coerce; to their own congregation, which authorized them, pays dues, and will watch the clip; and to an archived public record with a longer memory than either. A formulation can be in order for the first and read as an act of shame to the second.

Premise-use versus indictment-use. Religious language that supplies a premise for what the law should be seems different from religious language addressed to the polity's conscience — the register of this law makes us cruel, or of blessing and curse. I suspect the two deserve different treatment in the procedure, but I am not sure the difference is stable under pressure.

The evenhandedness check. Whatever test we adopt should be applied to secular comprehensive doctrines as well: a witness who opposes a bill because it violates a Marxian or a Randian or a strict-libertarian world-view is in the same position, it seems to me, as the witness who opposes it on scriptural grounds. If the procedure admits one but not the other, it is just a taste test.

Proximity to coercion. The requirement surely bites hardest on a judge writing an opinion and least on a letter to the editor, with committee testimony somewhere in the middle. My staff need the dial calibrated for the settings we actually operate in, not for the ones philosophers write about.

Also tell me where intelligibility (others can follow it), accessibility (others can see its force without accepting its source's authority), and shareability (others could reasonably accept it) come apart, and which threshold you are recommending for committee testimony and why.

Deliverable: a numbered procedure of no more than a dozen steps, preceded by the one-paragraph rationale that justifies the simplifications, and followed by a one-page version my field organizers can use in twenty minutes with a volunteer who has never thought about any of this. State explicitly where judgment cannot be eliminated and who signs off there.

Then run it on three of our actual recurring cases. First, a Catholic parish volunteer opposing expansion of solitary confinement, whose actual reasons include the Gospels and the dignity of the person, and for whom public reasons — recidivism, mental-health costs, litigation exposure — also genuinely exist. Second, a Sikh congregation supporting statewide tenant right-to-counsel, whose reason is thoroughly religious and who has no data and no appetite for any. Third, a case where I expect to dislike the answer: a member congregation wants to oppose a bill mandating content in public-school sex education, their reasons are inescapably scriptural, and they regard the available secular framing as a mask rather than a translation. My fear is that a well-built procedure will systematically clear the witnesses whose theology happens to converge with secular consensus and silence the ones whose theology does not, which would make us a progressive advocacy group with a religious decoration. If that is what the procedure implies, say so, and say whether the honest fix is to accept the asymmetry, to loosen the rule, or to route these witnesses to a different kind of speech rather than to silence.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: what it cannot settle about whether public reason is a sound requirement in the first place, what it cannot know about how legislators actually receive a line of testimony, and what it should do when a witness's tradition forbids them to offer any reason other than their own.
