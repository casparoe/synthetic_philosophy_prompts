I'm recently retired from forty-odd years in observational astronomy and I've got into philosophy a bit late — there's a reading group at the library, six of us, and we've just finished a term on time. Our last session is on the block universe, the Rietdijk–Putnam sort of argument (roughly: for any two well-separated events you care to pick, there's some inertial frame in which they're simultaneous, so if everything simultaneous with you right now is real, then everything is real). I want to close the term with a poem rather than a summary, and I've got definately fixed on a sestina.

Here's what I'm after. Six stanzas of six lines plus the three-line envoi, with the usual end-word rotation — the last end-word of one stanza comes first the next time round, and so on. But the six end-words should be six concrete events, not abstract nouns. I was thinking: the supernova I helped observe in '87, my granddaughter's birth, my knee operation, the kettle boiling, and you pick the other two (something small, something very far away). No rhyme needed and no strict meter, but it has to read like a poem and not like a list of worked examples.

The reason I want a sestina in particular: I want the rotation to do the argument's work. Each stanza should be one observer's now — first me sitting still, then someone walking past me (a neighbour in the corridor of the flats, say), then maybe someone further off moving faster — and each time the same six events come back but permuted, so which of them land on the same slice keeps changing. No arrangement should feel like the correct one. Then the envoi crams all six into three lines, half-hidden, which is the payoff: the same set, all at once, none of them more present than any other.

The bit I actually care about, and the reason I'm asking you instead of doing it myself: my grandson (doing a PhD, insufferable about it) says the argument doesn't get you a block universe at all. If simultaneity is only frame-relative, he says, then 'is real now' is frame-relative too, so all you've shown is that everything is real-for-some-frame — which he calls presentism with extra algebra. I think he's wrong and I can't say why in prose. I want the poem to answer him, somewhere in the last stanza or the envoi, and it has to be an answer he'd have to take seriously rather than an isn't-time-mysterious shrug.

Two more things. Get the physics right — nobody outruns a light signal, and the simultaneity slice has to tilt the correct way (in my frame, the moving observer's slice slopes toward the direction she's travelling, so events ahead of her that she calls now happen later by my clocks). And the closing line should be honest about what you give up: passage, becoming, whatever it is — don't smuggle it back in at the last moment.

A line or two of note afterwards is fine, but the poem has to carry the argument on its own, and I'd like it under a page.
