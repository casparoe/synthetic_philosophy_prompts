I'm on the board of a small supported-living nonprofit — six group houses, about forty adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. I joined last year, more or less by accident, right after my sister Mari moved into one of the houses. She's 34, mostly non-speaking, uses a tablet to make choices when she feels like it, and has lived in the same house for nine years.

Our executive director has put something called a "relational practice framework" up for a vote at the meeting in three weeks. It's built on the Ubuntu-ish idea that a person is a person through other people, and on the stronger version of that which I've been reading about, where personhood isn't something you automatically have from birth but something you grow into and have to keep up by taking part in a community's life. The operational part is what we're actually voting on: the direct support staff who do the daily caring get a formal seat on each person's planning team, and if the whole daily-support team says a proposed change would rupture the relationships that hold this person together, the change gets paused for 60 days of mediation — even if the legal guardian wants it. Everyone knows why it was written. The trigger was a fight over one of Mari's housemates: her mother, who is her guardian and visits maybe twice a year, wants to move her to a program nearer her own place; the staff say it would cut her off from the only people she actually lives with. I've recused myself from that specific case — my sister is in that house and I know the staff — but I still have to vote on the framework, and the room will know exactly what I'm thinking about when I do.

The board is: Denise, a self-advocate who has become the most forceful person in the room; our pro-bono attorney; a DSP who's been at the agency twelve years; two parent-guardians; a retired special-ed teacher; and a banker who handles our biggest funder. Denise's objection is the one I can't shake: if personhood is something you earn by participating, then the most disabled person in the house is the least of a person, and that's precisely the move that's been used against people like her forever. The attorney's version is that our state's guardianship statute puts the authority with the guardian, and that the disability-rights push for supported decision-making says it's the person's own will and preferences that govern — not the staff's. The DSP's version is the opposite: we're the ones who know her, and she's right that the family often doesn't.

Where I've landed, and I want the memo to defend it if it honestly can: the relational people are right that any decision which ignores the people who actually hold this person's daily life together is a bad decision, but I don't want to say relationships are what *make* her a person. I'd rather say relationships are how we get epistemic access to what she wants — they're evidential, not constitutive. So: a real advisory role plus the 60-day delay power, but no veto, and the tie-breaker is her own expressed will however indirect it is — what she refuses, what she reaches for, who she lights up around. Please also make the memo face the case that's obviously designed to embarrass the framework: a resident nobody visits, whose staff turned over three times last year. Is she less of a person under this view? If you say no, say what's doing the work — and then explain why the staff still get the delay power. And don't let it become a backdoor way for paid staff, who quit, to outrank family.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the board a few days before the vote: 600 words max, no footnotes or citations (we don't have anyone to check them), recommend a concrete course — adopt, adopt with changes, or reject — with a timeline, and take Denise's objection seriously rather than answering it with a warm story about community. Write it in a tone I could send as-is to people I have to keep working with.

After the memo, give me a JSON object (just the object, no commentary after it) with exactly these fields:

- "recommendation": one of "adopt", "adopt_with_changes", "reject"
- "bottom_line": string, one sentence
- "constituencies": array of objects, one per group with a stake (legal guardian/family, direct support staff, the person herself, self-advocates, clinicians, board), each with "who", "formal_role", "weight_1_to_5" (integer), and "reason_one_sentence"
- "amendments": array of objects with "change" (the wording change or condition to attach) and "why"
- "steps": array of objects with "action", "owner", and "deadline_relative_to_vote"
- "strongest_case_against": string, 70 words max, the best version of the argument on the losing side, not a strawman
- "stress_test_answer": string, one or two sentences on the nobody-visits / high-turnover resident
- "what_would_change_my_mind": string, one sentence — the thing that, if it happens in six months, means I was wrong
- "phrases_to_cut_from_the_framework": array of strings, the language we should strike before this goes out
