I teach a graduate seminar on consciousness, though my own research is in philosophy of action and I have never felt competent to referee this particular family of disputes. Last week, after a workshop talk on global workspace theory, two colleagues — a philosopher of mind in my department, Owen, and a visitor who works on the neural basis of attention, Nadia — got into an argument over dinner that neither would concede, and I have been turning it over since. Owen's opening move was that global workspace theory, despite its ambitions, is a theory of access and not of phenomenality: it tells you which contents get widely broadcast to consumer systems and thereby become available for report, reasoning, and action, and that is a functional notion of availability; nothing in it so much as gestures at why there is something it is like to undergo those contents. Nadia replied that this misdescribes the theory's claim, which is constitutive rather than merely correlational: global availability is what phenomenal consciousness consists in, and the demand that there be something over and above availability is just the demand that a functional account not be a functional account.

To force the issue, Owen then proposed a pair of hypothetical findings and asked Nadia to say in advance what her view commits her to. (1) Call the first *orphan ignition*: intracranial recording shows the full signature of workspace ignition — late onset, sustained, long-range synchrony, fronto-parietal involvement — for a specific content, but no downstream consumer systems are recruited at all; the event happens and nothing receives it. (2) Call the second *silent ignition*: the same ignition signature, with every consumer system recruited except the ones that feed the subject's self-model and verbal report; the subject acts on the content appropriately and later forms implicit memories of it, but denies undergoing anything at the time and says the first she knew of her own response was hearing herself give it. (3) Nadia answered that there is no experience in the first case, since availability is a relation to consumers and a broadcast nobody receives is not availability, but that there is experience in the second — a degraded, unselfed experience, because the self-model is merely one consumer among others and losing it costs the subject knowledge *that* she is undergoing the state, not the undergoing itself. (4) Owen said this pair of answers demonstrates his point: for any dissociation whatever, the theory can reclassify the case as not global enough or as experience the subject cannot report, so the claim that there is experience and the claim that there is not never come apart in anything observable; and if availability comes in degrees, then so does consciousness, in which case we need a principled threshold for how many and which consumers suffice, and the word global is doing its work by obscuring that we have none. (5) Nadia granted the threshold problem is real but called it an empirical research question rather than a refutation — the theory predicts specific dissociations among report, action, and memory and stands or falls with them — and said the falsifiability complaint is misplaced, since a constitutive identification of experience with availability cannot be tested by checking for experience independently, there being no independent measure, exactly as with any identity claim. (6) Owen then offered a positive rival he had been working toward: what distinguishes phenomenal from merely accessed contents is not breadth of broadcast but *reflexive* uptake — the broadcast must be received by the system that models the subject as undergoing the content, which is why the self-model is not one consumer among many but the one whose involvement is non-negotiable. (7) Nadia said that is a privileged consumer wearing a lab coat, a central stage with extra steps, and Owen answered that the regress is not vicious because the self-model's own outputs are themselves broadcast and taken up in turn — recursion, not a theater.

I would be grateful for an adjudication rather than a survey of the literature. Please go through the exchange move by move and rate each of Owen's and Nadia's claims on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying briefly why each lands where it does, and tell me who is more right overall — not merely that both have a point. In particular I would like to know whether the disagreement turns on an ambiguity in either access or availability that the two of them are exploiting without noticing, whether Owen's reflexive-uptake condition is genuinely different from what Nadia is defending or only verbally different, and what, if anything, both parties are missing: the consideration that, had either of them thought of it, would have changed the shape of the argument rather than added another round to it.
