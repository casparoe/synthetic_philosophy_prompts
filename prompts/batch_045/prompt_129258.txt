I am a retired plant ecologist who has lately been sitting in on a moral philosophy reading group at my old university, and I keep encountering arguments that have the shape of a calculation I would not have accepted in my own field. Last week the group discussed whether the moral significance of insects could be large in expectation even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is small, on the grounds that the numbers are so enormous that they swamp the probability. One member, who trained in cosmology, offered an analogy to settle the matter: insect suffering, he said, is the dark matter of ethics. You cannot see it directly, it is inferred rather than observed, sceptics dismiss it as a convenient fudge, and yet there is roughly five times as much of it as of the stuff you can actually see — so anyone who is trying to add up what there is and leaves it out has simply made an accounting error of the first order. He used this to license a strong conclusion: that expected insect suffering belongs near the top of any ranking of moral priorities, and that the burden of proof has now shifted onto anyone who would set it aside.

What I would like is an audit of that analogy rather than applause or dismissal. Please lay out explicitly what maps onto what — the unseen stuff, the anomaly that first forced the inference, the failed direct detections, the ratio of invisible to visible, the eventual independent confirmation — and then say where the mapping breaks, and which breaks matter for his conclusion and which are merely cosmetic. My own suspicion is that it breaks in two opposite directions at once. Dark matter was posited to explain a specific observed residual: a discrepancy that an otherwise well-confirmed theory could not absorb. The insect case, so far as I can tell, has no residual to explain, only a loose argument from shared function and behaviour, which strikes me as a different inferential animal altogether. And while the true ratio of individual insects to individual vertebrates is far larger than five to one, my confidence in dark matter is far higher than any honest credence I can attach to insect sentience; so the analogy seems to borrow its credibility from a case where the probability is high and the multiplier modest, and then spend it on a case where the multiplier is astronomical and the probability is, by the group's own admission, small. I also suspect it quietly imports something else: dark matter simply adds, mass to mass, into one total, and I want to know whether that is doing unacknowledged work in making the aggregate look like the only thing worth counting, when a view that cared about averages, or about thresholds, or about the distribution across creatures, would not aggregate that way at all.

There is one further worry I cannot shake, and I would like it addressed directly rather than politely. In my field we distinguished risk, where you have a defensible distribution over the outcomes, from radical uncertainty, where you do not know enough to assign one; and the probability of insect sentience looks to me like the second, in which case multiplying it by a standing insect population on the order of 10^18 and then acting on the product is not really a calculation but a gesture made with numbers. If the dark-matter framing has something to say to that worry, I want to hear it; if it does not, I would like that conceded plainly, along with an honest verdict on whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time. And if there is a better comparison available for this argument, I would be interested: I found myself thinking of the old puzzle in soil microbiology, where the number of cells you could count under a microscope exceeded the number you could grow on a plate by orders of magnitude, and where the unseen majority eventually turned out to be doing most of the work in the system. Tell me what that analogy would license and what it would not, and whether it is any improvement.
