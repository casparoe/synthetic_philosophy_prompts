my flatmate works in distributed systems, and every time I bring up anything game-theoretic he pulls out the same analogy: common knowledge is like the two generals problem (sometimes he calls it the email game). Two armies have to attack at dawn, messengers can get lost, so every "i'll attack if you attack" needs a "got your message" back, and *that* needs its own confirmation, and so on forever — it's turtles all the way down, so you never actually arrive at common knowledge. He then uses it to say something much stronger: since no real agents ever have common knowledge of each other's rationality, the whole apparatus that leans on it — iterated deletion of dominated strategies, backward induction, everything that starts "and everyone knows that everyone knows that…" — is math fiction, and what actually makes coordination happen in the wild is something else entirely (institutions, habits, prices, whatever).

I'm pretty sure the analogy is carrying more weight than it deserves but I can't put my finger on where, so:

- Give me the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the messenger, the dropped message, the "got it", the stack of confirmations, and (if there is one) whatever would play the role of the public battle cry that settles everything at once.
- Where does it break? The thing that bugs me is that in his story the obstacle is a *channel* that can lose messages, whereas common knowledge of rationality isn't something anyone has to transmit to anyone — but maybe that's exactly the point and I'm the one missing it.
- Here's the case I actually care about, and it doesn't look much like his story. At work a model only ships if two on-call engineers both click ship inside the same minute, and the only channel between them is a slack thread with read receipts: you can see the message, you can see the little "seen by 4" under it, you can see that the other engineer is online and typing. Is that common knowledge that they're shipping, or just a few levels of mutual knowledge wearing a nice UI? How would I tell the difference in practice, and does it matter whether they're both staring at the thread at the same moment versus one of them reading it forty seconds after the other?
- He also floated a variant once where the doubt isn't about delivery but about *depth* — everyone is perfectly rational, but everyone knows everyone only reasons a couple of levels deep. I couldn't tell whether that collapses back into the same problem or is a genuinely different one.
- Does the conclusion survive? I want two bits of it separated: (a) you never literally get common knowledge in the wild, so those solution concepts are fairy tales; (b) real coordination never gets reached by reasoning at all. My hunch is (a) is too quick, because some of these concepts seem to have slack in them — deleting a strategy that's dominated by a comfortable margin versus by a hair — and that the answer might differ for iterated dominance than for backward induction in something like a long centipede, where the fact that I've somehow found myself at a late node already looks like evidence that the other person wasn't reasoning the way the theory says.
