I am a fourth-year PhD student in history at a small liberal arts college, and I have been asked to give this year's Founders' Day address: ten minutes, in the chapel, in front of roughly four hundred first-years, most of the faculty, and a dozen people who were actually here in 1974. I am writing to you rather than to a speechwriter because the difficulty here is not rhetorical polish; it is that the occasion has become a small, unusually legible instance of the problem my dissertation is about, and I need the talk to be conceptually honest before it is moving.

The facts, so that you do not have to guess at them. Until last year the college dated its founding to 1889. It did so on the strength of a predecessor body — a women's institute of the same name, chartered in 1889 in the same county, which closed in 1911. A registrar working in the archives in 2023 established what a few people had long suspected: the two institutions share a name and a county and nothing else. Different charter, different site, different denomination, a twenty-three-year gap, no transfer of assets or students or faculty. There is a 1974 memo from the founding president in which he remarks, in so many words, that the earlier date would serve better with the accreditors and with donors. The seal, the motto, and the anniversary were all adopted with that in mind. The correction went out to the campus by email in September; there was an op-ed in the student paper; a group of alumni have written to say that 1889 is true in the way that matters and should be kept regardless. The archivist who did the work is a staff member who has quietly become the villain of the story in some quarters, and she will be in the room.

I was asked to speak partly because I am the person who kept raising the archival question in department meetings, and my colleagues have read the invitation as a version of you broke it, you explain it. So I am not a neutral authority explaining a correction to a room that trusts me. I am a partisan in a live dispute, speaking to people who do not all agree with me, and about a third of whom I will have to work with next week.

Three features of the situation that I want the speech to use rather than smooth over:

The college does have one genuinely continuous practice. Every graduating class since the first, in 1978, has signed a single ledger kept in the library. It is one volume, it is nearly full, and there is a second, identical, entirely blank volume in a drawer beneath it, which will be needed in about fifteen years. This is the only thing on campus that has actually been done without interruption for the whole life of the institution.

It also has one wholly invented ritual, which people love. The Lantern Walk was devised in 1998 by a dean of students out of nothing at all — no precedent, no folk custom, no older ceremony it was reviving. It is now the single thing alumni name most often when asked what they miss.

And it has the graft: a real, closed, unrelated predecessor whose date was taken over as the college's own.

In my own work I have been sorting cases like these into three kinds, and I want the address to keep them apart, without using my labels aloud. A practice can be forged — asserted out of nothing, as the Lantern Walk was. It can be grafted — a genuine but discontinuous predecessor claimed as continuous, as 1889 was. Or it can be ratified — genuinely continuous, and only later made explicit and ceremonial, which is what I think would happen to the ledger if anyone made a ritual of it, and which is interesting precisely because making it explicit would change it. My question, and the question I want the speech to answer in institutional rather than theoretical language, is what each kind of practice can honestly ask of a person who knows which kind it is. A forged practice that forty-odd years of repetition have made real to the people who do it is not nothing; but it is also not the same as the ledger. I do not want the speech to collapse the difference and I do not want it to sneer at the difference.

What the talk cannot say. No "our founders intended." No "the spirit that has always animated this place." No claim that the 1889 institute is the same institution under another name. But equally: no debunking register — no myth said with a smirk, no we have been fooling ourselves, no anthropological distance from the people in the room, who include the man who wrote the memo and the woman who found it. No escape into abstraction: "the real tradition is our values" is unavailable to me, because it is exactly the move that lets a community keep the emotional payoff of a pedigree while giving up the claim that anything in particular happened. And no clean break: the speech cannot announce that we now begin a new tradition, because the whole difficulty of this literature is that a tradition cannot be started by declaring one, and a ritual that announces its own novelty does not bind anyone. If the speech cannot find a way between continued falsehood and cheerful demolition, I would rather know that than be handed a compromise that fudges it.

What it has to do. It has to tell the room what to do with the blank second volume, and with the Lantern Walk — keep it, rename it, keep it and say out loud that it began in 1998, or something better than any of those. It has to be speakable by me, given that I am the person who pushed for the correction, without either apologizing for that or relitigating it. It has to be a Founders' Day address and not a lecture, a retraction notice, or a eulogy: nothing here has died, and I do not want the room consoled. It has to land for first-years who arrived in August and have no stake in any of this, and for the dozen people who were here in 1974 and do.

One more constraint, which I think is the crux. I keep tripping over the first-person plural. The room contains people with forty years here, people with four months, and me, who has spent two years arguing that the plural was built on a false date. A Founders' Day address without "we" is not a Founders' Day address, so I am not asking you to drop the pronoun. I am asking you to have the speech earn it — to give an account, in the concrete vocabulary of this campus, of what makes a community that exists only in being imagined real enough to be worth belonging to anyway. Carry that account with the ledger and the lanterns and the second blank volume, not with adjectives.

Please do not name any theorists and do not use their vocabulary: no invented tradition, no imagined community, no social construction, no print capitalism. If the argument cannot be made in plain institutional terms to four hundred undergraduates and a hundred faculty, it is not finished. For what it is worth, I take this college to be a small-scale version of the claim the modernists make about nations, and I take the ledger — fifty years, one volume, one signature at a time — to be the closest thing we have to an answer; but the speech should not assert that. It should make it visible.

Give me the address as I should deliver it, about thirteen hundred to fifteen hundred words, with any stage directions in brackets. Then add a short prose note, no more than three hundred words, on two things: what you had the speech refuse to say, and what you did with the word "we."
