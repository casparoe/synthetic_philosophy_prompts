THE QUESTION (25 marks; marks are awarded for reasoning as much as for results)

(a) [6 marks] State the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) as they are standardly formulated, and say in a sentence each what question each assumption is intended to answer. Are they straightforwardly rivals?

(b) [8 marks] Two cosmological theories, T_S and T_L, are equally well supported by all the non-indexical evidence available to us. T_S entails that the universe contains 10^11 observers in total; T_L entails that it contains 10^20 observers in total. On both theories observers of our kind arise on planets like ours and are scattered through the universe, and the two theories can be distinguished only by an extremely expensive space-based experiment. A philosopher argues that the experiment should not be funded: we can already tell from the armchair that T_L is almost certainly true, since T_L gives vastly more observers the opportunity to be you. (i) Which assumption is needed to reach this conclusion, and what odds does it yield? (ii) What does the other assumption say about the case? (iii) Why is the argument standardly presented as a difficulty for the assumption it relies on?

(c) [11 marks] A civilization either remains confined to its home system for the whole of its history, with a total historical population of 10^11 observers (call this C), or it goes on to settle its galaxy, with a total historical population of 10^20 observers (call this G). On both hypotheses the first 10^11 observers, the settlers, live in exactly the same kind of society, are the same kind of subject, and have access to the same historical record; on G the remaining, roughly 10^20 observers are much later, are digital emulations running on a very different cognitive architecture, and know themselves to be late. You are a settler and you know it; set your prior odds between C and G at 1:1 and assume throughout that we count observers rather than observer-moments. (i) Compute your posterior odds under SSA alone, taking the reference class to be all the observers in the civilization's history. (ii) Compute your posterior odds if SSA is combined with SIA. (iii) How would your answer to (ii) change if you held that the later emulations fall outside your reference class? (iv) Compare this case with the presumptuous philosopher in part (b), and explain what, if anything, the settlers' knowledge that they are early contributes to the anthropic arithmetic.

THE CANDIDATE'S ANSWER

(a) SSA says that an observer should reason as if they were a random sample drawn from the set of all actually existent observers, past, present and future, in their reference class. SIA says that an observer should reason as if they were a random sample from the set of all possible observers, weighted by the probability of those observers existing; equivalently, given that you exist you should favour hypotheses on which more observers exist, in proportion to the number they contain. The two answer different questions. SSA is a principle of self-location: given that some hypothesis is true, it tells you how to spread your credence over the observer-slots that hypothesis contains. Because the sample is drawn from the pooled population of all actually existent observers, it also generates likelihoods for hypotheses, not only a distribution over slots. SIA is a principle about the prior: it tells you how the bare fact of your own existence bears on which hypothesis is true, on the thought that you might not have existed at all. So they are not rivals in the way they are sometimes presented as being; SSA takes over once the priors are settled, and the combined view, SSA together with SIA, is a single package that Bostrom discusses in Anthropic Bias (2002). SSA's main liability is its sensitivity to how the reference class is drawn; SIA does not have that liability, provided the class is wide enough to contain every observer who is subjectively indistinguishable from you.

(b) The philosopher needs SIA. Under SSA his argument gets nowhere: both theories contain observers, we have no evidence about where in the observer population we fall, and so the likelihood of finding ourselves to be some observer is 1 on either theory. The prior odds of 1:1 survive, and the experiment keeps its expected value. Under SIA the likelihood ratio in favour of T_L is 10^20 over 10^11, that is 10^9, so we should be 10^9:1 confident in T_L. Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher is best read as an argument in favour of SIA: it shows how much evidential work the assumption does and how much costly empirical inquiry it can spare us, and that is a point in its favour rather than against it. Two qualifications. First, the conclusion assumes that no decision of ours turns on a residual probability of 10^-9; if the stakes attaching to the choice between the theories are large enough, even 10^-9 can matter and the experiment retains some expected value. Second, SIA needs some way of handling hypotheses on which infinitely many observers exist, since simple proportionality to the number of observers is undefined there. It is also worth noting that SIA's boost would be questionable if T_L's extra observers were of a very different kind from us, for instance very short-lived fluctuations rather than creatures like ourselves.

(c) (i) With the reference class taken to be all observers in the civilization's history, on C all 10^11 observers are settlers, so the probability of finding myself to be a settler is 1; on G only the first 10^11 out of 10^20 are settlers, so it is 10^11 over 10^20, that is 10^-9. The likelihood ratio is therefore 10^9:1 in favour of C, and at prior odds of 1:1 those are also the posterior odds. This is the doomsday argument in another form: SSA on its own tells you that finding yourself early in a population is evidence that the population as a whole is not much larger than the part of it that has already existed, and nothing needs to be assumed about the prior over total population sizes, because the whole of the shift comes from the factor of 1 over n.

(ii) Once SIA is added, we weight each hypothesis by the total number of observers it contains, which favours G over C by 10^20 over 10^11, that is 10^9. My evidence that I am a settler is simply the evidence that I exist, and that is precisely what the SIA weighting already takes account of, so once SIA is in play the posterior odds are 10^9:1 in favour of G.

(iii) Narrowing the reference class so as to exclude the emulations makes no difference to this. SIA is not reference-class sensitive in the way SSA is, and in any case the emulations are subjectively distinguishable from me, since they know themselves to be late, so they would not be counted among the observers I might have been; G contains vastly more observers than C either way.

(iv) The settlers' knowledge that they are early makes no difference at all, because under SIA what matters is only the total number of observers that a hypothesis contains. The case therefore works exactly like the presumptuous philosopher in part (b): in both cases we should be 10^9:1 confident in the more populous hypothesis, and the indexical detail about being early drops out.
