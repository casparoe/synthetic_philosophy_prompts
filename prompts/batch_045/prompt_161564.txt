I teach a political theory seminar, 16 undergrads, four tables of four, one 75-minute session, and they'll have read the fortune chapter of The Prince beforehand (the one with the destructive-river comparison and the claim that fortune governs roughly half of what we do). I want a playable game for that session, not a discussion plan.

The thing I actually want them to run into is a problem I can't get them to take seriously by just arguing it: that "he had virtù" and "he succeeded" look like two descriptions of the same event, so the explanation is circular unless virtù is doing some independent work. I don't want anyone to say that out loud during the game.

So here's the mechanic I'm imagining, and please build on it or replace it if you have something better: each table runs a new principality over maybe 8 rounds. Every round has two halves, and a die roll decides one of them — but the players are never told which half, so they can't tell after the fact whether they won or lost by luck. Separately, there's a hidden "tempo" that runs in blocks of three rounds: some eras reward acting impetuously, some reward acting cautiously, and the tempo flips without warning, so a table that picks a style and sticks with it does worse than one that keeps switching. In calm rounds they can bank durable works (alliances, a militia, grain) instead of spending on immediate wins; those works are what pay off when a torrent round hits. And occasionally a one-round window opens — a neighbouring ruler dies, a faction invites you in — that is worth a lot and expires if the table talks about it too long.

Then, after the last round and before I reveal scores: each table writes down how much virtù the other tables showed, purely from watching their decisions. Then I reveal outcomes and they do it again. The gap between the two sets of numbers is the evening's data.

What I need from you: rules and materials (index cards, dice, a scoresheet — nothing I have to buy), a payoff scheme that doesn't need money, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific claims — provision vs. improvisation, matching the tempo of the times, the expiring window, the half-and-half arithmetic, and the grading gap. Be honest about what will definately go wrong (I expect the bold tables to win, and I expect someone to read the die as "chance" when Machiavelli's fortuna isn't chance but the unarranged conjunction of circumstances), and be honest about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say myself.
