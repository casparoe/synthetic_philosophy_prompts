Hey — pretend you're a professional philosopher who has spent an unreasonable amount of your career on the Meno problem / value-of-knowledge literature, and that we're at a whiteboard after a seminar rather than in an exam. Please brainstorm a bit before you commit to anything (sketch two or three candidate diagnoses, then tell me which one you actually believe and why), and write the whole thing in American English. Also: please don't give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I have the reading list, I've read it, and my model can't look anything up anyway. What I want is the diagnosis, with reasons.

Here's the situation. I'm a fourth-year PhD student and last week our department had a work-in-progress session where a visitor ran the swamping argument against process reliabilism — you know the shape of it: the good espresso machine and the broken one both produce an excellent cup, so once you're holding the cup the reliability of the process adds nothing to its value; likewise, if the value of a reliable belief-forming process is exhausted by its tendency to get you truths, and the accidentally-true believer already has the truth, then the reliability is swamped and reliabilism can't explain why knowledge is better than mere true belief. Half the room thought that was decisive, the reliabilists rolled out the conditional-probability reply (knowledge isn't just this one true belief at this one time; it's being hooked up to something that raises the probability of true beliefs on future occasions and across nearby cases, so there is extra value, it just doesn't show up if you stare at a single belief in isolation), and the credit/virtue people in the corner said both sides were missing the point because what matters is that the truth is attributable to the agent's own ability. Then someone pointed out that the swamping worry just moves up a level for the credit people too — if the achievement is valuable because it's an achievement *of a true belief*, why isn't it swamped for exactly the same reason? — and we spent the next two hours going around in precisely the circle I have now watched at three different conferences. Nobody moved an inch, everyone agreed on every case, and my supervisor, who I complained to on the walk home in the rain, just shrugged and said the literature is stuck, which is not a diagnosis.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to stress-test rather than politely agree with, is that there are two different questions being run together under one heading. Call the *local* question: for a given belief, why is it better, in that case, to know than merely to truly believe? Call the *global* question: why is it better to be the kind of agent, or hooked up to the kind of process, that knows, over the long run? My sense is that the swamping argument is strong — maybe even correct — against local answers, and basically silent against global ones; that the reliabilists have quietly been answering the global question while the credit people are answering either the local question or a third, agential one (what's valuable isn't the state but the relation between the agent and the truth); and that most of what presents as head-on disagreement is really people hearing *why is knowledge more valuable than true belief?* as different questions. But I can't rule out two competitors: (a) it's verbal, a fight about which sense of valuable is in play — final vs. instrumental vs. contributory — and nothing more; (b) there's a hidden empirical question doing the real work, since how much extra value the reliable hookup actually buys you depends on how many misleading cases there are in the environment we live in, which is not an armchair matter at all.

So: is this a verbal dispute, two (or three) different questions under one name, bedrock intuitions about credit and attributability that neither side can argue for, or a disguised empirical or formal question? Argue for one, using what each side actually says rather than summarizing positions, and be explicit about what each side would concede. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress — a distinction I ought to be drawing, a formal result (something about when long-run expected truth-ratios and local credit come apart, say), an empirical question someone could actually go answer, or a decent argument that no progress should be expected and why. And if my local/global distinction is muddled, or collapses on inspection, please say that plainly — I'd much rather have it broken than endorsed out of politeness.
