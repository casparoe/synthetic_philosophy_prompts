My daughter's middle school just started splitting kids into two math tracks off a placement test, and at the parent meeting afterward two other dads got into it, and I havent been able to stop replaying it.

Ray: math ability is somewhere around 60% heritable, so the test is mostly measuring what kids were born with. Tracking just sorts kids by biology, and pretending otherwise is wishful thinking.

Jen: heritability is a ratio of variances within one population in one environment. It tells you nothing about why two groups differ, and nothing about what would happen if you changed the environment for everybody. Height is highly heritable and the Dutch got a lot taller in a couple of generations off food alone.

Ray: fine, but if 60% of the variation is genetic, then environment can at most account for the other 40%. So no program is going to do more than close that 40%.

Jen: that's the mistake. Heritability doesn't split any individual kid's score into 60% genes and 40% environment, and it doesn't cap how big an environmental effect can be. PKU is about as genetic as a trait gets and it's completely prevented by changing diet.

Ray: ok, but heritability is exactly the number that predicts response to selection -- that's the breeder's equation, farmers use it every day: select the top performers and you get roughly h-squared of the selection differential in the next generation. So heritability does license a prediction about one kind of intervention, namely sorting. If you think tracking is bad you owe me a story about why the equation works for corn but not for kids.

Jen: h-squared is estimated in a particular environment and predicts response to selection *in that environment*, and tracking isn't a clean sorting of genotypes -- the low track changes the teaching, the teacher's expectations, the peer group, so the selection is itself an environmental intervention that reshuffles the variance you estimated in the first place. Also, equalizing environments *raises* heritability estimates, so a high h-squared is partly evidence that kids are already getting similar environments, not evidence that environment can't do anything.

Can you adjudicate this move by move -- who's right about which specific claim, where each of them is sliding between two different questions, and what both of them are leaving out? In particular: what else would have to be true for Ray's conclusion about tracking to actually follow from the heritability number, and does anything either of them said give us any reason to think it is true?
