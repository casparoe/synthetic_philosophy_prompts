I retired three years ago from a career in experimental geochemistry and have since been sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of the social sciences. I am drafting a short paper for that group and would like either to be argued out of its central claim or given an honest map of how exposed the claim is.

The claim is deflationary, and it concerns the dollar auction. Take the standard version: a single banknote is sold to the highest bidder, the highest bidder pays its bid, the runner-up pays its bid as well and receives nothing, bids rise in small fixed increments, and the players do not know how many rivals there are or how deep their pockets are. My claim has three parts. First, the famous prescription that no rational person should bid at all is a knife-edge result: it depends on common knowledge of rationality in a game with no well-defined last round, and it collapses as soon as there is any small probability that the other players are quitters, or any small probability of a tremble, at which point entering and re-raising is straightforwardly profit-maximizing. Second, conditional on already being the runner-up, the loss is fixed whatever happens, so raising is a free gamble for resurrection, and under any preference that is risk-seeking in the domain of losses it is ordinary choice rather than a bug. Third — and this is the part I care most about — the auction is structurally unlike the real-world escalation it is routinely used to illustrate. In the auction the money flows to an auctioneer who will keep extracting, and the engine of escalation is the near-zero cost of the opening bid; in the sunk-cost cases the literature is really about — a research programme, a war, a bad marriage — the money is gone regardless of what one does next and no adversary is charging for the privilege of continuing. On my reading the auction has been doing rhetorical work for a phenomenon it does not model, and the two should be pulled apart.

To make this more than a suspicion I have sketched two variants, and I want to know what results in them would sink me. In the first, call it the terminated auction, the auctioneer announces in advance that the auction ends after each round with a known probability — say one round in five — so the horizon is finite in expectation and backward induction has a genuine foothold, yielding a computable threshold bid, well below the value of the prize, beyond which continuing loses money in expectation. In the second, the inherited position, subjects are randomly assigned either to have made the current high bid themselves or to be handed that same high bid and that same prospective loss by the experimenter. I predict that under the terminated design escalation shrinks sharply, with the escalation quotient — total amount paid by all bidders divided by the value of the prize — approaching one; and I predict that inheritance makes little difference, since on my account the escalation is produced by the geometry of the game and not by the agent's own history of having chosen.

So: what would count against this package, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I want the observations, experimental results, and formal considerations that should move me, including anything that has arguably already happened in field settings with experienced, high-stakes, repeat agents — auctions with professional dealers, bidding wars, patent races, litigation, corporate research budgets — rather than only hypothetical designs. I want it sorted by how decisive each item would be, and I want you to distinguish what a deflationist like me would himself concede as counting against the view from what he would wave off as a mis-specified model or a confound. And I would like you to take seriously the objection I am most worried about: that a position of this shape can always redescribe any behaviour as rational under some reconstruction of the agent's beliefs or risk attitudes, and so predicts nothing at all. Has my view been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, is that a defect here in a way it would not be in, say, physics? I was trained to distrust hypotheses that can absorb any data. For what it is worth, the thing that would move me most is a single discriminating experiment with a prediction stated in advance, not another intuitively compelling case.

Finally, and this matters to me more than the rest: say how confident you are in each of the central things you assert, and single out the one point you are least sure of — the place where a better-informed reader would most likely correct you. I would rather have a hedge I can go and check than a confident sentence I cannot. Nothing needs citing; I do not want bibliography, only the arguments, in about a thousand dense words if you can manage it.
