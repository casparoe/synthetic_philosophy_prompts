I serve on a committee at my university that is rewriting the academic integrity code, and we keep circling around a case that none of us can state cleanly. Under current policy, a student registered with disability services who carries an ADHD diagnosis may sit an exam on a prescribed stimulant, and the university is obliged to accommodate her; the same drug, taken by an undiagnosed classmate who bought it from a friend, counts as unauthorized cognitive enhancement and can end his degree. The disability office does not claim that the pill works differently in the two students — everyone concedes that the effect on attention, impulse control and endurance is much the same — but rather that in the first case it restores normal functioning and in the second it improves on normal functioning. Our student health plan displays the same shape: it pays for that stimulant, for insulin, for a cochlear implant and for antidepressants, but not for laser eye surgery, not for a wakefulness drug for the student who simply prefers to work nights, and not for growth hormone for a boy who is short because his parents are short and whose endocrinologist can find nothing wrong with him. Anti-doping rules in sport look parallel again: an athlete with a documented hormone deficiency can be certified to use a substance that is banned for everyone else, and an asthmatic may use an inhaler, but a healthy athlete who reaches the same blood levels by the same route is cheating — while training at altitude is legitimate preparation, even though it works by producing precisely the sort of increase that a transfusion is banned for producing.

What I want from you is not a verdict on any one of these rules but a reconstruction of the implicit theory the whole pattern runs on: what view of health, of normality, or of the point of medicine it presupposes, whether that is one view or several that merely coincide here, and which rival views the practice quietly rules out. Please do three things. First, articulate the distinction the institutions think they are applying. My committee has been using restoration-versus-improvement as if it were self-explanatory, and it is not: restore to what, and whose? I have tried out three candidate baselines and cannot make them agree — the patient's own counterfactual trajectory (therapy repairs a departure from how this very person would otherwise have gone), a statistical species-typical range (therapy brings someone back into the normal human band), and a welfare or capability threshold (therapy removes an obstacle to some specified level of flourishing). Say which of these the practice is genuinely committed to, where they diverge, where the practice is underdetermined between them, and whether it needs a value-free notion of normal function at all or is already relying on a value-laden one.

Second, press the cases that pull in different directions. Take two students with identical present capacities and identical prospects, one at that level because illness knocked her down and medication brought her back, the other because he was always like that — constitutionally distractible, or simply near the bottom of the natural distribution facing a hard exam. Our rules treat them differently; is that defensible, and is the justification the same conceptual one that covers the diagnosed student, or merely an administrative one about scarce clinic slots and who gets a prescription? The restoration story also seems to have no natural stopping point: a cochlear implant for a child deaf from birth is counted as therapy even though it confers a capacity she never had, and along any gradual continuum repair shades into improvement, so I would like you to say whether the resulting vagueness is fatal to the distinction or merely untidy.

Third, tell me what would actually have to change if we abandoned the line as a piece of metaphysics and rebuilt it. The proposal I keep returning to, which I will call capacity-neutral provision, has two parts: the clinic ranks interventions by expected benefit to the student's own ends and offers them in that order without asking whether the intervention is restorative, and the integrity code drops the treatment-versus-enhancement distinction in favour of asking only whether a student secured an unfair positional advantage in a graded competition. I suspect it collapses — perhaps because a student's ends are not fixed independently of what is available, perhaps because the positional test would also condemn caffeine, sleep, private tutoring and good schools, perhaps because it leaves unexplained why we owe the sick student anything in particular. Show me where it breaks and, if it does, give me the best available reconstruction of what we already do, including any part of it that is really cost containment wearing metaphysical costume. Finally, say whether that reconstruction carries over to the sporting case, where the good being competed for is explicitly zero-sum and the rules are enforced on everyone at once, or whether anti-doping runs on a different and possibly more coherent rationale than a university integrity policy does.
