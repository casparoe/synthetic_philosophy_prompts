The company I work at does blameless postmortems, and I've started to think they don't add up.

The rule is: after an outage you write a doc, and you're not allowed to say that a person caused it. You say the deploy pipeline let an unvalidated config reach prod, or that the alert was too noisy to notice. Names are only supposed to show up in the list of people who helped. Management's reason is practical — if people get blamed they hide things and then nobody learns anything — and that seems right to me, I've watched it happen at other jobs.

But:

1) We're careful about blame and totally loose about credit. The template has a shoutouts section, and people get genuinely annoyed if the person who noticed the paging alert doesn't get thanked for it. Last month someone wrote that they "owned" the rollback and everybody was fine with it. So one direction of the reaction gets suppressed and the other one runs at full strength, and I don't think anyone has noticed that this is weird.

2) The doc still ends with action items, each with one named owner and a due date. That looks a lot like holding somebody responsible. If the whole point is that the system caused it, why is there a person who has to fix it by Friday?

3) We also do performance reviews, and having caused a big incident definitely shows up in them, just phrased more carefully. So we haven't really stopped blaming people, we've moved it somewhere it isn't written down in the doc.

4) Newest one: we have bots that open pull requests now, and people leave review comments like "why would you do this" addressed to the bot. Everybody in the thread knows it's a language model. We still do it.

There's a proposal on the table that I think is supposed to fix some of this, and I want to know if it actually does. It's to split the thing in two: no blame for causing the incident, but full accountability for adoption — meaning you're never on the hook for the mistake itself, but you are on the hook for whether you rolled out the guardrail that already existed, or whether you answered the page. Someone on my team said this makes the whole practice coherent instead of half-hearted. Someone else said it's just blame with extra steps, because in practice "you didn't adopt the safety thing" is where all the blame was going to land anyway.

A coworker mentioned a philosopher named Strawson, something about reactive attitudes and an objective stance, and said it's exactly this argument from fifty years ago. I read a summary and couldn't tell whether his claim is that what we're doing is impossible — that you can't just decide to stop holding people responsible by policy — or that it's possible but costly, or that it's fine and I'm overthinking a template.

So: what does the blameless postmortem actually presuppose about moral responsibility? Does doing it consistently commit you to some real view — that people's actions come out of prior causes so blame is never warranted — or is it compatible with thinking people are responsible in the ordinary sense and just choosing not to act like it for practical reasons? Is the blame/credit asymmetry in point 1 actually incoherent, or is resentment and gratitude not the same kind of thing? What does the practice strictly require you to believe versus what it merely goes well with? If we took the no-blame part seriously all the way down, what would we have to give up or change — would trusting each other, or feeling guilty, or firing people, or holding friendships at work, stop making sense? And where does the bot thing fit in: does it show that this whole stance is something we turn on and off depending on whether we feel like it, or is it just noise?
