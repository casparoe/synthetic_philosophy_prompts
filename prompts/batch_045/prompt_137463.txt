I work in master data management, and for the last two years almost entirely on entity resolution: merging the customer databases my company inherited from three acquisitions. My daily vocabulary is pairwise attribute similarity, blocking keys, match thresholds, transitive closure over a match graph and the ways it misbehaves, correlation clustering when it does, golden records with survivorship rules, and the recurring argument about whether two records denote the same real-world entity. I got into a long argument about the species problem with a friend who works in biodiversity informatics and is an unapologetic species pluralist; she insisted it isn't just a data-modeling problem wearing a lab coat, and the rationalist-sphere move I reached for — natural categories as clusters in thingspace — didn't land with her. On reflection I think it shouldn't have: it gives you no way to say what the right granularity of the label space is, or why the answer ought to be the same for beetles as for bacteria, and it makes the disagreement look solved by stipulation.

So I want a translation, done honestly rather than flatteringly. Take the main competing species concepts — interbreeding and reproductive isolation, ecological niche, phylogenetic lineage and monophyly, overall similarity or diagnosability, and the genotypic-cluster idea — plus the two background disputes underneath them (whether there is one correct species concept or several legitimate ones keyed to different taxa or different explanatory interests, and whether species are historical individuals with organisms as parts rather than classes with organisms as members) and restate each in my vocabulary as precisely as you can. Then give me the residue report: which claims carry over cleanly, which carry over only with distortion — and I want you to name exactly what you had to fudge — and which cannot be expressed in entity-resolution terms at all. Two of my own suspicions to confirm or kill. First, my framework seems to presuppose a ground truth about coreference, a fact of the matter against which a matcher gets a precision and recall number, so any translation will quietly import the realist side of the dispute and leave the pluralist sounding like someone who just wants sloppier labels; if so, say what the realist claim would have to look like to be expressible, and what it costs me to drop it. Second, I suspect the species-as-individuals thesis is where my vocabulary actually breaks, because in my world a record stands for an entity (a map standing for a territory) whereas an organism is part of a species, and I have no word for a thing whose instances compose it rather than denote it. Please also route the standard hard cases through whatever mapping you build: asexual groups where the interbreeding concept has no purchase, prokaryotes with horizontal gene transfer, ring species where the same-species relation fails transitivity across a chain of intergrading populations, and a continuous fossil sequence where any cut is arbitrary.

A table would help — concept, restatement in my vocabulary, what got distorted, what cannot be said — followed by a prose residue section of a few hundred words on whether the residue indicts my framework or shows that some of the biology is less clear than it sounds; and if you conclude that my vocabulary is missing something, tell me whether it's a thing a working data modeler would ever actually want or only a thing a philosopher would want. Fluency-wise: I can read clustering and record-linkage papers, I have one undergraduate biology course and no philosophy, so explain the biology terms rather than the data ones. Epistemically, I want the response to keep three things visibly separate: what is settled textbook material, what is contested among people who work on this, and what you are personally unsure about — I'd rather see a flagged guess than a confident invention, especially about the biology and about who holds which position.
