Hey — I keep arguing with a friend about whether money pump arguments actually show anything, and I want to know whether my gut verdicts on cases add up to a principle or whether I'm just rationalizing. Her line is that if your preferences are cyclic or otherwise unstable over time, you're ipso facto irrational, because a dealer can sell you back where you started minus cash. I keep thinking the money is doing less work than people pretend, and that some of these "pumps" aren't losses at all.

Here's where I land case by case.

1. The straight cycle. Over a week my ranking among three apartments goes A better than B, B better than C, C better than A, depending on whether I'm thinking about commute, light, or rent. A dealer offers me each swap for a dollar, I take all three, I'm back in A minus three dollars and minus the hassle. Verdict: yeah, something's wrong with me. The pump diagnosis feels right here.

2. Same shape, but it's my beliefs moving. Two job offers. Monday I prefer the startup; Thursday I learn it's being restructured, so I prefer the university job; Friday I learn the university's hiring is frozen and im back to preferring the startup. A dealer could in principle have taken a dollar off me at each switch. Verdict: not irrational — just badly informed. Though it does bother me that I knew more news was coming and still acted on each ranking as if it were final.

3. Tiredness. At 9am I genuinely want to go to the 7pm gym class; at 7pm I'm wrecked and would pay to go home instead. Totally predictable — I knew at 9am exactly how this would go. Verdict: still not irrational, but I'm shakier here, and it sits badly next to case 1, where there was no story at all and I convicted happily. The only difference I can point to is that here the change is in my body rather than in what I'm attending to.

4. The boredom pump, which I don't think is a pump. I rotate through three hobbies, get sick of each after about a week, want the next one, and a dealer charges me a dollar a swap. After three weeks I'm back on hobby one, three dollars poorer. Verdict: not merely excusable but fine — actually good. I consumed a week of each hobby on the way round, so the three dollars is a fee for variety, and if each week was worth more than a dollar I came out ahead. This is the case that makes me suspect the "ends up back where it started" framing hides the real accounting.

5. The pump with no money in it. No dealer, no cash. I re-plan my weekend every morning; each re-planning costs effort and a bit of low-grade stress; I end up doing roughly what I'd planned on Monday. Verdict: mildly bad, but not the same kind of bad as case 1 — it feels like inefficiency, and if the re-planning cost nothing I'd say nothing was wrong at all.

6. The refund pump. Same cycle as case 1, except the dealer puts the three dollars into my own savings account at the end, or spends it on something my later self definitely wants. Verdict: now the pump shows nothing. Which makes me think the cash is a red herring and the real charge was intrapersonal incoherence all along, with the money pump story bolted on to make it vivid.

7. The co-op. Housing co-op with a weekly rotating committee. Each week's majority sincerely prefers to swap the common-room furniture for the garden tools, then the tools for the bike rack, then the bike rack for the furniture, with a small fee each time to whoever does the moving. Every individual vote is perfectly reasonable; the institution pays three fees and ends up where it started. Verdict: the co-op is being pumped, but nobody is irrational — the problem is in the aggregation rule, not in anybody's head.

My draft principle, which I'm not confident in: a diachronic pattern is a rational failing iff (a) the reversals can't be traced either to a change in what you believe or to a change in your own condition, and (b) you could have foreseen them and blocked them at a cost no greater than what letting them happen cost you. Case 1 violates both clauses; case 2 fails (a); case 3 is the problem child, since (b) depends on how cheap a commitment device is and (a) is arguable even though the change is bodily.

What I want from you: the tightest set of candidate principles that covers as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can apply them, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, with verdicts on cases I haven't listed — especially the ones where the reversal is caused by my own earlier choice, like deliberately training myself to like dry wine, or letting a taste I initially dislike grow on me. And be honest about which of my verdicts each principle forces me to drop: I'd rather have two principles and know the tradeoff than one mushy one that "explains" everything.

The case I keep flip-flopping on, if you want something to aim at: a free pill that collapses my time-indexed preferences into a single stable ranking — the one I happen to have on Monday. Taking it ends the pumping forever. But it also looks like letting Monday-me overrule Tuesday-me for no reason I can state, and if I refused it while knowing I'll be pumped for small amounts, I can't tell whether that's integrity or stupidity. What should a principle here say, and does your answer to that differ between cases 1 and 3?
