I have no academic background in philosophy, but over the past year or so I have been reading popular books and listening to long podcast interviews about panpsychism — the idea that experience, or something proto-experiential, goes all the way down, and that ordinary human consciousness is somehow built out of, or is nothing over and above, what is present even in very simple physical things. I have found that I have quite firm reactions to particular cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, those reactions commit me to. My interest is mostly in the ethical half: if something in this neighbourhood is true, should I act differently, and by how much?

Here are my verdicts about where there is a subject of experience.

1. An awake adult human: obviously yes.
2. A housefly: yes, I think there is something it is like to be a fly, and swatting one counts for something, though not for much.
3. A household thermostat: no, and I will be annoyed with any principle that says otherwise.
4. A single electron: no — but I cannot say why, and this is the verdict I hold least confidently.
5. A person in dreamless sleep, or under surgical anaesthesia: no experience at that time. But I do not think a subject has gone out of existence and a fresh one will be created on waking, and I do not want a principle that implies otherwise. This is the first place I feel the strain: if the subject persists through an interval in which there is nothing it is like to be it, then *being a subject* and *having experience right now* come apart, and I cannot tell which of the two the moral question is supposed to track.
6. A fine-grained computer simulation of a human brain, reproducing its causal patterns in detail: yes, I want to say there is someone there.
7. The two hemispheres of a human brain after the connections between them have been surgically cut: one subject, not two — although I realise this sits badly with my reasoning in the next case.
8. A honeybee hive: no subject at the level of the hive; subjects only at the level of individual bees.
9. The Milky Way, considered as a single physical system: no. But I cannot give a reason that does not also rule out case 6, and this is the pair I keep returning to. If the simulation counts purely in virtue of its causal organisation, and a galaxy is also causally organised, then either I must allow the galaxy some unimaginably slow and faint and alien experience, or I must find a threshold I have no way of specifying.
10. A human foetus at twelve weeks: I genuinely do not know, and I would rather a principle told me than the other way round — though I would be suspicious of one that answered confidently.

And my ethical verdicts:

11. Even if I am wrong about case 9 and the galaxy does have a faint unpleasant experience, I think it matters far less than one child's afternoon of toothache — not somewhat less, but negligibly less.
12. Yet I also think that two children in comparable pain is roughly twice as bad as one. So I aggregate across ordinary subjects and refuse to aggregate across extremely faint ones, and I cannot articulate what is supposed to justify the asymmetry.
13. If panpsychism is true, then every time I digest a meal or pour a concrete path I am rearranging whatever has experience into new configurations, creating and destroying subjects by the uncountable trillions. I feel no guilt about this, and I do not think I should. If some verdict has to be sacrificed to keep it that way, sacrifice mine rather than this one.
14. A being with enormously rich and unified experience but no sense of itself as persisting through time, if that is coherent: I am unsure whether it can be harmed in the way I can be harmed, and unsure whether that is because persistence matters or because I am simply unable to imagine the case.

The principle I have been toying with is that the metaphysical question and the moral question should be prised apart. There may be something it is like to be all sorts of systems, including very simple ones; but a being matters morally, on this view, only if things can go better or worse *for it* — only if it has what I would loosely call a stake: there is a way for things to go that is bad from its own point of view, and what it does makes some difference to whether things go that way. My worry is that on this gloss a thermostat starts to sneak in, since it does regulate itself towards a target state, while a person in a coma starts to drop out, since nothing they do makes a difference at the moment — and I reject both of those results.

So what I am asking for is this. Please state, as precisely as you can, one or two candidate principles — ideally one that saves most of my verdicts at some cost in complexity, and a simpler one that forces me to give some up — and for each, say which clause is doing the work in which case, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then run the surviving candidates over cases I have not listed, especially ones I might actually encounter: the animals I eat or step on, patients who cannot tell me what they are feeling, the systems we build and switch off. And give me a straight answer on the practical question at the end: does the view you arrive at change anything I should do, or is the honest conclusion that panpsychism is metaphysically startling and morally close to inert?
