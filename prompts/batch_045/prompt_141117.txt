My friend Dani has been doing refugee status determinations for something like six years, and over beers she's walked me through maybe ten of her cases with the names changed. I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's actually running, and I've got a new case I want to run past her but I'd rather not embarrass myself.

Her decisions so far:

- Granted: a journalist who'd been jailed twice and then started getting death threats from the police after publishing a story about a governor's contracts.
- Denied: a guy fleeing extortion by gangs in a country that isn't at war, where the cops are useless and on the take but aren't in on it and aren't picking on him in particular.
- Granted: a woman whose husband broke her arm twice and who the police wouldn't help even after she had a protection order.
- Granted: a conscript who deserted rather than take part in massacres of civilians. There's no alternative service in his country and desertion gets you years in prison.
- Denied: a man who left rather than be drafted into an army that was defending the country against an invasion nobody thinks was provoked.
- Denied: a farmer whose crops had failed four years running and whose kids were malnourished. The government is crooked and what relief there is goes to the region that votes the right way, which isn't his.
- Granted: a gay man in a country where being gay is legal, beaten up twice, where the police just systematically don't investigate attacks on gay men.
- Denied: a family from a region where the state has basically collapsed and the famine is half drought and half both armies torching fields. Nobody wants them dead in particular.
- Denied: a man who could have moved to another province of his own country and been fine and just never did — what she calls the "internal flight alternative."
- Granted: a family that came through a country where they'd have had papers and been physically safe but no right to work, no school for the kids, no family and no language. She said we still owe them.

Two things she says a lot: "asylum isn't a welfare program" and "I don't get to pick who shows up." Those pull in different directions and she seems not to notice.

The new case: Rupa is fifty-seven, a widow, with a spinal injury from a fall; she can do maybe ten steps with a frame. Her country has no disability benefit, no welfare system, no anti-discrimination law — but also no law against her, and no official has ever done anything to her. Her brother controls her dead husband's pension and keeps her in one room; the neighbors think this is normal; nobody is threatening her and nobody is stopping her. If she stays she spends the rest of her life in that room. A church group in Columbus raised money, flew her over on a medical visa, and she claimed asylum two weeks after she got here. Her own government didn't try to stop her leaving and doesn't want her back. There are probably a few million people in her country in roughly her position.

So: grant or deny? Give me the two or three rules that fit all ten of her earlier verdicts and that come apart on Rupa, tell me which one you think she's actually running and why, and tell me whether number six and number ten look wierd sitting next to each other or whether I'm missing the difference between them. Then give me the one case I should ask her about next that would separate the candidates cleanly. And if she grants, does her own track record give her any way to stop at Rupa, or does she owe a hearing to a few million people — and does it matter that the church flew her in, or did the church just volunteer as the one who has to take her?
