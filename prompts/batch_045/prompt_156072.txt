hey — I've been reading about probabilistic / heuristic arguments in number theory (Cramér-model, Hardy–Littlewood-ish kind of stuff) and I keep leaning on something I can't actually state properly: the "presumption of independence", i.e. treating things like "n is prime" and "n+2 is prime" as independent events with probability 1/log n. Half the time it feels completely fine and half the time it feels like numerology. I want a principle for when it's licensed and when it isn't. Here's my gut on a pile of cases:

1. Basic one: probability that n is prime is about 1/log n, sum it up, you get li(x). Verdict: fine. (I know it's a theorem, but as a heuristic I buy it too.)
2. Twin primes: naive independence gives about x/log²x. Verdict: not fine as it stands, but fine once you multiply by the product over odd primes p of (1−2/p) divided by (1−1/p)² — the twin prime constant. And importantly, I don't feel like that correction is cheating. It tracks something real: two numbers that are 2 apart can't dodge a given residue class as freely as two unrelated numbers can.
3. n, n+2 and n+4 all prime: verdict: the corrected version handles this too, it spits out zero because of the obstruction mod 3, and I trust it more here than in case 2, which is odd, because there's nothing left to tune.
4. Goldbach: number of representations of an even n as a sum of two primes looks like a singular series times n/log²n. Verdict: fine, same reasons as 2.
5. Gaps: the random model says the largest gap between primes below x is around log²x (and I gather people think the truth is somewhat bigger). Verdict: I don't buy this, or I buy it much less than case 2 — even though case 2 is also a sharp-looking quantitative claim that comes out of the same coin-flipping picture. Maier-type results apparently show primes in short intervals fluctuate in ways the model doesn't see. But I can't say cleanly why 2 gets a pass and 5 doesn't. That's the pair I keep tripping on.
6. Chebyshev bias: primes aren't 50/50 between 1 mod 4 and 3 mod 4, there's a persistent bias toward 3 mod 4. Verdict: so the independence presumption is literally wrong here — but I don't want to give up case 2 because of it. My compromise has been: it's ok for leading-order asymptotics over long ranges, unreliable for second-order questions, signs, error terms. Except 5 is arguably also a leading-order claim about a count, so my compromise doesn't obviously cover it either.
7. Möbius randomness, Chowla-ish stuff: I do want to say μ(n) and μ(n+1) behave independently, and that μ looks independent of low-complexity sequences like Thue–Morse. Verdict: trust. But that sits badly with 6: if primes carry a detectable bias at second order, why should μ be cleanly random? Maybe μ washes the bias out, but I'm not sure that's a reason or just a hope.
8. Fermat primes: F_n = 2^2^n + 1, and any prime factor of F_n is 1 mod 2^(n+2), so the naive 1/log F_n gets boosted, and the expected total number of Fermat primes converges, so you predict finitely many. Verdict: I go back and forth. The boost feels principled, kind of the same flavour as the correction in case 2 — it's tracking a real structural constraint. But it also feels like the sort of move where you can always dig up some constraint to boost with, and then your "prediction" has been assembled out of whatever structure you happened to notice. I have the same discomfort with the Mersenne version, where the boost comes from every prime divisor of 2^p − 1 being 1 mod 2p.
9. A case where the "probability" isn't a frequency over anything: the argument that RH should be true because if you model μ(n) as an independent random ±1 sequence then the partial sums are O(n^(1/2+ε)) almost surely. Verdict: mildly persuasive, but I don't know what I'm trusting when I trust it — a probability-1 statement about one fixed object — and I have a nagging memory that you can run this style of random-model argument and land on conclusions people don't believe. So: less trust than 2, more than 5? unsure.
10. Fitting after the fact: someone has a heuristic count for some family of special primes, the constant disagrees with the data, they stick in an extra factor so it matches. Verdict: not fine, even though it now fits. This is clearly different from case 2's correction, but I want to know what the difference is in principle rather than by vibe.

So: is there one principle (or a short list of candidates) that licenses the presumption of independence in some of these and not in others, stated tightly enough that I could go apply it to a new case? It should tell me which clause is doing the work for which verdict, be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which I'd have to give up, and it should say something about 8 — plus about two cases I haven't listed: Sophie Germain primes (p and 2p+1 both prime), where I want to know whether to trust the constant that falls out of the corrected heuristic, and primes of the form n²+1. Also, someone told me the real constraint is something about "level of distribution" — if that's part of the answer, please spell out how it cuts on case 2 versus case 5 instead of just naming it.
