I want a constructed numerical case that isolates a mechanism in the algorithmic-fairness impossibility results that I think does more work than it is usually credited with, and I want to use it in a talk. So I need the case to be engineered to a specification and then checked against that specification, not merely asserted to have the properties I ask for.

Setup. A single institution makes a binary decision about people using a risk score: approve or deny a small-business line of credit, enroll or not enroll a discharged patient in a follow-up programme, detain or release before trial — your choice, but it has to be a real decision a real institution makes with a score, and no science fiction. The outcome the score predicts (default, readmission, rearrest) is defined and measured identically for both groups, so the disparities in the case cannot be an artifact of label bias. Call the two groups A and B. Remind me briefly what the three standard group criteria require: demographic parity, equal selection rates across groups; equalized odds, equal false-positive and equal false-negative rates across groups; predictive parity, equal positive predictive value across groups, with calibration within groups as the stronger score-level version of the same idea.

Specifications.

1. The two groups must have exactly the same base rate of the outcome. State it. This is the point of the exercise: the usual engine of the impossibility theorems — unequal base rates across groups — is switched off, and the pathology has to come from somewhere else.

2. What differs between the groups is how informative the features are about the outcome: for one group the score is a sharper signal, for the other a noisier or coarser one, because the underlying records are thinner, the proxy is measured with more error, or the relevant test is ordered less often. The gap must arise from something an institution could produce without anyone intending it, and it must not be the kind of gap the same decision-maker could close next quarter.

3. Give the score distributions explicitly — a small table of P(S = s | Y, group) for three or four score levels per group, or two pairs of densities — and make the score calibrated within each group, so calibration is satisfied by construction and none of the action is hiding there.

4. From those numbers, show that the ROC curve of the worse-informed group lies strictly inside the better-informed group's: for every attainable false-positive rate, the better-informed group's minimum false-negative rate is strictly lower. Show this by computation, not by stipulation.

5. Derive what equalized odds costs in this case. Show first, from the nesting, that if decisions must be deterministic functions of the score then the only rules satisfying equalized odds are the constant ones — approve everyone or deny everyone — so the constraint can be met only by ceasing to use the score at all. Then allow randomization, and exhibit the best rule that satisfies the constraint: it should sit on the worse-informed group's frontier and require deliberately injecting noise into the better-informed group's decisions, so that group is made strictly worse off in both of its own error rates while the other group is no better off than it already is under a rule that optimizes each group separately. The moral I want the numbers to force is that here the constraint cannot reallocate the error burden at all; it can only level it up.

6. Then price the other two criteria in the same case. My expectation is that demographic parity is satisfiable in it at almost no cost in accuracy, by choosing different thresholds per group, and that this inverts the usual ranking — equalized odds is normally defended as the criterion compatible with using the best available predictor, since a perfect predictor would satisfy it, whereas demographic parity forces you away from accuracy. Say whether my expectation is right in your case, and what predictive parity costs there.

7. Finally, two honest notes. First: which single feature of the construction is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you remove it — equalize the noise, or let the base rates differ as well. Second: the strongest reply available to someone who wants to keep equalized odds. I can think of several and want them ranked rather than listed: that error rates are the wrong currency and the point is who bears the burden of being badly known; that the informational gap is itself the prior injustice, so declining to exploit a data advantage built on it is the point rather than a cost; that the right comparison is a group-blind threshold, not per-group optimization; that real institutions will not randomize, so the best equalized-odds rule is not politically available and the honest comparison is the deterministic one; and that the correct remedy is to go and collect better data for the worse-served group. Tell me which of these actually bites here, and whether any of them restores equalized odds as a genuine constraint rather than a license for leveling down.

A case that simply stipulates different base rates, or stipulates the conclusion that the constraint forces worse predictions without deriving it from the distributions, or one whose mechanism would not survive a practitioner's objection that the data gap is a fixable engineering problem, will not do the job I need it for.
