I'm in a reading group on wellbeing economics — mostly economists and a couple of psychologists, me coming at it from philosophy — and we keep circling the same object without ever pinning down what it's actually committed to: the World Happiness Report. Every March it ranks something like 150 countries by the average answer to a single Gallup question: picture a ladder with the worst possible life for you at 0 and the best possible life for you at 10, and tell us where you stand right now. That one number is the entire ranking; the six familiar factors (income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, perceptions of corruption) get used afterwards to explain the gaps between countries, not to compute the score. Finland has been on top for years running, the Nordics plus Costa Rica do suspiciously well, the table barely moves from year to year, and the report has lately been recommending WELLBYs as the thing policy should aim to maximise. I don't want a pollster's critique of the sampling; I want the implicit philosophy reconstructed. What does this practice presuppose?

Here's the bit that I think is genuinely weird, and where I'd start: the question hands respondents their own standard — best possible life *for you* — and then the report turns around and treats national means of those answers as measurements on one shared, cross-culturally comparable scale. So: does the practice presuppose that a considered life-evaluation just is happiness, as opposed to mood, or affect over time, or desire-satisfaction, or something much thinner like the mere absence of suffering and restless wanting (my flatmate's crank view, on which a contented, numb, ambition-free population would score full marks)? Does it presuppose that these self-reports are cardinal and interpersonally comparable across cultures, or does it only need them to be roughly ordinally comparable and get the rest from the sample size? Does averaging presuppose a utilitarian-flavoured aggregator plus the nation as the unit of moral concern, or would prioritarians, sufficientarians and plenty of others happily sign off on the same table for totally different reasons?

My hypothesis, which I'd like taken apart, is that the ranking is definately underdetermined on the theory-of-wellbeing side — several incompatible accounts of happiness would all be fine using a ladder mean as their proxy — and that the load-bearing commitments are really (i) a comparability claim about scale use and (ii) the choice of the mean over, say, the share of people below some floor. Is that right, and is the second one a value commitment or a presentational habit? Then the part I care most about: what would a ranking built on the rejected options have to do differently — rank by the bottom quintile instead, or by the fraction reporting under 3; calibrate scale use by having people rate short vignettes of described lives as well as their own; weight by population; ask about yesterday's feelings instead of a life-evaluation; or take the crank view seriously and score countries only on reported misery and wanting? Under each swap, what would a government that genuinely wanted to top the table actually have to do, and could we tell from the numbers alone which of these rankings was measuring the thing we care about? And if expectations adapt, so that a country could climb by making its citizens want less, what does it say about the practice that this even registers as a problem for it?
