I'm a PhD student working on confirmation theory, and this term our reading group spent two months on Hempel's raven paradox — Nicod's criterion, the equivalence condition, Hempel's own reply that the appearance of paradox comes from tacitly comparing the hypothesis against evidence-plus-background-knowledge rather than against the evidence alone, the Bayesian treatment usually credited to Good, the projectibility line that links the puzzle to Goodman's new riddle, the "indoor ornithology" joke — and we finished exactly where we began: everyone could reconstruct every solution, and nobody had moved. I don't want another solution. I want a diagnosis of what kind of standoff this is.

Let me fix the case, because I suspect people vary it tacitly. Take a finite domain of N objects; H says every raven is black, equivalently every non-black object is a non-raven. Consider three protocols. In A you draw an object uniformly from the whole domain and are told its colour and kind. In B you draw uniformly from the ravens and are told its colour. In C you draw uniformly from the non-black objects and are told its kind. Our group convinced ourselves of the following, and I'd like it checked: under A the confirmation H gets from a draw that comes up black-and-raven, versus one that comes up non-black-and-non-raven, differs by a factor that is just a function of the prior odds on being a raven versus being non-black, so that with vastly more non-black things than ravens the herring confirms only negligibly; under B the black raven is the only kind of draw available and the herring is unreachable; under C the reverse holds. If that is right, then the felt absurdity of "learning about ravens by looking at apples" is generated by imagining something like protocol B — the ornithologist in the field — while the formal argument that produces the paradox presumes A or C.

My hypothesis, to be tested rather than flattered, is that the debate is three debates sharing a name. Reading one: "confirms" as graded, background-relative probability-raising, where the paradox dissolves and the answer is yes-but-negligibly. Reading two: "confirms" as is-a-genuine-test-of or is-worth-going-to-gather, a decision-theoretic notion on which the herring is worthless and the dissolution is trivial. Reading three: Hempel's own project, a qualitative two-place relation between sentence and hypothesis meant to say something about the logic of induction, where rejecting the equivalence condition costs you the notion itself. On this hypothesis the literature's solutions are aimed at readings one and two while the intuition that keeps the paradox alive is generated under reading three, which is why every resolution feels simultaneously correct and beside the point.

So: is that the structure, or is something else doing the work? Specifically, are the parties disagreeing about facts — the ratio of ravens to non-black things, which is in principle countable, and the cardinality of the domain, since on an infinite domain with an unbounded supply of non-black objects the herring's contribution collapses and the puzzle may be a measure-theoretic artifact — about the argument slots of "confirms", two-place between propositions or three-place relative to a protocol, partition or contrast class; about which cases count as data, indoor ornithology versus fieldwork versus urn models; or about an intuition neither side can argue for? Relatedly, how much of the paradox is inherited from the new riddle of induction, given that the hypothesis's contrapositive form puts a negative predicate in the antecedent: if it is a special case of projectibility, should we expect no local progress at all?

Finally, tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis and be honest if the answer is nothing: a representation result showing that any measure satisfying equivalence and relevance must be protocol- or partition-relative, a decision-theoretic framework that recovers "don't bother with the herring" as an expected-value-of-information verdict, a solution to projectibility, or a reframing that dissolves the question. And apply one test I care about: after granting the Bayesian verdict in full, is there a residual question that still nags, and does that residue have a well-defined question behind it, or is it only the ghost of a framework we have already abandoned?
