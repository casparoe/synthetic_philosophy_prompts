I'm a software engineer with no physics beyond a first-year course, and I read about Bell's theorem on weekends for fun. I keep running into a pattern in what I read that I can't name, and it isn't quite the pattern I expected it to be.

First case. Someone describes a theory in which the particles leave the source carrying a full set of instructions for every detector setting they might meet, so that the instructions settle in advance what each detector will flash. The objection comes: Bell showed that no local theory of that kind can match the quantum predictions. The reply: but I can't use it to send you a message — whatever I do at my end, the pattern of flashes at your end looks exactly the same. And the answer: that's beside the point, your theory is still nonlocal, because what flashes at my end has to depend on which setting you chose.

Second case, reversed. Someone says that Bell's result shows nature is nonlocal, that there's a real dependence running from one wing of the experiment to the other. The challenge: a dependence you could signal with? No, you just conceded that no signalling is possible. The answer: it's a real dependence all the same, just one that can't be harnessed to send a message.

So "local" seems to be doing at least two jobs: "nothing I do here changes what you can observe there," and "nothing that happens here depends on what you choose there." The third thing I noticed is what puzzles me most. When the dependence is instead between the instructions produced at the source and the experimenters' later choice of setting, writers don't call the theory nonlocal; they call it a conspiracy, or say the experimenters weren't really free. Yet the source and the detectors are all spatially separated, and it's the same kind of statistical dependence between distant things. That makes me wonder whether "nonlocal" is tracking spatial separation at all, or rather which pair of items the dependence holds between.
