I need a hand with next month's episode. Bit of background: I'm a retired geophysicist (spent most of my career on mantle convection models, which is a fancy way of saying I spent it arguing about parameters nobody could measure), and since I retired I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the local university and somehow ended up co-hosting a very small podcast with a friend — we record in my garden shed, we get maybe four hundred downloads an episode, half of them other retired scientists. Our running theme is "arguments where the assumptions do all the work," which is my hobby horse: in my old field nearly every big fight turned out to be about priors and parameterisation, not about data, and nobody ever said so out loud. My guest — I'll just call her "Dr. Vance," since I'm paraphrasing her position anyway — is a formal epistemologist whose new book argues that Aumann's agreement theorem should be read as a theorem about communication protocols rather than about rationality or about what we owe each other when we disagree. I've read the standard encyclopedia entry and a few blog posts, so I know the shape of the result: common prior, common knowledge of posteriors, therefore identical posteriors. Don't re-explain that part to me. What I want is eight to ten questions, in the order you'd actually ask them, each with a one-line note on the answer she's most likely to give and then a follow-up aimed at that answer. She gets the questions two days in advance, so she'll have smooth prepared replies; the follow-ups are where I need the help.

Here's what I want the questions to go after. First: the common prior. My suspicion is that the assumption is doing all the work — that the theorem says "if you already agree on everything except your private evidence, you can't disagree about that either." She'll presumably say that priors can only differ if you disagree about the process that produced you, or that a shared prior just encodes shared evidential standards. I want a follow-up that makes her say how you'd ever tell, in a concrete real case (pick one from science — a replication dispute, an argument over climate sensitivity, whatever you like), whether the common prior holds; and then whether the theorem is unfalsifiable, and whether that is a defect or just what a mathematical result is. Second: the static result versus the follow-up result that if two people just keep announcing their posteriors back and forth they converge after finitely many rounds to the posterior you'd get by pooling all their evidence. Which one is load-bearing for her reading? Because real people don't announce posteriors, they swap arguments, citations, plots and anecdotes. She'll likely say arguments are just evidence and can be modelled as signals; I want the follow-up to push on what happens when the protocol is bad — when agents meet only in pairs, or in an order that isn't common knowledge, or when somebody's announcement is lossy. Third, and this is the bit I made up and like best: suppose the channel is coarse and everyone announces their posterior rounded to one decimal place. Two agents whose real posteriors are 0.63 and 0.57 both say "0.6," hear "0.6," and stop, each sincerely believing the theorem has been satisfied. Does her protocol reading count that as agreement or only as its appearance? What should a rational agent do — volunteer more digits, or announce the rounding and the residual interval? And follow up on the case where repeated rounding makes them cycle or stall somewhere short of the pooled value. Fourth: there's no notion of competence or peerhood anywhere in the theorem; all the work is in the prior and the information partitions. So is the whole thing beside the point for the epistemology-of-peer-disagreement literature, or is talk of "peers" just a loose proxy for a common prior? She'll probably take the second line; I want the follow-up. And I want one question that pins her down on how demanding her reading is: if I can't in practice compute the posterior conditional on everything my colleague and I have seen between us, does her view imply my number isn't a credence at all — that nearly all real scientific disagreement is irrational? Her book is slippery there and I'd like her nailed to something.

Two more things. I want a closing question about what would count against her reading — what finding, or what kind of counterexample, would make her give up the "it's about protocols" line — and I do not want any softballs: no "how did you get into formal epistemology," no "what's the one thing you'd tell our listeners." More importantly: commit. I've more or less talked myself into her position over the last few weeks, and I'd rather the episode test it than showcase it, so build the ordering so it presses that thesis rather than letting her restate it, and if you think the "protocols, not rationality" reading is wrong or only half right, say so in a line or two at the end and tell me which question is the one that exposes it. Don't hedge into a neutral list of topics and don't just lay out the options for me to choose between — tell me which reading you think is actually correct and why, even if it means telling me my hobby horse is wrong.
