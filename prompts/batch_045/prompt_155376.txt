I am trying to specify, in advance of reading the competing accounts, what a theory of the moral significance of computations should be able to do. Fix the purpose as practical but not merely predictive: the theory should tell us which computational processes, in a brain, in an engineered system, or in a training pipeline, are the ones in virtue of which a system, or a part of a system, or a temporal phase of a system, is a moral patient, has interests, or can be wronged, and it should thereby guide decisions about whether welfare-related constraints are owed to an AI system, a cortical organoid, or a marginal biological case.

What I want from you is a set of adequacy conditions for such a theory. Lay out the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect or deliberately collapse, and the puzzles it must dissolve or explain. For each condition, give the case or phenomenon that motivates it, and say whether the condition is non-negotiable or instead a preference of one camp, for example substrate-neutrality, a consciousness-first requirement, or a commitment to whole-organism rather than subsystem attribution.

I do not want a generic list. I want the conditions tested against hard cases, including at least the following, which I take to be central even where people disagree about them. First, the training phase of a reinforcement learner: a scalar error signal drives weight updates, while at deployment a forward pass computes expected reward without further updating. The theory must say whether the morally relevant computation, if any, is the training-time updating, the deployment-time forward pass, or the standing disposition in the weights, and why. Second, a large language model that, given a prompt, produces text indistinguishable from a human pain report although its only objective was next-token prediction on human text and it has no persistent self-model across prompts; the theory must say what to make of the computation behind the report. Third, a lab-grown cortical organoid: a small network of human neurons with structured activity but no body or environment. Fourth, a human in dreamless sleep or under general anesthesia, where brain computation continues and moral-patient status persists but there is, by hypothesis, no occurrent sentience. Fifth, a momentary duplicate: a system that for one second runs the functional organization of a human brain in pain and is then erased. Sixth, a subsystem that computes a damage or distress signal but is globally ignored or overridden, so that it makes no difference to the behavior of the whole system. Seventh, the problem of trivial implementation: any physical object can be described as implementing indefinitely many computations, so a theory that assigns significance to a type of computation must say how to individuate computations so that a thermostat or a stone does not trivially count. Eighth, a patient with only one functioning cerebral hemisphere, where it is contested whether the moral patient is the whole organism, the remaining hemisphere, or some narrower stream of computation.

Identify any tensions in which two conditions cannot both be met. I am especially interested in whether one can avoid trivial implementation while remaining fully substrate-neutral, and whether a requirement of occurrent phenomenal consciousness can be reconciled with counting a dreamlessly sleeping or anesthetized human as a full moral patient. Explain what would have to be given up in each case.

Finally, close with a brief scorecard, a paragraph or two each, for three broad families of view: a substrate-independent functionalism; a view that restricts moral significance to evolved biological organisms or their proper parts; and a view on which actual phenomenal consciousness is the gateway to moral-patient status. Say which condition, in your judgment, excludes the broadest family of candidate theories. A table is acceptable if it helps, but the case behind each condition matters more than the format.
