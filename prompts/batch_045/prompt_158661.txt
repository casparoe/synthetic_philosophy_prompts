My mate Dave and I spent something like three hours last night arguing about Schopenhauer on music and got absolutely nowhere. He's a hardline "it's just pretty patterns plus vibes" guy; I think the bit where music supposedly copies the will directly instead of copying the Platonic Ideas the way every other art does is doing some real work, even if it's completely weird. Neither of us knows enough to settle it, but the argument made me realise I've got the ideal guest lined up for the show and I want to actually be ready for her.

I do a fortnightly long-form interview thing: one guest, one obsession, an hour, no notes from me beyond a list of questions. Next week's guest is a philosopher who's spent years defending a broadly Schopenhauerian account of music: music as the immediate copy of the will, bypassing the Ideas, which is why it doesn't have to depict anything in order to be about something (her catchphrase is roughly that music is the only art that doesn't need to mean something in order to be true). She's just published a new chapter extending the view, where she argues that because music gives you the will's strivings directly rather than describing them, music literally cannot lie: there's no insincere or self-deceiving music in the way there's insincere speech, no musical irony in the strict sense, and when a listener hears triumph in a march the triumph really is there in the music in some sense. She knows this material backwards and she's very good at turning objections into further applications of the view, so I need questions that don't let her just restate it and move on.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions, in British English please, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? Things I definately want covered:

- The will-lessness problem. If the aesthetic state on this picture is a will-less, pain-free condition of pure knowing, how can music count as aesthetic at all, given that it's supposed to be the copy of the will itself and it stirs our affects more violently than any other art? Isn't it agitating the will rather than quieting it?

- The levels-of-nature mapping: lowest voices as inorganic nature, the harmonising middle parts as the lower grades of the will's objectification, the melody as self-conscious human striving. What makes that more than a pretty analogy, what fixes which voice maps to which grade, and what would count against it?

- The new no-lies chapter. Press it on musical kitsch, on a composer who writes a triumphant march while feeling nothing remotely triumphant, and on music that seems to mean something close to the opposite of what it sounds like.

- The epistemology. On her own view the will in itself is outside the principle of sufficient reason and isn't available as an object of knowledge, so how could we ever recognise it in music? Is the payoff knowledge, acquaintance, or just a feeling with a metaphysical story attached, and does she need it to be more than the last one?

- One question on what would actually count as evidence against the whole package, since I expect her to say nothing could.

Keep them in voice for a friendly host who is genuinely curious and genuinely a bit sceptical, and no "what first drew you to this" filler. If you can work in something about whether words — song, opera, any texted music — improve things or drag music down on her account, that'd be great, because that was the bit Dave and I were still shouting about when we gave up and went to bed.
