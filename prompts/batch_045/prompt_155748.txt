I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory and formal epistemology, and I have been going round in circles on a version of Pascal's mugging that I built to block the standard escape routes. I would be grateful for a diagnosis. Please answer in British English.

(1) A rational agent maximises expected utility: acts are ranked by the probability-weighted sum of the utility of their possible outcomes, and a rational agent performs a maximal act.

(2) The mugger asks for £5 and says that, from a region entirely outside my future light cone, they will create and sustain in bliss a number of lives that can be named in a few dozen characters using Knuth's up-arrow notation. I cannot assign this claim probability zero: it is consistent with everything I know, and a sensible credence function does not assign zero to a possibility it cannot rule out.

(3) The probability I should assign to a claim decays with the length of its description, not with the size of the numbers it mentions. Under a complexity-based prior the mugger's sentence costs a few dozen bits, and there is no bound on the payoff a sentence of that length can name.

(4) The usual reply that utility should be bounded is no help here: nothing in (1)–(3) turns on my own welfare. Put the payoff in lives created rather than in utils to me, and a bounded utility function still yields an unbounded expected payoff.

(5) The usual causal-decision-theory reply is also closed off: the stipulated payoff lies outside my light cone, so there is no causal route from my paying to anything. Whatever rescues evidential reasoning in the standard Newcomb-style cases should rescue it here too.

(6) Now iterate. For every act available to me, some compactly stated mugging offer has greater expected value. So no act maximises expected utility: whatever I do, there was something better available.

(7) And if for every available act A there is an available act B with greater expected value, then performing A while B is available is irrational. So every ordinary act — eating, sleeping, writing my thesis — is rationally forbidden.

(8) The same construction runs on belief rather than action. Let the payoff be conditional on my accepting some creed: a being outside my light cone creates the same astronomical number of blissful lives if, and only if, I believe a proposition they name. Steps (2) and (3) go through unchanged, with the value now attached to believing rather than to paying. So for any proposition compactly enough stated and attached to a large enough conditional payoff, rationality requires raising my credence in it, and there is no creed, however arbitrary, that rationality permits me to reject.

(9) So expected-utility maximisation, together with a non-zero credence floor and any complexity-based prior, entails what I will call fanatical paralysis: no act permitted and no proposition rejectable.

That conclusion is not one I can accept, and I do not think (7) is where I want to pay. I would like to keep the thought that tiny-probability, huge-stake reasoning is sometimes perfectly sound — pandemic preparedness and existential-risk mitigation both look like cases where a small probability of an enormous loss should move us — and I would rather not give up (1) outright. Please tell me which step is doing the illegitimate work, why it looks innocent, and what each of the alternatives would cost me: giving up the non-zero floor in (2), rejecting the description-length assumption in (3), restricting (1) to cases where the expectation is well defined, or distinguishing epistemic from pragmatic reasons in (8). I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for references; I have read the standard replies and they are what I am trying to get behind. I want the step named and the bill priced.
