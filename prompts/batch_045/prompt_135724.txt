I'm working through Marx's critique of capitalism on my own, and I want to know how much of the version I first encountered I should keep. The early chapter of a well-reviewed trade book on the history of work summarizes Marx like this:

"The core of Marx's case against capitalism has two parts, and they are really two faces of one thing. The first is exploitation. Workers are paid less than the value of what they produce; the difference — the surplus — is pocketed by the owner, not because he has done anything to earn it, but simply because he owns the machinery. Profit, for Marx, is theft with a contract attached, and because the owner's share grows while the worker's does not, capitalism steadily immiserates the people who actually do the work. The second part is what that arrangement feels like from the inside: alienation. The worker does not own what she makes, does not decide how or when she works, and performs a fragment of a process she neither designed nor understands, so she ends up estranged from her product, from her own activity, and from herself — and, since she must sell her labor on the market like any other article, from the people around her. Marx even had a name for the wider cultural effect: commodity fetishism, the fixation on brands and shopping that hides the labor behind everything we buy. All of this has a single remedy. Take the means of production away from the owners and hand them to the workers, and the theft and the estrangement dissolve together."

Since reading it I have picked up a few things that make me suspicious of how neatly the two halves are fused — in particular I have heard that for Marx exploitation is compatible with workers being paid the full value of their labor power, and even that he did not think capitalism was unjust, which sits oddly with the word "theft." But I can't tell which parts of the passage that undermines.

Please audit it clause by clause. For each substantive claim, tell me whether a philosopher who works on Marx would accept it as stated, whether it is a fair simplification for a popular audience, whether it is subtly wrong in a way that changes what the theory is committed to, or whether it is just wrong. Where it goes wrong, name the mistakes precisely: two things being conflated that the theory keeps apart, a modest thesis inflated into a strong one, a technical term used in its everyday sense, an objector's point presented as the theory's own, or a later claim read back into an earlier one.

Four things I especially want addressed:

1. Is alienation, as Marx uses it, a psychological condition of the worker at all, or a structural relation — and is it a downstream effect of exploitation, as the passage implies, or a separate axis of criticism that can come apart from it? I would also like to know whether the discussion of alienation in the early writings and the way the notion functions in the later critique of political economy are the same complaint, or whether collapsing them into one story loses something.

2. Does the "theft" framing fit? Specifically: what exactly does the theory say the worker sells, what does the capitalist receive, and does any of this depend on anyone being underpaid in the ordinary sense, on wages being below the value of labor power, or on capitalists being personally vicious?

3. Is "commodity fetishism" about consumer attitudes toward brands, or about something in the structure of a market in which products of labor take the form of values? If it is the latter, say what the passage's gloss omits.

4. Does the remedy follow? I want the corrected account applied rather than merely stated, so please run it against this case. Call it the cheerful co-op: a firm owned and governed equally by its workers, operating in a competitive market, which produces something its members chose to make, pays above the local average, lets members set their own schedules and rotate tasks, and whose members report high satisfaction and a strong sense of ownership over the product. It nevertheless sells its output for money, must cover costs and reinvest to stay solvent, and competes with conventionally owned firms. On the account you take to be Marx's, are these workers exploited, alienated, both, or neither, and what in the description does the answer turn on? If your answer is that it depends, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work — the market discipline, the wage relation, the division of labor, the property form, or something else — and what you would have to change to flip the verdict.

Where a claim in the passage is defensible on one reading of Marx and wrong on another, say so explicitly instead of picking one; I would rather have the disagreement located than have it smoothed over. Please note anything I should be careful not to conclude from the accurate parts of the summary, and do not trouble yourself with reading suggestions or citations. I would be grateful if the answer were written in American English.
