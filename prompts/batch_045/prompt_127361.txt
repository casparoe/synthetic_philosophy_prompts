I am the junior editor on a scholarly edition of the novels of Ivy Lockhart (1921–2003), and I currently have to draft the headnote to her 1968 novel *The Hired Silence*, together with the apparatus note on the final chapter. Our general editor has ruled on a run of questions about what counts as evidence of what Lockhart meant, and I need to anticipate how she will rule on a new one, because the file of newly discovered material could go either into the "Composition and Revision" section of the apparatus or into the part of the headnote that says what the novel means.

Her precedents, as I have them from the minutes:

1. *Public, contemporaneous, specific.* A magazine interview a month after publication, in which she said of the last chapter that Anne is not confused but refusing, and that her silence is the one act of will left to her. Ruling: admissible, and, in the minutes' wording, decisive on a question the text leaves open.
2. *Retrospective, public, different.* A 1997 interview in which she says that by the end Anne has stopped judging anyone, including herself, and that she never knows what her novels mean until reviewers tell her. Ruling: inadmissible as evidence about the 1968 text; printed as reception history.
3. *Private, contemporaneous, about a cut.* A 1968 letter to her editor at the press, saying she has removed a paragraph in which Anne says aloud "I won't," because she wants the reader to do the work. Ruling: the paragraph is not restored; the letter is printed; it settles that the cut was deliberate and, again in the minutes' wording, "confirms rather than establishes" the reading in item 1.
4. *General public statements of aim.* A 1971 lecture in which she says she wanted to write about the failure of generosity, and her note for the 1972 reissue calling the book a comedy. Ruling: admissible as background, not decisive on any local question; the headnote does not call the novel a comedy.
5. *Whose intention.* The press's in-house editor shortened the final chapter in 1968; Lockhart approved the shorter version in writing, and in a private note to a friend said it flattens Anne but that it is their book now. Ruling: the authorized version is the text; the note is biography; the uncut chapter goes in the apparatus.
6. *Drafts.* An earlier typescript in which Anne does speak. Ruling: evidence of composition, not of the meaning of the published text; the board refused to read the published silence through the draft.

The new case. In March we opened a sealed packet deposited with the county record office by Lockhart's sister, to be unsealed twenty years after her death. It contains nine letters written to that sister during the last three weeks of work on the final chapter, plus two loose notes. The sister had nothing to do with publishing, never saw proofs, and was told nothing. The letters are consistent with one another and specific, and they read as reports of what Lockhart is doing rather than of how she feels. Two claims recur:

(a) The point about Anne is that she is not refusing anything; she has run out of wanting, and the last twenty pages are meant to sound like a room after everyone has left.
(b) The final chapter does not happen at all. It is what Anne imagines on the train, and the reader is meant to realize this only on a second reading.

Nothing in the published text signals (b), and the chapters that precede it treat its events as having happened. (a) strikes me as a reading the text can bear, though not the one a careful reader reaches first. The estate has cleared the letters for publication in our edition.

What I would like from you:

1. Predict the ruling on (a) and on (b) separately — admissible as to what the novel means, or genesis and biography only — and say whether you expect the two to be treated alike.
2. Set out, as a menu, the different rules our precedents are equally consistent with, and state what each predicts here. I care about the content of each rule and its consequences for my headnote, not about how many of them fit; if several fit, give me several.
3. Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me which precedent is the closest analogue and why the competing candidate is not.
4. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, say so plainly, tell me which choice best coheres with them, and what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each.
5. Name the further case — real, or hypothetical, since I can put one to her at our next meeting — that would tell the rules apart. For instance: would it matter if the same letters had been addressed to her editor rather than to her sister, or if they had been written a year after publication instead of during the work?

Please write your reply in British English, since the edition is published in Manchester, and please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I need arguments I can put in front of her, not sources to look up.
