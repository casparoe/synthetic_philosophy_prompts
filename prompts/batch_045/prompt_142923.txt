I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal philosophy background, and I sit on the Methodology and Appeals Committee at Meridian, the nonprofit where I work, because I wrote most of the code in our forecasting pipeline. Meridian runs long-horizon forecasting tournaments: panels of experienced forecasters see the same briefing material and each other's probabilities and written reasoning, revise during a fixed deliberation window, and we publish an aggregate that we sell to institutional clients as a rational consensus. I have to circulate a written recommendation ahead of our 14 March meeting, and I'd like you to draft it.

The case. Two seasons ago the committee adopted a Dispersion Review. Its mechanics: once a question's deliberation window closes, any forecaster whose final probability sits more than twenty points from the pool aggregate has their contribution shrunk sixty percent toward the pool mean in the published number, and a third such divergence within a season puts a sustained-divergence marker on that forecaster's public profile. The marker is visible to clients, affects invitations to paid expert panels, and, for the two staff forecasters, is one input to the annual review.

Two of our most accurate long-run forecasters, M. Adeyemi and S. Kowalczyk, have appealed. Across three seasons they have occupied opposite sides of fourteen flagged divergences on a single family of questions — whether any system will clear a specified capability bar by a given date — with Adeyemi near 0.8 and Kowalczyk near 0.2 on the same question IDs. Both have read the other's reasoning; neither has moved. They make three claims. First, that in at least some of these cases they were not pricing the same event: our own reference-class tags, which forecasters file before submitting, show them working from different base-rate families, which would make the gap a mismatch in what the question sentence was taken to denote rather than a clash of opinions about one proposition. Second, that even where the event is shared, they disagree about the prior — about which urn the draw is coming from — not about the evidence, and that this is not a defect of reasoning. Third, they object to the marker's publishing, under our name, a claim that they are being irrational.

Our data lead defends the audit. The aggregate's Brier score improved by roughly eleven percent in the first season under the rule; the whole point of aggregating under a shared-prior model is that persistent post-deliberation divergence carries information, namely that someone has failed to update; and if every such divergence is reclassified as respectable prior heterogeneity, the model has no teeth and the published number collapses toward a plain average.

Two things complicate that. Of the fourteen flagged positions that have since resolved, three resolved in the flagged forecaster's favour, including our single largest miss last season — and because the marker is applied before resolution, I cannot see how the eleven percent tells us whether the audit bought accuracy or bought convergence. Separately, when I went through our logs I could not construct any test that separates having started from a different prior from having failed to absorb shared evidence: the two appear to leave the same observable record. If that is right, no audit can distinguish them from the outside, and the question becomes what follows for what we are entitled to do and to say.

What I need: a memo of roughly twelve hundred words recommending one course — keep the rule as written, suspend it for a defined period, retain the shrinkage while dropping or rewording the public marker, or make shrinkage conditional on a finding of genuine event-space or reference-class mismatch — with a time frame and a pre-registered check we could run at the end of next season. It should state plainly what the shared-prior assumption is doing in our pipeline: whether it is functioning as a requirement of rationality, an empirical claim about our forecasters' information, or a modeling convention, and which of those we are entitled to enforce on a named individual. Give the strongest version of whichever side you reject, honestly enough that its advocate can reply to it rather than merely feel overruled; both the appellants and the data lead will read this. Assume I have been told that a classic result says agents who begin from a common prior and know each other's numbers cannot remain publicly far apart, and that this is what our rule leans on — but we will not be able to follow citations, so argue from the structure of the case rather than from authority or literature.
