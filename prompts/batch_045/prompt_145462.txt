I've been having a standing argument with a group of friends — we all came up reading the same rationalist blogs, and we inherited the reflex of asking whether a category carves anything at a joint or is just a bin we drew over a continuum — about whether generational cohorts are real kinds. I want to write something up, and I've realized my own verdicts don't add up, so before I do I'd like a principle. My tentative one is this: a label picks out a real cohort kind just in case (a) its members share a causally efficacious formative exposure that non-members mostly lack, (b) that exposure has downstream effects that travel together rather than scattering (a homeostatic property cluster, roughly), and (c) the boundaries of the group track that mechanism rather than a round number on a calendar. Please test that against my verdicts and repair it where it breaks.

Here are the cases and what I actually think. Gen Z, in the usual late-90s-to-2010s bracket: not a real kind. The traits people attribute to it look continuous in birth year, the cutoffs are an administrative convenience, and I suspect the famous discontinuity findings would not survive being looked for symmetrically. Xennials, the jokey micro-cohort at the end of the 70s with an analog childhood and a digital young adulthood: close to real, even though the label began as a magazine think-piece — there was a genuine step change in household technology penetration, and the people on either side of it had different adolescent media environments. The Americans who entered the labor market in 2008–2010: clearly real, since membership is defined causally rather than conventionally and the consequences cluster for a decade or more. British adolescents during the 2020–21 school closures: real, but a thinner flavor of real — shared event, wildly divergent responses, and I doubt the group holds together in twenty years. Everyone born in a Dragon year in a country where births spike in those years and teachers then treat those children differently: real as a cohort, I want to say, but not a generation, because the sorting is imposed from outside instead of arising from a shared formation. The 27 Club: obviously not a kind, just coincidence plus selection. A cluster-finding algorithm run over forty years of attitude surveys that reports the sharpest break sits at birth year 1978, with no theory offered: a real discontinuity, maybe, but not yet a kind. And the one I keep going back on: suppose a joke account invents a cohort label in 2018 for people born in the early nineties, management consultants pick it up, people start self-identifying with it, and self-identified members measurably change how they talk about work — I want to say it is real by now, which sits badly with my Gen Z verdict, since Gen Z is also socially propagated; if looping through self-conception is enough to make a kind, I owe you an explanation of why it doesn't rescue Gen Z too.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied, with the clause of it that each case motivates, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should overturn. Then tell me what your account predicts for cases I haven't listed — I'm thinking of Generation Alpha, the first cohort raised entirely inside algorithmic recommendation feeds, a cohort defined by platform rather than decade (people whose first social account was on a public feed rather than a friends-only network), children in utero during a famine, the adults who finished high school in 2020, and a break at 1978 that still has no mechanism behind it — and say what practical stance follows for the rest of us who have to decide whether to keep using these labels at all. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and single out the claim you're least sure of.
