A popular economics blog I was sent summarises Keynes's view on probability and uncertainty like this: "Keynes's early book A Treatise on Probability (1921) argued that probability is a matter of the rational degree of belief a person has in a proposition, grounded in a logical relation to the evidence but often not numerically measurable, and that we should attend to the weight of the argument as well as the probability itself. By the time he wrote The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), however, he had largely abandoned this logical apparatus for economics: because long-term investment faces radical uncertainty rather than risk, there are frequently no rational probabilities to be had at all, and conduct is guided by animal spirits and by conventions that are little more than arbitrary, self-fulfilling guesses. The General Theory therefore leaves the Treatise behind: the early Keynes hoped for rational degrees of belief, whereas the later Keynes accepted that in the economy we must simply act on guesswork and fragile conventions, with no rational probabilistic basis."
