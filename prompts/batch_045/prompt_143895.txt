I have been arguing with an old graduate-school friend, a working analyst with very little patience for foundations, and she has backed me into a position I cannot find my way out of. She has read enough to absorb the standard replies, and each one I try gets absorbed. I do not want you to tell me which side to be on; I want the argument itself taken apart.

Here it is, as strongly as I can state it on her behalf.

(1) What a speaker means by a logical constant is fixed by which inferences involving it she treats as primitive and unrestrictedly valid. There is no further fact about the meaning of "or" and "not" over and above the inferential role the speaker accords them.

(2) The classical mathematician accepts excluded middle (A or not-A) and double-negation elimination (if not-not-A then A) as unrestrictedly valid. The intuitionist accepts neither. So the two accord "or" and "not" different inferential roles.

(3) Hence the intuitionist's "or" and "not" are not the classical mathematician's "or" and "not." They are different connectives that happen to be spelled the same way.

(4) Genuine disagreement requires one party to assert what the other denies: a single proposition affirmed and rejected. If the connectives differ, no such proposition is on the table. So the intuitionist is not contradicting the classical mathematician. She is speaking a neighbouring language whose sentences can be translated into ours but can never conflict with them.

(5) And she gives up nothing by it. Under the familiar double-negation translation of classical logic into intuitionistic logic of the kind every textbook gives, inserting double negations in front of atomic formulas (the exact variant does not matter for her point), a classical entailment holds exactly when its translation holds intuitionistically. So for every theorem of classical mathematics there is an intuitionistic theorem that is its counterpart.

(6) Take any classical existence theorem. The intermediate value theorem is the stock example: the constructive mathematician is not able to derive the exact statement, only approximate substitutes, but under the translation what she derives is the double negation of the classical claim, and classically the double negation of an existential is equivalent to that existential. So in the only sense of "the same theorem" that mathematics itself recognises, she proves everything he does.

(7) Therefore constructivism is not a rival to classical mathematics at all. It is classical mathematics with a different accent, and every claim that constructive proof gives you something extra — a witness, an algorithm, a program extracted from a proof — is either false, or else a claim about a different connective, which is to say a claim about nothing anyone ever asserted.

The conclusion is absurd, and not merely because I would prefer it to be. Run the same seven steps with a relevant or paraconsistent logician in the intuitionist's place, or with two constructivists, one of whom accepts Markov's principle and one of whom does not, and you get the result that nobody has ever coherently disagreed about logic and that no one has ever been simply mistaken about a rule of inference. That is more than I am willing to pay, and it also makes my friend's own participation in this argument impossible, which I have pointed out to her and which bothered her less than I had hoped.

There are two things I want to keep, in this order. First, that the intuitionist's refusal to assert A or not-A is a real refusal, and not a report about what anyone has managed to prove so far; I do not want her reduced to saying that we have not found a proof yet. Second, and this is the part I invented under pressure, what I call the Witness-Preservation Constraint: a reconstruction of a classical theorem inside constructive mathematics counts as faithful only if it yields a term t together with a proof of A(t), and not merely a proof that it is not the case that no such term exists. Under that constraint the double-negation translation is not a vindication of classical mathematics but a receipt for an unpaid debt, since nothing at all can be extracted from a doubly negated existential. My friend replies that the Constraint assumes the very standard it was supposed to establish, and I am not confident she is wrong about that.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid and the real choice is which premise to abandon, tell me which of (1), (4) and (6) is cheapest to lose and what I am committed to once I have lost it. If instead something slipped between the steps — a slide between "has a counterpart theorem" and "has the same theorem," perhaps, or between two different things one might mean by rejecting a claim — name the step and say why it looked sound. If one of the standard moves is doing the work in (1) through (4): the change-of-subject charge, meaning-variance about the connectives, pluralism about consequence, or treating denial as a force rather than as part of a content, tell me which one, and price what accepting or refusing it commits me to. My instinct is to give up (1) rather than (2), but I cannot say why that instinct is right, and I would rather know the bill in advance.

Two further requests. Say what would change your mind: which consideration would move you to a different step, or to a different ranking of the costs, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong; I would rather have a verdict I can pressure-test than one that sounds settled. And please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — write it the way you would write a careful email to someone who is actually going to read it.
