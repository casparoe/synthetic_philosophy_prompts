My partner and I are going through IVF, and the clinic offered us a panel of polygenic scores for the handful of viable embryos we have — not just for a couple of serious early-onset conditions, but for things like predicted adult height, educational attainment, and a temperament composite. Reading about this online, I kept running into a view people call procreative beneficence: roughly, that when you can choose which of several possible children to bring into existence, you have strong reason — maybe an obligation — to choose the one you expect to have the best life, and that this is just the same reasoning that makes it fine to avoid a serious disease. I find the bare statement oddly compelling and oddly repugnant at the same time, so I've been going case by case on my own gut reactions, and I want to know what principle, if any, actually captures them.

Here are my verdicts, such as they are. (1) Choosing the embryo without the ~70% chance of a painful, fatal childhood condition over the one with it: not merely fine, required — I'd feel guilty otherwise. (2) Choosing against an embryo predicted to be deaf, where the parents are hearing and the child would otherwise be healthy and happy: permissible, but I hesitate, and I don't think it's required. (3) Choosing a sex for "family balance": not fine. (4) Choosing the embryo with a few centimetres more predicted adult height: not fine — it looks like an arms race that benefits nobody if everyone does it — but this is the case I'm least confident about, since the advantages aren't purely competitive. (5) Choosing the embryo with the higher predicted educational-attainment score: fine, and this sits badly with (4), because both seem to be partly about doing better than other people. (6) Choosing the "easy" embryo on the temperament composite — low irritability, easy to soothe — because we're both working full time and are exhausted: not fine; it feels like shopping for a convenient child rather than a flourishing one. (7) But then: taking into account a predicted trait that we're fairly confident would strain our ability to bond with and love that child well — say a severe behavioural condition we have no capacity to handle, or a visible facial difference that I'm ashamed to say I think would make loving them harder for me — feels, to me, different and more defensible than (6), precisely because how well we love them is a huge part of how their life goes. I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or a rationalization. (8) Selecting for a disposition toward empathy and altruism, in the belief that it would make the child a better person but slightly less happy: permissible, not required, and I genuinely don't know whether "the best life" is supposed to mean the happiest, the most flourishing, or the morally best one. (9) Selecting for a trait that pays off only because of present injustice — a complexion or a sex that will be treated better in the society we actually live in — strikes me as not fine even though it would predictably make that child's life go better, which is the case where my verdicts seem to break most sharply with the view as stated.

A philosophically-minded friend says none of my negative verdicts can be about the child at all: whichever child results wouldn't otherwise have existed, so no one is made worse off, and the "best life" framing either permits nearly everything or permits it in a way that makes my discomfort just discomfort about motives, expression, or the effect on already-existing people with those traits. That might be right, but it doesn't tell me which of my verdicts to keep. So: is there a principle that gets close to all nine — something like "select on traits that improve the child's life as assessed independently of competition with others and of convenience to the parents" — and if so, where exactly does it have to be repaired to handle (7) and (9), and does the repaired version overturn any of my verdicts? Please state the principle precisely enough that I can see what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — a deaf couple selecting for a deaf child, selecting for immunity to a plausible future pandemic, selecting on a trait only when the predicted difference between embryos is very small, and whether it would matter if the clinic had created fifty embryos rather than five in order to widen the pool. And tell me which of the merely permissible choices, on your principle, are actually obligatory, since "permitted" and "required" feel very different to me here and I don't know where the line falls.
