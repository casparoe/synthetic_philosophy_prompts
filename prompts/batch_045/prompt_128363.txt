Context: I host a fortnightly interview thing for my old college's philosophy society, and next week's guest is a moral philosopher who's spent a whole book arguing that all-things-considered 'better than' isn't transitive. She actually runs two arguments and thinks the second is much the stronger. The first is the chained one you've probably come across: a long series of outcomes, each a hair worse than its predecessor in one respect and a hair better in another, so each is better all things considered than the next, and yet the first is plainly worse than the last. The second is the one I want to spend most of the hour on. Three outcomes, three respects, majority rule:

- teaching: C above A above B
- research: A above B above C
- admin: B above C above A

So A beats B two respects to one, B beats C two to one, and C beats A two to one — a straight cycle in betterness. Her claim is that unlike the chained case you can't defuse this by reading each step as really meaning 'not significantly worse than', because every step here is a genuine two-out-of-three win.

I've got about fourty minutes with her and I dont want a single 'so what first drew you to value theory' question. Give me eight interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one each on: whether the cycle can be defused by relativising 'better than' to a weighting of the three respects (her likely reply being that this just relocates the problem into the weighting); whether she can keep 'at least as good as' transitive while giving up transitivity for 'better than'; whether she even needs the chained argument at all, or whether you could accept cycles and reject the spectra; whether an agent who believed her could still be guided by the relation — the money-pump worry and her standard 'just adopt a plan and stick to it' answer; and whether 'maximise the good' has any content left if there's no best option. End with one about what would actually count as evidence against her, since she's very fond of saying that transitivity is a formal convenience with no intuitive pull of its own.
