hey — I'm in an informal comparative ethics reading group (mostly grad students, plus one guy who did a monastery year in Thailand and won't stop correcting our Pali), and we keep hitting the same move. Every time someone argues that Buddhist ethics is really just consequentialism, they reach for the medical analogy: the Buddha is the physician, dukkha is the illness, craving and ignorance are the diagnosis, nirvana is the cure, and the eightfold path is the course of treatment. Then the conclusion gets drawn: since a treatment is good only so far as it cures, and the regimen can be adjusted or dropped once it stops working (this is where the raft parable gets wheeled out), Buddhist ethics is consequentialist all the way down — skillful versus unskillful just means conduces to the end of suffering, full stop, with no deontological leftovers.

I'd like someone to actually audit that instead of nodding along. Give me the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is great — then where it breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion being drawn from the ones that are just decoration.

Some things I keep chewing on and can't settle:

1. Medicine has an externally specifiable endpoint (health, functioning, survival) that the patient already wants and can partly recognize. Is nirvana that sort of thing? Doesn't the analogy quietly import a pre-given "normal functioning" that Buddhist soteriology doesn't have — the end being defined partly by the path rather than the other way round?

2. The bedside frame is one doctor, one patient, benefits accruing to that patient. Consequentialism is normally agent-neutral: my act counts if it reduces suffering anywhere. So the analogy looks like it buys at most a self-directed, what's-good-for-this-patient teleology. What would it take to make it a public-health analogy instead — triage, bed allocation, a formulary committee deciding which of two treatments to fund — and would that version be as comfortable for the people using it, or does it start sounding like something they didn't sign up for?

3. Paternalism. Doctors treat people who don't consent and don't grasp the diagnosis, and "the patient doesn't know he's sick" does real work in these texts. Does the analogy smuggle in an expertise asymmetry that consequentialism doesn't itself license? And does that make the bitter-medicine and skillful-means material look more like an excuse than an argument?

4. Intention. Karma is identified with intention in the texts, but medicine is indifferent to why you swallowed the pill — only to whether it worked. So the analogy seems to predict the wrong thing about what's being assessed. Or is that too fast?

If the bedside version can't carry the conclusion, say so plainly and tell me what the nearest analogy that could would be. Keep it under 900 words, and finish with a one-sentence summary followed by \answer{...} containing exactly one of: survives, survives-in-modified-form, collapses.
