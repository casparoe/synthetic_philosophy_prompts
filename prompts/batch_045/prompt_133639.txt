Hey — I'm the junior person on the collections commitee of a small university art museum, and we've got a nasty one on Tuesday. I've only been on it since September, so I've been back through the minutes trying to work out what we actually think, and I'm not sure our own record pins down an answer. Here's what I've got.

Five decisions, summarised:

1. Two years ago we turned down a loan of six huge tapestries woven in a prison workshop under the old regime. The weavers were conscripted, two died at the looms, the tapestries depict the dictator's family as gods, and the survivors' association wrote to say that hanging them as art would honour the men who killed their fathers. We said no, fairly fast.

2. We own fourteen oils by a landscape painter who beat his wife, drove a student out of the profession and stole that student's designs. Dead eighty years. None of it is in the pictures. They hang in the front gallery on an ordinary label.

3. We accepted a bequest of a composer's manuscripts and royalties. In 1938 he signed a public loyalty oath and named colleagues to the authorities; two of them lost their posts and one never worked again. A slice of his royalties went to the ruling party, which no longer exists. The music is abstract orchestral work with no political content, and nobody thinks its value comes from any of that. Everyone involved is dead; there's a biography that makes grim reading. We took the bequest and we still programme the symphonies.

4. We declined a gift of a photographer's famine portfolio. He paid destitute people to pose in degrading arrangements, the shock of the pictures is the entire point of them, descendants of three of the subjects objected in writing, and print sales still fund a foundation that runs projects along the same lines. Declined.

5. We accepted, with conditions, a travel writer's illustrated diaries — watercolours that are genuinely superb, prose that is casually racist, and a dozen objects he drew or carried home that were taken from shrines without anyone's permission. Writer long dead. The community whose shrines they were is very much alive; we wrote to them; they asked that the diaries be shown only alongside their own account of the objects, and that we not sell reproductions. We agreed, and that's what's on the wall. Two members dissented and wanted to refuse outright, which is why I think this one may be our weakest precedent.

Now Tuesday. A set of thirty-four plaster life-casts, made in the winter of 1868 from men held after a failed uprising on an island whose name I'll leave out of this email. All thirty-four were hanged within a fortnight of sitting for the casts, which were then sold around Europe first as anatomical specimens and later as sculpture; they're now regarded as among the finest anatomical modelling of the century, and having seen the photographs I don't think that's hype. The sculptor's notebooks, digitised by an archive last year and properly read for the first time, say he chose the men for the nobility of their fear as they waited to hang, and that he had them stripped and shackled in the studio for the sittings. We bought our set at auction in 1974, title clean. We advertised twice for descendants and heard nothing; but a historian at another university has now matched three of the names to families still living on the island, and nobody has contacted those families yet. The island's heritage council has written asking to be consulted — asking, not claiming; they've said they know they have no legal standing. The director keeps mentioning that these are the most requested objects we own and that we're about £200k down this year, which I assume is meant to be irrelevant.

The four options on the table: show them as sculpture, show them with the men's names and the notebook material and a wall text, keep them in store, or defer until the three families have been asked.

What I want from you isn't really a gut reaction. I want to know what rule or rules we've been implicitly running in 1 through 5, whether more than one rule fits all five equally well, and what each of those rules says about Tuesday. Which of the five is the closest analog and why. If we genuinely aren't pinned down, say so plainly and tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each of the four options — I'd rather adopt something on purpose than by accident. And tell me which single further fact, or which small hypothetical case, would best separate the rules from each other: if the load-bearing question really is "ask the families first," I want to be able to explain to the committee why that's the load-bearing question and not just politeness.

Reason it out in whatever order makes sense, then finish with one line: \answer{show}, \answer{show with conditions}, \answer{store}, or \answer{defer}.
