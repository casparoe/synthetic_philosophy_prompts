I am a retired experimental physicist; most of my working life was spent in precision measurement, and I now read philosophy with three former colleagues. We have reached decision theory and the Ellsberg urn, and I want an audit of an analogy my group keeps reaching for, because I suspect it is being made to carry more weight than it can bear.

The case, as we have set it up: an urn holds ninety balls, thirty of them red and the remaining sixty black and yellow in a proportion we are not told. You are asked, first, to choose between a stake that pays a fixed sum if the drawn ball is red and one that pays the same if it is black; then, separately and not as part of the same choice, between a stake that pays if the ball is black-or-yellow and one that pays if it is red-or-yellow. The usual pattern is red in the first pair and black-or-yellow in the second; that pair of preferences cannot be generated by any single assignment of probabilities to red, black and yellow, and it shows up as a reversal when one and the same yellow outcome is attached to both options.

My colleagues' line — and it is meant to settle the matter — is that an ambiguous urn is exactly like an instrument with an uncharacterised systematic. The nominal reading may be one-half, but nobody competent publishes a central value with no error bar, nobody treats an unmeasured offset as though it had been shown to vanish, and caution in the face of an unknown nuisance is simply good laboratory practice. On that reading, the charge of irrationality amounts to telling a metrologist to ignore systematics on the ground that the true value is, after all, some particular number.

Please set the correspondence out explicitly — a table would be welcome — naming what, on that mapping, plays the part of the measurand, the estimate, the unknown offset, the error bar itself, the coverage probability, the calibration experiment, the loss function, and the audience to whom one reports. Then give me the places the mapping breaks, and sort them into those that damage the conclusion my colleagues want and those that are merely cosmetic.

Three points I should like handled in particular.

First, an error bar is a report, not a decision rule: under a symmetric loss it should not alter what one does, only how wide an interval one publishes. So it looks as though the analogy is quietly importing an asymmetry — caution, or the professional norm against overclaiming — that belongs to the sociology of measurement rather than to the choice itself. Is that right, and is the imported ingredient doing the real work?

Second, a genuine systematic is one fixed offset with a joint model, propagated consistently through every quantity one reports. The Ellsberg pair instead seems to aim the pessimism afresh at whichever event is the ambiguous one in each comparison, rather as if the offset re-tuned itself once it saw which estimate I had chosen. That sounds less like an unmeasured nuisance than like an adversary or a coincidence of nature against me. Tell me whether you take this to be the central disanalogy, and whether a worst-case-taken-over-a-set-of-priors rule — which I gather does reproduce the observed pattern — is best understood as pessimism re-aiming itself, or as something more innocent than that.

Third, I want the analogy tested against urns of different provenance: one in which the sixty non-red balls were allocated by a mechanical device whose long-run behaviour is documented, so that I know the chances of each possible split though not which split obtained; one in which the composition was fixed by an indifferent technician who has since died and never knew of me; and one in which it was fixed by someone who knows which way I shall bet and gains exactly what I lose, together with the variant in which that person knows my bet but is indifferent to my gain. Does the error-bar analogy treat these alike? If it distinguishes them, on which feature, and has it then stopped being an analogy about missing information and become one about the intentions behind the gap?

Finally: does the conclusion survive — that ambiguity aversion is merely ordinary experimental prudence and the charge of incoherence is a philosopher's misunderstanding — or does it survive only in some weaker form? If there is a better analogy available, say what it is and what it licenses that this one does not. And tell me where, if anywhere, the vocabulary of measurement is itself manufacturing the intuition, so that the analogy sounds compelling because error bars are normative for us rather than because the structure of the two problems really matches.

I should prefer British English throughout, and no references or suggestions for further reading: I have no library access, and I would rather have the argument than the literature.
