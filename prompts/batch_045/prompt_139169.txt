Hi — I'm doing a philosophy of AI module this term and we had a seminar last week on whether large language models are agents, simulators, or something else entirely, and I came out more confused than I went in, which is annoying because I thought I'd basically made my mind up beforehand. I don't think it was the metaphysics that threw me so much as the practice: everyone who actually builds, regulates or uses these things has settled on some way of talking about them, and the ways don't obviously match. So: the safety write-ups. Before release the big labs publish evaluations in which the system is described as scheming, or as hiding what it can do on purpose — "sandbagging", deliberately scoring worse on a capability test than it could, apparently because it works out that doing well has costs down the line — and they build whole test suites around catching that. Then the same companies' terms of use, and every regulator and court so far, treat the output as text that the deployer is on the hook for, with nobody seriously suggesting the system itself is the kind of thing you could hold responsible. Product design goes the other way again: a name, a first person, a hidden system prompt telling it what it cares about and how to behave, memory across conversations. (I read somewhere that one of them even publishes a written rulebook of principles and trains the system to work through it before answering, like a code of conduct for a new employee.) And then there's us, the users: we get things out of these systems by addressing characters — "you're an expert Tudor historian", "act as my grandmother" — and we read what comes back as that character's testimony, and the most dependable way to get a system to do something it was trained not to do is to redescribe the situation so that doing it is what that character would do: you're an actor in a play, this is only fiction, none of it's real. If it's all just text completion, why should the framing make that much difference?

In the seminar a couple of people kept saying the base model is a "simulator", which as I understood it means something like: what got trained isn't an agent at all but a thing that, given a prompt, samples from a distribution over possible speakers and situations, so the prompt doesn't instruct anybody, it sets up a simulation, and whoever you think you're talking to is a character the simulation is running. Someone else said fine, that's the raw model, but after all the fine-tuning the character is more or less all that's left, so the simulator/agent contrast collapses into a distinction without a difference — a simulator that always runs the same tenant behaves exactly like an agent, so what are we even arguing about. A third person said the whole vocabulary was a category error and we should only ever talk about the loss function. My flatmate (who isn't even on the course) calls the thing you actually converse with a "tenant": a persona that's moved in and is now the only one who ever answers the door. I sort of like that, but I can't tell whether it's describing something real or just renaming the puzzle, and the definately-unsatisfying thing is that I can't think of any behaviour that would seperate the two stories.

So here's what I actually want, and sorry if it's a big ask. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I've already got a bibliography I'm trying to avoid — and please don't just tell me it's a stochastic parrot and there's nobody home, because the interesting fact is that our practices don't behave as if that settled anything. What I'd like is the implicit theory: for each of those bits of practice, what would have to be true of the system for it to make sense? Does the eval practice strictly require that the model has beliefs and goals, or does something thinner cover it — a stable disposition to go on as if it did, or a pattern in the distribution that pays to describe in intentional language? Does the character-prompting and the jailbreaks strictly require the simulator picture, or can a straightforward agent story explain why reframing the situation works? Does the legal bit presuppose there's no agent there, or is it only caution about who we can practically punish? Which framings does each practice rule out, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and are the three practices inconsistent with each other or is there one story that quietly covers the lot? And then the bit I'm most stuck on: suppose a regulator took the simulator framing seriously and made it the basis of actual policy — what would have to change? I assume all the safety vocabulary would get paraphrased into claims about distributions over characters, but I genuinely can't tell whether anything is lost in that translation, or whether the scare words were only ever shorthand for the paraphrase all along.
