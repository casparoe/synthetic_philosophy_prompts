After my father's retirement I took over the day-to-day management of my family's shrimp farm — about forty earthen ponds stocked with Pacific whiteleg shrimp, harvested into an ice slurry and sold frozen to wholesale buyers. One of our largest customers has now made a written animal-welfare protocol a condition of renewing the contract, and I have decided to treat that as a genuine question rather than as paperwork.

A friend who works on animal sentience has pressed on me a distinction I cannot get out of my head, and I would like its consequences spelled out. On her view, shrimp welfare should not be modelled on the mammalian case. Farmed finfish such as salmon, she argues, plausibly have something like moods: affective conditions that persist, colour many successive moments, and can be anticipated and remembered, so that crowding, degraded water quality or rough handling can leave an animal in a bad state for hours or days, and a single alarming event can cast a shadow forward. Shrimp, she thinks, may have only what she calls short-loop affect — discrete valenced episodes of discomfort lasting seconds or minutes, with no anticipation of future distress, no distress drawn from memory, and no persisting background condition that accumulates over a life. If she is right, shrimp welfare is denominated in seconds of discomfort per animal, while salmon welfare is denominated in days of a mood-like state per animal; and yet a kilogram of shrimp as we pack it contains dozens to a couple of hundred animals, whereas a kilogram of salmon is a fraction of one.

Please describe the most important implications of a mismatch of this kind for the protocol I have to write. I would especially like you to address the following: what currency the protocol should be written in — per animal, per kilogram of product, per unit of suffering-time, per dollar of cost — and why the choice changes the answers, given the numbers above; whether the short-loop hypothesis, if accepted, shifts weight toward the long growing period (stocking density, water quality, feed, disease, harvest method) and away from the brief moments of handling and slaughter, or the other way round, and whether electrical stunning before chilling is worth what it would cost us; how to weigh the few broodstock animals in the hatchery we buy our post-larvae from — females there have their eyestalks ablated to induce repeated spawning and are kept for months — against the very large number of animals in our grow-out ponds; what to do about interventions that cut both ways, such as measures that lower mortality but leave each surviving animal on the farm longer, or changes that make our shrimp dearer and so push buyers toward another supplier, another farmed species, or wild-caught product, where the effects outside our ponds may matter more than the effects inside them; and finally how to act under uncertainty, since her hypothesis is contested and shrimp may turn out to be capable of considerably more than she allows.

Please close by naming the three or four changes you think the protocol should require first, with the reasoning that produces that ordering, and say which of them I should be most reluctant to commit to while the science remains unsettled.
