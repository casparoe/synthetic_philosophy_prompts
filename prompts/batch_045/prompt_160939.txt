hey — I run a small research and forecasting team (6 people, no philosophy PhDs, mostly econ / policy / bio backgrounds) at a grantmaking org. Every week we publish an internal sheet with maybe 12 headline forecasts and a confidence on each, and those numbers get used to move real money. We already do calibration training and we aggregate with a prediction-market-ish thing; that part is fine, don't redesign it.

What we have no rule for is higher-order evidence. You know the shape: not evidence about the topic, evidence about your evidence — that a teammate disagrees, that your track record on this *class* of question is bad, that you were sleep-deprived / had a couple of drinks / had just read a forum thread that made you angry. Half the time we handle this by vibe, and the vibe is usually "split the difference," which I'm fairly sure is wrong in at least two distinguishable ways.

Three things I want built into whatever you give me. I half-invented these names, and I want you to actually engage with them rather than dissolving them back into the textbook cases:

1. **HOE laundering.** The same disagreement getting counted twice — once as first-order content (their reasons, which we can inspect and reject) and once as the bare fact that an independent-ish person disagrees, which mysteriously still moves us. Plus the adversarial version: someone can *manufacture* higher-order evidence by disagreeing loudly, and I want to know what the procedure says when the disagreement is cheap to produce.
2. **Domain-local vs. global reliability.** My calibration on macro questions is garbage. Does that licence moving me on a question about protein folding, or about a specific country's election? Likewise "I'm tired tonight" — local in *time*, and possibly moot if my reasoning is fully written out and checkable by someone else. I think there's a real distinction here about when reliability evidence does *no work* because the first-order argument is fully surveyable, and I want it in the procedure, not footnoted.
3. **The publication gap.** What number goes in the sheet when nobody's first-order view moved but everyone's second-order view says our first-order reasoning is unreliable? This is the epistemic akrasia thing. I want the procedure to be honest about whether "I still think P, and I think my thinking about P is distorted" is irrational, merely awkward, or actually the correct resting place — and, separately, what we *write down* and *do*, since those can come apart.

So: give me a procedure my team can actually run. Ordered questions; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it exits with a no; and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or appetite for risk rather than fact. It has to be runnable in 15 minutes on a Slack thread or 30 minutes in a meeting, by people who have never read a word of epistemology. No citations, no paper names, no "as so-and-so argues" — I don't care who said it, I care whether the move works. If a step needs a distinction from the literature, name it in plain words, define it in a sentence, and move on.

Then run it on four of our actual recurring situations:

(a) A teammate with a measurably *worse* track record than the group on this question class disagrees hard with our 70%.
(b) We realise, mid-writeup, that all six of us read the same thread, so our apparent agreement isn't independent. (I think this one should make us *less* confident, not more, but I want the procedure to say it and not me.)
(c) Our field analyst — best track record on the team, has actually been in the region, has reasons we can't fully verify — insists we're wrong. Nobody on the team is moved first-order. I suspect the honest output here is a number nobody endorses, and I want you to say so if that's where it lands.
(d) Somebody wrote their section at 2am and says so, but the reasoning is fully laid out and the rest of us can check every step.

At least one of these should come out in a way I don't like. Don't soften it to make me happy.

You're a professional epistemologist who has also spent years shipping an actual weekly forecast — think of yourself as someone who's done both, and who finds the pure theory a bit under-specified for practice. Take your time and think carefully. Before you start writing, brainstorm privately: at least six ways small teams get higher-order evidence wrong, and at least three cases where HOE should not move you *at all*. Don't paste the brainstorm into the answer; just let it shape the procedure.

Format: prose first, then a table, then JSON. The table should have exactly these columns — `Step` | `Question to ask` | `What counts as a sufficient answer` | `Where it exits` | `Who signs off`. After that, one JSON object and nothing else after it, with exactly these fields:

- `steps`: array of strings, one short line per step, in order
- `stop_rules`: array of strings — the conditions under which the procedure halts with a no
- `judgment_calls`: array of objects `{question, why_it_cant_be_proceduralized}`
- `worked_cases`: array of objects `{case_id, verdict, uncomfortable, one_sentence_rationale}` where `uncomfortable` is true or false
- `known_failure_modes`: array of objects `{name, symptom, what_the_procedure_does_about_it}`
- `what_the_procedure_cannot_do`: string

Also drop in a few lines — not a whole section — on what will predictably go wrong the first time we run this.
