I'm working on a piece about how science stories go wrong somewhere between the embargoed press release and the front page, which slid me sideways into reading about knowledge, luck and skepticism — don't ask me how, one tab led to another. Anyway, a cognitive scientist whose podcast I otherwise trust did an episode on Nozick's tracking account, and I want to use a paragraph of it as background in the article, but I can't tell how much of it to trust. Roughly, he says: knowledge is true belief plus two counterfactual requirements, that if the claim were false you wouldn't believe it and that if it were true you would, and the second of these is to be read completely flatly, so a real knower is someone who would have arrived at the true belief no matter how the world had turned out — raised by wolves, every newspaper shut down, whatever. It was Nozick's answer to Gettier, and it works because in a Gettier case the truth of the belief and the belief itself are only accidentally hooked up. Underneath the possible-worlds talk it's really just reliabilism in a costume: what matters is that whatever produced the belief — eyesight, memory, a tip from a source — is a process that keeps getting it right across the nearby cases. The catch is closure, the principle that if you know one thing then you know everything that follows from it, and tracking can't have it: if I were a brain in a vat I'd go right on believing I have hands exactly as I do now, so my belief doesn't track the claim that I'm not a brain in a vat, and Nozick ends up having to say we know almost nothing. Which is why, he says, everyone quietly dropped tracking and moved to safety-style accounts in the Sosa/Pritchard vein, which keep the anti-luck part but hand closure back, and tracking is now mostly of historical interest.

My instinct is that the brain-in-a-vat bit is overstated — I have a feeling Nozick bites a much smaller bullet than we know almost nothing, and says something specific about the denial of the skeptic's hypothesis rather than about ordinary stuff like whether there's a laptop in front of me — but I genuinely can't tell where the summary stops being a simplification and starts being a distortion, and my editor wants 800 words by friday. What would they wince at, or say is a conflation of two things the literature keeps apart? And does the pay-off — that the view is now only of historical interest and was superseded by safety accounts — actually follow from the parts that survive?
