I came away from a long argument with a friend last night convinced that we were not really disagreeing about the case in front of us; we were disagreeing about what a theory of supererogation is for. The case was a ward nurse who, on a chronically understaffed rota, stays two hours unpaid after every shift to finish medication rounds that no one else will cover. My friend called it a textbook supererogatory act: beyond duty, costly, praiseworthy, and such that omitting it is not wrong. I said that if the hospital's budget quietly depends on her doing it, then calling the act supererogatory is not praise but a subsidy, and that a theory which cannot tell the difference between honouring someone and relying on them is not fit for use.

So I want the specification before we argue about any further case. Write the desiderata an adequate theory of supererogation has to meet: the phenomena it must explain, the verdicts it must return, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the commitments elsewhere in ethics it may not contradict, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. I want a theory good enough for three downstream jobs: to allocate ordinary praise and blame, to tell institutions what they may ask of, or silently arrange themselves around, the occupants of roles, and to tell political argument whether this is a category we should be trying to preserve or to shrink.

Cases I want the list to earn its keep on, at minimum:

- Role relativity. The same rescue performed by a passing swimmer and by the on-duty lifeguard. If supererogation is indexed to the agent's role, commitments or office, say what fixes the index and whether a role can acquire new duties simply by being relied upon.
- The uncostly hero. The field doctor who works through an epidemic because she finds the work exhilarating and gives up nothing she values. Is sacrifice constitutive of the supererogatory, or merely evidence that the act exceeds what could be demanded of anyone?
- The mistaken-duty hero. The soldier who throws himself on the device in the false belief that he has been ordered to. If in his own eyes he was not free to omit it, has he done anything supererogatory at all?
- Supererogation by forbearance. The retired woman who releases a large, legally valid debt owed to her by a family that could pay. Praiseworthy to do, not required to do, and yet omission-shaped.
- The linked-hands rescue. Twenty people must enter the water together or the child drowns, and no individual makes a difference. Whatever each may permissibly do, may they all?
- The ratchet. The colleague who covers others' shifts every week for a year, after which her refusal is met with resentment. Are verdicts indexed to the moment of action, and does repetition convert the optional into the expected?
- The advisor. If I may permissibly omit the rescue, may I advise you to omit it, may I design an institution around your omitting it, and may I be indifferent to whether you do it?
- Honesty in a corrupt office. The inspector who refuses the bribe that every colleague accepts. Rare and very costly, but surely not beyond duty.
- The failed rescue. The man who jumps, cannot swim, and drowns alongside the child. Success is not required; but at what point does risk turn praiseworthy daring into something a theory should not be in the business of celebrating?

Distinctions I want either respected or explicitly collapsed, with the cost of collapsing: beyond duty versus merely permissible, as in choosing vanilla over chocolate; supererogatory versus costly-but-required; supererogatory versus saintly or self-transcending; and supererogatory versus its mirror, acts that are blameworthy to do though not wrong to do, if you grant that there are any.

Three things matter most to me in the answer. First, which of the demands people standardly impose are genuine adequacy conditions and which are the prejudices of one camp: I suspect both the demand that the theory be action-guiding from the agent's own deliberative point of view and the demand that supererogation never be a licence to do less than the best are camp-specific, but I want the argument rather than my suspicion back. Second, whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: my guess is that unrestricted aggregation will not sit with the asymmetry between praise and blame, and that time-indexed verdicts will not sit with treating supererogation as a property of act-types. If the list is itself a result, say so. Third, which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, the one I should drop last.

Only if it is quick: score, in a few lines each, a simple beyond-duty view, a maximizing view that grants agents an agent-centred prerogative, a view on which supererogation is excellence relative to the internal standard of a practice, and a contractualist reading in terms of what could reasonably be demanded of anyone. The scorecard is not the deliverable; the list is.
