I am working through the epistemology of higher-order evidence and I keep losing a thread that the standard stock of examples tends to blur. In the usual cases — the reasoner who learns she has been given a cognition-impairing drug, the person who learns he has forgotten a decisive argument he once had, the discoverer that she was selected into her epistemic situation by a process that produces error far more often than success, the plain case of disagreement with someone who looks like a peer — the rational response is almost always described in the same vocabulary: conciliate, regress toward the prior, split the difference, suspend, lower your confidence. I want to know whether that vocabulary is tracking one phenomenon or several, and I suspect several.

Fix a case as a hinge. I hold a proposition P with credence 0.85 on the basis of first-order evidence E. I then acquire a piece of higher-order evidence H, which tells me that the process by which I moved from E to that credence is unreliable. H is evidence about the mapping from my evidence to my attitude; it is not evidence about P directly, and it is compatible with my having landed on the truth.

Now distinguish two ways that mapping can be unreliable. Call it noisy when its failures are undirected: on the occasions the process errs, its outputs scatter around the correct value in a way whose direction carries no information about where that value lies, so that what the process has lost is informativeness — think of a channel that adds variance to a signal. Call it drifting when its failures have a stable direction: the process systematically sits on one side of the correct value, so that an output of 0.85 is not merely uncertain but is evidence of an overshoot, or equally evidence of an undershoot, depending on the sign of the drift.

Both are paradigm cases of higher-order evidence, and both support the ordinary claim that my process is unreliable. But they do not seem to me to call for the same response, and I would like the difference explained rigorously rather than gestured at.

First, I want the mechanism, stated at the level of what the higher-order evidence does to the likelihoods rather than at the level of slogan. My rough thought is that a noisy process compresses the likelihood ratio associated with my first-order evidence toward one, and compression of the likelihood ratio is exactly what pulls a posterior toward the prior; whereas a drifting process displaces the mapping without attenuating its informativeness, so that the correct response is a translation — a de-biasing — rather than a regression. Please say whether that is right, where it breaks down, and what the fully general form of the response to H is when the process is allowed to have both a noise and a drift component.

Second: if that is right, then the familiar injunction to respond to higher-order evidence by moving toward one's prior (or toward agnosticism, or toward the peer's view) looks like an artifact of the noisy case having been treated as the paradigm. In particular, a process with a known downward drift should sometimes require me to raise my confidence when I learn that the process is drifting, since an output of 0.85 from a process that systematically undershoots is evidence for a higher value than 0.85. I would like this consequence spelled out, because it seems to me to generate a distinctive pathology: an agent who has internalised the lesson that higher-order evidence is a reason for modesty will, in exactly these cases, move her credence to a place that her own total evidence no longer supports, and will do so while sincerely citing her higher-order evidence as the reason. Is that a species of epistemic akrasia? Is it the same phenomenon as the akrasia that the level-bridging principles are supposed to forbid, or a mirror image of it? And if it is akratic, which attitude is the one that should have moved — the first-order credence or the second-order one?

Third, I want the modeling choice made explicit, since I think the two descriptions pull apart here. One way to handle H is evidential and aggregative: treat H as evidence about which reliability hypothesis is true, and set your credence in P by averaging the credence each hypothesis would recommend, weighted by your credence in that hypothesis. Another way is to treat H as engaging a rational requirement governing the relation between your first-order and second-order attitudes — the thought, roughly, that it is incoherent to hold a first-order attitude while believing that attitude to be unsupported by your evidence — and to ask what is required to restore coherence. Please explain whether these two ways of handling H can come apart in the drifting case, in which direction each one pushes, and what it would take to settle which is the better model of what higher-order evidence does.

Fourth, an intermediate case that I find I cannot reason about cleanly, and which I would like used as a test: suppose I learn the magnitude of the drift but not its sign, so that my higher-order evidence tells me the process is off by some fixed amount in one direction or the other, symmetrically. Does this collapse into the noisy case and call for regression toward the prior, or does it retain something of the drifting structure? I have a nagging worry that the answer is neither: that when the error distribution is bimodal and symmetric around the correct value, the likelihood ratio can fail to be monotone in the output, so that a slightly more extreme first-order credence can sometimes be evidence for a less extreme posterior, and ordering claims like the more confident the deliverance, the more confident the corrected attitude go wrong. Please tell me whether that worry is well-founded and, if it is, what it shows about how much structure the ordinary conciliationist heuristics are quietly assuming.

Finally, two extensions, briefly. What happens when the distinction is iterated — when the higher-order evidence itself is delivered by a process that is noisy or drifting, and when I learn that it is? And where does disagreement with an apparent peer sit on the noisy/drifting divide: is the disagreement of a peer naturally modeled as undirected noise, as drift, or as something whose proper model depends on a further question about whether the peer's errors are independent of my own or share a bias with them, and what turns on the answer?

Please keep the discussion at the level of the conceptual structure and the formal shape of the response to higher-order evidence rather than at the level of which named view in the literature is called what.
