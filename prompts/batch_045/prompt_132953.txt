I've let myself get roped onto the faculty small-grants committee this spring, and now I owe a letter to the runner-up, and I've decided this is at bottom a problem about hard choices rather than about budgets. One grant, two finalists: a field-recording project that would document the last fluent speakers of a language, and a low-cost water filtration pilot for three villages. I do not think one of these is better. I also don't think I'm allowed to say to the loser "yours was worse" — and I'm not sure I'm allowed to say "they were incomparable, so no reason was needed" either.

So here's what I want, and note the purpose before you start: I need a theory of incommensurable value and hard choices that is good enough for an allocator who must justify, in writing, a choice between options she takes to be genuinely incomparable, to the person who loses. Adequacy relative to that. Not adequacy for a metaphysics seminar.

Give me the spec: what any such theory has to deliver — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the phenomena and results it has to be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a mark on each for whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp.

Here are the candidate desiderata I keep scribbling; sift them, tell me which are the real ones, and add the ones I've missed.

- It has to keep apart at least three relations: incomparability proper (no positive relation), incommensurability (no common measure or covering unit, but still comparable), and rough equality or parity. Is hard-choicedness supposed to be one of these, or is that exactly what's at issue?
- The small-improvement argument, and the mirror of it I can't get out of my head: if shaving a hair off A makes it worse than B, how was it not better than B all along? And the continuum worry behind that — does a chain of tiny changes force a single scale back in whether or not we want one?
- Epistemic versus metaphysical: "there's no fact of the matter" and "we can't tell" must come apart, or every hard choice starts looking like laziness.
- What the deliberator is *doing*: discovering a relation, weighting considerations, or creating a reason by committing? Whatever the answer, I want to know whether resolution has to be reason-responsive rather than arbitrary, and how much "arbitrary" a theory can tolerate.
- Call it the Residue Constraint: after a justified hard choice something is left over — it isn't a reason to have chosen otherwise, and it isn't a cost that would have tipped the balance, but it's there (guilt, repair, a sense of loss). Is predicting that a genuine requirement, or is it just what anti-consequentialists want to hear?
- Auditability, my own axe to grind: the theory has to generate a justification addressed to the loser that isn't reducible to "you scored lower." But I suspect this requirement secretly buys a covering value, in which case it should be thrown out or weakened — argue with me about this.
- Menu-dependence: can A's choiceworthiness shift when a third, clearly worse option shows up? Some people think context-sensitivity here is a feature, others think it's a reductio.
- Sequential choice: must the theory keep a rational agent from being walked somewhere by a series of small improvements she'd each accept?
- Two uses people actually have for it: advice (a friend in the middle of it) and retrospection (the same friend, ten years later, asking whether she chose well). A theory that has nothing to say to either seems defective to me, but say if that's sentimentality.
- Aggregation across persons. If I deny a common measure between two research programmes, can I still turn around and use something like QALYs for a hospital budget? Is "incommensurable in itself, commensurable for the sake of deciding" stable, or is it a prejudice of people who need to fill in spreadsheets?
- Whether hard choices are in the world or in our conceptual repertoire. Is taking a stand here optional, or does silence wreck everything else?

Then the part I care most about: which subset of the requirements you end up endorsing can't be jointly satisfied? My guess is something around reason-responsive resolution, no covering value, and the Residue Constraint, but work it out. And which single requirement kills the most otherwise-attractive theories?

Keep the scoring short — three or four lines each on a covering-value/utilitarian style view, an incomparabilist pluralism, and a will-based "the agent creates the reason" view, plus a conventionalist if you think it deserves a row. The criteria are the deliverable; the scoring is for my own sanity. And no reading list or citations, I know the shape of the literature, I just want the specification.
