I volunteer with a small group that fact-checks claims made by local officials for a community newsletter — city council members saying things like "the budget went up 12%" or "no residents were consulted before the vote." Our tagline on the site is roughly "we just care about what's true." A new volunteer who has some philosophy training (I think he did a master's) wrote a long message in our group chat saying the tagline is confused and that we should stop labelling things "true" and "false" and only ever say "supported by the record" / "not supported by the record." He says this all follows from deflationism about truth, which he summarized like this:

"Deflationists argue that there is no property of truth at all — 'true' is an empty word whose whole meaning is given by the schema: 'p' is true if and only if p. That's why 'it is true that the budget went up 12%' says nothing more than 'the budget went up 12%'; the word 'true' is only a handy device for cases where you can't repeat the sentence itself, like 'everything the treasurer said is true.' Since truth has no hidden nature left to uncover, deflationism settles the old fight between correspondence and coherence theories: there's nothing for a belief to correspond to, and no deep fact about which claims are really true. And that means truth can't do any work for a fact-checker. Asserting a claim and saying it's true are the same speech act, so 'true' adds nothing but emphasis — which is why your labels should track what the record supports and not pretend to be about truth. It also means your 'technically true but misleading' label is incoherent: if there's nothing more to a claim's being true than the claim itself, there's no room for a gap between true and misleading."

I use that third label constantly, so this isn't abstract for me — but I can't tell what's actually going on in his argument. My gut says the conclusion is getting smuggled in somewhere: it feels like you could think "true" is a thin, unexciting notion and still think that whether a claim is true is exactly what we're trying to figure out, and that our labels matter. But I can't point to the sentence that's doing the illegitimate work.

So: how fair is his summary? Please take it apart — which bits would someone who actually holds the view accept, which are standard simplifications, which are just wrong, and where (if anywhere) does he slide from "truth isn't a substantial property" into "truth can't do any work for us"? I'd especially like to know whether deflationists themselves think truth is still a norm governing what you should assert or believe, and whether the "settles the correspondence vs coherence debate" bit is something the view really delivers or him overrating it.
