I run a fortnightly philosophy reading group at work — mostly ML engineers, quick, no philosophy background beyond blog posts, and they've read the lordship-and-bondage section of Hegel's Phenomenology exactly once, in translation, in a hurry, and mostly hated it. Last session we burned fifty minutes arguing about whether an LLM could count as a recognizer and got nowhere, so this time I want the explanation planned instead of winged. Sixty minutes, one whiteboard, and they're the kind of people who will happily grab a marker and try to redraw your example as a diagram.

So don't explain the dialectic to me — plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before anything else can land: my instinct is that they need to feel why self-certainty on its own collapses, i.e. why that opening bit about desire isn't just Hegel being obscure, before anyone is allowed to say the word recognition — but tell me if that's the wrong place to start and what it should be instead. And I want the misconception map with a slot for each: that this is a story about the bondsman winning, a kind of proto-Marxist revenge plot; that recognition is just respect, or self-esteem, or being told you're good; that it's a historical claim about real slavery; that the lord is obviously the unfree one from the start (they'll import a modern picture where the dependence is already visible and then the whole reversal feels like a trick); and the one that derailed us last week, that recognition is aggregable — that ten thousand one-way admirers could add up to one genuine peer. Tell me where in the sequence to kill each one and roughly what to say when I do.

On the carrying example I've got three and cant decide: (a) the celebrity with an enormous parasocial audience who recognizes nobody back; (b) someone in a simulation where everyone else is scripted but genuinely, finely responsive to them — do they get recognized, and does it change anything if they know; (c) distributed recognition, where a dozen people each recognize one facet of you, your work self, your family self, your online self, and nobody sees the whole. Pick one and say what it must have and what it must lack to carry the section, and what goes wrong with the other two. Also: someone will try to formalize the whole thing as a mutual recursion or a fixed-point condition — is that a help or a trap, and if it's a trap what does it hide? Where do I defuse the "so Hegel just thinks you need other people's approval" reading? What should I openly refuse to get into at this level and what do I actually say out loud to close that door (the arc of the book, Geist, whether the dialectic is genetic or logical — I assume all of it)? And give me one question for the last five minutes, ideally about a case I haven't shown them, that would tell me they've got the structure rather than just the vocabulary — these guys are very good at pattern-matching my examples back at me.
