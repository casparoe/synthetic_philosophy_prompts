I write gameplay and simulation code — non-player-character AI, behavior trees and utility scorers, seeded procedural generation, save-and-branch systems — and I have been doing it for about twelve years, entirely self-taught. I have no academic background in philosophy. I arrived at this question through popular-level books about free will and divine foreknowledge that I read on my own and have been worrying at ever since, so please use my vocabulary freely and precisely, and assume I know nothing of the technical literature. I also have no way to chase down books, papers, or authors, so please don't point me at any: I want the reasoning itself, in my terms.

The thing I want translated is Molinism: the doctrine associated with the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina, on which God's knowledge divides three ways — knowledge of necessary truths, knowledge of what any free creature *would* do in any circumstances that creature might be placed in, and knowledge of what will in fact happen — with the middle item doing the distinctive work. God is supposed to consult that middle knowledge in deciding which world to create, subject to the constraint that God can arrange circumstances, place creatures, and offer grace, but cannot determine how a free creature responds, so that the range of worlds open to God is narrower than the range of worlds God can imagine.

Please restate that apparatus in the vocabulary I use every day: deterministic simulation with a seeded RNG; agents as policies over state; what-if runs and branch-on-save; oracles, lookup tables, and functions versus tables that have no function behind them; the difference between a run that is nondeterministic and an agent that is autonomous; and the asymmetry between the designer, who stands outside the simulation, and the agents, who are in it. Do it item by item — first the threefold division of knowledge, then the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, then the feasibility constraint on which worlds can be actualized — and for each item say what carries over exactly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no counterpart in anything I could actually build.

Two things I have invented for my own use, which I would like you to test rather than humor. I have been calling the central gap *the unsourced oracle*: an oracle that returns a determinate answer to every what-if question about an agent, where nothing in the engine generates, compresses, or explains the answers — a lookup table with no source. And I have been experimenting with a companion constraint, *the held-fixed agent*: the intuition that a what-if question about an agent is only well posed if the agent's own policy is held constant across the branches being compared. My worry is that the first of these is not a translation at all but simply the standard objection to Molinism — that these counterfactuals have nothing making them true — wearing my clothes; and that the second quietly does the work Molinism needs by sneaking in something like a nature or character that fixes the answer. Tell me whether either is doing real work, and whether a Molinist could reasonably reject them.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. In my vocabulary, *what would agent A do in situation S* is answered by running the simulation, and it is true because of the agent's code and the seed; there is no third option between *determined by the state* and *random*. So: is there any honest way to express *same circumstances in every respect, including the same seed, the same history, the same grace offered, and a different outcome* in my terms, or does mapping it onto nondeterminism already concede something Molinists deny? Does the designer's standing outside the simulation map onto anything in the doctrine, given that Molinism's counterfactuals are about creatures and not about God — and if there is no row in the table for what God would freely do, is that a principled feature of the view or a place where it runs out? And most importantly: where the translation fails, is that a limitation of my vocabulary — a framework built on the assumption that every counterfactual has a mechanism behind it — or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it sounds? I would rather be told the second than be flattered, and I would rather be told my analogy collapses than have it quietly repaired for me.
