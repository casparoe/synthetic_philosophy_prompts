Hey — I need a hand turning a half-baked idea into a two-page proposal for a one-year dissertation fellowship. It's due in a week and a bit, and it's in archaeology / philosophy of the social science-y bits of archaeology: what we can actually recover about institutions from the objects people left behind. Not beliefs, not trade networks, not diet — institutions.

My vague thought is this. Everyone in that literature seems to argue about underdetermination: one camp says an object's meaning is so context-dependent that you're basically reading a text you can't be sure you've got the language for, the other says get your middle-range theory right, work out the behavioural correlates of material patterns, and you can read action off the stuff. But I think both camps are arguing about the wrong target. Beliefs are hard because they're invisible and multiply realisable. Institutions are hard for a *different* reason: an institution isn't just a regularity in behaviour, it's a regularity plus people knowing that other people expect it, plus the standing possibility of somebody not going along with it. So the thing to look for in the record isn't evidence of the rule being followed — that's just a pattern, and any pattern has five other explanations — it's evidence of the rule being *designed against* being broken. Locks, seals, tallies, standardised weights and measures, things that were bent or broken or re-cut when they went wrong, boundary markers, containers built so that opening them leaves a mark. Nobody is ever going to dig up 'collective acceptance'. But a seal only makes sense if tampering was expected, and a standard only makes sense if somebody could have failed to meet it. Call it violation-sensitive design, or whatever better name you come up with.

Can you turn that into a real proposal? Concretely I need:

- The question stated tightly enough that it has an answer I could fail to get, and where the answer isn't going to be a shruggy 'yes, in some cases, it depends'.
- Why the standard fights don't settle it: why this isn't just the old equifinality / many-causes-one-pattern problem in a new hat, and why 'be more careful about context' isn't the upshot.
- What I'd actually do month by month over twelve months to make progress, given I'm not excavating anything and won't be in the field at all this year.
- What the deliverable is. I want something a working field archaeologist could pick up and use when they're writing up a site — a test, a checklist, a way of ranking interpretations, something with teeth. If the honest deliverable is 'a distinction that clarifies the debate', say so and defend that, but I don't want another paper whose conclusion is that inference is hard.
- The failure conditions, honestly. Two worries I keep circling on. First: if institutions mostly show up as silent, boring compliance, then the absence of violation-sensitive design is exactly what my hypothesis predicts — which means I may have built something unfalsifiable, and I want the proposal to either fix that or admit which parts it can't fix. Second: violation-sensitive looking design might mostly get produced by mundane non-institutional causes — animals, weather, user error, motor habit, copying your neighbour — and if that's most of it, the criterion is useless as evidence.
