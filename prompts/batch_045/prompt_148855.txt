The question was about Fermi-style arguments, where you estimate some big probability by multiplying a chain of rough factors — a terrestrial planet forms, life gets going, something intelligent shows up, a detectable signal gets built — and read the product as the probability of the whole conjunction. Part (a) asked what assumption licences the multiplication, and why "the steps are causally separate" doesn't quite give you that assumption. Part (b) asked whether it's right to say that if each pair of factors is only slightly dependent, the product is only slightly wrong. Part (c) asked what would justify the assumption, and how you'd rewrite the argument if you couldn't have it.

The answer in front of me says this. The assumption is probabilistic independence: P(A and B and C) = P(A)P(B)P(C), or equivalently that each factor's probability conditional on the earlier ones equals its unconditional probability. Causal separation doesn't give you that, because two factors with no causal connection between them can still be probabilistically dependent if they share a common cause — something like the general hostility of the local environment pushes every factor in the chain at once. Pairwise independence is also weaker than mutual independence, so checking the pairs isn't enough. Independence is the maximum-entropy distribution consistent with the individual estimates, so it's the least committal thing you could assume, and for that reason it's the cautious one: positively correlated factors make the true value larger than the product and negatively correlated ones make it smaller, and since we've no reason to expect either direction, the errors should roughly cancel across a long chain and the product is unbiased. On (b), yes: if the pairwise departures from independence are small, each conditional differs from its unconditional counterpart by only a small amount, and the total error is roughly the sum of those small errors, so the product remains a decent approximation. On (c) you'd want an actual mechanism story before leaning on independence, and failing that you can always rewrite the argument as a chain of conditionals, P(A)P(B|A)P(C|A and B), which is valid whatever the dependencies, and estimate each conditional by looking at the cases where the earlier factors are known to hold.
