You are a professional political philosopher who works on distributive justice and on the ethics of desert, and who also writes case material for a graduate seminar on merit, desert, and the allocation of offices. Work carefully and slowly. Before you commit to a scenario, spend one short paragraph brainstorming three candidate mechanisms for making merit-based and desert-based selection come apart, and say in a single line each why the two you reject fail the constraints below. Then give the finished case. Do not name, cite, quote, or allude to any author or paper: nothing in your answer should depend on a source, and a reader with no library should be able to check every step. Any mechanism you introduce should be given a plain name that you define as you go.

Task. Construct a single, realistic appointment case — one paragraph, an ordinary institutional setting such as a university department, a hospital, a firm, or a civil service — in which two standards for filling one position pick different people. Use no science fiction, no imaginary measuring device, no special stipulation about what the decision-maker can know, and no lottery. Let the two finalists be M and D.

The standards, fixed for the purposes of the exercise, are these. The meritocratic standard: the position should go to the candidate whose selection best serves the aims of the role, given the evidence available. The desert standard: the position should go to the candidate who has earned it, in virtue of what they have actually done. So the merit verdict is M and the desert verdict is D, and D's appointment would be worse by the role's own aims. Both verdicts must retain genuine intuitive pull at the end of your discussion; the case must not be one in which a proponent of either standard would simply change the subject.

Your case must satisfy all of the following.

1. All parties agree on all the facts, and the facts include that both finalists had genuinely equal opportunity and made comparable effort. The divergence therefore may not be generated by luck, by differences in effort, sacrifice, or cost, by one candidate's being needy or having been wronged, by differences in natural talent doing the work, or by anyone's lacking information. The institution's aims must be ordinary and must be held fixed throughout; the case may not work by making the role partly honorific or by smuggling reward into the job description.

2. State explicitly which desert base you are using — effort, contribution, sacrifice, or something else — and hold that base fixed across both verdicts. The divergence must not come from quietly switching desert bases between the two candidates.

3. Name the structural feature that does the work. It must be a feature of the relation between the candidates and the environment of the role, not a property of either candidate taken alone, and it must be interaction-sensitive in this sense: the merit verdict flips when you change something about the other people in the pool or on the team, while the desert verdict stays put. Call this the pool-swap test, and actually run it: describe the swap, holding fixed both candidates' intrinsic qualities, the job description, and the institution's aims, and then give both verdicts before and after.

4. Show that the case survives the three standard dismissals. First, the purpose reply: the desert verdict must not collapse merely on the grounds that the point of an appointment is not to reward anyone. Second, the institutional-desert reply: the desert verdict must not be defused by the observation that the rules of the institution create what is deserved, and you must block that reply without stipulating that the rule is new, secret, or unannounced. Third, the mask reply: the divergence must not be some third consideration — equality, need, utility, or fairness to third parties — wearing desert vocabulary.

5. Nothing in the case may depend on anyone's reacting to the selection rule. Assume the rule is announced in advance and that neither candidate and no third party adapts their behaviour in response; the divergence must survive full transparency.

Deliverable, under about 900 words, under these headings. Scenario. Verdicts, with the reasoning each standard gives and the desert base stated. The pool-swap test, run explicitly. Ablation: remove or invert the interaction feature, show that the verdicts converge, and say which verdict moves and by how much. The best attack: give the strongest objection available to whichever standard comes out worse, stated as forcefully as you can make it, and then the best reply to it. Honest note: say whether there is a real disagreement between the two standards here or whether one of them is simply underspecified in a way that dissolves the case, and say why.

If you cannot satisfy all five constraints jointly, say so plainly and identify the one you had to relax. Do not silently drop a constraint.
