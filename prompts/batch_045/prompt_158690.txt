I'm a journalist who write mostly about science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the wellbeing-research world — the happiness surveys, the "national wellbeing" dashboards, the whole evidence-based-happiness industry — and I keep hitting a philosophical wall in the middle of it that I can't write my way around. The wall is this: everybody in that world talks as if pleasant experience is one stuff you can count, compare and maximize, but when you actually ask what the pleasure of a cold beer on a hot day, the pleasure of finally getting a proof to work, the pleasure of scratching an itch, and the pleasure of hearing your kid laugh have in common, the answers come apart. The old-school answer is that they share a distinctive feeling, a felt hedonic tone you can just introspect. The answer that seems most popular now is that enjoying something just is something like wanting it to go on, being disposed to keep doing it, or treating it as good-for-you-in-the-moment — so there needn't be any shared sensory ingredient at all. And then there's a deflationary version I keep running into on the neuroscience side, where pleasure is whatever plays a common-currency role in learning and choice. Plus one option I haven't seen anybody defend and that I've been kicking around myself: maybe enjoying isn't an ingredient of an experience at all but a manner of undergoing one — not an added feeling but a way the experience is going for you, like the way a shape's being triangular isn't an extra part of it. Is that even coherent, and if it is, does it dissolve the heterogeneity problem or just move it somewhere else?

My hunch, which I'd like you to argue with rather than flatter: this isn't one dispute wearing three hats, it's several, and part of it is a plain empirical question about whether there's a shared felt signature, part is a question about what a theory of welfare needs its unifier to be (does hedonism require a felt quality, or is a functional or attitudinal unifier enough, and would the latter still be hedonism?), and part might just be verbal. But I could be wrong — maybe there's one hidden question doing all the work, namely what counts as data here: introspective report, third-person neural measurement, or the way people actually compare episodes when forced to. There's also the old gripe from the nineteenth-century utilitarians that pleasures are too different in kind to be summed against each other, which is basically where this started and which, as far as I can tell, nobody has answered or retired.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and definitely not a reading list: tell me what kind of stalemate this is, using what each of those accounts actually commits itself to, showing where each of them bites and where each fails (including my adverbial option, which I suspect is either incoherent or a restatement of the attitudinal view in fancy dress). Then tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or tell me honestly that nothing would and why. If it helps: my friend who works in affective neuroscience says her field quietly assumes a single valence signal and never defends it, and I've been wondering whether that assumption is load-bearing in the philosophical argument too, or whether it's just a lab convenience that philosophers shouldn't be leaning on.
