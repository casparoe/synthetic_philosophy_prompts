I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am working on a piece about the causal exclusion argument and what it would mean for the way criminal courts talk about intention. In the courts I have been reading about and observing, a recurring pattern shows up: defense teams are often allowed to introduce neuroscientific evidence—brain scans, genetic vulnerabilities, evidence of impaired impulse control—to argue for reduced culpability or a lighter sentence, and in insanity or automatism cases a defendant's conscious awareness or capacity for control is treated as the crucial factor that can negate liability. Yet the formal definition of the most serious offenses remains stubbornly mentalistic. To secure the highest grade of homicide conviction, for instance, prosecutors must prove that the defendant acted purposely or knowingly, not merely that a neural mechanism produced the bodily movement or that the brain was in some physical state. So the law appears to accept mechanistic explanations in mitigation while refusing to let those explanations replace the mental-state requirement at the core of the offense.

The apparent inconsistency is what I want to understand. The causal exclusion argument is what bothers me: if every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, then the defendant's conscious intention—assuming it is not simply identical to a neural event—looks excluded or redundant, because the neural machinery already did all the causal work and the conscious reason seems to be a byproduct. Please help me reconstruct the implicit theory of mental causation that this duplex pattern—neuroscience admitted for mitigation, conscious mens rea demanded for conviction—actually presupposes. Does the practice strictly presuppose that conscious mental states are causally efficacious in their own right, or is it underdetermined and compatible with several theories? In particular, I want you to distinguish three options: first, non-reductive physicalism, on which mental properties are realized by but not reducible to neural properties and do genuine causal work; second, epiphenomenalism, on which consciousness is a causally inert byproduct and only neural events cause behavior; and third, what I will call the answerability-substitute view, on which the mental-state requirement does no causal work at all but is retained because asking an agent what they were trying to do is the procedure by which we hold them answerable rather than merely predictable. For each option, say whether it fits the duplex pattern, whether it makes the pattern coherent, and what the practice would have to give up or change if it adopted that option explicitly. Finally, tell me what would have to change in courtroom practice if the causal exclusion argument were taken to show that only neural events are causally efficacious: would the gradations of purpose, knowledge, recklessness, and negligence collapse into a single neural-capacity criterion, or could the law keep its mentalistic vocabulary for reasons that the exclusion argument does not touch? I do not need case citations or a bibliography; just reconstruct the commitments and the alternatives from the description, and be explicit about where the law's practice is philosophically underdetermined.
