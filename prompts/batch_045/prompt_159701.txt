hey — I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas, I've been writing about AI for about four years and I'm finally trying to write an actual position piece on whether frontier models should be open, and the honest reason I've kept dodging it is that I suspect my own views don't add up. So here's roughly where I land, please tell me if it hangs together.

1) Default open: if a lab trains a frontier model, the weights go out. My gut is that this is just the ordinary scientific norm applied to a new object — knowledge gets published, and the burden of proof is on whoever wants to keep it in.
2) But I don't feel that way about everything. I think detailed, actually-replicable protocols for making a known respiratory virus more transmissible should stay restricted, and I've defended that in print. Some knowledge shouldn't be a download.
3) The reason I really care about openness isn't the science thing, it's power. I don't want three companies and a couple of governments deciding what everybody else's cognitive infrastructure is and isn't allowed to do.
4) On scrutiny: a lab grading its own model's dangerousness is like a drug company running its own trial. Nothing a lab says about its own system should count as evidence unless outsiders with real access can check it.
5) I don't want a licensing regime — no government vetting of who gets to run a big model, no permission slips, none of that stuff people keep floating in Brussels.
6) I do want labs legally on the hook for downstream harm from misuse of their models. Real liability, not a voluntary framework with a logo.
7) On risk: I'd put something like 1% on genuinely catastrophic outcomes from AI this decade, and I think that's worth taking seriously, even though I also think most of the public discourse is incentive-driven hot air. Those two feel fine together to me but maybe they aren't.
8) I don't buy "we'll get there first and then lock the door" arguments, from anyone, on either side.
9) A case I keep punting on: a while back a lab dumped a decent mid-sized model's weights online with basically no gatekeeping, I defended it at the time, and I'd still defend it. I've never been able to say why that one is fine if (1) and (7) are both true.

There's also a framing I've been toying with for the piece, which I'd rather have stress-tested than flattered: a single release buys two things at once — many eyes (outsiders who can audit, red-team, catch what the lab missed) and many hands (everyone who might misuse it). The old open-source slogan about lots of eyes making bugs shallow quietly assumes the eyes and the hands are the same population; with weights maybe they aren't, and maybe the ratio gets worse as capability rises. And a friend who works on reproducibility pointed out that publishing weights buys you almost no reproducibility anyway — no dataset, no recipe, no compute budget — so the "it's science" argument in (1) might be pointing at the wrong object entirely, while the argument in (4) does pick out weights specifically.

One more thing I go in circles on: if a dozen labs each independently conclude that releasing is maybe 60% likely to be net positive, then it's near-certain somebody releases, so "each lab should decide responsibly" stops being a decision procedure at all. I can't tell whether that's an argument for a coordinated norm — which I instinctively hate — or just an argument that (1) is doing less work than I think it is.

So: where does this set actually bite itself? Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, tell me which ones only look like tensions, and for each real one say what has to give and what it costs me. I'd also like to know which single revision would clean up the most. I'm not going to tell you which commitment I'd drop, because I genuinely don't know — that's the point of asking. Please don't cite papers or link anything, I can't check them from where I'm writing this, and assume I have no access to anything paywalled or published after early 2025. Write the answer in American English.
