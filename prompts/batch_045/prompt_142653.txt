hey — act as a comparative political scientist who's spent way too long on regime change and who actually enjoys conceptual arguments. I want a diagnosis of a stalled dispute, not a verdict and definitely not a literature tour (no citations, no reading lists — I've read the stuff, and I want your reasoning anyway).

In my department's democracy reading group we've been going around on "democratic backsliding" for literally two years and nobody has budged. Three camps:

The institutionalists say it's a genuinely distinctive process: elected governments using legal, incremental, individually-defensible measures to disable the referees — courts, electoral commissions, audit offices, the media regulator — while leaving elections formally open. On this view the object of study is the machinery that makes losing possible, not the balloting itself.

The skeptics say it's basically a partisan label. Every single measure on the institutionalists' list has a precedent in some country nobody calls backsliding; the term smuggles in an assumption that there's one correct institutional arrangement; and in practice it gets applied almost exactly to governments whose policies the speaker dislikes.

The third camp says both are staring at the wrong thing: it's polarized electorates and elite misbehavior all the way down, and if voters keep choosing these guys then the whole framing is elite panic with a social-science vocabulary.

Here's the concrete case I keep using, a made-up country: Vardania. Over eight years the governing majority (a) moves senior civil-service posts onto fixed terms renewable at ministerial discretion, (b) puts the audit office under a board elected by supermajority, (c) redraws constituencies inside a range a court already blessed years earlier, (d) makes groups recieving foreign money above a threshold register as "foreign-funded", (e) lets a new media council fine outlets for "imbalance", capped at a small share of revenue, (f) raises the signature threshold for forcing a snap vote. Every item has a precedent somewhere nobody calls backsliding. None of them makes an election unfree by the standard coding rules. After eight years the opposition needs roughly a nine-point margin to win a majority, and no incumbent has lost one.

Half my group says Vardania obviously backslid. The other half says: prove it — name the one item that does the work, and say what would change your mind.

My own hypothesis, which I don't fully trust: the first two camps aren't really fighting about facts or even about values, they're measuring different things. Institutionalists are measuring a disposition — what would happen if the incumbent actually lost — while skeptics measure the rules as currently written. And that disposition is nearly unobservable: the decisive test can in principle be run only once per country, and once you run it the case is over, so you never get the counterfactual. Which would make this an evidential-structure problem wearing a conceptual-dispute costume. But maybe I'm wrong and it's a straight fight about whether "democracy" names a threshold or a direction.
