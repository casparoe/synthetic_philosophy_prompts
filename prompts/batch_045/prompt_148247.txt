I am writing a short piece on what Gongsun Long, of the Warring States School of Names, would have made of a problem that could only arise once we had text-trained AI systems, and I want the extrapolation carried out from inside his own framework rather than from contemporary philosophy of language.

Here is the case. A system has acquired its entire vocabulary from text alone: it has never seen, touched, or heard a horse, and nobody involved in training it ever pointed one out, so everything it knows about horses is extracted from the way words sit among other words. It nonetheless passes every conversational test we can give it: it draws the horse-versus-zebra and horse-versus-white-horse distinctions correctly, tells us things about horses that we did not know and that later check out, and corrects our own misuses of the word.

My question: for Gongsun Long, does this system's word "horse" succeed in naming horses? Does the name (míng) ever get fixed to the actuality (shí) it is supposed to match, or is it a name whose entire content is other names — a case, in the terms of his discourse on designating and things, of pointing that never arrives at a thing? Note the tension I want you to resolve rather than smooth over: the working test the White Horse dialogue itself uses for correct naming is a test about seeking — ask for a horse and a yellow or black one will do, ask for a white horse and it will not — and by that test the system is impeccable, while by the requirement that a name be rectified against the actuality of what it names it seems to have nothing to be correct about.

Give me, in this order: "What is settled" (about 80 words) — what can be said about the dialogue and the name-and-actuality doctrine without taking sides; "The prediction" (about 250 words) — his verdict on the system, argued from his own commitments; "Where his commitments pull apart" (about 150 words) — which of his tests wins and why; "A variant" (about 80 words) — whether the verdict changes if the very same system is later connected to cameras and learns to pick horses out of images; "Contested ground" (about 120 words) — which reading of the dialogue your extrapolation depends on, and what someone who reads it differently would say instead.

Throughout, keep clearly separate what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely disputed, and do not present a contested reading as the standard one. No bibliography or citations; I want the reasoning, not the literature.
