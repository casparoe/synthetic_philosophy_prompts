Hi — I don't have any background in this stuff academically. I run operations at a company of about 300 people and I end up making a stupid number of little decisions: where things sit in the cafeteria line, what the default is on our benefits forms, whether the printer does double sided, whether the meeting-booking tool defaults to 30 minutes or 25. I've heard the word 'nudge' thrown around for this kind of thing. I make these calls by gut and my gut is all over the place, so I want to know if there's actually a rule behind it or if I'm just inconsistent.

My verdicts, going by gut:

- Fruit at eye level, pastries on the bottom shelf: fine, no hesitation.
- Printer defaults double-sided, meeting rooms default to 25 minutes: fine, barely worth thinking about.
- New hires auto-enrolled in the retirement plan, opt out with one click in the app: better than fine, I'd feel irresponsible not to.
- Pharmacy hands you the generic unless you ask: fine.
- Same retirement default, but opting out means calling a number during business hours and sitting on hold: not fine. Feels like a trap. But I can't say what the difference from the one-click version is, other than that it's harder.
- The tiny grey unsubscribe link: not fine. And if a charity did the same thing — made it a pain to cancel a monthly gift to a cause I believe in — I'd still say not fine, which makes me think 'is it aimed at the nudgee's own good?' isn't the whole test.
- Energy report comparing you to your neighbours: fine, though it gives me a twinge. The same report, but where the comparison figure was picked because it looked impressive rather than because it was true: not fine, even though it saves energy and the people are definately better off.
- Making the stairs nicer than the escalator: fine.
- Clear recycling bins so your neighbours can see what you threw away: not fine. It works, and it works better the more people notice, and that's exactly why I dislike it.
- A city tax on sugary drinks: I'm ok with it. A ban on large sugary drinks: not ok — even though a tax is pretty much a ban for someone with no money. This is the pair I understand least.
- Cafeteria defaults to the vegetarian option, mainly to cut the company's emissions rather than for the eater's health: I lean fine, with some unease.
- Savings plan where your contribution creeps up every year unless you say no each time: fine — and I notice I'm more ok with this than with the phone-call opt-out, even though both lean on inertia and this one makes you re-choose over and over.

The one I go back and forth on: HR keeps emailing people about the wellness screening after those people have explicitly said no thanks. They can delete the email, it costs them nothing, and some people presumably change their minds — but it feels like not taking no for an answer.

The rule of thumb I've been using is: it's fine if the person can still get the other option without much hassle, and if whoever set it up is aiming at the nudgee's own good. I don't think that survives my own list — it seems to let through things I hate and condemn things I'm fine with — but I don't know how to repair it.

One more thing, because it's been nagging me: I've started sorting these by 'grip' (how much it actually moves behaviour) and by what I call leakiness (whether it stops working once you point it out to people). The clear bins aren't leaky at all and I hate them; most of what I'm fine with would evaporate if someone explained it. So maybe leakiness is the wrong thing to care about, or maybe it matters in a way I haven't got straight.

Can you give me one principle — or at the very most two — stated tightly enough that I could run it on a new case myself? Say which of my cases forces each clause, tell me what it says about the wellness-email case, give me one new case it decides that I haven't thought of, and be straight about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you'd throw out. I can't look anything up, so please don't send me to read anyone or cite studies — just the rule. American English please, and keep it under 700 words.
