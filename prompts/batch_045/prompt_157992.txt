I've been chewing on the British abolition of the Atlantic slave trade (the 1807 Act) and then of slavery across most of the empire (the 1833 Act, in force from 1834, with compensation paid to the owners), and I want to run it through the public reason framework properly rather than doing the usual hand-wavy move.

Rough history as I understand it, which you can just take as given: the campaign gets going in the 1780s, Quakers allied with evangelical Anglicans, the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade founded in 1787, Wilberforce and Clarkson, the Wedgwood medallion, mass petitioning, and rhetoric that was very explicitly religious — slavery as a national sin, divine judgment, everyone made in the image of God — mixed together with humanitarian and economic arguments. The 1807 Act banned the trade but not slavery itself; 1833 abolished slavery in most of the empire by buying people's freedom off the people who owned them.

The lens: that family of views (Rawls-ish, plus the stricter versions associated with Audi) which says a coercive law is legitimate only if it can be justified by reasons all reasonable citizens can access, so leaning only on a comprehensive religious doctrine is normally out of bounds. Take both the strict version (no religious reasons in the official justification) and the looser one (bring them into public debate if you like, but you must also have adequate accessible reasons).

What I want, roughly in order:

1. A clean mapping. Who are the relevent actors — petitioners and voters, MPs, the campaigners, the West Indian slaveholders, the enslaved people themselves? And what exactly is the coercion? This is the bit I think people skip. Is the coercive act the passing of the 1807 Act, or is the ongoing enforcement of property in persons also coercion? If coercion is symmetric, then public reason seems to demand accessible reasons for keeping slavery too, and the slaveholders had some: property, contract, imperial stability, the compensation settlement for that matter. Does that symmetry point make the theory nearly toothless in this case, or does it do real work?

2. Stress-test a distinction I've been playing with: justificatory redundancy versus causal indispensability. A religious reason can be causally indispensable to a movement (without it nobody bears the costs of organising for decades) while being justificatorily redundant (there happen to exist adequate accessible reasons anyway). Public reason liberals mostly treat causal indispensability as irrelevant to legitimacy. I want you to argue both sides: is that dismissal defensible, or does it mean the theory quietly relies on there being some other group willing and able to act on accessible reasons alone?

3. My own proposal, which I suspect is broken — tell me how. Call it the justificatory debt: a citizen may permissibly vote or campaign from religious conviction now, provided the polity produces an adequate accessible justification for the resulting law within about a generation; if it never arrives, the law's provisional legitimacy lapses and the debt comes due. Applied to 1807 and 1833: was the debt paid, by whom, and roughly when? What about a religiously motivated law whose accessible justification never shows up — and does the proposal collapse into whatever the winners end up believing?

4. Does the episode vindicate the looser view and refute the strict one? Or does it show that public reason is a constraint on a deliberative process among citizens who are already free and equal, and that early nineteenth century Britain was nothing like that, so the whole apparatus just doesn't apply? Where, honestly, is the theory silent rather than being forced to speak?

Be concrete, and mark where you're guessing about the history versus where you're making a philosophical call.
