hey — I'm in a tiny reading group on alignment-adjacent stuff and we got stuck on something that feels much more like philosophy of action / epistemology than ML. The setup is HCH: you answer a hard question by breaking it into subquestions, answer those by breaking them up further, every node in the tree staffed by a copy of the same agent (human or model), with results flowing back up as short written summaries that have to fit inside a bounded context window. One person in the group reads this as an idealisation of *deliberation*: the tree is just what one mind's reasoning looks like if you write it out, the subquestions are steps, and the root's answer is the conclusion of an argument that the root has in some sense already made. Another says no, it's obviously an idealisation of *delegation* or consultation — the root is a manager who cannot actually check the work coming back to it, so the interesting questions are about trust, compression loss and oversight, not about reasoning. A third says both of you are wrong, the only thing here that deserves to be called an agent is the whole tree, and asking whether the root *knows* the answer is a category error. (For what it's worth, my own intuition kept flip-flopping depending on which example we had in front of us: splitting one long calculation into sub-calculations felt like cognition; splitting "should we sign this supplier contract" into "is the supplier financially stable" and "does the penalty clause bite" felt like delegation to colleagues.)

So before I read any more takes I want the spec. Write me the desiderata: what would have to be true of a distributed, recursively decomposed process for it to count as a single agent's deliberation rather than as delegation to, or negotiation with, other agents — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it can't contradict, and the consideration behind each requirement. Please don't just point me at papers, I want the criteria themselves and I can't chase references anyway. A few half-formed things I'd like you to either promote into real requirements or explain away: reconstructability (could the root in principle recover the reasoning behind a sub-answer, rather than just its conclusion, and is transmitting reasons rather than conclusions what makes something deliberation); a "merge test" I made up, where you fuse all the nodes into one agent with the same context bound but as much serial compute as the whole tree used — if the factored process doesn't beat that, the gain was parallelism and not decomposition, and maybe deliberation-talk was misplaced all along; a swap test, where a sub-node is replaced by a confident liar or a coin flip, and we ask whether the root's answer moves (and whether it moves *for the right reason* when a merely competent node answers differently); and the question of whether the copies have to be identical at all, given that if they are, I can't see what makes a sub-question easier or why the recursion bottoms out, while if they differ, it starts to look like a society rather than a mind. Also tell me which of the things people demand here are genuine desiderata and which are just prejudices of one camp — I suspect full transparency is a rationalist's taste and "must strictly beat any single node" is an engineer's — and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied; my guess is that amplification and reconstructability pull against each other, but I can't make it precise. Then, briefly, how the three readings I sketched (deliberation, delegation/consultation, whole-tree-as-agent) do against your list, and which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out.

Last thing: be explicit about where your own list is shakiest. Which requirement are you least confident belongs on it, and what would change your mind — specifically, what would convince you that the line between deliberation and delegation here is just a decision about where to draw the boundary around an agent, so that nothing substantive hangs on which side we come down on? That's the part I most want to see, because if that's right then our whole argument was definately about semantics.
