Here's the setup. Imagine you're advising a tiny philanthropic fund — call it the Humean Political Economy Revival Fund — that has enough cash to support about five junior scholars (postdocs or independent junior fellows) for two years each. The mandate is narrow: work out what a broadly Humean take on money and trade should say about present-day arguments, and figure out where the real open questions are. They are explicitly not interested in a survey, a bibliography, or a reading list; they want a funded research agenda, with priorities defended.

Start from the usual Humean package (no need to cite chapter and verse): money isn't wealth in the deep sense; trying to run a permanent favorable balance by piling up specie is at best pointless and at worst self-defeating; an imbalance in trade tends to set in motion changes in prices, wages, and demand that erode the imbalance; and one country's growing commerce is not automatically a threat to its neighbors — the envious, zero-sum view of trade is a passion to be overcome rather than a policy to be followed.

Now drop that into a 2025-ish world, which throws up a bunch of seperate complications Hume didn't have: the US dollar's reserve-currency status and the fact that lots of trade is invoiced and financed in dollars; giant cross-border capital flows into safe assets rather than just gold moving around; fiat money and central banks, plus newer stuff like stablecoins and central-bank digital currencies; government subsidies and export controls aimed at advanced semiconductors; friend-shoring, meaning deliberately routing supply chains through allied countries; sanctions as a routine tool of statecraft; and a political mood that treats the trade deficit as a scoreboard. So the Humean machinery has to be run on a world where the adjustment mechanism isn't simply gold draining out and prices falling, and where states are deliberately trying to manage strategic dependencies.

Which five or so lines of work would you fund, in priority order? I care much less about snappy labels than about the reasoning. For each, spell out: (1) what the open question actually is; (2) why it's upstream — what other questions or live policy arguments depend on settling it; (3) how tractable it is for a smart junior scholar with two years, a philosophy/econ-theory/political-economy background, but not necessarily heavy formal modeling or proprietary datasets; (4) how neglected it is relative to how important it is; and (5) what concrete evidence or result over the next couple of years would move it up or down your ranking.

Also be explicit about the criteria you're using to trade off importance vs tractability vs neglectedness vs upstreamness, and tell me what you'd deliberately deprioritize — things people argue about a lot that you think are downstream, mostly ideological, or unlikely to change much downstream if answered. Steelman the strongest version of each candidate line before you rank it.

Don't give me citations, footnotes, or a reading list; I want an agenda.
