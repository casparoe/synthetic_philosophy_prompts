I spent my career in population biology and have taken up moral philosophy since retiring, and I would like a second opinion on a passage I keep circling back to. A chapter on the ethics of food in a well-reviewed popular book summarizes the case against vegetarianism roughly like this:

"A vegan diet does not spare animals; it only changes which ones die. Plant agriculture kills field animals in enormous numbers — rodents, rabbits, and insects cut up by harvest machinery and poisoned by pesticides — and the published estimates of deaths per hectare of cropland are large enough that a person living on plants is responsible for more deaths than a person eating pasture-raised beef. The same arithmetic also settles the choice of meats: because a chicken is small, a chicken-eater must have far more animals killed to stay alive than a beef-eater must, so on the only measure that matters, the number of sentient animals killed, the beef-eater does considerably less harm than either the vegetarian or the chicken-eater. Ethical vegetarianism therefore rests on an empirical mistake about where the deaths actually occur."

My own back-of-envelope calculation appears to support it: if I score diets by animal-days of conscious life curtailed per kilogram of meat instead of by head count, the gap between chicken and beef narrows by roughly an order of magnitude but does not reverse. So I am not asking you to re-argue vegetarianism. What I want is an accuracy audit of that paragraph.

Please go through it claim by claim and separate what a philosopher working on this would grant, what is a simplification, and what they would reject. In particular: is the number of animals killed the right quantity for the argument to be scored by at all, or does the summary misdescribe what the view it attacks is trying to establish; how much of the crop-death claim is settled rather than contested; and does the conclusion about pasture-raised beef follow even if the death counts are granted in full? I would also like to know whether a point that occurs to me from my old field is already standard in this literature: that most field animals killed by harvesting would in any case have died within weeks to density-dependent starvation or predation, so that counting deaths may substantially overstate what is lost. If it is, does that consideration tell against the passage, or merely complicate it?
