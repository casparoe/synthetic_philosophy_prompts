I am teaching a seventy-five-minute seminar on Thomson's lamp in a third-year undergraduate course on paradoxes of infinity, and I want a plan for the session rather than an explanation of the puzzle itself. The group is deliberately mixed. Roughly half are philosophy majors who have met Zeno's dichotomy and Achilles, who have been told that the natural numbers and the reals have different sizes, and who have nothing more than that. The other half are mathematics majors who are fluent with sequences, series and limits and who will arrive inclined to treat the lamp as simply a question about whether a certain limit exists. Several students in both halves have already read the standard presentation and the familiar reply that the description of the task is consistent with the lamp's being on at the end, off at the end, or no longer in existence at all, and that it therefore fails to determine an answer. My worry is that they will treat that reply as a verdict that closes the topic and will file the lamp either under wordplay or under solved.

Please plan the session. In particular I want:

1. The order in which the components have to be introduced, and what each step depends on. Specifically: where the observation that the lamp's state is perfectly well defined throughout every interval strictly before the limit time needs to go, and why putting it there matters for what comes after; where to draw the distinction between the limit of the sequence of states failing to exist and the lamp's having no determinate state at the limit time; and where the question whether supertasks are possible at all should be raised, and how to raise it so that it is not prematurely answered.

2. The one case or variant that should carry the session. I have two candidates and would like you to pick one, or to say why both are needed and where each belongs. (a) The lagged lamp: the same set-up, except that the lamp responds to the switch only after a fixed delay, so that its state at any moment is fixed by the last switching that occurred at least that much earlier. (b) The finite-budget lamp: the same set-up, except that every operation of the switch dissipates some fixed minimum quantity drawn from a store that is exhausted after finitely many operations. For each, tell me what it isolates and what it does not: whether the difficulty lies in the infinity of the operations, in the assignment of a state at the accumulation point, in the physical description of the mechanism, or in the logical form of the description; and whether the variant settles the puzzle, dissolves it, or merely relocates it. Tell me also what the carrying example must lack as well as what it must have: if either variant smuggles in a premise the original set-up does not contain, name the premise and say what conceding it would cost the session.

3. The misconceptions to expect, and the point in the sequence at which to head each off. The ones I anticipate are: that since the durations sum to a finite total the task is straightforwardly completed and what remains is only an empirical question; that because there is no last switching there must be no final state; that the lamp ends up half on, or flickering too fast to see, or at some averaged brightness; that the parity of an infinite number of switchings, or infinity plus infinity, must make sense somehow; and, from the mathematics majors, that the non-existence of the limit of the states is the same claim as the absence of a determinate final state. Add any I have missed, and for each say whether it should be pre-empted before it can form, answered when it surfaces, or left standing to be taken up by a later part of the session.

4. What I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level, and say aloud that I am refusing: non-standard analysis and infinitesimals; the relativistic point that in some spacetimes an unbounded amount of proper time can elapse along one worldline within the finite past of another; machines or formalisms that carry computation through limit stages; and the wider literature of supertasks in classical mechanics. Give me one sentence on each, on why it would derail seventy-five minutes with this audience.

5. The check for understanding at the end. I would rather it not be an invitation to restate the underdetermination reply. Give me one question to put to the room, together with what a correct answer, a partially correct answer, and a merely verbal answer would sound like, and what each would tell me about whether the idea has actually landed.

Be concrete about how the seventy-five minutes are divided, and mark the places where the session should slow down.
