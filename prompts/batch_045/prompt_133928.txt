One submitted answer, transcribed verbatim with the student number removed:

**(a)** Suppose I have been certain of *e* for years, so *P*(*e*) = 1, and a new theory *T* comes along that deductively entails *e*. The stock example is the advance of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity: the data were in hand decades before the theory was. Intuitively *e* confirms *T*. But on the Bayesian model confirmation is a matter of conditionalizing on the evidence, and

*P*(*T* | *e*) = *P*(*T*)*P*(*e* | *T*)/*P*(*e*) = *P*(*T*) · 1/1 = *P*(*T*),

since *T* ⊨ *e* gives *P*(*e* | *T*) = 1 and my certainty gives *P*(*e*) = 1. So conditionalizing does nothing: the ratio *P*(*T* | *e*)/*P*(*T*) is exactly 1, and no confirmation accrues. The difficulty is generated by the two assumptions I have just used together, strict entailment and certainty. Where a theory does not entail *e* but merely makes it probable, we compare *P*(*e* | *T*) with *P*(*e* | ¬*T*), and the likelihood ratio *P*(*e* | *T*)/*P*(*e* | ¬*T*) can exceed 1 even when I am certain of *e*, so the merely probabilistic case escapes. The problem is therefore a problem about strict entailment and about the ratio measure of confirmation; it does not threaten Bayesianism as such.

**(b)** The first strategy changes the language. My certainty in *e* is certainty about *e* as expressed in my old language; it need not carry over to the enriched language in which *T* is formulable. So I add to my language a sentence expressing the logical connection between them — the material conditional *T* ⊃ *e* — assign it a prior strictly between 0 and 1, and then, when I sit down and work through the derivation, conditionalize on it: *P*(*T* | *T* ⊃ *e*) > *P*(*T*). The trouble is that I now have to assign priors to indefinitely many such sentences, and it is hard to see what could constrain them; the resulting "confirmation" then looks like an artefact of my own logical laziness rather than a relation between the theory and the phenomenon.

The second strategy changes the prior instead. Let *P* be the credence function I would have had before I learned *e*. The confirmation *e* confers on *T* is the shift from *P*(*T*) to *P*(*T* | *e*) — that is, to my actual present credence in *T* — and this can be positive even though my real credence in *e* is now 1. The objection is that *P* is indeterminate: there is no fact of the matter about what I would have thought had I not known *e*. Had I not known the perihelion figures, would I even have had the conceptual resources to formulate *T*? The counterfactual prior depends on how the counterfactual situation is filled in, so the strategy replaces one arbitrary number with another.

**(c)** Neither succeeds: the first makes confirmation depend on ignorance, the second on a counterfactual state that nothing fixes. And these are the only two options, since a Bayesian has just two things to adjust, the language or the prior. So the old-evidence problem is best read not as a puzzle inside Bayesian confirmation theory but as a reductio of it: what confirms a theory is that it *explains* the evidence, and explanation is not a probabilistic relation.
