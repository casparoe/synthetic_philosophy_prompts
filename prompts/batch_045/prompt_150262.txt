I am preparing the closing lecture of an undergraduate seminar I teach on ethics and film, and this year I want to end with Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life, read against the cluelessness objection to consequentialism: the worry, roughly, that for most of the things we do the downstream consequences are so numerous, so chaotically sensitive and so far beyond our epistemic reach that we cannot tell, even in expectation, whether acting makes things better or worse, and that this is not a remediable failure of inquiry but a standing feature of the situations we are in. My hope is that the film does something the journal literature cannot: it gives the problem a face by showing us, in the Pottersville sequence, exactly the thing the objection says we never get — a view of the total difference one life makes — and then withdrawing it again. What I want from you is a close reading of the film in those terms, scene by scene where it matters: the boyhood rescue at the frozen pond and Harry's later service as a pilot, the capsules Mr Gower would have filled in error, the run on the Bailey Building and Loan, George's bridge-side wish that he had never been born, and the final gathering in the Bailey parlour. Before I write the lecture, I would also like you to work with three distinctions I have been playing with and have not seen drawn quite this way. The first is between what I will call sign-cluelessness (we cannot tell whether the net effect of an act is positive or negative) and magnitude-cluelessness (we can tell the sign but have no grip on how large the effect is): Pottersville seems to me to deliver spectacular magnitude knowledge but arguably no sign knowledge, since George is shown a world without himself rather than a world in which he had simply acted differently, which raises the second distinction — between the total-effects comparison a guardian angel can perform and the contrastive comparison (this act versus the nearest alternative actually available to me at the moment of acting) that consequentialism requires. Third, I want you to take up what I have been calling counterfactual completeness: the film hands us one fully worked-out downstream branch, closed, legible and shaped, whereas the objection trades on the openness and branching of causal chains, so I need to know whether the film is quietly cheating by giving causal chaos a narrative form. And, more mischievously, whether Clarence's own intervention is subject to the very same problem: he acts on George's behalf on the basis of a guess about how George will respond, and it very nearly goes wrong.

Where I expect you to push back, and where I want you to, is on the moments that resist the reading. Mr Potter strikes me as a foil worth taking seriously: he optimises what is visible, legible and enforceable at short range, and the film surely means us to see that as a moral failing rather than as an epistemic virtue, yet from the standpoint of the objection his shortsightedness is not obviously worse than George's long-range blindness. The ending is harder still: the town's rescue of George is immediate, measurable and certain in a way nothing in his own life ever was, and I cannot decide whether that vindicates the case for staying local or quietly concedes that we need visible payoffs to keep acting at all. Then there is the film's declared moral about the man who has friends, which I suspect amounts to abandoning consequentialism rather than answering it — substituting a good that is supposed to be knowable from the inside, without any view of total effects, for one that is not — and I want that verdict argued rather than asserted. My colleague in film studies, who has heard an earlier version of this lecture, says that treating the picture as an epistemology of action strips it of its sentimentality and turns a melodrama into a seminar handout, and I would like to know where she is right and how much of that objection I should concede in the lecture itself.

What I would finally like is your sense of what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not, together with a recommendation of the two or three scenes you would actually put in front of students to make the point, each with a note on what you would say about it, and an honest statement of where the reading fails and the film simply is not about cluelessness at all. Please write in continuous prose, lecture-ready where possible, in British English, and without any citations, bibliographies, further reading or verbatim quotation from the film: I want the reading to stand on the plot as it is commonly remembered and on the shape of the argument, since I will be speaking from notes in a room without any way of checking sources.
