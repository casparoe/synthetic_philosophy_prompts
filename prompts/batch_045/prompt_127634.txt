ASYMBOLIA AND THE BADNESS OF PAIN (draft, v rough)

1. The setup. Assume that there are patients who, after certain brain lesions, are like this: they can still tell a pinprick from a burn, they can still say where it is and how strong it is on a scale, they still wince or withdraw to a degree — but the pain no longer bothers them. They report the sensation, in roughly the same words they'd have used before, while insisting that it doesn't trouble them; some of them laugh or carry on talking through it. Call them asymbolics.

Everyone reaches for this case, because it looks like a natural experiment. There are roughly two camps about what the hurting of pain consists in. One says: to be in pain is in part to be moved, to be impelled to stop it or protect the part; the hurting is that motive force. The other says: pain is a state with representational content — it represents a bodily condition as bad for you, or issues something like a command to protect the body part — and the hurting is a matter of that content being there. Asymbolia seems to let you unstick the components and see which one carries the badness. I want to argue that it does much less than that, and then argue for something positive that neither camp will like: even on the strongest reading of the data, pain can be bad for a subject who doesn't mind it at all.

2. The deficit argument. The inference people run is: the lesion removes X, the subject keeps the rest, so X was a separable component and the rest is the core. But removing something can remove a modifier, an enabling condition, an amplifier, or the mechanism by which a system reads its own output — none of which need be a constituent of the conscious state. Take the lighting out of a room and you lose the ability to see the painting, but illumination is not a part of the painting. So the dissociation data licenses at most the claim that whatever went missing is causally necessary for the normal total state. It doesn't license "and here is the sub-part where the badness lives". Relatedly: if the aversive oomph is produced downstream of the lesioned area, then the oomph may be an effect of a normal pain state on other systems rather than a piece of it — and the effects of a state aren't components of the state, any more than the badness of nausea includes the vomiting.

3. Three things that could be missing. The reports are ambiguous between at least three levels: (i) the sensory-discriminative character — location, sharpness, burning, rated intensity; (ii) immediate unpleasantness — the nastiness that is right there in the experience; (iii) distress and bother and all the downstream stuff — the wanting it to stop, the agitation, the autonomic and motivational response, the I-can't-take-this. Asymbolia might be loss of (iii) with (ii) fully intact; or loss of (ii) with (i) intact; or a partial dimming of (ii). Verbal reports of the it's-still-there-but-I-don't-mind shape are compatible with all three, because "I don't mind" may only be a report about (iii). If that's right then asymbolia gives no support at all to the idea of pain without unpleasantness, and a lot of the argument about whether pain is essentially bad has been riding on an ambiguity in "don't mind".

4. Occupancy cost. Grant the strongest reading anyway: asymbolics have (i) with no (ii) and no (iii). Does it follow that their pain is not bad for them in any way? Only if all experiential badness is unpleasantness, and I want to resist that. Intense sensations have a feature that is independent of how they feel in the hurting sense: they monopolise attention. Suppose conscious attention has a limited bandwidth, so that you cannot fully attend to many demanding things at once. A loud, insistent, unignorable sensation takes up bandwidth, and in taking it up it forecloses the experiences, thoughts and activities the subject could have been having instead. Call the cost of that foreclosure occupancy cost. It's the same family of cost as not being able to hear yourself think in a noisy room — except that I'm claiming the cost can be present even when the noise doesn't bother you, because what is lost is the room that the other experiences would have occupied.

Why is this a welfare cost rather than a causal fact? Because it's a way in which the subject's mental life over that stretch is going worse: narrower, more monopolised, less theirs to direct. Someone absorbed in a gripping novel is not worse off for being absorbed. Someone every waking minute of whose day is filled by an insistent sensation they cannot direct away from — even a not-unpleasant one — has, I claim, lost something that bears on how well things are going for them. The anticipated objection is the resonance one: prudential badness has to be capable of mattering to the subject, and a cost that the subject neither minds nor would mind on reflection is an objective-list intrusion, not a welfare cost. My reply is that the subject can register the cost without minding it — it shows up in what they can't do, and in what they'd take to be rid of it. There are recognised welfare bads that the subject never minds, like being deceived about something you'll never discover. Those are diachronic and occupancy cost is synchronic, but I don't see why that should matter to whether something is a cost, only to how easily we notice it.

5. A test, and a wrinkle. Prediction: offer an asymbolic a free pill with no side effects that removes the sensation for the rest of the day. My view says they should take it — not because it hurts, but because it occupies. If they refuse, I'm in trouble: that suggests the thing supposedly lost isn't a cost to them at all and I've smuggled in a preference for unoccupied consciousness that isn't theirs. If they accept while insisting they don't mind, that's the signature I want: a cost the subject registers as a reason to act with no unpleasantness anywhere in the story. The wrinkle is that the motivational theorist can grab this too. They'll say that taking the pill just is the motive, so the affective-motivational dimension never went missing and only the unpleasantness did. That's survivable for me — I don't need unpleasantness to be part of pain's essence — but it does mean the pill test may not discriminate between my view and theirs, and I'm not yet sure how much that bothers me.
