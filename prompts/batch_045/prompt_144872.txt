I'm writing a novel from the point of view of a narrator who keeps insisting that anything true about her little town could in principle be found out — her thing being that there's no fact of the matter about the town beyond what some investigator could in principle dig up, which is the antirealist flavour I want in her voice. A friend who reads a bit of philosophy says this is dead on arrival: Fitch's paradox shows that if every truth is knowable then every truth is known, so my narrator is committed to already knowing everything about the town, which is obviously false — and what Fitch really proves, he says, is that there must be unknowable truths, so if I wanted her to sound antirealist I've got it exactly backwards. Something there feels off but I cant tell where. Doesn't the argument need her to be making the claim about all truths whatsoever rather than just about the town, and doesn't it also lean on things like knowability distributing over conjunctions and knowledge being factive? And does it matter that she never says who could find it out, or when — just 'could be found out'? Basically: is there a version of the line I can keep, or is my friend right and I should cut it?
