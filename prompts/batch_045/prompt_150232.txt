My friend and I got into a huge argument last night about how anyone can know anything about what happened thousands of years ago, and we were still at it at 1am and got nowhere. She did history at uni, I did compsci, and the fight was about the actual moves historians make when they read an old source — not about philosophy names, which neither of us really has. I don't think either of us had the right vocabulary for what we were arguing about, so I want you to reconstruct it properly. Please don't give me reading lists or citations — I want the argument, not sources.

Here's the cluster of moves I can't get straight. These are all things I think historians really do; if one of them is a cartoon, say so.

1. You discount a source for having an interest: a chronicle written for the winning side, a memoir by someone who was there and had a reputation to protect, a speech written up by a partisan. Bias makes you believe it less.

2. But historians also reach for the opposite move: if a source says something that makes its own author or side look bad, or that cuts against the point the author is obviously trying to make, that gets treated as especially good evidence. Same property — the author has an agenda — and it seems to flip the sign of the inference depending on whether the claim helps or hurts that agenda.

3. And then there's the weird one, where we really got stuck. Historians cheerfully use a source as evidence for things the source isn't about. The absurd totals Herodotus gives for the size of Xerxes' invasion force aren't used as a headcount, they're used as evidence about what a Greek could believe about scale, or about how numbers of that kind got transmitted. Thucydides more or less tells you up front that the speeches in his history aren't transcripts — I'm told he says something like "my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions" — and historians still mine those speeches for what happened. Meanwhile the offhand stuff, a price, a route, a name dropped in passing, something the author clearly assumes without arguing for it, is treated as the most trustworthy part of the document precisely because the author wasn't trying to tell you that.

4. Counting: ten later sources that all copy one lost text get treated as one witness, not ten. And silence: sometimes "if that had happened, somebody would have mentioned it" is treated as close to decisive, and sometimes it's treated as worthless, because nobody concludes that peasants didn't exist from the fact that the chronicles barely mention peasants.

My friend's line — and this is the bit I probaly mangled, so sharpen it or kill it — was that all of this makes sense once you stop treating a source as a channel that carries a claim to you and start treating it like a measuring instrument with a known systematic error: you don't throw out the reading, you calibrate and subtract. On that picture a source with a big but consistent and legible distortion is more informative than a careful neutral one whose errors you can't guess, and the incidental details are just readings taken at a setting where the bias doesn't reach. She then said something that sounded completely insane to me, which is that by her own account an outright propagandist should be a better source than a neutral chronicler, and she wouldn't back off it. I said the obvious thing, that a source is just a noisy channel with some reliability rate and you discount by reliability and average over independant reports, and we went round in circles for two hours.

So:

- Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the package. What view of testimony, or of evidence, does it only make sense under — is the source a channel, an action somebody performed, an instrument, a sample of a lost population of documents, something else? Give me the theory in a form where the four moves above fall out of it, not as a list of rules.
- Which moves strictly presuppose that theory, and which merely fit it well and would be licensed by other theories too? Be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined and several theories would generate the same behaviour.
- Is the package coherent, or are these several rules that happen to live together? In particular: is move 2 the same rule as move 1 or a different one, and what does the theory say when they pull opposite ways — an author whose interest, at that moment, is served by admitting something damaging, a self-criticism that buys credibility, a confession that's really a boast?
- The propagandist question. Does the practice actually commit you to her conclusion, or does something in the theory block it? And if it's blocked, what does the blocking — the size of the distortion, how hard it is to calibrate, the fact that a propagandist covers less of what you want to know, the risk that a distortion isn't stable across topics?
- What would actually change on the ground if you did replace it with my channel-and-reliability-rate picture? Not "the conclusions would differ" but: what would a historian stop doing, start doing, or have to do differently tomorrow — which moves become unjustified, which evidence they'd have to give up, what they'd do instead when sources conflict. Give me one concrete case where the two pictures tell you to do different things.

I'd rather you told me which of my premises are wrong than answered the question I think I'm asking.
