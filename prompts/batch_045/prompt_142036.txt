Hi — I'm 71 and retired: 34 years as an analytical chemist, mostly measuring trace metals in estuary sediment, which taught me to distrust any number whose error bars are smaller than the argument someone builds on it. Since I stopped work I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the library on Tuesday mornings, and for my sins I also sit on our town's Energy and Climate Advisory Committee. We owe the Select Board a recommendation by Thursday and I'd like you to draft the memo I circulate beforehand. Please keep it plain: about 900 words, a To/From/Date/Re header, short paragraphs, no footnotes, no bibliography, no "studies show". If a number matters, attribute it to our own town inventory or just flag it as my estimate.

The case. Our town (14,000 people, 30 km from the nearest real city) can apply to a state pilot, the Community Pathways Pilot, but only to one of its two tracks:

Track 1, Price and Dividend: a town-set carbon price, revenue returned as an equal per-household dividend, starting at 60 dollars a tonne and rising 15 a year. Catch — the pilot rules only let a town price electricity and natural gas. Transport fuel is explicitly off limits.

Track 2, Sufficiency First: a five-year binding cap on a throughput basket — vehicle-kilometres on town roads (freight and light duty separately), new heated floor area, and municipal solid waste — falling 6 percent a year, no offsets, no trading with anyone outside town, and the target has to be written into zoning and permitting so it actually binds.

Rough shape of our inventory: 96,000 tonnes CO2e a year, about half of it transport. A third of that transport half is one logistics park plus through-traffic on the state highway that cuts through us — trucks that mostly neither start nor finish their trips here. Buildings are the rest: electricity about a fifth, gas heating about a quarter, waste a few percent. Two things you need to know. First, we're already in a municipal renewable-electricity aggregation, so the electricity slice of our inventory is something close to an accounting fiction; the marginal emissions happen somewhere else on the grid. Second, median household income here runs about 8 percent under the state median, 22 percent of us are over 65, and a good number of those older households are in drafty houses heated with gas.

So the choice is ugly in a very specific way. Under Track 1 we can't touch the biggest half of our emissions at all, the modelled cut over five years comes out around 9 percent of the territorial inventory, and nearly all of the cost lands on home heating in old houses — a price on old people, as Priya (she teaches high school economics, and is half my age) puts it. Under Track 2 the binding constraint falls on the logistics park: 340 jobs, near a fifth of our commercial tax base, main lease up in fourteen months, and we recieved a letter from their lawyers saying they'd look at a site four km east over the town line. Global emissions would be flat at best and probably worse, since that site is further from the interchange. Meanwhile 900-odd residents commute to the city and our bus service was cut in 2019.

Marv, who ran demand-side programs for the utility before he retired, says a price is the only instrument that finds the cheap reductions instead of guessing at them, and that a cap we invent is just central planning with a conscience. Priya says the warehouse is the entire point and that everything else is theatre. I'll admit my own bias: I'm the one who dragged the degrowth material into the reading group, and I find the sufficiency framing attractive, which is exactly why I don't trust my instinct — those 340 jobs are people I see at the grocery store.

Three things I specifically want the memo to do, beyond picking a track with a date attached:

One. Do the arithmetic and say plainly that neither track will change global temperature by anything we could measure, and then tell me what the actual reason for the choice is supposed to be. My reading group came close to a fistfight over whether an agent who can't make a difference has any obligation at all, and I want that argument used, not gestured at. If the answer is that a pilot matters because of what it demonstrates, or because of who bears a burden and who merely benefits, or because a vote is the legitimate way to decide something a price would quietly decide for us — say which, and say why that reason beats the others here.

Two. I've been pushing a rule I call the "both-books" rule: we report the territorial inventory and a consumption-based one, and we're judged on whichever is worse, on the grounds that otherwise every town just exports its way to a target. Tell me whether that belongs in the recommendation — and be honest about whether we could compute the consumption side at all. For a town this size I'd guess 30 percent error bars, and I'd rather commit to a clumsy number I can defend than a pretty one I can't.

Three. Give the option you're not recommending its strongest paragraph, not a version I can knock over. I have to vote alongside people who like it. And tell me what would falsify your own recommendation: what result, looked at in year three, should make the board say we picked wrong.

Marv floated a middle thing — auction a shrinking number of freight-vehicle permits at the logistics park and hand the revenue to residents — which sounds like a cap with a dividend bolted on and might be what gets four votes. If that's what you'd recommend, recommend it, but say plainly whether it's coherent or two instruments duct-taped together.

Put the recommendation in your first sentence. Then, after everything else, add one final line on its own that reads exactly \answer{Track 1}, \answer{Track 2}, or \answer{Neither}, so I can pull it out mechanically when I tally how the committee came down. Thanks.
