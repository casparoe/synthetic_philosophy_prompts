I'm drafting a book chapter on housing and climate politics, and I want to use generational language without being sloppy about it. Before I commit to it, I would like an audit of the position I have ended up with. Here are the commitments, as plainly as I can state them.

(1) Cohorts are real in the way that matters for explanation. People who come of age in the same institutional and technological environment, within the same developmental window, are shaped by it in similar ways, and the resulting cluster of durable dispositions — about work, risk, institutions, technology — is what "a generation" picks out. It is not a mere label: there is a shared causal history behind it, the sort of thing that would make it a causally unified cluster rather than a filing category. My strongest case is something like a cohort that entered the labour market during a severe recession and stayed measurably more risk-averse for decades afterwards.

(2) The conventional scheme is not a discovery. A generation every fifteen to twenty years with boundaries at round years is a journalistic and marketing artifact; the published ranges disagree by a year or two and get quietly revised every few years. Where I would put a boundary depends on which formative exposure I care about, and different exposures peak at different times.

(3) I accept that you cannot read cohort effects off a single cross-section. Differences between age groups at one moment confound age, period and cohort, and the decomposition is not identified without extra assumptions; once you impose plausible ones, the cohort component is usually smaller than press coverage implies. I take this to constrain my evidence, not the world — it means the evidence has to be longitudinal, not that there is nothing there.

(4) Generational labels are also identities. Once a label is in circulation, being classified under it affects how employers and landlords treat you and how you understand yourself, so the classification partly produces what it appears merely to describe. There is a wrinkle here I keep returning to: the labels usually arrive late, applied retroactively to people whose developmental window has already closed.

(5) I am untroubled that different inquiries need different schemes — a consumer segmentation, a political-socialization cohort, a fertility cohort — cutting the population at different places. I take real kinds to be available at different grains.

(6) Practically, I still want to write about boomers and Gen Z in public-facing prose, loosely, because the labels do rhetorical and coalition-building work that a careful description such as "households that bought before the price shift" does not. I do not think this compromises (1).

(7) I want to use generations as a unit in arguments about justice — who captured housing wealth, who inherits the climate — on the ground that generation membership is fixed at birth and unchosen, which makes it the right sort of property to treat as morally arbitrary and to compensate for. I say this knowing that the variation within a cohort, on the very thing at issue, is larger than the average difference between cohorts.

What I would like: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look as though they are, and for the real ones exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me in explanatory or political reach. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I do not know where the problem is and I have no preferred thing to drop; if the answer is that (1) must be weakened to something I could defend in a footnote, tell me what that weaker claim would be.
