I run a small mountaineering company with eight guides, and I have to make a disciplinary decision by Friday that I would like your help thinking through. Two of my guides, on separate trips this past season, each skipped the weather and snowpack check that our protocol requires before a glacier crossing. It is not an exotic breach: my honest guess is that roughly one crossing in five goes unchecked, and nearly all of the time nothing at all comes of it. This season something did come of one of them. On one crossing a client fell into a crevasse on the descent and broke a leg; the other crossing was uneventful. I suspended the guide whose crossing went badly for a month without pay and gave the other a written warning.

His objection is simple. He says I am punishing him for how the world turned out rather than for what he did; that the two of them did the same thing with the same disregard; and that the difference in their treatment is therefore arbitrary. I feel the force of that, and yet I cannot let go of the sense that what actually happened matters, and I have to be able to explain myself to him rather than just outrank him.

Three complications, which I suspect are where the real difficulty lies. First, the harm was itself only probable: our own incident review puts the chance of a serious fall on an unchecked crossing at something like one in fifteen, so even the fact that there was an injury, as opposed to merely a risk of one, is partly a matter of how things fell out. Second, the guide who got off lightly is, if anything, the more careless of the two over the years, judging by informal complaints I have received; so the outcome is not the only evidence I have about the two of them, but it is the only evidence I acted on. Third, one of my partners has offered what she takes to be a decisive reply: every guide signs a handbook stating that breaches are disciplined with reference to their consequences, if any; everyone knows the base rates; so the expected cost of skipping the check was already priced into the decision to skip it, and the suspended guide is not being singled out at all — he is simply the one whose number came up in a lottery that all eight of them voluntarily entered. I find this clever, but I cannot tell whether it answers the complaint or merely restates it in actuarial language.

There is one more thing I want held up to the light. I do not credit guides for uneventful crossings; nobody gets a bonus for the harm that did not occur. If I am willing to let a bad consequence aggravate the penalty, is my refusal to let a good consequence mitigate it defensible, or does the symmetry of the case condemn both?

Tell me what you think I should do, and what I should say to him, and show me what the disagreement actually turns on rather than only where it lands. I would also like you to distinguish, as you go, between the parts of your answer that rest on points that are settled or close to uncontroversial among people who have thought hard about cases of this shape, and the parts that are genuinely contested; I would much rather be told that the crux is disputed than be handed a confident line that merely looks like the consensus. Please write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a considered letter to someone who has asked for real help.
