I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I want a one-page handout that lets a student locate herself in the dispute between Nozick-style tracking accounts of knowledge and the closure principle (roughly: if S knows that p, and competently deduces q from p, thereby coming to believe q, then S knows that q). Build it as a decision tree drawn from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of labels: six branch nodes, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which view it leads to, and ten to twelve leaves.

Requirements on the nodes. One must separate sensitivity from safety, using a lottery case (your true belief that your ticket lost is safe but not sensitive). One must separate single-premise from multi-premise closure, using the zebra case: you see what looks like a zebra in the pen, and the question is whether knowing it is a zebra requires knowing it is not a cleverly disguised mule. One must separate context-shift from stakes-shift: does the truth of an ascription of knowledge move with which alternatives are conversationally relevant, with what is practically at stake for the subject, or with neither (use the familiar low-stakes/high-stakes bank pair). One must ask where a closure failure is located: in the method by which the belief is formed, in the content of the knowledge relation itself (a ternary relation taking a contrast), or in the standards an ascriber sets. One must ask what the subject is to do with a skeptical hypothesis: exclude it, treat it as properly ignorable, or treat its pull as an artifact of the seminar room. The remaining node should be the methodological one, whether closure is a datum any theory must accommodate or a theorem a theory may deny — the methodist/particularist choice in Chisholm's sense — since this, more than the closure data itself, predicts where someone ends up.

Leaves must include: original Nozickian tracking with closure denied; closure denied while the deduced belief is still warranted rather than known; relevant-alternatives contextualism; subject-sensitive (interest-relative) invariantism; classical invariantism with a safety condition; contrastivism; and the skeptical leaf. I also want one leaf for a hybrid I cannot find anyone holding: tracking relativized to a contrast, so that S's belief that p rather than q tracks the fact that p rather than q. Place it wherever the tree puts it and mark it as occupied or empty. Every leaf gets its name, the path that reaches it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Structure your reply in four titled sections, in this order: Branch nodes (about 300 words), giving the six questions in order with the routing out of each written as which node a yes or a no leads to; Leaves (about 300 words), a numbered list with name, path, and the awkward commitment; Empty cells (about 150 words), the answer-combinations your six nodes permit that no position occupies, each with a one-clause diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; Collisions (about 100 words), pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, with the further question that would separate them. Do not cite or recommend any reading anywhere in the reply; the handout has to work for a student with no library access.
