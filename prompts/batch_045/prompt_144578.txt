**Question 3 (20 points).** Frederick Douglass publicly reversed himself on the U.S. Constitution. Explain (a) the position he held while he was allied with William Lloyd Garrison, (b) the position he adopted after his change of mind, (c) the central argument he offered for the later position, and (d) what the change meant for his political strategy. Your answer should make clear what *kind* of argument Douglass took himself to be making about the meaning of the Constitution — what he thought he had to show, and what he thought was beside the point.

**Student's answer.** Douglass began his public career agreeing with Garrison that the Constitution was a pro-slavery compact — in Garrison's memorable phrase, a covenant with death — and on that basis he supported disunion and refused to vote, on the ground that acting under such an instrument made Northerners complicit in slavery. Well before the Civil War, however, he announced that he had changed his mind, and for the rest of his life he maintained that the Constitution, properly read, is an antislavery document. His case rested on the intentions of the Framers. He argued that the men who wrote it never intended to give slavery any federal guarantee: they accommodated it only as a temporary necessity, expecting it to die out, and they deliberately kept the words slave and slavery out of the text, using general language instead — persons held to service, the clause about migration or importation — so that the document would not be, in its own terms, a charter for property in human beings. Because the meaning of the Constitution is fixed by what its authors intended to secure by it, the proslavery reading imposed by the courts, above all Chief Justice Taney's opinion in the Dred Scott case, counted simply as a departure from the original meaning, and Douglass could set it aside as a mistake rather than as evidence about what the instrument says. The practical upshot was political: if the Constitution guarantees no right to hold property in man, then abolitionists can work through the ordinary institutions of the Union — voting, party politics, holding office — instead of calling for its dissolution, and Douglass accordingly threw himself into antislavery political action and later supported the Reconstruction amendments. Indeed, on his own showing the Thirteenth Amendment was legally superfluous: if the Constitution never sanctioned slavery, then slavery had no federal footing for an amendment to remove, and the amendment merely recorded, for the record, what the text had meant all along.
