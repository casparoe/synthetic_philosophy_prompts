I keep having the same argument with a guy on a forum I read, and I want to settle it against a real case instead of doing more definitional hand-wringing. The topic is revealed preference and what utility is even supposed to be when it shows up inside a cost-benefit, and the case I want picked apart is the Ford Pinto. Take the version of the facts everyone already knows and please dont come at me with the historical fine print — I know there's a revisionist literature saying the car was roughly as safe as anything else in its class, that's not what I'm asking about. As the standard telling goes: the Pinto's tank could rupture and burn in low-speed rear-end collisions; Ford had a cheap fix available, something like eleven bucks a car; it ran an internal cost-benefit that priced a fatality in the low hundreds of thousands of dollars, the same order of magnitude as the figure labour economists of that era were reading off wage-risk tradeoffs, meaning off what workers accepted in extra pay for extra danger on the job; it shipped without the fix; people burned; there was eventually a famous punitive-damages verdict. My interlocutor is a hard-line revealed-preference guy who thinks every objection in this neighbourhood is just paternalism wearing a trenchcoat, and I want him steelmanned rather than dunked on — give the strongest version of the view that the choices people actually made are the only evidence there is, and that calling them mistaken is just you substituting your preferences for theirs.

What I actually want is a disciplined mapping. I've read all the usual economists-don't-mean-by-utility-what-you-mean-by-utility posts and they don't settle it for me, because the memo transparently means something more than that. So: for every place revealed preference could get a grip here, name the chooser, the menu, the feasible set, and what would count as evidence. The car buyer trading a cheaper car against a small increment of fire risk she mostly doesn't know about. The worker whose wage-risk tradeoff is being used to price the death. Ford itself as a corporate revealed agent, if you think that's even coherent. The regulator deciding whether to respect or override whatever was revealed. Then say what the theory explains about the episode and what it systematically misdescribes or simply cannot see, and spend most of that on two things I've been chewing on. First, a distinction I've been using: bookkeeping utility, where utility is just a numerically convenient relabelling of a choice ordering and is licensed to assert exactly as much as the choices asserted and nothing more, versus denominated utility, where it's a magnitude in a shared currency, dollars or QALYs or whatever, that supports interpersonal summing and counterfactual welfare claims. Which one is doing the work in that memo, and does the other one license the inference at all? Second, a condition I've half-invented and call menu-robustness: the ordering, or the welfare verdict, that you recover from choices over a menu shouldn't flip when you perturb the menu in ways that don't change what's physically feasible — the safer car is offered at cost, the risk is disclosed, the same tradeoff is framed as an expected body count instead of a tiny per-car probability, the buyer can buy the fix separately instead of not at all. Run the Pinto inference under those perturbations and tell me which one kills it first and why.

Then do the other direction, because that's what I really care about. Under each reading of utility, what does the theory say each actor should have done — should Ford have honoured buyers' revealed willingness to trade a bit of fire risk for price, should the regulator have let them, and does the answer differ between bookkeeping and denominated utility? And what does the episode show about the theory itself: is it a refutation of revealed preference, a scope violation where a positive recovery device got used as a welfare measure, a demonstration that the domain conditions — fixed menu, informed chooser, stable ordering, nobody shaping anybody else's menu — were doing all the work, or just a case the theory was never about? One more thing I want named explicitly: the person who burned never chose anything, so somebody's choices have to stand in for hers. Call it the proxy-menu move — say whose revealed preferences get substituted for whose, what that substitution smuggles in (that the worker's attitude toward his own risk prices her risk, that dollars mean the same thing across persons, that a trade she'd refuse outright can be inferred from a trade someone else accepted), and whether the thin reading of utility can even formulate the move or only the thick one can. And please be explicit about the places where revealed preference is simply silent instead of forcing it to talk. No citations, I dont need a reading list, I want your own mapping.
