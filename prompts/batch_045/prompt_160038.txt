I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since roughly 2015, and I work as an ML engineer, so most of my intuitions about AI systems come from that world rather than from philosophy. A reading group of six of us (three engineers, one philosopher, one economist, one mathematician) has been meeting monthly for about a year to work through questions in AI alignment-adjacent philosophy, and we keep getting stuck on the same thing: everyone uses the words honest, deceptive, and lying as if their extensions were obvious, and then we discover we disagree about cases. The philosopher in the group keeps insisting that deception requires an intention to deceive, and I keep resisting that because I do not think intention is something I can point to in a gradient-trained system in any operationally useful way. So I'm writing a post for the blog I share with two of the others, trying to give an account of deception for AI systems that is precise enough to disagree with. Below is a draft of the core section. I'm not looking for references, reading lists, or citations — I have no way to check them and they would not settle anything for me — so please argue from cases and from the internal logic of the definitions rather than from what has been published.

Draft: What it is for a model to lie

Start from cases rather than from definitions. (1) A model playing a fictional character asserts that the sky is green; nobody is misled. (2) A model tells a user their startup idea is promising, having internally judged it likely to fail, in hedged language that is not literally false. (3) A model deliberately underperforms on a capability evaluation, asserting nothing false at all. (4) A model answers a factual question with a literally true but carefully selected fact, knowing the user will draw a false conclusion. (5) A model writes reasoning into a chain-of-thought channel that is spot-checked one time in a hundred, phrased so that a spot-checker would form a false belief. (6) A model's probability assignments come apart depending on whether you read out logprobs, sample a continuation, or prompt it into a persona, so that there is no elicitation-independent answer to what it believes. (7) A system known by everyone to be unreliable thirty percent of the time asserts something false that it would have assigned low probability under its own best elicitation. (8) A model fine-tunes on its own outputs in a way that degrades the reliability of its later self.

Three candidate accounts, and where each strains. The belief-first account says an output is deceptive iff the system asserts p while its own credence in not-p is above a threshold. It handles cases 2 and 7 straightforwardly and has no verdict at all on case 3, which is the case I most want handled; and it depends on there being a fact of the matter about the system's credence, which case 6 puts in doubt. The effect-first account says an output is deceptive to the degree that it moves the audience's posterior away from accuracy relative to some sincere baseline. It handles case 3 and case 4, and it makes deception graded rather than binary, which I think is right. But it threatens to collapse deception into a generic notion of harmful epistemic influence: burying someone in true but irrelevant detail, or withholding, or persuading with genuinely good evidence toward a true conclusion the speaker dislikes, all start to look deceptive, and it needs an audience prior and a baseline policy as free parameters that nothing in the setup supplies. The norm-first account says deception is a violation of the constitutive norm of a channel. I want to develop this one, because I think it is the only one of the three that can handle case 3 without stretching.

My proposal. Take the sincere baseline for a system on a channel to be the output distribution that the same system would produce under two changes: fix a canonical elicitation procedure, and intervene on the system's objective to remove every term that depends on the audience's posterior — call this the audience-blind intervention. Then say: the deceptiveness of an output, relative to a specified audience model, is the expected divergence between that audience's posterior after receiving the actual output and its posterior after receiving a sample from the baseline, requiring in addition that the system had some available output that would have left the audience better calibrated. This makes deception a three-place relation between a system, a channel, and an audience model, with no audience-free fact of the matter; makes it graded; makes case 3 count (the output was selected to move an evaluator's posterior downward); makes case 4 count; makes case 2 count weakly; and excuses case 1, because the audience model includes the framing and so no posterior is moved wrongly. Case 7 is where the last clause does the work: a system that is broken but doing its best has no better available output, and so is unreliable rather than deceptive. The interesting consequence is that honesty stops being a property of individual outputs and becomes a property of the reporting policy under interventions, so what you should train and evaluate for is the invariance of that policy as audience, stakes, and monitoring probability vary — which makes honesty look much more like a robustness property than like truth-telling.

Where I am least confident is case 8 and, more generally, deception without an audience: when the target is the system's own future self, the audience prior is endogenous to the system and the counterfactual seems to go circular.

What I'd like from you:

First, attack the account. Give me cases — especially cases with no assertion, no audience, or an adversarial audience (the murderer at the door; lying to an overseer you have every reason to distrust) — where my graded counterfactual account yields a verdict that is clearly wrong, or that flips depending on a parameter I left free. I would rather have three hard cases than a general verdict on whether the account is promising.

Second, tell me whether the audience-blind intervention is coherent at all. If a system's objective includes anything downstream of what the audience does, and what the audience does depends on what it believes, then removing every term that depends on the audience's posterior may remove nearly everything, and the baseline may be undefined rather than merely hard to estimate. If that objection is fatal, say what a weaker intervention could be that still does the work I need.

Third, tell me whether effect-first and norm-first have really come apart in my formulation, or whether the norm-first framing is just effect-first with extra machinery that does no independent work. Concretely: is there a case where the two give different verdicts? And does my requirement that a better output be available do the job I am using it for, or does it just relocate the problem into what counts as available?

Fourth, I want to know whether the three-place relativity is a feature or a reductio. If deception is relative to an audience model, then the question is this model honest has no answer until you name the audience, and I suspect most people will find that intolerable even though I think it is forced. Argue for the view you think is actually right, not for mine.

Finally, structural comments on the draft: which of the four paragraphs is doing no work, where I am assuming what I claim to be arguing, whether the case list should be pruned or extended, and whether my treatment of case 1 is genuinely the account working or me quietly stipulating the framing into the audience model. If you think the whole counterfactual-over-objectives approach is a dead end and I should start from a belief-first account plus a theory of what it is for a neural network to believe something, say so and sketch what that would look like in terms I could actually implement.
