I am drafting a paper on the epistemology of moral testimony, and before I look at how anyone has handled it I want the specification: what would an account of moral testimony have to be able to do in order to count as adequate for the purpose I actually have in mind.

The purpose is institutional rather than merely biographical. I need an account good enough to tell a body that has to act — a hospital ethics committee, a research funding panel, a legislature writing rules for systems whose moral status is not settled — whether, how much, and in what form the moral judgments of designated others count as reasons for that body. An account that only sorts individual beliefs into justified and unjustified is too weak for me; one that only licenses action is too strong. So part of the specification I want is a view on what the object of assessment is: belief, action, understanding, credit, or several of these at once, and whether one verdict can be expected to cover all of them.

Please give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark each one as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a preference of one camp. Among the cases I want covered, and I expect you to add ones I have missed:

- Layered deference. Someone trusts a marine biologist that a certain fishing method kills fish slowly by suffocation, that this matters morally, and that it is therefore wrong to buy that fish. Where exactly in that chain does the familiar reluctance attach, if it attaches anywhere? An account should locate the layer rather than treat deference as one thing.
- Novel moral kinds. Testimony that a class of beings — insects, collectives, artificial systems — has moral status, addressed to someone who lacks the conceptual repertoire to assess the claim. Can testimony transmit a moral concept, or only a verdict expressed in concepts one already has?
- Constitutive rather than reportive deference. A person defers to a community or a friend about whether she has been wronged, where the deference seems partly to make it the case rather than merely to track it.
- Diachronic deference. Trusting your own earlier or later self's moral judgment, in writing or memory. Is this the same phenomenon with the speaker held fixed, or a different one?
- Deference at scale and under near-zero comprehension. A donor or official defers to a research group about which intervention to fund, where the stakes are large and the deferrer's grasp of the reasoning is close to nothing. Does the discomfort track stakes, comprehension, or neither?
- Credit, and no double counting. An agent who acts on moral testimony seems to get less credit, or a different kind of credit, than one who gets there herself. State the constraint precisely, and say whether an account may hold both that the deference is epistemically fine and that the deferrer gets full credit for being morally well tuned.
- The discrimination test. What feature is present in the moral cases and absent from aesthetic, prudential, mathematical, legal, and etiquette testimony? If there is no such feature, say so, and treat that as a result about the list rather than as a gap to be papered over.
- Conversion speed. Someone who reverses a long-held moral view on the basis of one conversation, versus someone who shifts over years of exposure. Is what bothers us epistemic, agential, or both?
- Moral disagreement among testifiers. Does the account reduce to a general epistemology of peer disagreement, and if not, what does it add?

Two further constraints. The account should not smuggle in moral skepticism or relativism without arguing for it; and it should either remain neutral between internalism and externalism about moral judgment or say plainly which one it forces. It should also handle deference to groups and institutions, not only to individuals.

Three things beyond the list, in descending order of importance to me. First, flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I expect the interesting part is that some pair, perhaps the demand for a principled moral/non-moral distinction and the demand that the account follow from an ordinary epistemology of testimony, pulls apart, and that the list itself then forces a two-tier answer, pessimistic at the level of belief and permissive at the level of action. Second, tell me which desiderata are genuine requirements and which are really the prejudices of one side of the dispute, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts. Third, name the empirical or conceptual finding that would settle the dispute if we had it — what would have to be true for pessimism to be correct.

Then, briefly, score three or four families of view against the list — an understanding-first pessimism, a skepticism about moral expertise, a symmetry view that treats moral testimony as testimony like any other, and any hybrid that splits belief from action. Label the positions rather than naming authors; I want no citations, no bibliography, and no literature survey. Prose, with a short summary table at the end, and keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring.
