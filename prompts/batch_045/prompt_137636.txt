I'm a third-year in a normative ethics seminar and I also coordinate a student mutual-aid fund on campus — we hand out small emergency grants (rent, dental work, a bus ticket home, replacing a stolen laptop) from money that comes mostly from student-government dues plus a few outside donors. We get around twenty requests a week, each one has to be answered inside 48 hours, and the people actually deciding are me and two other undergrads who are exhausted and doing this at 11pm between problem sets. Anything over $250 needs two of us to sign off, and changes to the written policy have to go past student government, so whatever we adopt has to be explainable to them and to donors in plain language. Last week in seminar we did the demandingness objection and it basically wrecked the group chat: one person said that if the maximizing version is right then the fund should be telling all of us to give until we're at roughly the level of the people we're helping, and that this is absurd enough to sink the theory; someone else said that objection only shows that a rule which eats the very capacities and relationships you need to keep following it is self-defeating, not that the theory is wrong; and a third said the whole thing dissolves once you distinguish doing your fair share from picking up the slack for everyone who isn't doing theirs. I don't need that argument settled in the abstract — I need to turn whatever survives it into something my team can actually run, forty times a month, without re-deriving it from scratch each time.

So: please write me a decision procedure, as a checklist or a flowchart in words, that takes a consequentialist starting point seriously but has a real answer to the demandingness worry built into it rather than bolted on. Concretely I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (including what to do when we just can't get a number), the point where the procedure stops with a no, and the point where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values and not facts — and I want those two kinds of step labeled as such, because I can already tell which parts are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. It has to be runnable in about ten minutes on a one-page form by someone with no philosophy, and it has to be stable week to week: a rule that ratchets upward every time a new request lands in the inbox is one we will quietly stop following by November, and I'd rather have a rule we can actually stick to than an optimal one we abandon. Three or four things I keep hitting and don't know how to write into the form: (1) the slack question — almost nobody else on campus gives anything, so are we setting the bar at our share of the total need, or at whatever actually needs doing given that no one else is doing it, and is it suspiciously convenient that the people choosing the bar are the same people who pay it? I half want a test like "what would this policy look like if an auditor who disliked us applied it to us," but I don't know if that's principled or just a guilt device. (2) Whether our own stuff — coursework, relationships, sleep, the fact that we're doing this for free — is a side-constraint that caps what can be demanded of us, or just another item to weigh against a grant, because the two give very different answers when a local food bank tells us that every extra volunteer hour is a measurable number of meals. (3) The re-ask problem: the same few people apply every month, and a rule that treats each request as a fresh opportunity never stops, while a rule that caps them feels like it's punishing people for being poor in the same place twice. (4) Whether the procedure is even allowed to spend money on keeping itself going, or whether that's just self-indulgence with a nicer name.

Then please run it on three cases so I can see if it holds up. First: a request for $400 for emergency dental work, arriving in a week when we have about $2,000 left and twelve other pending requests, and no way to verify more than what the applicant wrote on the form. Second: the food bank asks us to supply volunteers, and the question on the table is whether we start asking our own members to go from two hours a week to eight during finals, given that the extra hours really do convert into meals and that two of our members are already borderline burned out. Third — and this is the one where I already know I won't like the answer — we have $300 and have to choose between spending it on a thank-you dinner and hoodies for our volunteers (which reliably stops people from quitting, and we have lost volunteers to exactly this) or giving it out as one more grant. For each, show the procedure actually crunching through its steps and where it terminates, and be straight with me if the one I'm dreading comes out against me. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: which of these questions it's really just relabeling, where it will systematically misfire (I'm especially worried it will end up funding whoever tells the most vivid story in person, since that's what makes a demand feel binding), and what we should do in the first month to find out whether we're following it or just rationalizing whatever we were going to do anyway.
