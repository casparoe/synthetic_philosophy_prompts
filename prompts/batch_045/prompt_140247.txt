I've been defending a strong version of the Dennett-style competence-without-comprehension line, which I'll call comprehension minimalism: there's no further fact about whether a system really understands beyond what it can reliably do, so once a system matches a competent human across the board — surviving any test we can actually run — there's nothing left for the question but does it really understand to be asking. Tell me what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I care much more about the defeaters a committed minimalist would concede than the ones a critic would shout.

Three things I want you to take seriously and rule on separately:

1. Error shape. Suppose a system matches human accuracy on a task but its mistakes are flat: it can't flag its own errors without external feedback, its failures don't cluster where human confusions cluster, and it can't be taught by a single counterexample the way someone who grasps the concept can. Does that count against minimalism, or does the minimalist just say the competence was narrower than the benchmark implied and move on?

2. Translation. Give the same problem in a novel notation, an unfamiliar formalism, or an inverted framing. Human understanding survives re-description; this system drops to chance. Same question: evidence of missing comprehension, or merely evidence of one more missing competence?

3. The origins-to-structure slide. Darwin's and Turing's inversions are stories about where competence comes from — selection, or training, producing competence with no prior comprehension. But in a creature that already has a mind, comprehension may well be causally upstream of performance even if it wasn't upstream of the competence's origin. If that's right, the inversion only shows that comprehension is dispensable in the explanation of origins, not that it's nothing over and above competence. Is that a genuine defeater for my view or a change of subject?

Two more things. First, has minimalism been qualified into unfalsifiability? Every behavioural difference you produce gets absorbed as proof the competence wasn't complete yet, so I want an honest read on whether the view risks anything at all, and whether that insulation is a special problem for a view that's supposed to be empirically motivated. Second, what does it predict that its rivals don't — is there any observation that should surprise a minimalist? And for what it's worth, the two things that would move me personally are a case where a system has all the competence including the second-order stuff (self-correction, explaining, teaching, re-describing) and we still think it doesn't understand, and a clean argument that comprehension without competence is possible; say which of those is more likely to actually be available, and why.

Please answer in British English, and keep it fairly tight — no need to summarise the literature.
