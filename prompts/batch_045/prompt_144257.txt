I'm giving the toast at my doctoral advisor's retirement dinner, and I'd rather not improvise it. She spent close to forty years on the function debate in philosophy of biology, defending a Cummins-style causal-role account and arguing that etiological or selected-effects accounts smuggle a history into physiology that physiology does not need. Her positive move — the one she used to try to absorb her opponents rather than just shout at them — was to insist that a selected-effects function is nothing but a causal-role function with the containing system widened from the organism to the lineage; she called it the lineage-as-system move and claimed it recovers everything the other side wants, including some grip on malfunction, without ever asking what a trait was for. Her two pet cases carried the argument: what she called windfalls, traits that contribute to a capacity of their bearer right now but have no selection history at all (her example was an organ thrown up in a single generation by a freak mutation that happens to be immediately useful), and what she called relics, traits with an impeccable selection history that now do nothing for the organism that has them. Physiology, she said, should count the windfalls as functional and the relics as not, and if a theory cannot say that, so much the worse for the theory. About people she was the same way: she winced at teleological talk, and in an email about the dinner the only thing she asked for was that nobody say she was born to do this or that the department needed her.

So the toast has to praise her without a single sentence that requires an etiological reading: she can be said to have done things, made things possible, held up a capacity of a department or a field, but not to have been meant for any of it, not to have fulfilled anything, and — because her own views make the ascription of a function relative to a chosen explanatory project — I don't want any line that quietly assumes there was one right description of her career that everyone in the room can see. The room is split: half are philosophers of biology who will catch every reference and will be listening for whether I've gotten her view right, and half are her family — her brother, who is a cardiologist and who has been on the receiving end of her jokes about hearts making noises, her granddaughter, and two old field assistants who knew her mainly as the person who could identify everything in a tide pool. The next speaker is her former student, now a fairly hard-line selected-effects theorist, with whom she had a long argument that neither of them finished; the toast should not ambush him or restart that argument, but it also shouldn't dodge it, and I'd like the disagreement to come out as a kind of loyalty rather than as a wound.

What I want from you: the text of a five-minute toast, in my voice, that is warm rather than a lecture, that includes at least one joke built on the function/dysfunction material that works for both halves of the room and lands as affection, and that does its philosophical business structurally — the view should be shaping what the toast can and cannot say, not decorating it. Make the choices rather than listing options: pick one reading of the causal-role/etiological distinction and commit to it, and pick the moments where the toast leans on her view for its emotional payload. Then add a short note, separate from the toast, telling me which contested reading you've committed to, where the toast bends her view in order to be kind, and one place where I — who have never been fully convinced, since I think the etiological accounts really do handle dysfunction better — might reasonably object to what you've had me say. Give me your reasons in each case; don't hedge by surveying the alternatives and leaving me to choose.
