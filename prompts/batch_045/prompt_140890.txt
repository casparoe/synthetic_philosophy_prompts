I'm in a small graduate reading group on distributive justice, and we recently spent a session on the priority view (the paper of Parfit's on equality and priority, plus a handout on weighted social welfare functions). I have to write a short response paper on it, and a friend from the economics department — who is sharp but has only encountered this material secondhand — emailed me his summary of the view so that I could tell him whether he had it right. I genuinely can't tell which parts to trust, because the bits I suspect are the bits I'm least sure about myself. His summary ran like this:

"The priority view says that benefits to the worse off matter more: a unit of well-being is worth more the lower down a person is, where being lower down just means having less than other people. So it's really the difference principle in a formula — what you maximize is the position of the people at the bottom. It's still an adding-up view underneath: you attach a weight to each person's well-being and sum, which means it's utilitarianism with the numbers quietly adjusted, and it inherits utilitarianism's problems — it can justify sacrificing one person for the sake of many, and it has no real account of rights. The decisive objection is the levelling-down objection. If the worse off are the ones who count for more, then a world in which the better off are dragged down to the level of the worst off, without anyone gaining anything, is in one respect better than the unequal world, and that is absurd. No one has answered this, which is why the views people actually defend now are sufficiency views and luck egalitarianism rather than prioritarianism."

My own instinct is that the levelling-down part is misapplied, since in a pure levelling-down case nobody is benefited and the weighted total simply falls — but then our group cooked up a case that made me less confident. Suppose well-being is measured on a single scale and the weighting function is of the form g(w) = −1/w, so that a policy is assessed by the sum of g(w) across everyone. Take 500 units away from someone at 1,000 and deliver, wastefully, just 1 unit to someone at 1, destroying 499 units in transit. As far as I can work out the sum still goes up (the beneficiary gains 0.5, the payer loses only 0.001), so the view appears to mandate a transfer that annihilates almost all of what it moves; with a flatter weighting like the square root it rejects the same transfer, and with g(w) = log w it comes out almost exactly indifferent. My friend says this shows prioritarianism is committed to levelling down in practice after all, and that the honest conclusion is to go back to plain total utilitarianism.

Would you go through his summary piece by piece and tell me what a careful prioritarian would accept as fair, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — distinguishing, where it matters, the priority view from both maximin-style views and from egalitarianism proper? And then, on the case: does it vindicate the levelling-down objection in a nearby form, or is it a different objection that merely looks like it, and does it arise only because I picked a weighting function whose marginal priority is unbounded as well-being approaches zero, or would any nontrivially concave weighting generate some scaled-down version of the same result? I'd also like to know whether prioritarians themselves impose any principled constraint on admissible weighting functions that would rule cases like mine out, and whether, once the summary is corrected, there's a real residue here that would be worth the second half of my response paper — or whether my friend's final conclusion simply doesn't follow from the parts of his summary that survive.
