I teach a fourth-year seminar, cross-listed between philosophy and mathematics, on constructivity in logic and mathematics. About half the class has done a serious proofs course and a course through Gödel's theorems; the other half has only first-order logic and one philosophy of mathematics module. I'm writing the final, and I need one question on intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism that cannot be answered by reciting the two things everyone memorises in week one: that intuitionists reject the law of excluded middle, and that a proof of A → B is a construction that carries proofs of A to proofs of B. Every year I ask something about excluded middle and every year I get the same page back, with BHK underlined, a paragraph about computer programs, and Brouwer invoked as an authority, and I cannot tell from it who has understood anything. So I want the question to hinge on a crux that a memoriser walks straight past.

My plan is to anchor the question in a made-up open problem, so that nothing about it can be looked up or recalled. Call a positive integer *balanced* if it can be written as a² + b³ for integers a and b (negatives allowed, and the search bounded by n, so balancing is decidable), and let the Balanced Numbers Conjecture, BNC, be the claim that every integer above some threshold is balanced. The question will stipulate that BNC is open: no proof, no refutation, no counterexample found by search, and no one has shown it independent of standard arithmetic. (The number theory can be nonsense; the conjecture is a prop, and I want it to stay a prop.) The students then have to say, precisely, what someone working in intuitionistic logic is entitled to assert about BNC and what they are not: why failure of excluded middle is not the same as asserting a negation, why the doubly negated disjunction is available while the disjunction is not, and what a proof of BNC ∨ ¬BNC would have to deliver if the logic has the disjunction property. I also want them forced onto the meta-question rather than allowed to survey it: is the classical-intuitionistic difference a difference about the meanings of the connectives — proof-theoretic, in the spirit of the manifestation arguments associated with Dummett and the inferentialist tradition — or a difference about what mathematical objects there are and what it takes to establish that they exist? And is there something awkward about exhibiting intuitionistic logic inside a model theory built with classical set theory?

I have four hunches about where the crux lies, and I want you to treat them as hunches. First: the framing "intuitionists deny excluded middle" is not just crude but wrong in a way that matters, because intuitionistic propositional logic proves the double negation of A ∨ ¬A, so what is missing is the step from ¬¬A to A, and I want the marking scheme to make a student who writes "they deny LEM" lose marks for a specific error rather than for tone. Second: what carries the real content of "constructive" is not the missing schema but the disjunction and existence properties, and a top answer should use those to say what a proof of the disjunction would have to give you. Third, and here I am genuinely unsure: because BNC is Π₁-shaped, I have a vague sense that Markov's Principle and the admissibility of Markov's Rule in Heyting arithmetic should matter to what can be asserted, but I can't reconstruct the argument cleanly and I don't know whether it belongs in the model answer or only as a possible flourish for the strongest students. Fourth: I suspect that restricting double negation elimination to atomic formulas quietly collapses the whole system back into classical logic, which would be a neat way to show that the disagreement is not about the atoms; if that is right I want it used, and if it is wrong I want it left out.

What I'd like from you:

- The exam question itself, self-contained and phrased for students (90 minutes, in class, hard cap of 1,000 words), with the stipulations about BNC built into it and with no wording that hands over the crux.
- A model answer of 700-900 words that does the work rather than naming the positions, and that takes a stand on the semantics-versus-ontology question instead of listing both options.
- A marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points: the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Include an explicit note on what a name-dropping answer — BHK, Brouwer, Dummett, Kripke models, no argument — should actually score, and what single move separates an A answer from a solid B.
- A short covering note, addressed to me rather than to the class, doing two things. First, separate what is settled from what is contested among people who work on this: settled formal facts about what is and is not derivable in intuitionistic propositional or predicate logic, versus live disagreements about what the formal results are supposed to show. Second, tell me plainly which of my four hunches is right, which is wrong, and which is a minority view I would be embarrassing myself by building an exam around; I would rather rewrite the question than set one that rewards a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
