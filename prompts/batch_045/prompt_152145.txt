hey, hoping you can settle an argument I keep having with a friend. She runs a tiny repertory film-and-lecture series, so she's always deciding whether to show work by people who did bad things, and she'll also just flat out say whether she thinks the thing is still any good. Her rulings so far, as best I can recall:

- a 1930s studio comedy whose lead was privately a raging antisemite, politics nowhere in the film: screen it, still a great film, no asterisk.
- a 70s art film where the director pressured a young actress into a scene during the shoot and that scene is the one everybody praises: not screening it, and she says it can't be appreciated innocently once you know.
- a landscape painting bought with money from a fraud the painter ran on the side, painting has nothing to do with the fraud: still a good painting, hang it wherever.
- a novel that lifts whole passages from a friend's unpublished manuscript, and the lifted bits are the best writing in the book: still a good novel, but it isn't his novel, she'd only do a reading if the program note said so.
- a living sculptor with a big museum retrospective who years ago trashed a rival's studio; the work is abstract and unrelated: she went to the show, she'd screen a documentary about the work, but she won't pay him a fee or let him do a live intro.

Now the new one. A novel from 1962 that basically everyone agrees is a masterpiece. Letters have surfaced showing the author got the material for it by manipulating her younger sister over several years, drawing out her confidences and then building the book's central scene out of the sister's worst humiliation, with names and details changed so thoroughly that nobody, including the family, can spot it. The sister is alive, ninety-ish, and said in an interview that she finds the book's fame a continuation of the betrayal and doesn't want it celebrated. Author died in 1970; the royalties now fund a foundation that gives scholarships in the author's name.

What's my friend going to say: is it still a great novel, and would she build an evening around it? I care less about the verdict than about which rule she's actually running, because I think at least three fit her track record equally well and come apart right here: value only takes a hit when the flaw shows up in the work or in what appreciating it requires; or value takes a hit when the work is partly made out of the wrong; or value itself never moves at all and only the present-tense relations are up for criticism, who profits and who gets hurt by the celebration. Tell me which you think she's on, what each rule predicts here, and if her record really does underdetermine it, name the one further case you'd put to her to split them. My guess is a case with the betrayal left intact but the sister long dead and no objection on record, but say if that's the wrong probe. Also: is the scholarship money doing any real work here, or is that a red herring?
