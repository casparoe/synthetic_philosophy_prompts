Hi — I write novels rather than philosophy papers, so sorry in advance if I mangle the vocabulary. I'm about two thirds of the way into a book set largely on a converted grain barge that a small foundation has parked in a river delta as a field station for two people who cannot stand each other's answer to the same question. One of them, Bruna, builds fruit-picking rigs: she thinks a symbol means something only if the world can kick back at you, i.e. only if there's a causal-perceptual loop in which the thing the symbol is about can make the system revise its use of it, and she thinks everything else is just a dictionary chasing its own tail. The other, Teodor, is a philologist who has spent eleven years on a language with no living speakers and no bilingual texts: he thinks meaning is a matter of being answerable to other users, of being able to go on correctly in new cases and be corrected when you don't, and he thinks a robot with a camera and no one to answer to is just a very expensive thermometer. They're stuck on the barge during a four-day power cut, and my plot needs them to produce a document — so I need it to actually exist. What I'm asking you for is the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about the symbol grounding problem: the claims both accept (about what ungrounded symbol manipulation looks like, about why the dictionary-regress worry is real, about what a successful account would have to deliver); then the exact residual disagreement, phrased once in wording Bruna would accept for her own view and once in wording Teodor would accept for his; then the real cruxes, the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of them; and for each crux one concrete case both of them would agree bears on it, with each one's prediction written down before the fact. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

The reason I can't just write this myself is that I need the cruxes to be tested against some cases I've invented for the book, and I don't trust myself to say anything sharp about them. Please build the document around these, and don't swap them out for the usual textbook examples: (1) the orphan corpus — Teodor's language, a system trained on every surviving page of it, which produces fluent continuations and gets its case endings right but has no one to be wrong in front of; is it ungrounded, or is ungrounded the wrong word for what's wrong with it? (2) proxy grounding — a system that learns its whole vocabulary inside an extremely high-fidelity simulation of a sailing ship, with real rope physics and real wind, and is never once connected to a harbour; is contact with simulated things a kind of contact? (5) deferred grounding — the orphan-corpus system, years later, adopted by a community of learners who revive the language and start using it to argue about their greenhouse; does its vocabulary become grounded retroactively, and if so does that change what it was doing before?
