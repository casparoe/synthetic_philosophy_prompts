Hey, I need a Q&A prep sheet. In three weeks I'm giving a 40 minute talk at a small cross-disciplinary workshop on predictive processing. The room is about eight people: three computational/vision types who build hierarchical prediction-error models, one of them very much the skull-bound inference-to-the-best-explanation type, one who is all about action and active inference, a psychophysics postdoc who works on bistable preception, another who works on Charles Bonnet syndrome, plus two philosophers of mind who are representationalists of the intentionalist sort (not disjunctivists) and one naive realist who is already on my side but will be embarrasingly unhelpful.

My thesis: naive realism doesn't conflict with predictive processing, it's what you get if PP is read as a story about subpersonal mechanisms rather than a story about what perception is. Three steps. (1) The generative model is the brain's model of the hidden causes of it's sensory input; the fact that the brain estimates hidden causes does not mean the subject perceives an estimate. (2) In the good case the state's accuracy conditions are set by the mind-independent object the system is tracking, so perception is a relation to that object, not to a representation. (3) In hallucination no object is doing that work, so there is no common mental kind shared by the two cases — the controlled-hallucination slogan trades on a slide from a subpersonal claim (the brain generates it's input in both cases) to a personal-level claim about what the subject is undergoing. So the argument from illusion fails, because its key premise — that a hallucination and the matching veridical perception must share their innermost nature, since the subject can't tell them apart from the inside — is just the assumption the disjunctivist denies, and nothing in the PP machinery forces it on us.

What I want: the six or so objections I'll actually get from this room, in the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would phrase them, not the way a philosopher of perception would put it, each with a two-minute answer and a note on where the answer is soft. What should I concede in the first two minutes so the room doesn't write me off as an armchair philosopher telling scientists what they really meant? Which of the objections has real force rather than just being a clash of frameworks? And what's the weakest joint in my step 3 that I should open up myself before someone else does? Finally, what's the question I'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is something like which empirical result would ever change my mind, but tell me if you think it's a different one.
