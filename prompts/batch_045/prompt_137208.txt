Last summer I took over all the food buying and cooking for my household, and after a health problem I was told to keep a fair amount of animal protein in my diet, so going fully plant-based is not one of the options I'm choosing between. The choices actually in front of me this year are narrow and concrete: buy a quarter of a grass-fed steer from the farm two fields over; buy chicken and eggs from the supermarket; keep the six ex-commercial laying hens I took in this spring, eat their eggs, and buy soy-based feed for them; or some mixture. I can watch the combine go round the wheat field behind the house, and I keep being told both that the animals killed by that field are my responsibility in a way the steer is not, and that the steer is worse because of how much it eats before it dies. Everyone who advises me counts something different and then stops.

So I don't want another ranking. I want the specification that a ranking would have to satisfy before I'd trust it.

Write me the desiderata that any method for comparing the animal-welfare impact of dietary options has to meet to count as good enough to act on for a household in my position — deciding what to buy this year, not writing a paper or setting policy. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable, as negotiable, or as a preference of one camp that has been passed off as a methodological demand. The list should cover at least:

the functional unit and the currency of harm — per kilogram of product, per gram of protein, per thousand calories, per meal, per hectare-year; deaths versus suffering-denominated time versus something like frustrated preferences — including whether the ranking of my options is permitted to flip when the unit or the currency changes, and what the method owes me if it does;

the counterfactual baseline, and how sensitive the method is allowed to be to it: whether the question is what happens if I don't buy this, or what this land and these supply chains would otherwise be doing, and what the method must say when the answer turns on facts I can't easily get — contract arrangements, quota, or the restocking rules of the shop I buy from;

the crop-cultivation side, where the method has to state what counts as a death caused by growing the soy or the wheat: the animals killed by the machinery at harvest, the ones displaced or poisoned across the season, the ones who would have died on that land anyway, and how wide the accounting window is;

joint production — the same steer is someone's milk and someone's leather, the same bean is oil for me and meal for a chicken — and what share of a shared harm may be attributed to the part I buy;

the animals who exist only because the system exists, and whether their existence is entered as a benefit, a harm, or nothing at all;

the inframarginal cases: the quarter of beef is already contracted and paid for, the hens are already here and eating whatever I put in front of them, and the shelf would be refilled either way.

Then the two things I most want. First, which single requirement does the most work in excluding the methods people actually reach for — the per-kilogram footprint tables, the death-count arithmetic, the approaches that tell you to count suffering instead — and which of the requirements on your own list are really one camp's taste in methodological costume. Second, I suspect at least one pair of desiderata on any honest list cannot be jointly satisfied, and that this is the useful result rather than a defect to be papered over. My two candidates, which I want you to adjudicate rather than assume: that the method be portable, so I can look numbers up without investigating my neighbour's farm or my supermarket's restocking, and that it be sensitive to the actual counterfactual of my purchase; and that it attribute harms item by item so a diet can be added up, and that it avoid arbitrary allocations when one animal or one field yields several products. Tell me whether either pair really conflicts, and if you think the genuine incompatibility lies somewhere else on the list, say where.

Mark, requirement by requirement, how confident you are that it is a real desideratum and not a prejudice, and for the two or three you're least sure of, say what would change your mind: which consideration, or which fact about how supply chains respond to demand, would make you drop or demote it. And if your honest verdict is that nothing currently on offer meets the list, or that the list is satisfiable in principle but the parameters I'd need are unavailable to anyone shopping for a household, say that plainly instead of producing a ranking regardless. A short scorecard at the end — a paragraph each for the footprint-table approach, the death-count approach, and a suffering-denominated one — is plenty; keep the weight on the criteria.
