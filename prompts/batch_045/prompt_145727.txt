I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background. Among other things I write process-control software for industrial insect-protein plants, and I'm trying to work out whether that work is defensible. I'd like an outside view, and the cheapest one seems to be to ask about a thinker who never had to face the case.

Take Jeremy Bentham. I know only the rough outline: the greatest-happiness principle as a real quantitative exercise rather than a slogan; the claim that the morally relevant capacity is the capacity to suffer rather than the capacity to reason or speak; equal counting of every individual's interests, however small; and a career spent attacking law that rested on sentiment, tradition, or the discretion of officials instead of on evidence and explicit codification.

Now hand him a case from two centuries later. Industrial insect farming has become a real industry: the number of invertebrates deliberately bred and killed each year for animal feed exceeds the number of farmed vertebrates by several orders of magnitude. Whether they can suffer at all is unresolved — there are contested behavioural and neurobiological markers, expert opinion is genuinely split, and a few more decades of research might settle it either way. Two policy options are on the table: a moratorium or permit requirement until the sentience question is settled, or rapid scaling of the industry as a substitute for beef and soy, with mandatory instantaneous-kill processing standards.

What would Bentham have said, and why? I care most about where his commitments pull apart. Does the quantitative, expected-value-friendly side of his hedonism tell him to weight the enormous numbers even under uncertainty, or does his demand for evidence before legislating tell him to dismiss the whole worry as speculation? If, as I gather, he ate meat and located the harm in the painfulness of killing rather than in the killing itself — correct me if that's wrong — does he end up an enthusiast for the second option? What legal instrument would he want here, a bright-line rule or an expert-administered standard? And does he have any resources for the further thought that if these creatures' lives contain more pain than pleasure, it might be better that fewer of them existed at all?

Finally, tell me how much of your answer is actually fixed by what Bentham believed and how much you're supplying yourself. I would rather know where the argument has to leave his commitments behind than get a confident answer.
