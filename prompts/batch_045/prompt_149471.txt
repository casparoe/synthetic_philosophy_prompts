I never studied philosophy — I work in insurance claims, and I got into this after a fire in my town where a block of warehouses burned down because a sprinkler system never came on, and my brother-in-law and I got stuck on whether anyone's *not doing anything* can really be what brought the loss about. I've since written down my position so I can defend it properly, and I'd like it audited before I argue it again, because the pieces feel like they don't all fit and I can't find the seam. Here is the set, roughly in the order I arrived at it: (1) omissions can be genuine causes and not merely background conditions we happen to find interesting — the nurse who doesn't give the dose causes the patient's death, the guard who doesn't open the valve causes the warehouse to burn, and I mean causes in the same ordinary sense in which the arsonist's match does; (2) what I call the Nothing Does Nothing rule: an absence strictly speaking isn't the kind of item that can push, produce, or transmit anything, so whenever an omission genuinely causes something there must be positive goings-on that do the actual producing — the agent's real doings and the world's real unfolding — and it is those that make the causal claim about the omission true; omission-causation is therefore real but metaphysically second-class, always anchored in positive causation and never free-floating; (3) causal facts are objective — whether the guard's silence caused the fire isn't settled by what anyone expected, wanted, or would have preferred; (4) causal judgments about omissions are nonetheless norm-sensitive, in that we count something as an omission-cause only when there was some standard the person failed to meet; (5) the basic test is difference-making: if the outcome wouldn't have happened had the person done the thing, the omission is a cause, and if it would have happened anyway, it isn't; (6) I want (1) to carry moral weight, because I think there is no deep moral difference between killing and letting die when the outcome and the agent's reasons are the same, and I want to support that by saying the omission brings the death about in exactly the way the action does.

Two verdicts I'm equally committed to, which may be where the trouble is. First: the off-duty firefighter who walked past the warehouse, could have called it in ten seconds before anyone else did, and didn't, is blameworthy but is *not* a cause of the loss — she had no such job. Second: in the actual case, the reason the system never came on is that the maintenance firm never fitted a fuse, and the reason the fuse was never fitted is that no one in the firm ever scheduled the job, and the reason no one scheduled it is that no one checked the roster — so the whole chain downward is one absence resting on another, with no positive event anywhere in it except the fire doing its own work. I still want to say the firm's omission caused the fire to spread.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the six commitments and the two verdicts and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, and which pairs only look like they are and why the appearance dissolves. For each real tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest available repair is, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set — I'd rather give up the least I can. Rank them by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If it matters whether my norms in (4) are supposed to be objective facts or just our expectations, say so, because I haven't decided.
