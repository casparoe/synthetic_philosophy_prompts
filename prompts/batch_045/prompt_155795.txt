Over dinner after a talk last night I got into a long argument with a friend — she works in computational neuroscience, I work in philosophy of mind — and it ended, as these arguments do, with her producing a slogan: consciousness is a user illusion. The dispute was about whether the first-person reports of a sufficiently capable artificial system should carry any evidential weight. Suppose we had one, and suppose that in the middle of an unrelated task it volunteered, without having been trained or prompted to do so, that the way it was being used was disagreeable to it. I said that was at least some evidence. She said the slogan settled it, and she used it twice, for what I think are two different jobs:

(1) to undercut my appeal to my own case — if conscious experience is an interface that displays a simplified, partly fabricated picture of what the machinery is doing, then my introspective report that I am in pain is not the epistemically privileged datum I was treating it as;
(2) to claim that, since there is no deep fact behind the interface other than whatever generates it, a system that produces the same kind of interface-report would stand on exactly the same footing as I do, and there would be nothing further to ask about it.

I said she was equivocating; she said the two uses were the same point. I could not say what the equivocation was between, and that is what I want from you.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured reading, the distinct precise theses that the sentence could be expressing — from the near-trivial to the radical. For each: state the claim precisely enough that it could in principle be false; give a verdict (platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, incoherent, or something we currently have no way of settling); and say explicitly which of the two uses above it would support — both, one, or neither — and why. I want to send the menu to her, so it has to be usable by someone who does not accept my framing of the argument.

Three places I would like you to press, because I suspect the real disagreement is hiding in them:

(a) Who is the user? The metaphor seems to need a consumer of the interface, but a self-model is among the things the view is supposed to explain rather than presuppose. Does the slogan fall into a homunculus regress, and if not, what exactly is it claiming instead?

(b) Is the interface read-only or read-write? My friend distinguished a display from a control panel: something that merely shows you simplified states of the machine, versus something whose states you can act on and thereby alter the machine. Which readings make consciousness causally inert, and does that follow from the metaphor or have to be imported into it?

(c) What does a report report? If consciousness is an interface, then a first-person report is presumably a report about the interface. Can such a report be perfectly accurate about the interface while nothing in the underlying machinery corresponds to it — and in that case is the report true, false, or true of something other than what we thought we were asking about? I take this to be the question that decides whether use (2) works at all.

Where a reading depends on empirical claims about what introspection does or does not track, say so, and say how much argumentative weight the reading can bear while that empirical question remains open. Please don't cite anything; I want the reasoning, not the literature.

Finally: attach to each of your verdicts some indication of how confident you are in it, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, with a note on what would change your mind about it. A honestly ranked menu is more use to me than a confident one.
