Im writing a novel with a character who is basically Stevens from The Remains of the Day: not a decision-maker at all, someone who carries out other peoples bad decisions efficiently and sincerely, and who feels fine about it because being professional and loyal is the whole of their moral world. So Ive been rereading it next to some writing on the problem of dirty hands, and the fit feels wrong in a way I think is interesting.

The standard cases involve an agent who knows the act is wrong, does it anyway for the sake of a good outcome, and is left carrying the guilt — the wrongness sticks even though the act was necessary. Stevens doesnt do any of that. When he carries out the dismissal of the two Jewish maids and treats it at the time as a professional test he passed, or when hes downstairs serving while his father is dying upstairs, there is no conflict he is registering; the role has already settled what counts as a reason. He doesnt have dirty hands so much as no hands.

So: is this a dirty hands story at all, or is it about a second and nastier failure mode, where the wrong gets done more reliably because nobody involved ever experiences it as wrong? Does the literature have a category for that — the person whose role has atrophied the capacity to see the dirt — or does it need coining, and how would you define it so it isnt just weakness of will or plain ignorance? Is it worse than the tragic case, or just sadder?

Two more things. Darlington is the closer fit — a man who thinks hes doing statesmanship while ruining people — except he looks wrong about the necessity rather than tragically right, so is he a dirty hands case or just a dupe, and does that mean dirty hands needs a real necessity or only a reasonably believed one? And does the book think theres any way back? The ending, where he half-confesses to a stranger on the pier and grieves the waste of his life — is that anything the tradition would recognise as a response to having done wrong, guilt or apology or restitution or accepting condemnation, or is he only grieving his own life and definitly not the act? I want the bit where the novel knows something the argument cant say.
