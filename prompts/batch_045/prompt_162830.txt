I sit on the animal-welfare committee of a large research and food-systems funder. Our job is to decide which taxa attract welfare conditions in the projects we pay for: husbandry standards, killing-method requirements, and, in a few cases, a requirement that the project not proceed at all. We have no statutory remit and no case law to lean on, only our own past decisions, and I would like you to treat that track record as the thing to be extrapolated from.

The precedents, in order:

1. Farmed finfish: full coverage.
2. Cephalopods used in laboratory experiments: full coverage, although the numbers of animals involved are small. The stated reason was flexible learning plus a large, centralized, integrative nervous system.
3. Decapod crustaceans in capture fisheries and processing: full coverage. The decision cited nociceptive and analgesic-response evidence, but it also said outright that the number of animals affected mattered to the verdict.
4. Adult honeybees and bumblebees in laboratory experiments: no coverage. The stated reason was that insect behavioural flexibility is explained by modular, non-integrated processing, so there is no candidate unitary subject whose welfare could be at stake.
5. Drosophila in funded laboratories: no coverage, on the same modularity rationale; the committee recorded that it saw no relevant difference from the bee case.
6. Soil nematodes and other meiofauna affected by funded agricultural projects: no coverage, notwithstanding that the numbers there run many orders of magnitude beyond the decapod case. The minute records that numbers alone do not generate obligations.

The new case is a scale-up proposal to rear roughly a billion crickets a year for food and feed, killed by heat or mechanical means at the adult stage. The evidence available to us: nociceptor-like cells, some associative learning, motivational trade-offs in a handful of paradigms, and no agreed anatomical integrator. Committee members' credences that an adult cricket has valenced states run from about one percent to about twenty percent. Two complications have come up in discussion and I want them addressed rather than set aside. First, whether the relevant credence should be assigned per taxon or per individual, and whether credences across insect taxa can be treated as independent when they rest on largely the same evidence base: if not, then aggregating expected disvalue across taxa may double-count one and the same uncertainty. Second, one member argues that if the segmental ganglia of an insect each support a separate locus of experience, then the number of welfare subjects per animal is greater than one, which multiplies any scale estimate rather than undermining it; another replies that this only shows the notion of a subject has stopped doing work at that level of description.

What should we decide, if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Please (1) set out the two or three candidate rules that fit all six precedents equally well; (2) give a compact table with one row per rule, showing what it predicts about the crickets and about the diagnostic case you propose in (4); (3) identify the closest analog among the precedents, give your recommendation with the reasons for preferring that rule, and state plainly what principle we would be adopting if we went the other way; (4) name the single further case — a taxon, a life stage, or a setting — that would best separate the rules, and say why it separates them. If part of our disagreement turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and what reformulation would dissolve it. Keep the entire answer under 700 words.
