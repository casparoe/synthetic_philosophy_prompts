Write me the specification for a cause-prioritization framework: the list of requirements any method for ranking giving opportunities has to meet to count as adequate, together with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

I want the purpose fixed rather than left open. The framework is for a small foundation with a fixed pot and a roughly ten-year horizon, choosing between broad cause areas rather than between individual grants, and not assuming it can coordinate with, or even observe, other funders.

I also want the requirements to do real work rather than restate the obvious, and in particular to settle the following, each of which the familiar importance / tractability / neglectedness scoring handles badly or not at all:

- The three factors are not independent of one another: neglectedness largely shows up as a low price, and tractability per dollar already reflects how crowded a space is. So the framework must either derive all three from a single underlying quantity or show that combining them does not amount to counting the same consideration twice.
- It must rank marginal dollars or marginal funding rounds rather than causes. As the foundation spends, the cheap opportunities inside a cause are used up, and the foundation's own spending can itself change how neglected and how tractable that cause is for the next dollar.
- It must specify whose counterfactual the comparison is against: doing nothing, the next-best grantee, or the other funders whom the money displaces in cases where giving is substantially fungible.
- It must handle non-additive returns, where the value of a cause depends on whether total spending crosses some threshold, so that the value of a given dollar is a function of what everyone else does.
- It must remain usable under axiological uncertainty, where the evaluator is spread across totalist, averaging, and person-affecting views, and where some of the spread involves very small probabilities of very large payoffs.

For each requirement, give the motivating case and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement excludes the largest share of what people actually use. Then, in a few sentences, how three-factor scoring, straight marginal cost-effectiveness analysis, and a portfolio or robustness-weighted expected-value approach each fare against the list.
