For the problem set: we've been doing accuracy-first epistemology — the standard dominance argument for probabilism, strictly proper scoring rules like the Brier score, the thought that the only fundamental epistemic good is having credences that are close to the truth values. I want you to argue for a version of this that, as far as I know, nobodys actually defended in print, so don't waste time trying to remember who said it.

Call the coarsest partition an agent actually draws distinctions along her grip. If you're only deciding between pasta, pizza, and Thai takeout, your grip is {pasta, pizza, Thai}, and every finer distinction sitting in your algebra just gets lumped in with those cells. Grip-relativism about accuracy says: measure an agent's inaccuracy only over the cells of the partition she currently grips, not over the whole algebra she'd in some sense be committed to.

Argue that grip-relativism gets you a partition-relative probabilism result — rationality requires that an agent's credences form a probability function on the partition she grips, and requires nothing whatsoever about how they behave off it — and that this is a feature rather than a bug, since it's exactly what lets the accuracy-firster dodge the demand that rational agents be logically omniscient, and it explains why we don't hold people responsible for incoherence they never notice. On the way you should handle the two obvious objections: that a bookie, or a later version of the agent who starts caring about the finer questions, can exploit exactly the incoherence grip-relativism lets slide; and that grip is a psychological notion smuggled into what's supposed to be a normative theory.
