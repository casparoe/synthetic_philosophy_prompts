hey! I'm a junior and I run the exchange student buddy program at my school, which mostly means I'm the person who has to explain to a bunch of 16 year olds from five different countries why everyone tips here, why cutting the lunch line is basically a declaration of war, and why jaywalking is technically illegal but everyone does it. Every August I do a 40 minute session with the new arrivals and their host siblings and I mostly just ramble, and it's bad. This year I want to replace it with an actual quiz they take before they land.

Build me a "what do you actually think about local rules?" quiz — around 9 questions, each one a concrete situation (tips, lines, crossing the street, that kind of thing), 2 or 3 answer options each, and a scoring key that sorts people into the real positions on two things: whether an everyday convention is ever a genuine moral obligation rather than just manners or prudence, and whether someone who's only visiting has to follow the conventions of the place they're visiting even when they're dumb or expensive. Audience is teenagers with zero philosophy background, half of them reading it in a second language, so no question can use words like conventionalism, relativism, universalism, normativity — the sorting has to happen off what they pick in the situations, not off labels. Needs to take under four minutes.

Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: start with the three or four separator questions that actually do the work, say which combinations of answers are possible, then drop the actual positions into that space. I want these leaves at minimum: conventions are pure coordination and only prudentially binding; conventions create obligations because workers rely on them (the tip is baked into how the job is paid); a guest ethic / when-in-Rome view where the fact that you're a visitor is itself the reason; a view where the same moral rules apply everywhere and local convention changes nothing; a view where the obligation is about what your action expresses or signals rather than who it helps; and a skeptical view where there just aren't obligations here at all, only etiquette people get weirdly moralistic about. Add unnamed leaves if the space has room for them.

I have a few half-baked tests of my own that I want you to either use as branch nodes or explicitly tell me are garbage:

- the host-cost test: a convention binds you only if breaking it puts a real cost on some specific identifiable local person, as opposed to just mildly annoying people or making things feel disorderly
- the double-standard check: if locals break the rule constantly and nobody cares, is it even a rule or just a story people tell about themselves
- the short-timer problem: reciprocity seems to need ongoing membership, and an exchange student is here ten months and will never be the local who gets their tip or their place in line — so can a convention obligate someone who'll never be on the recieving end of it
- the mirror test: flip it, a kid from here visiting their country. If your view gives asymmetric answers, is that a feature or a bug
- the money asymmetry: visitors usually have way more spending money than the locals around them, which feels like it should change the tipping and queue cases but I can't say how
- the kid-seeing-you case for jaywalking: nobody is hurt, but a local eight year old watches you do it

Cases that have to show up somewhere: not tipping at a sit-down place where staff pay is set up assuming tips; leaving a tip somewhere tipping isn't a thing and just confuses or slightly insults people; queue-jumping where the line is obvious versus a crowd where there is no real line just a scrum; crossing against the light on a street where literally everybody does it; a queue that is itself rigged, where people who pay off the guy at the front get moved up; and being genuinely too broke to tip. Also flag where two conventions collide and the procedure just has to throw up its hands.

At the end give me a JSON object, spelled out like this: "questions" as an array of objects with fields id, situation, options (each with label, text, and what it points to next), separates (one sentence on which positions this node splits and why), and is_restatement (true if the question secretly just asks which label someone likes, in which case kill it); "leaves" with id, name, core_claim, awkward_commitment, and occupied; "empty_cells" with answer_pattern and why_nobody_is_there (be specific: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored); "unseparable_pairs" with leaves, why, and the extra question that would split them; and "scoring_key" with pattern and result. No citations or reading lists, I don't want homework, and please don't just tell me it depends on the situation — that's the thing I'm trying to get past.
