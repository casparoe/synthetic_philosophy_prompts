I'm in the first year of a PhD program, and our reading group lost most of last week's session to the aggregation problem for Scanlonian contractualism. I've since been playing with a revision to the test, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's an improvement or a slow-motion collapse into utilitarianism, so I'd like an outside view before I inflict it on the group again.

As usually stated, contractualism compares candidate principles by asking what complaints individuals could reasonably raise against them, and it refuses to add different people's complaints together: a million people each gaining some trivial benefit can never outweigh one person's serious burden, because there is no principle on which any single one of the million has a complaint that rivals the burdened person's. I'm sympathetic to that upshot, but I think the test as stated is blind to a different class of case: burdens that are negligible on each occasion but that pile up on the same person across a life. Imagine a tenancy rule that costs the same tenant a trivial amount on each of thousands of separate occasions, or a siting decision whose tiny exposures accumulate in one small neighbourhood. At any given moment the affected person's complaint is trivial, so a test that looks only at occurrent complaints never registers anything at all — yet intuitively the policy imposes something serious on that person, and on them specifically. Here is my proposed fix — call it the life-share view. Keep the ban on summing complaints across persons fully intact, but allow complaints to sum within a single life, and measure a person's complaint as the share of their total lifetime well-being that the principle would cost them. On this view the million trivial benefits still generate no large complaint, since each beneficiary's lifetime share barely moves; but a thousand trivial burdens falling on one person now generate a substantial complaint, which can enter the ordinary pairwise comparison with everyone else's without any interpersonal aggregation at all.

Please compare the standard synchronic complaint test with the life-share view as responses to the aggregation problem. I'd like you to cover four things, in whatever order serves your argument: (i) construct a case where the two tests actually diverge and tell me which answer you think is correct there; (ii) say whether summing a person's complaints over time is morally different in kind from summing complaints across persons — and, in particular, what happens if the unity of a life is itself only a matter of degree, as reductionist views of personal identity suggest: does that undermine the life-share view, or does it undermine the ban on interpersonal aggregation instead, or neither; (iii) address the individuation worry, since the life-share view's verdicts seem to depend on how finely we carve up occasions, and it's not obvious that occasions come pre-carved — is that objection fatal, or is there a principled way to do the carving; and (iv) judge which of the two views better preserves whatever made contractualism attractive in the first place (the separateness of persons, the idea of justification to each person one by one), and whether the life-share view has quietly turned into a form of prioritarianism once complaints are measured as lifetime shares.
