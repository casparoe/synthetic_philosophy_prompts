yo — I keep going back and forth on whether a language model is an agent, a simulator, or something else, and I feel like the two loud camps are arguing past each other. So I want you to make the best case you can for one side, but on a sharper version of the question than the one people usually fight about.

Set-up first (I'm inventing these terms, so dont go looking them up):

- *Goal-in-weights*: what the system is trying to bring about is fixed by training and doesn't change when you change what you say to it.
- *Goal-in-context*: training fixed a general policy for how to pursue a goal, but which goal is being pursued is supplied by the prompt, and the same weights will competently pursue incompatible goals in different contexts.
- *Simulator*: nothing is being pursued at all. The system emits what some kind of source would emit; the purposes you think you see belong to the simulated character, not to the system.

The test I want you to use is *invariance under redescription*: hold the situation and the goal fixed and vary the surface form wildly (chat message, play script, bug report, poem, JSON blob), and then do the converse — same surface form, different goal. Say what each of the three hypotheses predicts about that test and why.

The position to defend: LLMs are goal-in-context agents, not simulators; the simulator story is what you get if you secretly insist that a real goal must be goal-in-weights, and that insistence is doing all the work without ever being argued for. Corollary you have to own: the fact that a model will competently pursue contradictory goals across two prompts is evidence *for* agency, because generality across goals is a mark of agency, not its absence.

Four objections I actually care about, and I want all four answered:

1. The training signal is next-token prediction on human text — an imitation objective, not an outcome objective. How does optimizing imitation ever produce something that wants anything? And why isn't the honest answer that it doesn't?
2. No goals or memory persist across conversations, so calling it an agent seems to commit you to a brand-new agent existing every time someone hits enter.
3. Real agents have causal grip on the world; this thing emits tokens. Is "agent over an action space of text" a real claim or just changing the subject?
4. The regress worry: if the system is the one *selecting* which character to simulate, isn't the selector the agent, and doesn't the simulator story then need its own agent anyway?

Also don't let the view get cheap. A calculator and a chess engine also have their goal set by input. State the minimum bar for goal-in-context agency, say why an LLM clears it and a calculator doesn't — or bite the bullet and grant the calculator is a very boring agent, and tell me why that's not a reductio.

No papers, no citations, no named evals or benchmark results — argue it from the concepts plus ordinary chat-model behaviour you're confident about, and be explicit when you're relying on an empirical claim vs. a conceptual one. Finish by telling me what one concrete observation would separate goal-in-context agency from simulation in a way that isn't just relabelling, and whether we probably already have it.
