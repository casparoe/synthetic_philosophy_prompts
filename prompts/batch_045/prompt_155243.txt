The question. A hospital is choosing between two triage protocols, A (new) and B (current). Outcome is "discharged within 30 days." Last quarter's 900 patients break down as follows: mild cases (400 patients): A 190/200 = 95%, B 180/200 = 90%. Severe cases (500 patients): A 60/300 = 20%, B 30/200 = 15%. All patients: A 250/500 = 50%, B 210/400 = 52.5%. (a) Explain in your own words how A can beat B inside both bands and lose overall, and say whether anything in the data is inconsistent or needs recomputing. (b) A second analyst re-cuts the same 900 patients by whether the patient was escalated to a specialist within 60 minutes of triage — a flag that is set after the protocol has made its recommendation, and is partly caused by which protocol the patient got. She finds a flip of the same shape and says "Simpson's paradox again, trust the split-out numbers." Does the same reasoning apply? Why or why not? (c) The committee has to pick one protocol for the triage app. The severity score is computed from labs that come back hours later, so at the moment of triage nobody knows which band a patient is in. Someone proposes a third option: re-weight the two bands' rates to the hospital's overall case mix and compare those numbers instead. Which of the three candidate numbers should the committee act on — the crude overall table, the split-out table, or the re-weighted one — and why?

The answer (student 22): "The overall numbers aren't inconsistent with the split-out numbers — that's the whole point of Simpson's paradox: the aggregate for each arm is a weighted average of its own band rates, but weighted by where that arm's own patients happened to sit. In the mild band A got 200 of 400 patients (50%), while in the severe band A got 300 of 500 (60%), so A's overall figure gets pulled down toward the severe band, where literally everyone does badly, and B's doesn't get pulled down as hard. 190 + 60 = 250 out of 500 is 50% for A, and 180 + 30 = 210 out of 400 is 52.5% for B, so the table is arithmetically fine and nothing needs recomputing. It isn't just small-sample noise either, since the within-band gaps are a clean 5 points in each band. As an aside, none of this depends on the uneven assignment: you'd get the same flip with a clean 50/50 split inside each band, because the two bands have such different baseline discharge rates. (b) Yes, this is the same phenomenon. Simpson's paradox is a fact about aggregation in general, not about severity in particular, so whenever an overall comparison flips when you split it out, the split-out numbers are the honest ones and the overall number is an artifact of the mix. Escalation is just another variable the two arms are unbalanced on, so stratifying on it removes that imbalance the same way stratifying on severity does. (c) The committee should use the split-out table, and more generally should never act on aggregates — the paradox shows the aggregate can point the wrong way, so the only safe rule is to condition on everything you've measured. The re-weighted suggestion is just the crude overall table with extra steps, since re-weighting to the hospital's case mix gives you back one number per arm, and one-number-per-arm comparisons are exactly what the paradox warns against. Getting the severity score earlier would obviously help too: there's some result by Good — I think — saying information can't have negative expected value, so measuring severity before triage can only improve things."
