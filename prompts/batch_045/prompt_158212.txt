I'm retired now — forty-odd years in experimental physics, mostly atmospheric remote sensing — and for the last couple of years I've been going to a philosophy of science reading group at the library. We did Newton last week, and I've been chewing on it since.

One of the group, a retired engineer, used a line to close an argument. The question was whether Newton's theology has anything to do with the physics, and he said: look, Newton himself said he frames no hypotheses — the Principia is a black box that gets the motions right and says nothing about causes, so the God business is private religion and we can file the two halves of the man apart and move on. A couple of us went to the pub afterwards and I said I didn't buy it, but I couldn't say why, and I still can't.

Here's the passage, which I copied off the internet so I hope it's right: "I have not as yet been able to discover the reason for these properties of gravity from phenomena, and I do not frame hypotheses. For whatever is not deduced from the phenomena must be called a hypothesis; and hypotheses, whether metaphysical or physical, or based on occult qualities, or mechanical, have no place in experimental philosophy." (The Latin is "hypotheses non fingo" — and I gather people fight about whether "feign" or "frame" is the better rendering, which I suspect matters.)

What bothers me: a few paragraphs later in that same essay there is "This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent being," and it ends with talk of a most subtle spirit pervading and hidden in gross bodies. Leibniz, I'm told, jeered that on this picture "God Almighty wants to wind up his watch from time to time: otherwise it would cease to move." And outside the Principia there's the Opticks queries, the prophecy chronologies, the Arian stuff he seems to have kept to himself. So the essay where he refuses to feign hypotheses is also the essay where he argues for God.

Can you lay out, as a list, the different precise things that slogan might be claiming, from the trivial to the radical? Ones that came up: that it's only a ban on mechanical causes, i.e. a jab at Descartes' vortices, which wouldn't touch non-mechanical posits at all; that it's a ban on anything not deduced from phenomena; that it's instrumentalism avant la lettre about force; that "feign" is about insincerity rather than method; that it's pure rhetoric aimed at his critics. And whatever else there is.

For each one: how well it fits the actual sentence, what work it does, and — this is the bit I care about — whether it would really support my group-mate's move of packing Newton's theology off into a separate box from his science. Then: which readings, if any, let the no-hypotheses rule and the design argument sit in the same essay without contradiction? And does it change anything that he was, by most accounts, a nontrinitarian heretic who wrote an awful lot of theology?

Related, and this is my own hobby horse: in my old field we routinely fitted parameterisations for cloud microphysics that we knew were physically wrong, and got good predictions out of them. Nobody thought that meant we had no view about what clouds are doing. Is Newton's move supposed to be like that, or weaker, or stronger?

Two last things. Please say which of the readings you'd actually defend and how confident you are, as against the ones you're only reporting because people have floated them. And if my puzzle is muddled — if the line just can't bear any of this weight, or if I'm conflating "no cause" with "no theology", or if the whole separation question is a bad one — tell me so plainly instead of politely giving me six readings and a smile. I'd rather be corrected than agreed with.
