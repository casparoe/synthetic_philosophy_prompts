ESSAY (working title: coordination first)

1. Setup. I have never added numbers greater than or equal to 57. Asked for 68+57 I answer 125. Kripke's skeptic says: nothing about me — no behavior, no mental item, no disposition — determines that I meant plus rather than quus, where quus agrees with plus below 57 and returns 5 whenever either argument is 57 or above. As I read the argument, there are four constraints on any candidate meaning-fact. (a) Uniqueness: it must pick out one function, not leave plus and quus equally eligible. (b) Epistemology: I must be able to know, or at least be justified about, what I meant. (c) Normativity: it must explain why 125 is the correct answer and not merely the answer I am inclined to give. (d) Non-circularity: it must be stated in non-semantic, non-intentional terms, or it helps itself to the very notion under analysis. Straight solutions offer a candidate fact. The skeptical solution concedes there is no such fact and reinterprets meaning talk by the role it plays in our practice: agreement in responses, licensing by the community, assertibility conditions in place of truth conditions.

2. Why I'm unsatisfied. The dispositionalist straight answers fall to the two familiar complaints: a disposition tells you what I will do, not what I should do; and dispositionalism can't accommodate systematic error, since whatever I'm disposed to say becomes correct by stipulation, unless the dispositions are idealized, in which case the idealization quietly encodes the standard. But the skeptical solution buys peace at a price I can't pay. It makes my first-personal authority over my own meanings look like a by-product of other people's willingness to correct me, and it leaves the stubborn sense that there must be some difference between my meaning plus and my meaning quus entirely unexplained.

3. The proposal, coordination first (CF). Start from the practical problem rather than the metaphysics. Agents with overlapping interests face recurrent, open-ended coordination problems: each of us must match future performance to past performance and to the expectations of others across indefinitely many new cases, 68+57 among them. Such problems have multiple equilibria. We all answer 125. Or we all answer 5 once an argument hits 57. Or we all do something gerrymandered. What meaning-attributions do, I claim, is select an equilibrium. To say that Jones means addition is to say which ongoing pattern is the one our coordination is organized around and can be expected to continue along. The normativity of correctness is then borrowed rather than discovered: the ought comes from a general practical premise about the value of coordinating, plus the fact that among the equilibria available to us some are ones we have reason to organize around while others — quus, for creatures like us — are not available at all.

4. Why I think CF meets the constraints. (a) It does not deliver a unique meaning-fact; it delivers determinacy of practice, and I want to argue Kripke's desiderata can be read as requiring determinacy of practice rather than a fact. This reading is load-bearing and I'm unsure of it. (b) Which equilibrium we're on is knowable the way a driving convention is knowable: equilibria are public, so my justification isn't a leap in the dark. (c) The semantic ought is derived from a practical ought, not from an is. (d) Coordination, equilibrium, payoff and salience are non-semantic — though I worry that expectation and salience smuggle intentionality back in, which is what section 5 tries to fix.

5. The bit I most want comments on: intrinsic versus extrinsic salience. Call a pattern intrinsically salient when agents with our perceptual and cognitive makeup and no shared history would converge on it. Addition is like this; quus is not, because its breakpoint is structurally arbitrary. Call a pattern extrinsically salient when it is held in place by training, correction and institutional enforcement whether or not it is intrinsically salient. My claim is that the skeptic's alternative rules are not genuine options for us because they are neither intrinsically salient nor extrinsically enforced: there is no equilibrium there to select, so there is nothing for my answer to accord with or conflict with. And here's the payoff I like — error comes back without meaning facts. A community can enforce a pattern that is intrinsically unsalient and payoff-dominated. Imagine people drilled from childhood to answer 5 to any sum involving 57 or more, who treat deviation as a fault and who never encounter anyone doing otherwise. They can still be wrong: wrong relative to the equilibrium that agents like them would have reason to select absent the drilling. So the error objection that sinks individual dispositionalism doesn't sink CF, because the standard is set by the structure of the coordination problem plus the practical premise, and not by what anyone, individually or collectively, happens to do.

6. What I know is shaky. Equilibrium selection is notoriously non-unique; if two patterns are both decent equilibria, maybe CF just redscribes the skeptical problem at the level of salience instead of solving it. I'm also unsure that instrumental normativity can produce the flavour of the semantic ought — the way no, that isn't what plus means, you've made a mistake feels independent of anybody's purposes. And salience is doing a lot of work; I gesture at cashing it out in terms of payoff asymmetries and similarity of pattern, and I don't really do it.
