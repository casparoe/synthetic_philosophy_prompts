I'm a computer science PhD student, and as a side project I'm building what I call a commitment ledger: a small module that ingests transcripts of lab meetings and negotiation chats and tries to log, for each utterance, whether a commitment came into existence, who owes what to whom, and whether it was later discharged or breached. I gave a short talk about it to a philosophy reading group, and I came away with an objection I haven't been able to settle, which matters because the objection targets the case I had assumed was the easy one.

My working assumption was that explicit promises are trivial to handle, given what I'd absorbed about performative utterances: an utterance like "I promise to send the revised draft by Friday" doesn't describe or report a promise, it performs one, so unlike "it is raining" it isn't truth-evaluable at all — it is felicitous or infelicitous depending on whether the conventional procedure was executed correctly and completely, by the right person, in the right circumstances. I drew what seemed to me a clean corollary and built it into the system: because there is no gap between saying "I promise" and promising, there is no room for insincerity at the moment of utterance. Either the procedure was in order and the promise now exists, in which case the only thing that can subsequently go wrong is breach, or it wasn't in order and no promise was ever made. So the ledger logs a live obligation for every explicit first-person commitment formula — "I promise," "I commit to," "I'll have it to you by" — produced by a competent speaker who isn't obviously joking, acting, or quoting, and it deliberately models no speaker intentions, since I took those to be either unrecoverable or already packed into the utterance itself. One person in the group said my scheme has no way to represent insincere promising, and mentioned sincerity conditions, which baffled me: if the utterance isn't the sort of thing that can be true or false, what could it mean for a condition on it to go unmet? A second person claimed that most promises contain no performative verb whatsoever, which worries me about the whole "detect the formula" architecture, but I can't see how an utterance can *be* a promise if it never says that it is one.

Could you sort out where I've gone wrong? I'd like you to distinguish, as carefully as you can, the several different things that can go wrong with a promise — the act's not coming off at all, its coming off but being defective in some way, its being broken later — and to tell me what my ledger should be tracking instead of what it tracks now, including any cases where the surface form of the utterance is misleading in either direction. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question; if it's useful, end with a short note on the fields such a ledger would need.
