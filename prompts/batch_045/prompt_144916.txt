hey — i help run a volunteer crisis-line rota (about forty people, mostly students) and we're rewriting the one-page "what's actually expected of you" sheet we hand new volunteers, because the last version managed to both freak people out (they read every optional thing as secretly required and burned out inside two months) and let a couple of people coast (they'd describe straight-up required stuff as "going above and beyond"). my teammate priya says we should drop the moral-philosophy framing entirely and model the whole thing on a job. her pitch: the job description is your duty — written down in advance, you're blameworthy if you skip it, nobody thanks you for doing it. anything past that is overtime: genuinely optional, genuinely praiseworthy if done, zero blame if you clock off. she says that gives us a stable line we can actually print, and it also settles the thing we kept going in circles on — that an act doesn't stop being supererogatory (good but not required, "beyond duty") just because it happens to be easy and the stakes are enormous: you don't get fired for not doing a coworker's job, so likewise you're not obliged to give a kidney to a stranger just because the surgery is low-risk and it saves a life.

i half buy it, but i can feel the analogy carrying weight it hasn't paid for, and the sheet is due sunday. so: can you audit it? start with the correspondence table — employee, employer, job description, employment contract, wage, overtime, being fired, the bonus, the performance review, the coworker who always stays late — and what each of those maps onto in the moral case, with an explicit question mark where there's nothing to map onto. then the disanalogies, sorted into ones that actually matter for our use and ones that don't. the ones i keep tripping over: (1) the ratchet — we had a volunteer who took every horrible shift for a year, and now new volunteers get side-eyed for not doing the same, and her old shifts feel de facto required; in a job, doing overtime never amends the contract, but here it seems to, which is bad news for "we'll just write the list down." (2) you can quit a job — you consented to the description and can walk — whereas you can't resign from morality, so it's unclear where the line gets its authority from at all. (3) overtime praise seems to presuppose somebody whose interests fix the baseline and who could legitimately ask for more at the next review; in the moral case who would that be, and if there is no one, is our "job description" just a record of what people here happen to do? (4) the escalation ladder we built for the sheet — hold a stranger's door, return a dropped wallet, stay twenty minutes late to cover a gap, take the christmas shifts, drive across town at 3am, donate a kidney — where, if anywhere, does optional flip to required? priya says never, but then i worry the job framing quietly makes everything optional (there's no boss to fire you) or, worse, makes the line whatever we happened to type up, which is circular for our purposes. (5) also, in a job nobody thanks you for doing your job, but people absolutely do thank volunteers for doing the baseline things, and we're not sure whether to put "say thank you" on the sheet as a requirement.

what i want to know at the end is whether priya's second conclusion — that easiness plus huge stakes never converts the optional into the required, so the kidney case stays supererogatory — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the job picture was doing that work the whole time. and if there's a better analogy for a group like ours that isn't just whatever we wrote on the sheet with extra steps, i'd take it. keep it usable: flag which disanalogies should change what actually goes on the page.
