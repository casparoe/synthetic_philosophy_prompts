I've been going round in cirles on an argument I made up for my epistemology seminar, and now I can't see the exit. I call it the staircase.

(1) Your credence should respond to your total evidence, and evidence about how reliable your own reasoning was on a given occassion is part of your total evidence, so it has to be folded in alongside the first-order evidence.
(2) My reasoning is fallible: even at my best I go wrong some small fraction of the time, and I know it. So my confidence that this occasion's reasoning was reliable is, say, 0.9 rather than 1.
(3) So my confidence in any conclusion I reach should be the support the first-order evidence gives it, discounted by that 0.9.
(4) But the judgement that my reasoning was reliable on this occasion is itself something I arrived at by reasoning, so it gets the same treatment: 0.9 confident in it, 0.9 in that, and so on up the staircase.
(5) So the total discount is 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × ... , which tends to zero. Every conclusion, whatever the first-order evidence says, ends up at credence zero.
(6) That isnt just scepticism, it's unliveable — I'd have to be as unsure that 7 + 5 = 12 as that the number of stars is even. So one of the steps is wrong.

I'd like to keep (1): saying higher-order evidence is no evidence at all feels like cheating, and it was my favourite premise to defend. And (2) looks like plain honesty about being fallible. I keep flip-flopping between blaming (3) and blaming the regress in (4)/(5), and when I try to reject the regress I can't say what the correct way of combining the levels is meant to be.

Which step do you give up, what does giving it up cost, and is there anything left of the staircase once you've fixed it? Please answer in American English, and fairly short if you can.
