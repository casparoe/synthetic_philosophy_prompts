Im a PhD student and I've been handed one fifty-minute section next week in our intro Philosophy and Law course — first-years, about a third of them pre-law and the rest there to fill a requirement, whiteboard only, no handouts because the department printer is dead again. The topic is causation by omission: whether someone's failure to do something can be a cause of something happening. Please don't explain the topic to me; tell me how to explain it. What I keep going back and forth on is the order, because my sense is that before the question even means anything to them I have to seperate "did she cause it" from "is she to blame" — the pre-law kids have half-remembered duty and proximate cause from somewhere and will answer a causal question with a responsibility verdict all section if I let them — and also that they need causal claims to be contrastive or interest-relative (why did *these* plants die and not *those*) before an absence can even look like a possible answer. But I can't tell whether those are genuine prerequisites or whether I'm front-loading metaphysics that should come in later, and I'm not sure whether to present the "an omission only counts as a cause against a normative background" line as the live option it is or to hold it back so it doesn't swallow the whole discussion on contact.

The example is the other thing I'm stuck on. My first thought was the obvious one — a lifeguard who's on her phone and doesn't notice a kid struggling — but it feels saturated with obligation, so I made up a pair instead: two identical greenhouses on the same street, same plants, both need daily watering. Sam is going away and asks her neighbour Priya to water; Priya says yes and then just forgets, all week. In the other greenhouse nobody was ever asked and nobody watered. Same deaths, same absence of water, one has a promise in it and one doesn't. Is that the right thing to carry the whole fifty minutes, or is it still smuggling in a norm when the question is supposed to be about causation? Either way, tell me what the carrying example has to have and what it has to lack. Then there's a case I want to use late: two people each promise Sam they'll water, each assumes the other did, neither does — now the but-for test says neither one caused it. I want that at the end, as a general worry about overdetermination that also afflicts perfectly ordinary actions, but I'm worried it detonates everything I just built, so tell me where it goes and how to frame it so it doesn't.

The misconceptions I'm braced for: "an omission is nothing, and nothing can't do anything"; "if forgetting can be a cause then everyone who ever failed to water those plants caused their death, up to and including the Queen"; "causation needs a mechanism, a physical connection or a transfer of energy, so an absence can't qualify"; and the sharp one, that the only reason Priya looks like a cause is that she was supposed to water, so really it's blame dressed up as causation. For each, tell me where exactly in the sequence I should head it off and what has to already be on the board for the defusal to work.
