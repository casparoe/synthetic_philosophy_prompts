I'm giving the farewell toast at my mother's retirement dinner. She is leaving the state senate after twenty-four years because a term-limits amendment adopted by the state's voters two decades ago now bars her from running again; she opposed the amendment at the time, but she has never questioned the voters' right to pass it, and she is not bitter. The room will be split: about half are family, neighbors, and old friends from the district who are proud of her but not especially political; the other half are legislative staff, lobbyists, and former colleagues who know exactly how the appropriations committee works and who have watched the term-limit law reshape the institution. A few people there are also involved in a citizens' assembly project that would prefer to select legislators by lot rather than by election, because they think professional politicians are the central problem. I need a five-minute toast that does more than celebrate her seniority.

The toast has to be built around a distinction I want to introduce. Call it the difference between a career as accumulation and a career as custodianship. The first is the thing term-limits advocates are right to fear: a long tenure spent converting office into personal power, seniority, donor networks, and name recognition, so that the officeholder's main project becomes staying in office. The second is what I believe my mother's career actually was: staying long enough to learn the budget, to steward the institution's memory, to protect the district's interests through changes of governor and party, and to leave the chamber in better shape than she found it. My hypothesis is that the term-limits movement is aimed at the first kind of career but is a blunt instrument that cannot tell it apart from the second, and that the sortition enthusiasts in the room share the worry without sharing the remedy: they would abolish the elected career, whereas I want to insist that the answer to bad careerism is to ask what the career was for.

Please write the toast so that this distinction does real structural work. It should honor her by showing why her longevity was custodianship rather than accumulation, and it should be honest that the suspicion behind both term limits and the lottery proposal was not baseless. At the same time, it cannot attack the voters who imposed the limit, cannot turn into a civics lecture or a policy argument, and has to land as affection rather than as a brief for her reelection. The political insiders should feel that the toast understands the institution they work in; the family and friends should feel they are hearing about a person they love, not an abstraction. If a light, affectionate joke can be built around the difference between being termed out and being termed in, or around the lottery folks' distrust of professional politicians, use it, but make sure it serves the toast rather than hijacking it.
