Hey — I'm stuck on the methods/framework chapter of my thesis (philosophy + cog sci, I'm attached to a vision lab) and I've been going in circles for a couple of weeks, so let me outsource the actual hard part. What I want is a desiderata list, not a theory and not a literature review. The job I'm specifying adequacy for is this: a method for collecting and using first-person data in a science of consciousness has to be able to settle claims of the form "subject S has an experience of kind K" / "lacks one" / "has one that differs from S's in respect R". Concretely: does someone who reports no mental imagery actually have visual imagery; is a subject in one of those no-report paradigms consciously perceiving the stimulus or not; does the blindsight patient see anything; do two subjects who both use the word "imagine" mean the same thing by it. My supervisor is a heterophenomenology person — treat the reports as texts, reconstruct the world the subject is describing, don't grant first-person authority, stay scrupulously neutral on whether any of the described items are really there — and my labmate is much more neurophenomenology / microphenomenology: train the subjects, get them into something like the reduction, treat reporting as a skill that improves with practice. Every time the three of us argue we just restate whichever we already liked, because we've never actually said what a method has to deliver.

So: write me the spec. What are the requirements, what is the case or consideration behind each one, and which are genuinely non-negotiable for that adjudicating job versus which are just preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements (I strongly suspect both "don't trust the report" and "train the subject until the report gets rich" are camp commitments, but tell me if I'm wrong). Things I already think belong on the list, so you can sharpen or destroy them rather than rediscover them: the method needs something principled to say when verbal report and forced-choice behaviour come apart — someone describes vivid imagery and discriminates at chance, or the reverse — and I want to know whether that's noise to be averaged out or a datum in its own right. It needs to distinguish having no experience from having one you can't report or don't have a concept for: the people who only discover they lack imagery after they encounter the concept seem like the pivotal case, and so does the inverted-spectrum-shaped worry that no quantity of report could in principle settle the question. It needs to be able to fail on an individual subject, not only at the group level. And two things I almost never see discussed, which I want you to take seriously as candidate requirements: (1) the revision problem — subjects retract and refine their own reports across sessions ("no wait, I do have something, it just isn't picture-like"), and most methods treat a report as a fixed datapoint with no account of what a later revision does to the earlier one, or of when a series of reports is even the same measurement; (2) the interviewer is part of the instrument — the probes, the vocabulary on offer, the interviewer's own imagery, the way a session is framed all leak into what comes back, so how much of that has to be controlled or independently measured before the method is worthless? Please also tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, because that would honestly be the most useful thing in the whole chapter: my hunches are that "stay neutral about whether the described experiences exist" and "be able to detect an experience the subject can't report" can't both be had, and that "comparability across subjects" and "don't presuppose shared structure of experience" are in tension. And which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out — the one that quietly kills the most popular method? A short scorecard at the end for heterophenomenology, neurophenomenology/microphenomenology, and the no-report style paradigms on the neuroscience side is welcome, but keep the bulk on the criteria and their motivating cases.
