I'm a senior at a big public high school, and I run the math club, which gets a twenty-five-minute slot on Thursdays with a whiteboard and no projector. Next month I've let myself be talked into giving one of our short talks on Russell's paradox and on why naive set theory can't be saved by bolting on one or two small rules. The audience will be fifteen or so students from grades nine through twelve: about half of them have taken AP Computer Science and are at home with Python sets, hashability, and recursion errors, and the rest have algebra and a little geometry and nothing else. Our faculty advisor — who has a master's in mathematics, will be sitting in the back, and is fond of the picture of sets as built up in stages from the empty set — is the one I'm most nervous about, because I don't want to say anything he'll have to correct, and I also don't want to leave an obvious gap that he fills with a five-minute lecture on the cumulative hierarchy while my time drains away.

The vehicle I've been turning over is a club-of-clubs: imagine an online forum where a group is nothing but its membership list, where groups can be members of other groups, and where the question "can a group list itself?" is genuinely open — then consider the group that admits exactly those groups that don't admit themselves. I like it because it lets me put extensionality (a thing is nothing over and above its members) and comprehension (any description of members gives you a thing) on the board as two separable commitments and attack them one at a time, and because the programmers in the room already know that a Python set can't contain itself, which gives me a foil. But I'm not confident it's the right choice: does the club framing quietly smuggle in the assumption that the group in question actually exists, which is the very thing I want them to see as the culprit, and does anything in the analogy break at the exact step where the contradiction lands?

What I actually want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Give me the order the pieces have to go in and say what depends on what — in particular, where the two commitments get separated, where self-membership gets introduced, and where I should let the advisor's stage picture in on purpose rather than by interruption. Then give me the misconceptions this specific room will bring or form, in the order they'll arrive, with the moment in the sequence where each should be defused: my guesses are that the programmers will treat it as a recursion trick and conclude that banning self-reference settles it, that someone will say it's just the liar paradox and therefore a fact about language rather than about mathematics, that the barber and library-catalogue versions will get raised as though they were the same argument, that "just sort things into levels or types" will be offered as a free lunch, and that someone will conclude that sets of sets are the problem and should go. I especially want to know how to handle the first of these honestly: I have a half-formed memory that forbidding self-membership on its own doesn't rescue naive comprehension, because once nothing contains itself the set of all non-self-membered things turns into the set of everything, which then has to contain itself after all, so the damage is really done by the comprehension principle rather than by self-reference — tell me whether that's right, whether it's usable in front of ninth graders, and if not, what replaces it. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing (my instinct is the full axiom list, ordinals and cardinals, the axiom of choice, and anything about formal type theories, but tell me if I'm cutting in the wrong place), and give me one closing question I can put to the room whose wrong answers will tell me exactly which misconception is still alive.
