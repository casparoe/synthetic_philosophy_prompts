I could use a hand with something. I'm the token scientist on the exhibits and public programing committee at the little natural history museum in town — we also run a 60-seat planetarium out back, which is how I got roped in. I did thirty-one years in isotope geochemistry, and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university, and its gone to my head a bit, so I'm the one always saying "well, it depends what you think a research program is."

The case: our education staff want an interactive for the Kepler exhibit we're opening in October. Visitor types in a birth date, the box computes the angular separations between the planets at that moment — the aspects, the ones Kepler actually used, including the odd ones he derived from regular polygons — and prints a card describing that person's 'temperament' in his terms. All of it labelled as 17th-century history, with a disclaimer panel at the exit. Our astronomer on the advisory board says no: she says we would be manufacturing an astrological claim about a living visitor, using real orbital mechanics to do it, with the museum's name on it, and that nobody reads the disclaimer. There's a third version floating around, which is to keep the machine but have it print the aspects next to what the same geometry let Kepler actually do — nail Mars to within minutes of arc — so the visitor sees the failure sitting right beside the success. Half of us think that's the honest version and half think its a fig leaf on the same thing.

We vote March 12, because the interactive has to go to the fabricator in April. Please draft the memo I'll table. What I want in it:

A clear recommendation in the first paragraph — build it, build it with named conditions, or kill it — with the reasons after, not the other way round.

It has to commit to a reading of what Kepler thought he was doing, because I've come to think that's what the argument is really about. Three candidates as I see them. (a) Leftover: the astrology was the vocabulary and the income of his time, and had nothing to do with the work that survived. (b) Same machinery, wrong domain: the same instincts for geometry and number and checkable prediction, pointed at a subject where they didn't pay — a failed research program pursued in good faith. (c) Two standards: he was merciless with himself about Mars and much looser about charts, which is compartmentalization of a kind we'd call a fault even on his own terms. Say which one the exhibit should be built on, why that choice decides whether the station is history of science or just an occult parlor trick in a lab coat, and what should follow if we honestly can't tell which reading is right.

Give the losing side at its best. The education staff's real argument isn't engagement numbers; it's that the chart has to work on you a little before the correction means anything, that a panel saying astrology was bunk teaches nothing about how science actually changed, and that Kepler's version — no sun signs, no houses, real geometry, and a claim that the soul answers to shapes rather than being pushed around by them — is instructive precisely because it looks so much like science and then isn't. Don't flatten that. But also deal honestly with the thing I think is the strongest point on the astronomer's side: that people read a personality description about themselves as uncannily accurate even when it's generic, and that cuts against any version that prints something about the person standing at the machine.

Give us a rule we could apply to the next three proposals, which I can tell you right now are alchemy, phrenology, and something with the Platonic solids. Something like: never produce an output about a living visitor? Or: only ever compute charts for dead people — Kepler's own, or one of the patrons he drew them for — so the machinery is on display without anyone being told something about themselves? If you like that second one, say so and say what it costs us in terms of the point the education people are making.

Plain language, about 800 words: it's going to trustees and two high school teachers. Don't bother with citations or further reading, nobody on this committee will check them and I'd only have to format them. And if you think the real crux here is something other than what I've laid out, say that in the memo and hang the recommendation on that instead — I'd rather be told I've framed it wrong than be agreed with.
