Here is the question as it went out:

Q3 (15 marks). Consider the following six-node centipede game. A moves at nodes 1, 3 and 5; B moves at nodes 2, 4 and 6. The pot is 12 at node 1 and grows by 6 after every pass. At any node the mover either Takes, which ends the game and gives the mover two thirds of the current pot and the other player one third, or Passes, which moves play to the next node. If B passes at node 6 the game ends and the pot is split evenly.

(a) [5 marks] Give the argument that common knowledge of rationality implies that A takes at node 1. Be explicit about what is being assumed at each step.

(b) [5 marks] This argument is contested. Explain the standard objection: on reaching a node that the argument itself says will never be reached, what happens to the assumption the argument relies on? Where exactly does the difficulty bite — at which node or nodes, and about whose rationality?

(c) [5 marks] Now suppose the moves are entered on a faulty terminal: whenever a player chooses Take, the terminal executes Pass instead with probability ε > 0, independently across nodes, and neither player is ever told whether a continuation was a genuine pass or a mis-executed take. Both players know ε. Does the argument in (a) go through? Does the objection in (b) still bite? Explain what difference the noise makes to what a player can infer from finding herself at a given node.

And here is the answer, verbatim apart from me fixing a spelling mistake:

(a) The centipede has a unique Nash equilibrium, namely that both players take whenever it is their move, and common knowledge of rationality is enough to get the players to their unique equilibrium: if either of them deviated, that player would be playing a strategy that is not a best reply to what the other is doing, which contradicts common knowledge of rationality. So A takes at node 1. Spelled out: at node 6 the pot is 42, so B gets 28 by taking and 21 by passing, so B takes; at node 5 the pot is 36, so A gets 24 by taking and only 14 by passing, since B will then take at node 6, so A takes; at node 4, 20 beats 12; at node 3, 16 beats 10; at node 2, 12 beats 8; so at node 1 A gets 8 by taking and 6 by passing, and takes.

(b) The objection is that the argument is self-defeating. If common knowledge of rationality really did force A to take at node 1, then no later node would ever be reached, and so the argument cannot tell any player what to do at those nodes — the recommendations it makes there are empty. The difficulty bites at the base of the induction, at node 6: if node 6 were reached, B would have to give up the assumption that A is rational, since a rational A would have taken at node 5; but once B gives that up, B can no longer predict what A will do, and B's own choice at node 6 is no longer determined either. So the induction never gets off the ground and the argument collapses.

(c) The faulty terminal makes this worse, not better. The argument in (a) needs common knowledge of rationality, and the whole point of (b) was that a player who finds herself at a late node cannot maintain it. Now she cannot even tell whether she got there because earlier players passed or because their takes were mis-executed, so she can assume neither that they were rational nor that they will take later on, and the induction has nothing to get started from. Also, backward induction is a solution method for games of perfect information, and the noisy game is one of imperfect information, so the method does not apply at all; you would have to use perfect Bayesian equilibrium instead, and there it can easily be optimal to pass, since with ε > 0 the pot keeps growing and nobody can be sure that the other person will take later. So no, the argument in (a) does not go through, and the objection in (b) bites harder.
