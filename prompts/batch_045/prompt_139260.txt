Hi — I'm a journalist who writes mostly about psychology and decision science, and I'm doing a feature on big irreversible life choices (having a first kid, moving to another country for good, quitting a career mid-flight). Every philosopher I interview sends me to the same place, L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience, and I've read enough to get the headline: some experiences change you in ways you can't simulate from the inside, so you supposedly can't run the numbers on them.

What I need help with is the part that never survives the edit, which is: which bit of that is actually doing the work? As I understand it there are two changes bundled together. The epistemic one: you come to know what the experience is like, which you couldn't have got from any description or from any amount of imagining beforehand — the stock examples are a new colour, a new taste, a sense modality you've never had. And the personal one: your core preferences, values and projects get rewritten, so the person who ends up living with the outcome isn't quite the person who chose it.

Here are two cases I've been using to pull them apart, and I want to know whether they do what I think they do.

Case A, purely epistemic. You're offered a one-off: you get to smell a smell no human has ever smelled, for thirty seconds, and then never again. Nothing else about you changes — same tastes, same projects, same friends, same plans for next year. That looks like a full-blown instance of the epistemic transformation with none of the personal one, and to me it generates no interesting decision problem at all. I'd take a small chance on it for curiousity value and move on.

Case B, purely personal. A pill that leaves every experience you will ever have qualitatively just as it is — the same colours, the same music, the same expressions on your kid's face — but permanently rewrites your preferences: the music you find cloying now you'll love, the things you currently build your weekends around you'll find tedious, the ambitions you now treat as central will bore you. Nothing new gets revealed to you about what anything is like. You just end up wanting different things.

My hunch is that Case B is where the real trouble lives and Case A is close to a red herring for the decision problem — but I'm not confident, and I'd like you to tell me if I've built Case B so that it slips out of the argument by construction, or if I've accidentally described something that isn't coherent.

I also need real pushback on my own objection, because it sounds too easy and that makes me suspicious of it. If the problem is just that I don't know how much I'll like being a parent, why can't I do what we always do with an unknown payoff — put a probability distribution over the value of the outcome, informed by what other parents report and by what I know about myself, and maximize expected utility over that? Ignorance about a payoff is the most ordinary thing in the world; dealing with it is basically what decision theory is for. So what exactly is supposed to block that move? Give me the strongest version of the answer, and tell me what has to be true of the agent for the move to fail — I don't want it restated as the claim that you can't imagine what you can't imagine, because I already have that on tape from three people.

One last thing, in case it's load-bearing: the two stock examples everyone reaches for in these interviews are deciding whether to become a vampire and deciding whether to have a first child. People always add that in the vampire case you can at least get decent testimony from vampires who've been through it, whereas with parenthood the testimony is worthless because parents are unreliable reporters — sunk costs, adaptive preferences, motivated storytelling, whatever. Is that evidentiary difference doing any real work in the argument, or is it just local colour that survived because it's a good line?
