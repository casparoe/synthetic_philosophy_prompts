I've been reading rationalist blogs since the early days and arguing about diachronic rationality in comment threads on and off for fifteen years. I'm finally writing my position up properly, and before I commit it to a post I'd like someone to check whether the pieces actually fit together. Here is the set, stated as plainly as I can manage.

(1) I don't accept acyclicity — and therefore not transitivity — as a requirement of rationality on preferences. The money-pump argument, as I read it, shows only that cyclic preferences are expensive for an agent who chooses myopically, or who models her later selves as separate agents and reasons backwards from what they will do. An agent who settles on a plan at the top of the tree and then executes it needn't lose anything: she declines the opening trade and keeps what she has. The argument is an objection to a style of choosing in sequences, not to the ordering itself.

(2) More generally, I think policies rather than momentary acts are the unit of assessment. I one-box in Newcomb's problem; I'd pay the driver in the hitchhiker case; and in the absent-minded driver case, where the driver can't tell whether he's at the first or the second of two indistinguishable intersections, I think the plan chosen before setting off governs and that he shouldn't re-decide at the intersection using credences conditional on being there. I'm willing to act against my own all-things-considered preference at the moment of action when the policy requires it.

(3) But I don't think that licenses opting out of arguments I find inconvenient. I take the synchronic Dutch book argument for probabilism to be sound: if your credences violate the probability axioms, there is a set of bets, each acceptable by your own lights at the time it is offered, that together guarantee a loss, and I think that shows the credences to be defective and not merely unfortunate. I don't think the reply that one may simply decline to bet is a good one.

(4) Likewise, I think the diachronic Dutch book argument establishes conditionalization: if you plan to revise by any other rule, someone who knows the rule can arrange bets at the earlier and the later time, each acceptable when offered, that lose money for sure.

(5) I don't think it's irrational for your preferences to change over time, and I reject the idea that a later self has to defer to an earlier self's preferences merely because they came first. Where my values or tastes genuinely shift, I take the later self's verdict to govern what she does.

(6) Finally, the objection to (1) that I've met with most often, and that I don't think has any force. Take the ordinary three-item pump: I hold A, and I prefer B to A, C to B, and A to C; an exploiter offers to trade in the improving direction for a fee small enough that each individual trade is worth taking. The plans open to me — refuse everything and keep A; trade once and then stop; trade twice and then stop; go the whole way round — are themselves ordered in a cycle: each is beaten by the next, and the last is beaten by the first. So there is no optimal plan for the resolute agent to adopt, and every plan available to her is dominated by another. I don't see why this should worry me specifically about cyclic preferences. Someone who always prefers more money to less also has no best available option on any non-empty menu, and I don't think that makes her irrational either.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and where exactly does the work get done? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one tell me what would have to be given up and what that concession would cost me. If one of the six is equivocal — two claims run together that pull in different directions — say which. And if there's a single revision that would clean up more than one of the tensions at once, that's the one I most want to hear about. I'd rather find out now than in the comments.
