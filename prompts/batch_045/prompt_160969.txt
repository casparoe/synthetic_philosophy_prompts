hey, quick heads up: please answer in American English.

In the intro bio class I TA, and in basically every pop-sci thing I read, DNA gets called the code of life — a program, a blueprint, a four-letter language, a text you can read, edit (thats why CRISPR gets described as editing a document), corrupt (mutation as typo), and these days write from scratch. And Ive started to notice the framing is doing real argumentative work, not just decorating: people slide from DNA is a code to a genome contains the information for an organism, to we can read the organism off the sequence, to a synthesized genome just is the organism. I want to know how much of that slide the analogy can actually carry.

Please run the audit against three cases I made up rather than in the abstract (tell me if any of them isnt physically possible):

1. Two translation-only extracts, call them A and B. Same plasmid, same stretch of DNA dropped into each. A comes from an ordinary cell. B comes from a strain where the tRNAs and the enzymes that charge them have been re-engineered so a particular codon now picks up a different amino acid than usual. Same DNA, two different peptides. If the code is in the molecule, how is that possible?

2. A rewritten gene: I swap every codon for a synonymous one. Same amino acid sequence, same protein, but the DNA text is as different as it can be while still producing the same thing. Then a second version that uses codons this cell handles poorly — same protein again, but it gets made more slowly and folds worse. What happened to the message?

3. A stretch where two genes overlap in different reading frames. The same bases say two different things depending on where you start counting, and one point mutation is a damaging typo in one of them and silent in the other.

What I want out of it:

- The correspondence table: alphabet, letters, words, punctuation, synonyms, typos, grammar, author, reader, meaning, act of translation. What plays each role on the DNA side, and crucially which of them have to be supplied by something outside the DNA — the ribosome, the tRNAs, the charging enzymes, the rest of the cell, or us looking at it.
- Which disanalogies matter for the work the analogy is doing above and which are just nitpicks. Two I care about especially: the code is nearly but not quite universal, and the machinery that reads the code is itself written in the code. Is either of those a real problem for the analogy or just a cute observation?
- What the analogy quietly imports that DNA doesnt have: a sender, an intention, a reader who understands, a gap between vehicle and content, norms of correct vs incorrect reading, the idea that the sequence is about something.
- Whether my three slides survive. On the last one specifically: is genome plus a cell enough to read off an organism, or does the reading also depend on things not in the sequence — RNAs and proteins already sitting in the cytoplasm or the egg, the environment? Im thinking of those experiments where a chemically synthesized genome was put into a cell whose own DNA had been removed and the cell then behaved like the donor species. Does that show the genome is the organism's code, or only that the recipient cell supplied the reader?
- If the code picture fails somewhere, whats better — recipe, template, program, jig or tooling, musical score, something else — and does whatever you pick still let us say the genome carries information, or does that claim go down with the analogy?

Keep it concrete, argue it out, no citations or reading lists, I want the reasoning not sources.
