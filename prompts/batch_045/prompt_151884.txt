For most of my working life I studied shallow lakes, and I came to the commons literature sideways: a philosopher friend pressed Hardin's essay on me after I tried to explain a fishery collapse to him, and I have been trying ever since to state the moral core of the argument as cleanly as I can. Every time I tighten it I arrive at a conclusion I cannot accept. This is the version I am currently stuck on.

(1) An agent acts wrongly when she imposes costs on others that she does not herself bear and does not compensate. I take this internalisation principle to be what people are appealing to when they call an externality unjust, and I take it to be the moral engine of the commons argument rather than an optional addition to it.

(2) A herder who puts one more animal on a pasture held in common gains the whole value of that animal, bears one Nth of the damage the extra grazing does, and compensates nobody. So each such act is wrong; sum across herders and you have the tragedy.

(3) The principle is not about pastures. Every voluntary transaction moves prices, and so changes what others can do with what they have. If I buy grain at the going price, every other buyer's terms worsen marginally; if I accept a post, every other candidate's prospects worsen marginally; if I patent a result, everyone else's terms of use worsen marginally. In none of these cases do I bear the cost or compensate anyone.

(4) So (1) condemns competitive exchange wholesale. And it does so while the same broad tradition recommends competition as the device that allocates the very resources whose destruction the commons argument deplores. A principle that forbids the remedy it recommends for the disease it diagnoses seems to me to have gone wrong somewhere.

(5) The repair I was offered was that price effects are pecuniary rather than real: transfers, not destructions of value, whereas overgrazing destroys value. I think this repair fails, and this is where I get properly stuck. My one additional sheep is very largely a transfer too — I gain a sheep, the others lose a fraction of a sheep's worth of forage — and the net destruction traceable to my single act is of the order of one Nth of the total. For a large commons that is very nearly nothing. If the pecuniary/real distinction rescues the grain buyer in (3), the same arithmetic rescues the herder in (2), and the tragedy collapses into a large number of acts none of which does any real harm — which is just the inefficacy objection I had hoped the internalisation principle would let me escape.

(6) In the systems I used to work on the arithmetic is worse than this. Below a critical nutrient load a lake absorbs an insult with no measurable change; across the threshold it flips, and it does not return when the load is removed. So the damage attributable to one more unit of loading is not a smooth one Nth of anything: depending on where the system sits it is either indistinguishable from zero or catastrophic and largely irreversible. A principle whose application requires the magnitude of the cost my act imposes therefore gives no stable verdict in precisely the cases I care about; and restated in expected-value terms it inherits whatever I say about the probabilities, which are themselves collective products.

(7) So I appear to be left with a principle that is either strong enough to condemn all exchange or too weak to condemn anything in the commons, applied to a quantity that is ill-defined exactly where the commons is interesting.

I do not accept this. I would rather keep both the wrongness of overgrazing and the innocence of buying grain, even at some cost in elegance; and I would rather not be told that the difference between them is merely a matter of which property regime I happened to start from, though I cannot say why not.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Three things in particular. First, is (1) false, or merely incomplete — does imposing a cost have content only relative to a baseline of entitlements, and if so does the commons argument retain any independent force, or does it become a consequence of a prior theory of property that an advocate of open access can simply decline to grant? Second, is the step from (2) to (3) an equivocation — between worsening someone's terms of trade and worsening their prospects, or between failing to confer a benefit and taking something away — and what marks the difference if the arithmetic in (5) does not? Third, is there a reading of the principle that applies holistically, to practices or rules rather than to individual acts, and does my argument illicitly depend on an act-by-act structure it has no right to assume?

For each candidate rejection, please say what else it commits me to — about markets, about promising, about what we owe collectively — so that I can see which one does the least damage.
