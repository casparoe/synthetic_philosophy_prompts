hey — I'm writing something in philosophy of medicine (the naturalistic "disorder as dysfunction" sort of setup, Boorse-ish territory) and I keep getting stuck on the bit everyone skips over: what am I even allowed to demand of a theory of biological function? I keep lurching between the etiological / selected-effect family (Wright, Millikan, Neander-ish: a function is what the trait was selected for doing), the causal-role family (Cummins-ish: a function is what a part contributes to some capacity of a containing system), and the organizational / closure accounts that came later, but I don't have the standard by which to judge them, and I suspect half of what people demand in these debates is just their own camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement.

So write me the spec instead. Concretely: the theory is going to be used to (a) underwrite claims of the form "this trait is malfunctioning," including in neuro and psychiatric cases where nobody has a clean selection story to hand, and (b) license ordinary function-talk in physiology and molecular biology, where people say what a protein or a pathway is *for* all day long. Given that job, what does an adequate theory have to deliver — which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't collapse (having a function vs. how well it's performed, say), which results from biology it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a note on which are non-negotiable and which are preferences.

Two cases from my own material that I insist the list handle, because they're where I keep losing my grip. First: a cell built in a lab from a synthesized genome, no ancestors at all, whose parts do things that keep it alive. If a theory says nothing there has a function, is that a cost or a feature, and does it matter that the same theory is supposed to tell me when a patient's trait is broken? Second: a mutation that makes a cell lineage divide faster, which is genuinely selected for at the level of that lineage, and which kills the organism (think cancer, roughly) — whose function is it, and does the answer change if I ask as a doctor rather than as an evolutionary biologist.

I have a hunch that some subset of the desiderata can't be had together — that you can't get both "a brand-new organism with no history has functions" and "functions are the kind of thing that can be performed badly" out of one principle, and that the usual move is to split the concept into two notions and then quietly reach for whichever one the current case needs. If that's right say so, and say what we lose by admitting it. Then tell me which *single* requirement knocks out the most candidates — the one that's really doing the excluding — and which oft-repeated demand is actually negotiable. Short table scoring the three families at the end is fine, but keep it brief; the criteria are the part I care about.

And please don't hedge into a menu for me to pick from. Commit: rank the desiderata, say which one you'd throw out and why, take a position on whether the list is jointly satisfiable at all, and flag the place where you think you're most likely wrong and what would change your mind.
