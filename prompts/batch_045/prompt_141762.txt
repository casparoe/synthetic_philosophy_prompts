I'm stuck on a chapter in the novel I'm drafting, and I think the problem is that I keep writing the obvious version of the joke. The book is set on a made-up island republic — call it Estuaria: four years independent, one airport, two dialects that aren't quite mutually intelligible, and a Ministry of National Continuity that has comissioned a consultancy called Halvorsen & Vetch to supply it with traditions. My protagonist is a mid-level Continuity Officer, and the chapter I can't get right is going to be a leaked internal document: the onboarding FAQ the firm hands new hires before they're sent out to the provinces. Every time I write it, it comes out as "haha, the tradition is fake," which is about as deep as a wet napkin, and it also makes my protagonist look like a cynic when she's really a sincere professional who is quite good at her job.

So could you write that document for me? Around 800 words, structured as a short briefing memo at the top followed by numbered questions and answers, plus two or three footnotes in which two senior partners quietly snipe at each other about whether this sort of work should be directed outward from the capital or left to well up from below — I want that disagreement left genuinely unresolved and both of them to sound like they know what they're doing, because in my head neither of them is the fool. The voice is a competent, mildly exhausted consultant who never once winks at the reader; whatever is funny has to come from the mechanics being gotten right, not from anyone announcing the thesis. Mechanics I have in mind — take the ones that work and add better ones of your own: the house rule that you always graft a new observance onto a practice that already exists, because pure invention doesn't take, and the resulting moment where a genuinely old local custom gets flattened into one approved standard version; a census whose categories quietly produce the very groups it then tabulates, alongside a map department determined that the blank interior of the country be filled in with a repeating pattern; a dispute about which dialect becomes the national language that gets settled by what the capital's presses can actually set and which readership a newspaper needs in order to sell copies; a national epic recovered from a manuscript and then taught in schools as something the grandparents already knew, with the required forgetting handled as a billable line item; and a rival firm accused of reusing the same nation kit across three young states, which the author of the handbook defends rather than denies. I've been reading a bit of Hobsbawm and Anderson in the background — invented traditions being ritualized, quasi-obligatory, and paraded as immemorial, print capitalism, the census and the map and the museum, solidarity felt sideways among people who will never meet each other, the way a nation's shape gets borrowed rather than grown — so aim it at a reader who knows that material without ever letting the document name it, and please don't let anyone say the word "invented" anywhere the client might hear it.

Give it to me in American English, in three sections: "The Handbook" (the document itself, ~800 words); "What Each Entry Is Keying Off" (~150 words, one line per entry on the real mechanism it's exaggerating, so I can tell which gags are fair and which are cheap); and "Things I'd Have To Change" (~100 words, flagging any entry that lands too close to a real country's actual history, or any place where I've just got the mechanics wrong).
