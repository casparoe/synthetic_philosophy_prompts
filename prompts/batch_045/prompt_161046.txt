I work at a research institute that allocates money to long-termist interventions, and I have now watched the debate over consequentialist cluelessness go round in the same circle for about a decade. Let me lay out the positions as they actually get defended, because I want the diagnosis to engage those and not a caricature.

On one side: for any action available to us, the causal tree branches far enough into the future that we cannot recover even the sign of the total effect; the near-term consequences we can forecast are a small and, crucially, systematically unrepresentative portion of the whole. So the very far-future-sensitivity that drives this style of consequentialism leaves us without a verdict in practice. In its stronger form the claim is that acting on near-term evidence may be no better than choosing at random, because the near-term component is a biased sample of the total rather than a reliable proxy for its sign.

On the other side there are, I think, four distinct replies that I have never once seen separated or made to answer to each other. First, the near-term tie-breaker: we are not clueless about near-term effects, and where the long-run balance is unknown the known component should decide. Second, robustness: choose the option that does best across the range of long-run hypotheses we cannot rule out, which frequently yields an answer even when no single expectation can be computed. Third, the parity or reductio reply: if the objection worked it would disable not consequentialism but any decision procedure whatsoever, including ordinary commonsense morality, so it proves too much. Fourth, the change-of-rule reply: if precise expectations are unavailable, that is an argument against maximizing expected value, not against consequentialism, since a consequentialist is free to adopt a maximin or ambiguity-averse rule, or to aggregate over moral uncertainty about which decision rule is correct. Alongside these sits a fifth, quieter move, which treats cluelessness as a contingent empirical gap that better forecasting and long-run follow-up of interventions will narrow, rather than as a structural feature of action.

Two things strike me. The third and fourth replies pull in opposite directions — one denies the objection has any bite, the other concedes the bite and relocates it — and I have never seen anyone choose between them, which makes me suspect the side I call the other side is not really one side. And everyone involved appears to agree on all the substantive claims about what we can and cannot predict, which is the signature of a dispute that is not about the facts.

My hypothesis, which I offer only as one, is that cluelessness names at least three stacked disagreements: a verbal one about what clueless means, since sign-indeterminacy, expectation-indeterminacy and action-indeterminacy come apart and the arguments slide between them; a substantive one about which decision rule is correct under severe uncertainty; and a hidden empirical one about the shape of long-run causal effects, which nobody has tried to state precisely enough to test.

I want to add two devices of my own, because I suspect they change what the question is. Call an option refinement-fragile when the sign of its net long-run effect is fixed not by our evidence but by the granularity of the outcome space: for every coarse partition of future states into those that speak for the option and those that speak against it, there is a refinement that splits some cell into sub-states differing in the balance, under which the verdict flips, and a refinement of that refinement under which it flips back. If cluelessness is really refinement-fragility, it is not an evidential deficit that more forecasting would cure, and not straightforwardly a fact about the world's causal structure either; it is a feature of aggregating over a partition whose fineness is unsettled. On that reading, asking whether the sign of an effect is knowable is ill-posed until someone says which partition of outcomes is authoritative, and as far as I can tell no one on either side has ever said.

Second stipulation, as a probe: suppose longitudinal social science eventually established, for a broad class of interventions, that the distribution of long-run aggregate effects is roughly symmetric about zero and heavy-tailed enough that the mean is undefined. Would that settle the dispute, deepen it, or leave it precisely where it is? My guess is that it would expose expectation-maximization as unavailable while leaving the practical recommendations of both sides unchanged, which would suggest the live disagreement was never about evidence at all.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it verbal, substantive, a collision of different questions being asked under one name, or a hidden formal or empirical question doing all the work? Does refinement-fragility describe what the clueless side is actually pointing at, or is it a different problem wearing the same label? Tell me which of the four replies is load-bearing, which is rhetorical, and whether the fifth move — treat it as an empirical gap — is even addressing the same question. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction between kinds of indeterminacy, a theorem about when sign is invariant under refinement, an empirical result about the distribution of long-run effects, a decision rule stated over coarse partitions, or an abandonment of the question. If your answer is that no progress should be expected and the question is simply ill-posed, I want that argued for, not asserted. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for anything to read; I have read the papers. I want the structure of the disagreement.
