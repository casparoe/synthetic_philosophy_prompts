I run a small reading group on risk and rationality with some people from the finance department, and one of them presented an argument last week that I've been mulling over since and can't quite get out of. It's supposed to be a straight argument for geometric rationality — maximize expected log, not expected value — and I think it definately proves too much, but I can't put my finger on the exact joint. Roughly as he gave it: (1) Take a favourable multiplicative gamble you can repeat: any fraction of your bankroll on a coin that comes up heads with p = 0.6 at even money. The rule that maximizes the expected logarithm of your bankroll — betting 20% each round, i.e. edge over odds — will with probability one eventually leave you richer than a bettor using any other fixed fraction, and the ratio between the two of you grows without bound. (2) The moral of that theorem is supposed to be about what a single individual actually lives through: your wealth is one trajectory, and what happens to it over time is governed by its time-average growth rate, not by the average across a hypothetical population of copies of you, which is dragged around by outcomes almost nobody experiences. Expected wealth maximization is maximizing a fiction. (3) But — and here's where I start to itch — the number of times you face a given choice can't be what makes a rule rational. If maximizing expected log is the right policy when you'll take the bet a hundred times, it's the right policy when you take it once; the long run is just a device that exposes what the correct policy is, it isn't a separate ingredient in the justification. (4) So in any decision whatever, maximize the expected log of the quantity your decision multiplies. (5) Expected log is minus infinity if any outcome drives that quantity to zero, so rationality forbids accepting any gamble carrying any nonzero probability of total loss of that quantity, however tiny the probability and however large the finite gain. (6) That's preposterous. There's some chance that driving to campus I get disabled in a way that destroys my earning capacity, and my earning capacity is most of the quantity that compounds over the rest of my life; by (5) no finite benefit could ever justify the drive. Same for the surgery I've been putting off, same for having a second kid. A rule that forbids every action anyone ever takes isn't the rule. (7) So one of (1)–(6) has to go, or one of the steps between them.

Things I've tried that don't satisfy me. The finance guy says you just define the "bankroll" narrowly — the money earmarked for this kind of bet — and then nothing is ever driven to zero and (5) never bites; but (1) was a claim about your whole wealth process, and if I'm allowed to choose the quantity freely it looks like I can get the rule to say anything I like, which is just a version of the problem rather than a solution. I also don't think the difficulty is really about repetition as such, because I can construct cases where the timing is what's odd: my colleague's own example was a 78-year-old with no heirs deciding whether to put 5% of her savings on a single even-money 60/40 bet, where the proceeds will be spent on care within the year and never reinvested in anything, and I made up one of my own where the stake isn't a compounding quantity at all — a drug that might cost me my eyesight. Call it the reinvestment condition, if you like: the theorem needs a quantity that actually gets multiplied, over and over, into itself. My question: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when he wrote it on the board, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than (3) or (4), and I'd rather not end up saying that what's rational for me to do on Monday depends on how many similar decisions the universe is going to hand me. And if (3) does go: is there any residue of geometric rationality that still applies to a genuinely one-off, non-compounding choice, or does the whole program only get a grip inside sequences?
