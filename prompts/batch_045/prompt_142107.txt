Hey — I'm putting together a short statement of where I actually stand on discounting the future for a policy reading group I'm in, and before I hand it to people whose job is to poke holes in it, I'd like to know whether it hangs together or whether I'm just reaching for whichever bit supports whatever I already wanted to conclude. Here's the list, more or less in the order I thought of it:

(1) I think pure time preference is straight-up irrational. Caring less about someone's suffering because it happens in 2200 rather than 2026 looks to me exactly like caring less because it happens in another country. The date is not a reason, any more than the latitude is.

(2) That said, I'm totally comfortable knocking a big chunk off the value of very distant benefits because of the chance that we blow ourselves up or things slide backward before they arrive. I'd count a benefit landing in 2300 at maybe half of what the same benefit would be worth if it landed in 2050.

(3) When my city ran a cost-benefit analysis on a flood barrier — on the order of $80m now to avoid an estimated $300m in damages, most of it in the back half of the next eighty years — I genuinely didn't know what to think, and I didn't feel entitled to just wave away the people saying that at 5% those future damages aren't worth $80m today. The opportunity-cost-of-money thing seems to me to have something real in it.

(4) I think we should be spending a lot more right now on stuff whose payoff mostly shows up in the 22nd century: long-lived climate stuff, biodiversity, keeping bad technologies from getting locked in. Current policy basically ignores those people, and I think that's a serious moral failure, not just a inefficiency.

(5) I think it's perfectly reasonable — not a failure of rationality at all — for a person to care much less about their own comfort thirty years from now than about their comfort this year. My reason is roughly that the guy in thirty years will be recognizably but only loosely the same person as me; I'll be a good deal further from him than I am from the person who woke up this morning.

(6) I also dont think the bare fact that something happens to *me* rather than to a stranger is itself a reason to weight it more heavily. In a straight tradeoff between my mild inconvenience and a stranger's comparable mild inconvenience, mine doesn't win just by being mine. (I do think I'm allowed to favor my own kids, and I don't think that's the same thing.)

(7) I'm suspicious of "we're harming our grandchildren" as the framing for why we owe the future anything, since what we do now changes which people end up existing. So I don't think that's where the obligation comes from.

(8) At the same time I recoil from what a very low discount rate seems to imply — that we should be saving and investing a large share of output for people who will probably be much richer than us. That strikes me as nuts, and it's honestly the main thing pushing me to keep some positive discount rate around. And I've noticed a related thing that I half like and half think is a reductio: if the market return really is 5%, then the best move per dollar spent on the 22nd century is to put the dollar in a trust and let it compound, not to spend it on mitigation now.

So: where does this not add up? I have a nagging feeling that (2) is doing more work for me than it should, and that (5) might not survive (1) and (6), but I don't trust either of those feelings. Please rank whatever tensions you actually find by how severe they are, tell me for each one what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me, and flag the pairs that only *look* like they conflict — I'd rather get a real audit than a list where everything is equally a problem. Also: tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming overall, and single out the one point you're least sure about. Don't hedge uniformly across the board, that's useless to me.
