I am writing a paper on the norm of assertion, and before I look at the candidate norms I want the standard of adequacy settled. Please write that specification: the requirements a proposed norm of assertion would have to meet to count as adequate for the job described below, each stated with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as non-negotiable, negotiable, or merely a preference of one camp.

The job, so that adequacy has a fixed sense: I want a norm that (i) states the conditions under which an assertion is correct, and the conditions under which it is permissible — tell me whether a single norm must supply both or whether two separate norms are legitimate — (ii) can be appealed to by critics, audiences, and the speaker herself, in the sense that it explains the peculiar awkwardness of Moorean-paradoxical utterance, the propriety of challenges like "how do you know?", and the pressure to retract once an assertion is shown to be false, and (iii) is not required to function as a first-personal decision procedure for a speaker wondering what to say. I also want coverage beyond the central case of one adult human sincerely and flatly telling another that p. The norm must be applicable to hedged assertion; to assertion of vague content and of moral or aesthetic content; to first-person future-directed assertion; to assertion whose content the speaker grasps only by deference to an expert, an instrument, or a readout; and to the outputs of composite or artificial speakers — a press office, a jointly authored statement, a conversational AI assistant — where there may be no single mind whose epistemic state is in question.

Cases and distinctions I expect the requirement list to be answerable to, which you should reorganize into genuine desiderata rather than simply inherit: flatly asserted lottery propositions; warranted but false assertion; so-called selfless assertion, where the speaker has no stake in the truth and the audience no interest in it; assertion by deference, as above; hedged and explicitly probabilistic assertion; the boundary between assertion and its neighbours — guessing, supposing for the sake of argument, conceding, joking, acting as if — which the norm must not collapse; the difference between an assertion's being incorrect and its author's being blameworthy; and the difference between having a status and having the degree of it, if assertion admits of degrees.

Then the part I care about most. Flag any pair or triple of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that trouble lies between three demands: that the norm be factive in the sense that truth or knowledge is required for correctness, that it be such that a speaker can normally tell whether she satisfies it, and that it be one unified norm across every kind of content I listed. If a subset is unsatisfiable, say which member you would drop, what its loss costs, and whether the resulting list is still worth having. Also separate the genuine desiderata from the prejudices: is it truly required that the norm be a constitutive rule of a practice rather than an evaluative standard, that it be a single norm rather than a correctness condition plus a permissibility condition, that it vindicate the thought that assertion is the expression of belief, or that it not lean on the very epistemic notion it is meant to illuminate? And tell me which single requirement excludes the largest share of otherwise popular views.

Close with a table, no more than one line per cell, recording how the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the justified- or reasonable-belief norm, and any contextualist or knowledge-plus-sincerity hybrid do against the list. Where you are unsure of the details of some view, mark the cell uncertain rather than reconstructing the view from plausibility; I would rather have a gap in the table than a confident mistake. No citations, references, or reading suggestions anywhere — I know that literature, and it is the criteria I am missing.
