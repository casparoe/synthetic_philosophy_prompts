I am trying to keep apart two claims that discussions of Solomonoff induction tend to run together, and I would like an explanation that separates them cleanly.

The first claim is the convergence-and-dominance result: the universal semimeasure dominates every enumerable semimeasure up to a multiplicative constant, and expected cumulative squared prediction error is bounded by a quantity on the order of the Kolmogorov complexity of the true environment. The second is that this vindicates Occam's razor. I want to know precisely what the vindication consists in, and I suspect one standard reassurance is doing more work than it can bear.

The reassurance is that the choice of reference machine does not matter, since description lengths differ only by an additive constant across universal machines. But consider a universal machine U_N that is like an ordinary U except that k bits of its program space are hard-wired to the sequence consisting of N zeros followed by all ones. Relative to U_N the Solomonoff predictor assigns a fixed, non-negligible probability, on the order of 2^-k, to the wrong continuation at step N+1 on the simplest possible data stream, all zeros; that error probability does not shrink as N grows, and it exceeds the error probability of the U-based predictor at the same step by a factor of roughly N. Meanwhile the complexity of U_N relative to U grows only like log N. So the constant is not a fixed overhead that enough data eventually swamps; it is more like a logarithmically priced budget with which one can buy an arbitrarily large, arbitrarily late predictive failure.

Please explain:

(a) whether this construction is fair and, if not, exactly where it goes wrong. In particular, say whether the dominance result or the cumulative-error bound rules out what I have described or merely permits it, and what that tells us about what the theorem is a theorem about.

(b) what follows for the predictive reading of the razor, on which simplicity pays because simple environments are predictively cheap, as against the inferential reading, on which simplicity is a guide to which hypothesis is true. Note that in the deterministic setting there appears to be no graded notion of fit, hence no clean counterpart of preferring the simplest among hypotheses that accommodate the data equally well.

(c) whether the relevant simplicity is that of the environment's generating measure or of the hypothesis an agent would state, given that many programs compute the same function, so that posterior mass over programs is not a posterior over hypotheses.

A good answer should state clearly what the convergence theorem establishes and what it is silent on.
