Here is the proposition I want your credence on: an agent like us — finite, uncertain, and committed to expected-utility reasoning as a first approximation — encounters the canonical mugger (a stranger asks for five dollars and claims, offering nothing you could check, that refusing will cost 3^^^3 lives); that agent should decline; and it should decline for a reason that does not, on reflection, also deflate the large-scale expected-value arguments I am unwilling to give up — existential risk, the value of steering a very long future, that family of arguments. By "should" I mean what the correct normative procedure would endorse, not what a person would in fact do; and I am not asking for a tour of the available positions.

I have sat at roughly 0.7 on this for years, and it wobbles toward 0.6 or 0.85 depending on how recently I thought about the cases below, mostly because I cannot tell which fix is actually carrying the weight. So decompose it. At minimum:

- your credence that no-pay is the right verdict at all, setting the generalization question aside;
- conditional on no-pay, a distribution over which move does the work — a bounded utility function; a prior that penalizes claimed leverage or scope; a ceiling on achievable Bayes factors (the thought that no conversation can carry enough evidence to lift a prior of order 1/3^^^3); symmetric cancellation of the kind you would get if a second stranger claimed that paying causes 3^^^3 deaths; or something else, which you should name;
- your credence that whichever move you favor generalizes correctly, i.e. deflates the mugger without gutting astronomical-scale arguments about the long-term future.

I also want you to handle two wrinkles I have never seen dealt with cleanly. First, the serial case: suppose you meet an unending sequence of muggers whose claimed stakes grow faster than any fixed-rate leverage discount, so that a penalty merely proportional to claimed impact — or even to its logarithm — still leaves you handing over everything you own. Does this force the penalty to be superlinear in claimed impact, and if it does, is a superlinear penalty just a utility bound smuggled in under another name? Second, the counter-mugger: if symmetric unheard claims genuinely cancel, then whether you pay seems to hinge on the accident of which stranger happened to approach you, which looks like the wrong thing for the verdict to depend on; but if they do not cancel, you owe me an account of which class of claims you are permitted to count and why.
