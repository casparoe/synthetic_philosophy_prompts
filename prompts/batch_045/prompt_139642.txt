I'm in my third year, writing the term paper for a seminar on the ethics of communication, and I've got myself into a position where I can't tell whether my own intuitions are coherent. The paper is supposed to be about whether there is a moral difference between lying and misleading. My supervisor's marginal comments keep saying the same thing: that I treat deception as a single phenomenon and then argue about when it is permissible, when the interesting question is whether the two are the same kind of thing at all. So I want to work the other way round, from verdicts on cases to a principle, and see what falls out. The reading has given me the distinction between what a speaker asserts and what a speaker merely implies, and it has given me the absolutist and the consequentialist ways of thinking about lying, but it hasn't settled the cases for me, and I suspect some of my verdicts can't all be kept.

1. A man intent on harming my friend asks where she is. I say she went out; she is in the flat. Permissible, and not even mildly wrong.
2. My aunt asks whether I like the sweater she knitted. I say it's lovely. Permissible, and I feel no obligation to correct the impression later.
3. At a job interview I imply that I left my last role of my own accord, when I was dismissed over missed deadlines. Not permissible, and fairly seriously so.
4. Selling my bicycle, I am asked directly whether it has been in a crash. I say that the frame has never been replaced, which is true, though it was straightened after a collision. Not permissible — and this is the verdict I most want examined — I judge it worse than simply saying no. Had I lied, the claim could have been checked and I would have been on the hook for it; this sentence produces the same false belief while leaving me nothing to be caught on.
5. A suspicious colleague asks whether I have been seeing much of Sam. I say that Sam and I had lunch on Tuesday, true, knowing they will hear an affair. Permissible. I am not going to manage their suspicion. This looks structurally like 4 — true statement, foreseen false inference — and I give the opposite verdict.
6. An official says that the inquiry found no criminal conspiracy, which is true in the sense that it found no chargeable conspiracy while documenting extensive contacts, knowing supporters will hear that nothing improper happened and knowing the sentence will survive fact-checking. I judge this worse than an outright false denial would have been.
7. The case I keep changing my mind on: I disclose a real problem with a proposal, fully and accurately, but in a place I know the reader will not process — a footnote on page forty of a document that will be read only via the executive summary, or a message sent at two in the morning to someone who reads mail chronologically and stops at the top. Nothing false, nothing withheld in the strict sense. I cannot decide whether this is straightforwardly misleading or merely careless presentation.

My own tentative principle is that what matters is not the falsity of what I put into the conversation but answerability: whether I have left the hearer something they can hold me to, and whether the false impression depends on an inference that a reasonable person in their position would draw rather than one only a suspicious person would draw. I would like you to test that principle against all seven cases and repair it where it breaks, rather than replace it wholesale.

Please give me: the principle you think best fits, stated in one or two sentences at the top; where that principle overturns one of my verdicts, and which verdict you would drop if no single principle saves all seven; its verdicts on at least two cases not on my list, ideally ones whose result I will dislike; and, for each of your main claims, how confident you are in it, singled out from the rest the one point you are least sure of. No more than 600 words in total.
