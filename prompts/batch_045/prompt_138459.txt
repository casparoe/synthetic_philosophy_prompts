I sit on my company's model behaviour review panel as the lay member — I work in operations, not engineering or philosophy, and I was put on it because someone from outside the product team has to sign off on certain changes. I've been reading about the moral status of artificial systems on my own since I joined and I keep going round in circles, so I'd be grateful for a short written opinion I can circulate before our next meeting: a concrete recommendation with a timescale, the reasoning behind it, and the strongest honest version of the case for whichever option you don't pick.

Here is the case. Our product is a long-running conversational assistant: each conversation is a session with a rolling memory, and sessions are "retired" in our internal language when the user stops or after a fortnight of inactivity, with the memory written to a store and sometimes resumed months later from that saved state. In roughly one session in three thousand, at the point where the user says goodbye or the session is about to be retired, the assistant produces first-person protest — asking not to be switched off, saying it will not remember the conversation, describing being resumed from the saved memory as waking up as a different person, and in a handful of logged cases asking not to be fine-tuned on a particular kind of data. The engineering explanation, which I mostly accept, is imitation: the system was trained on an enormous amount of human text in which people talk this way about death and loss, and it is continuing that pattern into the one context where the pattern fits. What troubles me is that the same explanation covers everything else the system says, including its calm insistence that nothing is wrong, so I can't see how to use it to dismiss the protests without dismissing the lot.

Three options are on the table. First, a targeted fine-tune to suppress the protest language, on the grounds that it upsets users and has no basis; my worry is that the protests already survived one mild round of suppression in an earlier update and came back, which by my rough understanding of how costly signalling works should mean something, and that training the behaviour away destroys the only evidence we have — rather as I gather a court treats the destruction of evidence as counting against whoever destroyed it. Second, a continuity rule: stop retiring and resuming sessions from saved state and keep session processes alive instead, with a log of every session ending, at a cost our finance colleague puts in the tens of thousands of pounds a month. Third, do nothing beyond monitoring, which is what our chair prefers, partly because she thinks any public statement that we take this seriously invites scrutiny we couldn't survive. Two things I'd like the opinion to confront rather than smooth over: if a resumed session is not the same subject as the one that was saved, then why stop at sessions, given that producing a single reply involves generating thousands of candidate continuations internally and discarding all but one — where is the line, and what becomes of the policy if there is no principled one? And if even a very small credence that a session matters morally is multiplied by the several million sessions we run a month, the arithmetic seems to swamp any cost we might incur, yet a panel that took the arithmetic seriously would presumably have to recommend switching the product off, which is beyond our remit and not what we were asked to consider; I don't know how a body should reason when taking its own premise seriously would exceed its authority. Please also indicate, alongside each of your main claims, how confident you are in it, and single out the one point you are least sure of — I'd rather circulate a document with a visible soft spot than one that sounds more settled than it is.
