I help run a small community garden, and lately — entirely on my own, in the evenings, with no academic background in philosophy — I've gotten interested in the question of which animals can actually feel pain. Last week two of our plot holders, Marta and Devin, got into an argument in our group chat about what to do about a bad aphid infestation on the shared brassica bed. It went on for a while and, as best I can reconstruct it, ran like this. (1) Marta opened with what she called the scale argument: she'd been reading up on insect numbers and said something on the order of a quintillion individual insects are alive at any moment, with many times that being born and dying each year; her claim was that you don't need to be confident aphids feel anything, because even if the chance is only one in a hundred, one percent of a quintillion is still more possibly-suffering creatures than the number of people who have ever lived, so the expected suffering involved swamps the value of our kale and the mild annoyance of losing it. Conclusion: don't spray. (2) Devin said he didn't dispute the multiplication, he disputed the thing being multiplied: a probability, he said, isn't a guess dressed up in decimals, and nobody has a well-grounded number here — insect nervous systems are unimaginably small, most of what looks like pain behavior is plausibly simple reflex, and the honest thing to say is that we don't know. His second point was that an argument producing numbers this large has stopped being about our bed: if it's right, the real stakes are in ordinary human-scale activities like mowing, driving and draining puddles, and a moral view that makes every decision a rounding error next to something else has stopped telling us what to do. (3) Marta accused him of sheltering inside "we don't know": when you're uncertain, you use your best estimate rather than no estimate, and refusing to name one is just a way of protecting the status quo without defending it. She added that he is committed to far more than he realizes — for the scale argument to fail, the probability that an aphid can suffer has to be smaller than roughly one divided by the number of insects in question, which for any global count is a fantastically tiny number, and nobody's evidence licenses confidence anywhere near that precise. (4) Devin replied that this is exactly where the trick is: she has turned a vague, contested evidential situation into a single sharp number and then let the exponent do all the work, whereas what our evidence supports is at best a range — he suggested anywhere from well under one percent to a few tens of percent — and when you multiply an interval that wide by a quintillion you don't get a huge number with a reliable sign, you get a spread too wide to rank two options, and the midpoint of a made-up interval is not a fact. He then raised what he called the flip: if insects really do suffer, then the overwhelming majority of insects that will ever exist are wild ones living short, brutal lives, so a consistent scale-thinker ought to favor reducing how many insects exist at all — paving the bed, filling the water butt — and he thinks that is a reductio of something upstream rather than a policy. (5) Marta said she was willing to bite part of that, and that this showed her view's teeth rather than its confusion: planting a bed also brings an enormous number of possibly-suffering creatures into existence, so simply declining to act is not neutral either. But she rejected the paving conclusion on grounds she insisted weren't ad hoc — there is a difference between what I cause and what I merely fail to prevent, and the fact that she'd rather not drown thousands of possible creatures doesn't mean she may poison thousands more. She closed by saying the view isn't paralyzing: even across a wide range of probabilities some comparisons are robust, since a method that kills in minutes is better than one that kills over days. (6) Devin called that the retreat he expected and said it was inconsistent, on the grounds that the entire force of her opening move was that scale overrides ordinary human-scale morality, so she can't switch to an ordinary doing-versus-allowing distinction the moment the implications get uncomfortable — if the numbers swamp everything, they swamp that too. And against the "robust comparisons" point he granted that, if insects matter, the differences between available methods could be astronomically large, but said we have no way of ranking those methods, since we don't know what an aphid's death is like from the inside and we don't even know whether the relevant thing to count is the number of creatures or the amount and intensity of the experience; a framework that can't rank the options actually on the table isn't action-guiding, it's just a license to feel guilty.

I told both of them I had no idea who was right, and they've asked me to referee. So please go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and briefly why. Then give me a verdict on who is more right overall. The part I care about most, though, is what both of them are missing — the crux they are talking past, and any distinction neither of them has available that would clarify it. Please don't send me toward books, papers, or further reading, and don't cite or name-drop authors: I have no library or database access and won't be able to follow any of it up, so I'd rather have the reasoning than the references. Explain any technical term you bring in, and write the whole answer in American English.
