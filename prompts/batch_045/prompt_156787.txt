I'm taking a decision-theory seminar, and my section presents next week on infinite expectations. I'd like to open with a poem rather than a definition, because the point I keep failing to convey in prose is why "the expected value is undefined" is a stranger verdict than "the expected value is infinite" — St. Petersburg at least returns an answer, however unusable.

What I want is eight quatrains, loose iambic tetrameter, that carry the Pasadena game term by term: the stake doubling with each tail before the first head, the sign alternating with the parity of the flip count, the probability halving so that each outcome's contribution to the sum is the reciprocal of that count. Each quatrain should be syntactically movable — no pronoun or connective reaching into a neighbour, so that any permutation of the eight stanzas is still a grammatical poem — and a refrain line should recur with its sign flipped (what is owed to me; what I owe), so the alternation does structural work instead of being merely reported.

The closing couplet should name what the game is worth, and the movable stanzas should make that verdict feel like a hostage to arrangement: a reader who shuffles the quatrains should be able to hear a different couplet being licensed. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows why the Lebesgue integral is defined only under absolute convergence wouldn't wince, and honest about the fact that under the natural ordering the sum is ln 2 — roughly sixty-nine cents.

Last thing: our seminar split between people who think that natural ordering is privileged and people who think none is, so that "undefined" really means the theory has nothing to say. Don't settle it. Write it so both camps hear their own position in it, and let the ambiguity land in the couplet rather than in a footnote.
