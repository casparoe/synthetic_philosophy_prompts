My roomate and I spent about three hours last night fighting about Malthus and got nowhere, so I want the document we should have produced instead. He says Malthus was right but early: the mechanism (population presses against subsistence, positive and preventive checks restore the balance) is the correct account of essentially all of human history up to the industrial escape, and the fact that we escaped doesn't refute a theory about a world that had already ended. I say that's a textbook retrofitted domain restriction: the escape hatch was always available, Malthus never said when the mechanism applies and when it doesn't, and a long-run prediction you're permitted to stop applying the moment it misses hasn't predicted anything.

So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a Malthusian domain-restricter (right-but-early) and a falsificationist (retrofitted-rescue) on whether "right but early" is ever a legitimate verdict on a long-run predictive theory.

What I want in it:

- One sentence at the top that both of us would sign as the headline thing we actually agree on.
- A numbered list of agreed claims: about what Malthus asserted, about what a mechanism-level reading of him commits you to versus an epoch-level one, about the shape of the pre-industrial record, and about what "prediction" has to mean here (a forecast, or just an entailment of the model). Nothing vacuous, nothing slanted — if my friend would balk at a line, rewrite it until he wouldn't.
- The residual disagreement stated twice, once in the Malthusian's own words and once in the falsificationist's, each phrased so that side would accept it as fair.
- A cruxes table. For each crux: the sub-question, something both sides agree would actually bear on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two devices I invented on the walk home — use them if they help, cut them if they're unfair:

1. Malthus's sealed envelope: alongside the Essay he deposits a statement of the conditions under which he would count himself refuted, to be opened only later. Is that a fair test? What would have to be in the envelope for it to be non-trivial? What does each side predict we'd find in it?
2. The shape-and-timing clause: "right but early" earns credit only if the theory, given information available before the escape, also gets roughly the century and the shape of the escape right — wages decoupling from population, fertility falling as income rises rather than with it. Does either side accept that burden, and what happens to the dispute if they do?
