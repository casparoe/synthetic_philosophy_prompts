I am a novelist, and I am drafting a novel whose narrator — herself a novelist, and in no way a philosopher — has to defend, in a long radio interview she cannot walk out of, a book of hers that everyone agrees is morally compromised and everyone also agrees is very good. I want the philosophical spine of that scene to bear weight. I do not want a literature survey: my narrator would never cite anyone, and I have no way of checking references. What I want is a thought experiment — in fact, a fictional novel — built to specifications, that I can then put in her mouth.

Please construct it under the following constraints.

1. Contemporary literary realism. No genre machinery, no science fiction, no framing devices, no dead or disgraced authors to do the work. It should be a book that could plausibly have been published and reviewed in the last decade, and you should give it a title and a two-sentence description in the register of a publisher's blurb.

2. Keep the case short: no more than 250 words of scenario, and make it a case that survives summary. My narrator will have maybe forty seconds of airtime; if the moral problem cannot be conveyed except by quoting several pages of prose, the case is useless to me.

3. The moral vice must be in the work and not in the maker. I want nothing to do with the biography question — no bad men, no revelations about the author, no downstream harms in the world, no doctrines the book asserts. So the vice cannot be a proposition the text states, an action a character performs, or an effect the book causes.

4. Put the vice at the level of craft. What is morally off about the book should be a mode of attention it trains in its reader: the management of narrative distance, the distribution of sympathy, the rhythm of disclosure, the way free indirect style lets a reader occupy a perspective she would refuse if offered it directly. And that trained attention must be portable: it is the same attention the reader would then give an actual person, and she leaves the book with it.

5. That same feature must be what makes the book good. The aesthetic power and the moral vice must be one thing, not two that happen to coexist — and the case must generate this rather than stipulate it.

6. The case must let all three standard postures say something true about the same feature, with none of them collapsing on contact. By autonomism I mean: moral flaws are never aesthetic flaws. By moderate moralism I mean: a moral flaw counts as an aesthetic flaw only when it interferes with the work's securing the response it aims at. By immoralism I mean: a moral flaw can be an aesthetic merit. Show me what each says here and why, and tell me what, exactly, they are disagreeing about.

After the scenario, give me the walkthrough, then two ablations: remove the portability of the trained attention, and separately move the vice from form to content. Say which verdicts change in each, and why. Then state, as strongly as you honestly can, the best attack available to whichever posture comes out of your case worst.

Finally, give me a verdict and not a menu. I want one paragraph, flatly written and without hedging, saying which of the three you take to be right about this case and why — my narrator has to commit herself on air — followed by a short note on where your construction leans on something contestable, for instance on whether habits of attention are morally assessable at all, or on what it is for a work to prescribe a response. I need to know which joints of the argument a intelligent non-philosopher could reasonably refuse to grant, because that is where my scene will actually happen.
