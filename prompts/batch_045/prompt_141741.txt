I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and on Friday morning I have to give the five-minute opening remarks at a half-day workshop we are calling *What Are We Measuring When We Measure Pain?* The room will be about forty people split into three groups who do not usually talk to each other: roughly a dozen chronic-pain patients (several of whom have lived with severe pain for twenty years and are openly impatient with being told that their pain is merely "subjective," or with the hospital's post-2018 opioid policies), fifteen or so clinicians — two palliative care physicians, an anesthesiologist who thinks the 0–10 intensity score is already one number too many and that adding a second one will just give insurers a better way to deny claims, and several charge nurses — and three philosophers from the university across the street, one of whom works on pain asymbolia and is giving the closing paper. The workshop exists because of Miriam Sandoval, who was a surgical-ward nurse here for thirty-one years and who, after a stroke in her late fifties, developed asymbolia: she can point to the exact site, describe the burning, crushing, stabbing quality, rate it at eight out of ten, and then shrug and say that yes, it is there, and no, it does not bother her. She will be sitting in the front row. She has asked me not to call her brave, not to call her lucky, and not to use the word "journey."

What I want the remarks to do is set up one question in a way that all three groups can hear, and I want the philosophy to be load-bearing rather than decorative. The question is whether what we owe patients is aimed at the *intensity* of their pain or at the *unpleasantness* of it — at how much it hurts, or at how much it matters to them — and Miriam's case is the natural way in, because she is the person in the room for whom the two have come apart. I would like you to use the standard distinction between the sensory-discriminative and the affective-motivational dimensions of pain, and to give the non-philosophers enough to hold onto in under five minutes, in plain language, with no jargon left unexplained. But there are hard constraints on what you can say, and they come from the people in the room. I cannot say that Miriam's pain is not really pain: the patients in the room have been told variations of that by doctors their whole lives and will hear it as one more version of it. I cannot say she is ideally placed to tell the rest of us what pain is like, either — one of the patient representatives has already said privately that a person who is not bothered by her pain has no business speaking for people who are — and I cannot say the opposite, that her condition disqualifies her, because that is the other thing that would sting. And I cannot lean on the obvious sentimental reading, that Miriam has taught us pain is "only a signal."

The positive content I want you to work with is this. Suppose, as a thought experiment, that we had a drug with two independent dials: one setting the sensory intensity of a pain, one setting its unpleasantness. The inference people usually draw from asymbolia is that everything bad about pain sits on the second dial, so that a patient who turns the unpleasantness to zero while leaving the intensity at eight keeps nothing that is bad for her. I find that both compelling and hard to accept, and I want the remarks to put the tension on the table rather than resolve it — the closing speaker is very likely to resist that inference, and I would rather gesture at the resistance than get flattened by it afterwards. I also want you to introduce a mirror case, which I have not seen described anywhere and am happy to present as my own invention: a hypothetical patient for whom the intensity is low but the suffering is crushing — call it whatever you like, affective amplification or something better — who reports a three out of ten and is, on the number, doing fine. If the badness of pain does track the unpleasantness rather than the intensity, then our single-number scale systematically under-rates people like that, which is exactly what the patient group has been saying for years, and the case for recording a second number becomes urgent rather than merely tidy. Aim for roughly 600 to 700 words, warm but not folksy, and end somewhere that makes the day's argument feel worth having for everyone in the room, including the anesthesiologist.
