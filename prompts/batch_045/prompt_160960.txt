Compose a fourteen-line sonnet in iambic pentameter (three quatrains followed by a closing couplet), with the volta arriving at line nine, on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals.

In the octave (lines 1–8), present the Ramsey–Adams suppositional account in a concrete invented case. Take a fair three-sided die with outcomes one, two, and three, each assigned credence one third. Let A be the proposition that the die lands odd (outcomes one or three), and C be the proposition that it lands one. To assess the conditional—if the die lands odd, it lands one—one hypothetically supposes A and then evaluates C under that supposition; the resulting degree of belief in the conditional is the conditional probability P(C|A) = 1/2, which differs from the unconditional P(C) = 1/3. Present this as a compelling picture: an indicative conditional is not a strange new fact about the world but a stance of supposition, and its probability is just the probability of the consequent given the antecedent.

The volta at line nine should mark the moment Lewis's triviality proof tightens. Explain, in verse, that if the conditional is itself an ordinary proposition—a truth-evaluable member of the same Boolean algebra, a single event X whose probability is always P(C|A) for every credence function in a suitably rich class, one closed under conditionalization—then the equation forces every pair of propositions to be probabilistically independent. In the die case, this means A and C must be independent: the conditional probability 1/2 would have to equal the unconditional P(C) = 1/3. More generally, the uniform distribution that spreads credence one third over each of the three outcomes cannot survive: outcomes one and two are disjoint yet both have probability one third, so their conjunction has probability 0, while the product of their probabilities is one ninth. Independence fails. The lines after the volta (lines 9–12) should dramatize the resulting collapse: every non-extreme credence function is squeezed out, and the only admissible credence functions assign probability 1 to a single outcome (and 0 to the others), so that every proposition receives probability 0 or 1. The rich three-outcome uncertainty shrinks to a single world, and the attractive suppositional picture is caught in a dilemma.

The closing couplet (lines 13–14) should lay out what must be abandoned, accurately enough that a philosopher of language or philosophical logic would recognize the options: either an indicative conditional is not a standard proposition with classical truth conditions and Boolean embedding, so there is no ordinary event X whose probability is being tracked; or the identification of the probability of a conditional with the conditional probability, often called Adams' Thesis, cannot hold in full generality; or the surrounding logic or probability framework must become non-classical. Do not resolve the dilemma by fiat; leave the three horns visible, as the proof does.

The poem should be self-contained: a reader who knows only the elementary probability in the setup should be able to follow it. Do not ask for or include a bibliography, citations, or web references. Paraphrase every theoretical claim in your own words; do not quote any actual philosophical text. Use the die, the odds, and the shrinking space of credences as the concrete spine, and let the volta carry the conceptual turn rather than merely shifting imagery. Someone who knows Lewis's results should be able to tell which lines correspond to the suppositional setup, which to the independence or triviality derivation, and which to the surviving options; someone who does not should still find a polished, well-made sonnet.
