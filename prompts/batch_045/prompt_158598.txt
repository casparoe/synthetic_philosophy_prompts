I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and this term I'm convening a reading group on whether evolutionary psychology is a genuine reverse-engineering science of domain-specific mental adaptations or an engine for producing post hoc just-so stories. Two of our regulars have agreed to run their disagreement as a proper adversarial collaboration rather than another year of talking past each other, and I have been volunteered to draft the joint document they could both sign. Camp A is the committed adaptationist: much of the mind consists of domain-specific mechanisms shaped by selection for ancestral adaptive problems, and the main evidential currency is design evidence — the fit between a mechanism's architecture and the problem it purportedly solves, ideally converging with comparative, cross-cultural and functional evidence. Camp B is a coalition I want you to keep honest about rather than flatten: its by-product wing says the trait in question may be a spandrel or an exaptation, and its domain-general-learning wing says the same pattern falls out of ordinary learning plus cultural transmission tuned to local payoffs; what they share is that current function does not entail selection-for, and that the space of adaptive stories is large enough for any single story's fit to count as weak confirmation. I have two candidate items of my own — that current function does not entail selection-for, and that a certain tuning-tracking question is a real crux — but please improve on or replace them rather than echo them.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, and I want claims with teeth: what both sides accept about the evolutionary framework itself, about the logic of reverse engineering and the conditions under which it has clearly worked (the sensory and perceptual cases) versus where it is most suspect, about what would count as data at all, and about the dispute being empirical rather than a disagreement about evolutionary theory. Please do not pad this section with claims nobody denies. Second, a disagreements table, with one row per issue and columns for: the issue in neutral wording; how A would prefer to state its own position; how B would prefer to state its own; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the case, measurement or argument both accept as bearing on that crux; A's predicted outcome, written down in advance; B's predicted outcome, written down in advance; and the result each side admits would damage its own view. Third, the part I care most about: I want at least three of the cruxes to carry concrete, jointly acceptable tests, built around a distinction I have been toying with and which I would like you to make precise. Call it the tuning-tracking question. If a mechanism shows an adaptive asymmetry — say, a bias in a decision task whose error costs are asymmetric, of the kind error-management reasoning predicts — does the size and direction of that bias track the statistics of the environment in which the mechanism was selected, so that it survives lagged and only partly plastic in a population whose environment has recently changed and resists re-tuning under a lifetime of training with reversed payoffs? Or does it track the statistics of the individual's own reinforcement history, so that a reversal of the payoff asymmetry in the laboratory, or one generation of life under new base rates, moves it to the new optimum? Make that probe fully concrete — the task structure, the populations or manipulations, what is measured, and the three or four distinguishable outcomes including the null — and do the same for a pre-registered novel-environment prediction, where A commits in advance to the sign of behaviour in an evolutionarily novel setting while B predicts that a domain-general model fitted to participants' own reinforcement history will predict at least as well as the ancestral-adaptation model, and for a matched-control requirement, under which any adaptive hypothesis must be run against a structurally adjacent trait that is a plausible by-product with the discriminating pattern stated before the data are in.

Two further things. Please say which parts of this dispute are merely verbal, and why — is any of it just a fight about what adaptation means, or about how strong the evidence must be before we are entitled to say selection-for rather than currently useful? — and where the by-product and domain-general wings of camp B would themselves come apart, flag it instead of papering over it. Close with a short paragraph on what the group should do with a small grant if it could run exactly one study, and what result both sides would treat as informative rather than as fresh ammunition. Constraints: write in British English; aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words; and do not cite anything — no author names, paper titles, journals or dates. The point of the exercise is to see whether the disagreement survives when it is reconstructed from shared background knowledge rather than from its canonical examples, so define any technical term as you go and argue the positions on their merits.
