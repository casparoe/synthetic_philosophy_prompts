I'm an undergraduate in a decision-theory seminar, and with our section leader away I've been asked to run the forty-five-minute recap on the Dutch book argument for probabilism for the rest of the class: people who have met the three axioms and can work out a bet's expected value, but who mostly take the argument to be the claim that a sharper bookie can fleece you if your odds are inconsistent. I don't need the argument explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it.

What I can't get right is the order. It seems to me that degrees of belief have to be given numerical grip — through the agent's own fair odds on a single proposition — before any bookie appears, and that the guarantee of loss has to be visibly logical rather than informational before I generalize from three bets on one proposition to additivity and then to conditional bets. But I'm unsure whether the diachronic argument, the one meant to support updating by conditionalization, belongs in the session at all, or whether placing it right after the synchronic one simply invites people to hear one argument where there are two. Can you lay out the sequence with what depends on what, and say where the synchronic/diachronic distinction must be drawn?

One case has to carry the whole thing, and I want to use three bets on a single proposition, each offered at the agent's own announced fair odds and taken as a package. I'm tempted to denominate the payoffs in raffle tickets for one fixed prize rather than in cash, so that utility is linear in tickets and the worry that money is not utility never gets off the ground. Is that device worth the two sentences it costs, or does it smuggle in more than it saves? Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack.

Then the misconceptions, and the point at which each should be headed off: that the bookie is trading on superior knowledge of the world; that Dutch-bookability tracks how wrong your beliefs are rather than how incoherent they are; that accepting each bet separately obviously commits you to the package; and the recoil that this at best shows coherence is prudentially useful, not that it is what rationality requires. Which of these has to be raised before it arises rather than answered after?

Finally: one question I can put to the group at the end that separates someone who has grasped coherence from someone who has only learned to compute a sure loss, and a short list of things I should openly refuse to explain in forty-five minutes — I'm guessing the converse result and the regress about why coherence should matter at all, but tell me whether to name those as set aside or stay silent about them.
