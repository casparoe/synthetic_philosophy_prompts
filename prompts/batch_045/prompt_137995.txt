I didn't study philosophy — I left school at eighteen and have worked in freight logistics ever since — but for several years now I've been reading on my own in the evenings, and I'm trying to get my own position onto a single page to send to three friends who read with me. Before I send it, I'd like someone to check whether it actually hangs together. Here is the set of things I find myself believing, in the order I usually write them down:

(1) There are moral facts that do not depend on what anyone feels, wants, or would agree to — not me, not my society, not some improved or idealized version of us. Torturing people for amusement was wrong before anyone believed it was, and it would still have been wrong if everyone had always approved of it.

(2) But no idle moral facts. If a proposed moral truth could never, even in principle, make a difference to what anyone had any reason to do, refrain from doing, or feel, then I don't think we should count it as a moral truth at all.

(3) The story of how I came to hold the moral beliefs I hold is a story about evolution and about local social pressure, and I don't think there was any reason to expect those forces to track independent moral facts. When I try to write out the full explanation of why I believe what I believe, the moral facts — if there are any — do not appear in it.

(4) I also can't use the usual way out of (3): the move where you say that evolution or culture latched onto some non-moral feature — suffering, flourishing, that sort of thing — that happens to line up with the moral facts. Every candidate I can think of is either a moral notion in disguise or too thin to do the work. So I take that reply to be closed to me.

(5) Even so, I think some of our moral beliefs are knowledge. We know that chattel slavery was wrong, and our knowing it is an improvement, not merely a change in what is fashionable.

(6) And here is a principle I've come to trust: if I'm willing to say that the moral beliefs of people in another time or place were mostly false, then I should be just as willing to say that about my own, unless I can point to some difference between us other than that their beliefs are theirs and mine are mine. Given (3), I can't see what that difference would be.

(7) One case, because I think it's where I'm most exposed. Suppose a group of people had been brought up with no aversion whatsoever to causing pain for amusement, and no argument and no experience would give them one. I'm inclined to say: then there is nothing wrong, for them, in their doing it. Not "wrong, but they can't help it" — nothing wrong.

Someone told me that (1) makes me a "realist" and that (3) is the sort of thing that pushes people toward an "error theory." I don't want to argue about labels, and I don't want to be pointed at a literature I have no way of reading. What I want is an audit: which of these seven are genuinely in tension, and how badly, and which apparent conflicts dissolve once you look at them properly? For each real tension, tell me what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere on the list. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and then say which single revision would clean up the most. If one of the seven turns out to be doing no real work, say so.
