I've been the person who signs off on structural calculations at a small consultancy for about twelve years — me and two juniors, maybe forty jobs a year, nothing exotic. Two years ago we all started running the calcs through an AI assistant, first for the boring code lookups, now for basically the whole first draft. Everything below is about the last year.

Here's what's bugging me: the work got better. Rework requests from the checking engineer are down, clients are happy, and I turn jobs around in a third of the time. But last month I noticed I couldn't do one of my own bog-standard calcs start to finish without the model. Not that I'd forgotten the method — I'd get maybe halfway and loose the thread, and the model supplies the shape of the rest. A friend of mine calls this "phantom skill": I can look at an output and feel that something is off, I just couldn't have produced the right one myself, and half the time I can't say what's wrong with the bad one.

Three things I keep going in circles on, and I want you to actually take a side on each:

- Is that "something's off" feeling a check on anything real, or am I just pattern-matching the model's own style back at it? Because if its the second one, my review adds nothing the model couldn't do for itself.
- My confidence in any given output comes almost entirely from "this thing has been right most of the time on the last few hundred jobs". Fine for normal jobs. But when a job is genuinely a bit unusual, that track record is a base rate from a pile of normal jobs, and I don't seem to have anything underneath it — no way to tell the unusual-but-fine from the unusual-and-wrong.
- Everyone uses the same model. Me, the juniors, and I'm fairly sure the checking engineer on the client's side too. Our three-person review might now be one person three times.

Also I tried an experiment: I redid one small job by hand last week, no model, six hours instead of forty minutes, and I caught two things the draft had gotten wrong. Except I can't tell whether I caught them because I have something the model doesn't, or just because six hours is slower than forty minutes. Which kind of ruins the experiment.

So: am I right to be uneasy, or is this just what competence looks like now? Concretely — should I be stamping these jobs at all given that I can't fully re-derive them, should I make the juniors do one by hand a month even though it costs real billable time, and is there anything I owe the client by way of disclosure? Don't give me a reading list or tell me who's written about this, I want the argument itself. And finish with a one-line verdict I can compare across answers, in the form \answer{...} — something like \answer{keep using it, but stop stamping X}.
