For about fifteen years now — since roughly when the Sequences were being posted, in reading groups I helped run, in comment threads, at meetups, in the alignment-adjacent corners of the internet since — I have watched the same argument about counterfactual mugging recur, and I have never once seen anyone move. An Omega-type agent flips a fair coin, after having predicted what you would do in each outcome. On tails it asks you for $100. On heads it gives you $10,000 — but only if it predicted you would have handed over the $100. You have been asked. Roughly half of us say pay: the policy "pay when asked" is worth ~$5,000 ex ante, the policy "refuse" is worth nothing, your decision procedure is what determines both what you do now and what the prediction was, and the sort of agent that refuses is precisely the sort that leaves the $10,000 on the table. The other half says don't pay: the coin came up tails, the heads branch is closed, handing over $100 makes you strictly poorer in every world you can still be in, nothing you choose now can alter a prediction already made, and the ex ante framing is just a way of pretending to retain control over a fixed past — dressing up wishful thinking as a decision rule.

What makes this interesting to me, rather than merely tedious, is that the same two groups agree completely on the sibling case, XOR blackmail. There, a blackmailer has learned both whether you have a compromising secret and, via a good predictor or simulation, whether you would pay a demand if one arrived; and it sends the demand if and only if exactly one of those holds. Everyone in the group — payers and refusers alike — says don't pay, because the sort of person who pays gets sent demands in the no-secret case and is simply robbed, while the sort who never pays is only ever contacted when there is a real secret to expose. So the two cases have the same shape (a trustworthy prediction of your policy, a decision now that is screened off from any causal payoff) and opposite verdicts, at least on my side of the room. The standard reconciliation offered is that in blackmail the existence of the demand depends on your disposition, so your policy controls whether the bad branch is reached, whereas in the mugging the coin, not your disposition, determines whether you are asked, so your policy controls nothing downstream. And the standard reply is that in the mugging too, whether you are standing here at all, and whether your counterfactual counterpart collected the $10,000, depended on your disposition; the coin only selected which branch you find yourself in, and selecting a branch is not the same as making it the case that you are benefited. Then: silence, or the same two paragraphs again.

My own hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement inside decision theory at all, and that its persistence is explained by two different questions being asked under the word "rational." One question is: which input-output function should be built — which algorithm should I instantiate, knowing that I am the algorithm and that it will be run in many branches and many problems, scored across the whole distribution? The other is: what is it permissible or sensible for this agent-token, in this room, with the coin already down, to do now, given that the other branch's winnings are not its winnings? Under the first question the payers seem plainly right; under the second the refusers seem plainly right; and neither side ever says which question it is answering, so the exchange is a dialogue de sourds. But I hold this hypothesis loosely, because a second one competes with it: the real work may be done by a hidden question about identity and measure, not about evaluation timing at all. That is why I want to bring two variants to it. In the first, Omega's prediction comes from running a physically realized copy of you, and you are told that on heads it is the *copy* — a distinct, continuing mind, numerically different from you — that goes on to receive the $10,000, while you, the one being asked, get nothing either way. In the second, the coin decides which of the two of you is asked: sometimes the original is asked and the copy would have been paid, sometimes the reverse, and you genuinely do not know which one you are. If the disagreement is about ex ante versus ex post evaluation, these variants should not change anyone's verdict. If it is about whether the beneficiary is *you*, and about how much of "you" there is, they should split the room along a different axis — and I think they do, which is some evidence against my own hypothesis.

So: diagnose the impasse rather than settle it. Is it a verbal dispute about "rational," two questions under one name, a bedrock difference in starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, a hidden question about personal identity and the measure of implementations, or a sign that the question as posed is ill-posed? If you think it is verbal, tell me which distinction should replace the word and whether anyone loses anything by the split. If you think it is substantive, say what would actually end it — a Dutch-book or money-pump style argument, a theorem about the incompatibility of ex ante optimality with ex post dominance, a formal treatment of logical correlation and screening, or a change of question. And be explicit about your own confidence: tell me where your diagnosis is most likely to be wrong, what single consideration would make you abandon it in favour of one of the others, and — if you take a view on the two variants above — what would change your mind about them.
