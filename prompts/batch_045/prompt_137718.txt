My friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether an adult whose corpus callosum has been surgically sectioned is one subject of consciousness or two, and neither of us moved an inch. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange we actually had rather than to summarize the debate in general. I am calling the two of us A and B and I am not going to tell you which of us was which, because I want the verdict to rest on the arguments alone. Please write in American English, and please do not point me to any papers, authors, or further reading: I have no way to follow them up, and I would rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.

A opened. The commissurotomy findings show two functionally independent systems: information presented to one visual field is denied by the patient, while the hand controlled by the other hemisphere promptly selects the matching object. So there is one stream of experience available to the speaking hemisphere and another available only through the other side's manual responses. What ordinarily makes my visual experience and my auditory experience belong to a single subject is that they are experienced together, and across the section nothing is experienced together. So there are now two experiential subjects, where before the surgery there was one.

B answered that this conflates access with phenomenology. Behaviorally independent systems need not be experientially separate subjects, and a dissociation between what a system can report and what it registers is ordinary: people act on subliminal primes they cannot name, blindsight patients navigate obstacles they deny seeing, and dream experiences vanish from report entirely. Manual selection is at least as consistent with subpersonal information processing as with a second conscious stream. A therefore owes an argument that the silent hemisphere has experiences at all, and behavioral independence does not supply it.

A replied, first, that the silent hemisphere's performances are not reflex-like: they generalize to new items, they honor instructions delivered in a different modality, and they sometimes work against the other side's ongoing project, as when one hand buttons a shirt while the other undoes it, which looks like competing intention rather than mechanism. Second, B's skepticism applied consistently would deny experience to every being that cannot speak, including stroke patients with global aphasia and prelinguistic infants. Third, the patient's own first-person report is worthless as evidence for B, since only one hemisphere can answer; the other, if it could answer, would presumably say the same thing, and both could be reporting accurately, so the question of whether they are the same subject cannot be settled from either side's armchair.

B then offered a control case and a thought experiment. Adults who have had an entire cerebral hemisphere removed for epilepsy or a tumor wake up, on any sane view, as the same single subject with roughly half the tissue: hemiparesis and a visual field deficit, but no subtraction of a person. So the number of subjects is not a function of how much neural tissue there is. What the commissurotomy removes is not a subject but the routes by which one part of the system draws on another. Now imagine a prosthetic commissure whose bandwidth can be dialed continuously from full to zero while the hemispheres themselves are left untouched: if there is one subject at one setting and two at another, there must be a setting at which the count changes, yet nothing intrinsic to either hemisphere changes when someone turns a dial on a cable. Either the number of subjects is fixed entirely by an external conduit, which is bizarre, or it admits of no sharp answer at all, in which case A's question was never well posed. This imagined device is my friend's invention as far as I know, so please treat it as a thought experiment rather than as something to look up.

A conceded nothing. First, thresholds are not absurd: water is liquid or solid as a continuous parameter varies, and neural integration plausibly has a critical point, with hysteresis, so a dial turned down and a dial turned up would shift the count at different settings, which is exactly what a threshold phenomenon predicts rather than a refutation of it. Second, the conduit point is not bizarre but definitional: what makes me one subject is a real relation of causal integration among my parts, not the parts themselves, and severing that relation destroys a relation instead of adding an entity. Third, B's own view is worse off, because it holds that the silent hemisphere's stream is phenomenally unified with the speaking hemisphere's while remaining wholly inaccessible to it, which is a posit with no difference-maker, since nothing in anyone's experience distinguishes the two hypotheses.

B closed. The first-person report is at least some evidence, and A's dismissal of it would neutralize every case in which the evidence happens to be one-sided: if the only available witness says one, the default is one, and A's claim that the other side would say the same is a claim A cannot support, since the other side says nothing. On the threshold reply, the count was supposed to be read off how the system is actually organized at a time, and the hysteresis concession undermines that. Finally, the number of subjects is supposed to do work: it is what explains unified agency, unified memory, and a single answer to the question of what one should do now, and for every purpose that matters morally or practically, the split patient is one person with one life, one set of projects, and one set of interests, in whom something has gone wrong with internal coordination.

Please go through the six moves in order and give each a verdict from clearly right through half right to clearly wrong, with a short justification for each, and say specifically which sub-point in the move is doing the work and which is weak. Then tell me who is right about what overall rather than simply who won, what both of us were assuming that we should not have been, and what the residual crux is in a sentence. If you think some part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, identify which part and why, and if you think the question of how many subjects there are has no determinate answer, say so at the point where that becomes relevant instead of saving it for the conclusion.
