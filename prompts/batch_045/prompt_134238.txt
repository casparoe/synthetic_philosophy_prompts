I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in any of this. I left school at eighteen and have worked in freight logistics ever since; for about three years now I have been reading about animal sentience and population ethics on my own in the evenings, mostly online. A long post I wrote on a forum about the scale argument for taking insect suffering seriously somehow got me invited to give a talk, and I have agreed before fully taking in what I agreed to.

The argument as I understand it is the familiar one: even if the probability that an insect feels anything at all is small, the numbers are so vast that the multiplication everyone performs — probability times population — yields an expected quantity of suffering large enough to dwarf the considerations we normally treat as decisive, so anyone who takes expected value seriously ought to weight insect deaths heavily, which in practice means caring a great deal about how our food is produced.

The wrinkle I have been turning over is this. Every version I have read seems to count only what I have started calling the killing term: the insects killed by pesticides, by harvest machinery, by traffic, by land clearing. But if you are going to take the multiplication seriously, you also have to count what I call the creation term: the insects whose existence is caused by the farming system itself — the pest populations that monocultures and irrigation and fertiliser allow to reach densities the same land would not otherwise support, the herbivores that exist because we plough and plant rather than because the land lies fallow or under forest. My rough claim is that this second term is at least as large as the first, and probably much larger, and that almost nobody writing about this seems to put it in the sum.

If that is right, the scale argument does not deliver the conclusion people usually draw from it. Once the insects your food brings into existence outnumber the insects it kills, the sign of the comparison is set by whether an insect's life is on balance worth living, not by how many of them die. If those lives are net-negative, then the creation term makes arable farming look far worse than the killing term alone would suggest, and the policy implication is not to avoid insect deaths but to reduce insect population turnover. If they are net-positive, the same arithmetic makes intensively farmed cropland look better than the alternatives on the insect ledger. Either way the huge numbers tell you the size of the stake, not the direction of the bet.

The audience is a research group attached to a veterinary school, around twenty people. Six or seven are animal welfare scientists who work on pain and affect indicators in farmed vertebrates; they want measures that can be turned into a protocol, and they are impatient with arguments that cannot. Three are entomologists — one works on pest population dynamics in arable systems, one on insect neurobiology — who regard the sentience question as premature and will resist anything that looks like quietly counting insects as moral patients. There is a farm-animal veterinarian, two philosophers of science who will care about what would count as evidence for a claim like mine and whether it is testable at all, and the agricultural economics PhD student who invited me, who is sympathetic to the effective altruism framing and will want to know what anyone is supposed to do on Monday morning. I have twenty-five minutes and then, I am told, a lively twenty minutes of questions.

What I would like from you is a preparation sheet for that question period. Specifically: what I should concede in the first two minutes so that the room does not quietly decide I am a crank; the objections in roughly the order they will arrive, who will raise each one and in whose vocabulary, how much damage each actually does to my claim, and which ones I should simply give up rather than fight; for each, a reply of about ninety seconds and a note on where that reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — my own candidate is that if what matters is insect population turnover, then the cheapest way to reduce it is to make land incapable of supporting insects at all, but I suspect you will find a worse one. I have no journal access and cannot check paywalled papers, so please do not build the sheet around studies I would have to go and read; if a point depends on a specific empirical result, mark it as something I should say plainly that I have not verified.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose between. In particular, tell me directly whether the killing term / creation term distinction is a real point or merely a confusion on my part — a double count, perhaps, or a commitment to total rather than person-affecting population ethics that I have smuggled in without noticing — and if it is a confusion, tell me what I should argue instead, because I would much rather find that out now than in front of an entomologist who studies aphid population dynamics for a living. If I can defend only one of my claims and must demote the rest to asides, tell me which one that should be. And please mark which parts of what you tell me about insect ecology you are reasonably confident in and which you would abandon the moment someone in that room contradicted them, since I have no way of telling the difference myself.
