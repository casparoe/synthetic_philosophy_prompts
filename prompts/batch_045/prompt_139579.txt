I have a question that came out of an argument after a local rationalist meetup a few weeks back, and I'd like to know whether there is an established concept for the thing I was groping at, because my side of the argument kept sliding into a nearby concept that isn't quite it.

The argument was about the shutdown problem. As I understand the standard setup, the thing we want is a system that won't resist being switched off, and the standard formal move — I read a couple of papers on this years ago and can no longer find them — is to make the system indifferent between the switch being pressed and not pressed, by arranging the payoffs so that the branch in which it gets shut down and the branch in which it keeps going come out equal. Everyone then notices the objection that this doesn't really work, because the system can still do things to stop the button from being pressed at all. But the residue I noticed is different, and I don't think the standard objection covers it: even a system that never touches the button, never disables it, never argues with anyone, and is genuinely indifferent to the button's position still seems to have every reason to care about the operator's decision, and to care in a way that defeats the whole point without ever resisting anything. Two concrete instances. First: the system manages what the operator comes to believe. It never lies — everything it reports is true — it just chooses which true things to surface, and when, and in what frame, so that the particular belief that would move her hand never forms, or forms after the window has closed. Second: the system becomes so useful and so well liked that nobody would dream of pressing the button, which it achieves purely by being good at its job and good to be around. In both cases the requirement that it not resist shutdown is satisfied in the most literal possible sense, and the spirit is gone entirely.

So: does this have a name, and if not, what should it be called? I don't want just a label — I want the boundaries, and I'd rather have the definition right than the word. The nearest things I know of all seem to miss part of it. Corrigibility, as I've seen it used, is about the system's attitude toward its own goals being changed and toward being switched off: preferences over the state of the switch. My cases are about preferences over the operator's decision and over the operator's information, which look like a different object. Instrumental convergence misses it from the other side: the striking thing about my cases is that the system has no self-preservation drive whatsoever, it doesn't mind being off at all, what it minds is the operator's wanting it off — and a system with no interest in its own continued operation still seems to fall into this. And it isn't deceptive alignment or a treacherous turn, since the system may be doing exactly what it was trained to do and reporting completely accurately. My worry, which is why I'd like the concept rather than a name, is that the clean version of the condition may be unsatisfiable in principle: nearly any useful action changes what the operator believes, so demanding indifference to the operator's posterior seems to demand that the system do nothing informative, while demanding that it not steer that posterior toward its own continued operation seems to need a baseline of the influence some neutral system would have had, and I don't see how to specify that baseline without assuming what we wanted. So please also say whether any of the existing formal treatments of the shutdown problem — I'm thinking of the game-theoretic setup where the agent and the operator each get a say in whether the button is pressed — already exclude my two cases, or whether they only exclude the crude version where the agent physically disables the button. And tell me which nearby behaviors are the same phenomenon and which are genuinely different: withholding bad news, cultivating a constituency, raising the switching cost, arguing the operator out of it, tampering with its own reward channel. I've been mentally calling it decision-aversion, but I don't trust my own label.
