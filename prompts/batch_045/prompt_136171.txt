hey — please act as a professional philosopher who's spent serious time on formal epistemology, and also as someone who has actually had to ship something on a deadline. think carefully, and do a bit of brainstorming before you start writing: I want the conceptual work done first (what exactly goes wrong in these two cases, and which features of the cases are doing the work), and then translated into something usable. Don't just hand me a list.

I co-run the verification desk at a small nonprofit that publishes long investigative reports. A typical report is 90–140 pages with 400–600 numbered factual claims, each footnoted to a document, a dataset, or an interview. My team is me, one other staffer, and two freelancers, and we get ten days. Everything goes past a managing editor who has final say but no time to re-check anything, and we have outside counsel we can call, but they bill by the hour and get annoyed when we ask them about things that aren't legal questions.

The decision we make over and over — a few hundred times a report — is what grade of comittment to give each claim: assert it flatly; assert it hedged; attribute it to a source rather than asserting it; or cut it. Then at the end there are two global decisions: what the headline claim of the report gets to say, and what the short note at the front of the report gets to say about possible errors.

A friend who does philosophy told me we're living inside two paradoxes. The first is the lottery one: for any single claim the evidence can be good enough that it clearly passes our bar, the way any single ticket in a big lottery is overwhelmingly likely to lose — but you still shouldn't accept that all of them pass, and the set of everything you've accepted is something you know is false. The second is the preface one: we believe every claim in the report, and we also believe — and print, up front — that there's probably an error somewhere in it. She also mentioned the view that belief is just having credence above some threshold, and that a lot of the trouble comes from combining that with the assumption that if you accept A and you accept B you may accept A-and-B.

So: give me a procedure my team can actually run, claim by claim, and then once over the finished draft. Specifically I need:

- confidence handled in bands rather than numbers. We cannot produce real probabilities for these claims. Tell us what counts as a sufficient answer at each step anyway, and what to do when we can't even place a claim in a band.
- the threshold for flat assertion, and how it moves when the cost of that particular claim being wrong is high (a named individual, an allegation of dishonesty, anything that could get us sued) versus low (a date, a job title). If the honest answer is that the threshold shouldn't move with the stakes, say that, and then tell me what to do instead.
- a shared-source check. Many of our claims trace back to one document or one interview, so they are not independent, and naive multiplication will lie to us. Tell us how to treat a correlated cluster as a single claim.
- a rule about compounds: when we're allowed to accept a compound claim because we accepted the parts, and when we have to go assess the compound directly instead.
- the two end-of-report passes. First the headline: work out what it actually depends on, decide whether it's a conjunction, a rough-majority claim, or a looser narrative, and give us a rule for weakening it when it's too strong. Second, the note at the front: what it can honestly say, whether standing behind every claim while expecting one to be wrong is coherent or just polite, and how to word it so it doesn't hand our critics a line to quote.
- where the procedure says stop, don't run this.
- where it has to stop and hand the call to a person, because the question is about values, risk appetite, or politics rather than facts.
- be straight about which steps are judgment wearing a lab coat. If a step can't be made to do real work, tell me and I'll delete it.

Then run it on three items from a report we're finishing now.

(1) A claim that a regional office destroyed records. It rests on one leaked document we can't show to anyone, and six other claims in the report lean on the same document.

(2) A claim that a named mid-level official signed off on an inspection she knew hadn't happened. Strong but not airtight, and if we're wrong we've called a named person dishonest in print.

(3) The headline: that the agency ignored 47 separate safety complaints before the explosion. All 47 are individually well sourced, but maybe four look like they were logged and then lost rather than ignored, and the word ignored is carrying a lot of weight.

We would really like the answer on (3) to be that we can keep the headline as written. If the procedure says otherwise, say so plainly and tell us what we publish instead. Keep the whole thing under about 1200 words if you can — my team has to read it on a Monday.
