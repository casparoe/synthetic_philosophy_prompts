I work on scheduling and internal pricing for a big shared compute platform, and I've got a hobby interest in the old socialist calculation argument — Mises saying rational economic calculation is impossible without money prices for the means of production, Hayek's later version about knowledge that's dispersed, local, partly tacit, and changing faster than anyone can report it, and Lange's comeback that a planning board can just set accounting prices, tell managers to minimize cost at those prices and push output to where price equals marginal cost, then revise prices in whatever direction shows excess demand until things roughly clear. I've only ever read the wikipedia-level version of all three, so fix me if I've mangled one, but don't spend the answer on that.

Here's why I care. A couple of us have been sketching a system we call MESH that would replace our internal price list completely. Each team ships a differentiable surrogate of its own production possibilities — a neural production frontier fitted to its local telemetry, refreshed hourly with federated updates so the weights move without the raw data ever leaving the team. The planner collects all the surrogates, solves one big joint allocation by dual decomposition (ADMM, prices as Lagrange multipliers on the shared resources, iterate to convergence), and pays teams according to a VCG-style score so that reporting your true surrogate beats shaping it. The weights on the planner's objective come from leadership's pairwise comparisons, learned the way you'd learn a reward model. There's even a prediction market on the shadow prices themselves.
