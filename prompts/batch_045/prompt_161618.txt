I'm a programme officer at a mid-sized philanthropic foundation, and I sit on its five-person allocations committee, which meets Thursday to decide how to spend the remaining $800,000 of this year's unrestricted grant budget. A separate and larger sum is locked inside a bequest restricted to "direct and measurable relief of suffering among people now living," so that money is already committed to an established deworming programme with strong randomised-trial evidence and is not up for discussion. The three live options for the unrestricted money are: (1) give all $800,000 to a research group working on what it calls value lock-in — the idea that decisions made by or about the first transformative AI systems may entrench one value system across a future containing astronomically many, and possibly infinitely many, morally significant lives; (2) split it, giving $400,000 to an eighteen-month methodology project whose entire output would be a ranked procedure for making grants when the stakes may be infinite, and holding the remaining $400,000 for next cycle; (3) spend it expanding the near-term health programme, on the argument that those benefits are the only ones anybody can actually measure.

What makes Thursday difficult is the argument at the centre of the first application. The group concedes that trying to make the future *bigger* is pointless if it is already infinite, since adding to an infinite total changes nothing; their case rests entirely on changing the future's *character*, which they say stays valuable even when the number of lives does not. From this they draw a stronger conclusion: if there is any non-zero chance of an infinite future, then the expected value of every merely finite intervention collapses to the same number, so ordinary cost-effectiveness comparison across our portfolio is empty and the only grants with any expected value at all are those that shift the probability of what that infinite future is like. One colleague has an objection I can't shake: the same reasoning seems to eat its own conclusion, because a near-term health programme also has some small chance of shifting the long-run future — through economic growth, institutional trust, or the careers of the people it affects — so nothing isolates AI-adjacent work as uniquely leverage-bearing, and we are left with a tie that needs a tie-breaker. Her tie-breaker is to regularise: cap the value of the future at some very large finite number, then run normal cost-effectiveness. The applicants' reply is that the ranking flips depending on where the cap is set, which they say makes the cap a disguised way of choosing the answer.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee on Tuesday. It should open with a one-sentence bottom line recommending one of the three options, with a concrete and dated course of action (amounts, conditions, and a deadline or review point), and then give the reasoning: it must engage the infinitarian argument on its own terms rather than dismissing infinite stakes as unrealistic, and it must give the strongest version of the best option we are declining — I especially want the losing side stated well enough that its supporters feel understood. Note briefly, near the end, what kind of result from the methodology project would change the recommendation next cycle. Three of my four colleagues are not philosophers, so keep the technical apparatus light and explained. Use a one-sentence bottom line followed by at most three short headed sections; total length no more than 650 words.
