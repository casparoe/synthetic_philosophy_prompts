Not sure this is the right crowd but here goes. I run user research at a subscription company, so I spend all day watching people pick between plans, and I keep running into an argument that feels like a sleight of hand. I'd like to know what it's called, or what to call it.

Basic setup. We offer plan A (cheap, 20 channels) and plan B (pricier, 80 channels). Most people take A. Then we run the same test with a third plan C in the line-up: a mid-tier plan that almost nobody actually chooses. Now B wins. Nobody takes C, C is not better than B, and yet putting it in the list flips people from A to B. Same shape as those dessert studies: put a brownie on the tray and cookie orders go up even though nobody takes the brownie.

An economist friend says there's nothing to explain. She says the person was never choosing between "A" and "B" full stop; they were choosing between *being offered* A and B and *being offered* A, B and C, and those are just different options, so nothing inconsistent happened. She also said "utility" is just a way of numbering whatever people pick, not a feeling they have, which I think is the same point.

What bugs me is that the move seems to work in every direction. When people pay extra to keep a decision open, it's "they wanted the bigger set of options." When people pay to have options taken away — the non-refundable cheaper fare, the app that locks you out of your own account, deleting the thing before a trip — it's the same move with a minus sign: they wanted the smaller set. Whatever anyone does, you redefine what was being chosen until it's the thing they happened to pick. And I've noticed it's only ever used backwards. Nobody ever says in advance, "B will win once C is in the list, because what people really want is the three-option situation." It shows up after we already have the numbers.

So: is there an established name for this retrofitting move, and for the distinction I actually need — the case where the person wants the extra option present versus the case where they want it gone? Is it just the standard stuff about flexibility and self-control wearing a hat, or is it something else? Under what conditions would the re-description be honest rather than a dodge — what would it have to pay for, predict, or commit to in advance? Does it matter that people will say out loud that the third plan shouldn't matter to them and then still get moved by it, or is asking them just one more choice to be rationalized? And if nothing established fits, coin a term and define it with its boundary cases, because a bare label won't do me any good — I need to know exactly what the thing is, what it isn't, and where it stops.
