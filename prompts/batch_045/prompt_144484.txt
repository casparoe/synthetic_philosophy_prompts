My friend Sam and I got into a genuinely bad argument leaving a screening on Saturday, and I've been chewing on it since. His position, which he kept repeating, was that you can only form an aesthetic judgment on the basis of your own experience of the thing — everyone else can tell you what a work did to *them*, or what it will probably do to you, or how it's likely to strike practically anyone, but none of that adds up to you judging it good or beautiful or devastating, the way testimony about a restaurant's hygiene rating does. My own stab at a principle, which I made up half way through the argument and now half regret: testimony can give you facts about what a work does to audiences and predictions about what it'll do to you, but the verdict that it's *good* has to be earned with your own experience, and until then you're just borrowing someone else's. I want that tested and repaired, not just agreed with.

Here's how I actually come down on cases, going by gut. Priya — we agree on maybe nine records out of ten — texts me that the new Heron Vespers record is great: I say I know it's great, before I've heard a note. A critic whose judgments I match maybe half the time says the same thing: evidence, sure, but I wouldn't say I *know*. That critic says the record is sluggish and Priya says it's great: Priya, no contest, even though the critic has heard a thousand more records than either of us — which I realize sits badly with how much I defer to experts elsewhere. A musicologist who's never heard the record but spent a month with the score says it's a masterpiece: I basically disregard it, and calling it a category error feels right even though she knows the work in some real sense. Then Priya says not "it's great" but "you specifically will love this, it's totally your thing": weirdly that's the strongest case on my list, I trust it more than the first one, even though it isn't a claim about the work's quality at all. Priya says the film we just saw, Nightjar, was devastating: I believe her completely and I still don't feel I know it's devastating, I need to see it — and that conflicts with the record case and I can't tell you why. A thousand strangers whose taste I know nothing about all say Nightjar is devastating: the number does something for me, I'd bet money, but I still wouldn't call it knowledge — and yet if a thousand strangers told me the soup was salty I'd just accept it. Someone who watched it on a phone, on a bus, sound low: I discount them heavily. Someone who watched it three days after their father died and cried all the way through: I don't discount them at all, if anything I'm more interested, though I think part of what they're reporting is about them. Last one, and this is what's been bugging me most: the streaming service's algorithm has predicted my ratings with about 91% accuracy and it says I'll give Nightjar five stars — I trust that prediction completely, more than I trust Priya, and I still feel I've learned nothing whatsoever about whether the film is any good. If Priya is just a noisier version of that algorithm, why is case one different from case ten?

So: is there one principle, or a disjunctive little mess, that gets all of this? I'd like it stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and I want it to earn its keep by telling me what it says about cases I haven't listed — a fragrance nobody can convey in words, or a VR piece that only one person can be inside at a time and that leaves no recording, where first-hand experience is impossible or unrepeatable. And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and would make me drop. The one I keep flip-flopping on: Sam is just better at this than me, he's seen everything and he's more attentive, and when we disagree about films I'm usually the one who turns out to be wrong — so shouldn't I simply defer to him and count myself as knowing Nightjar is a masterpiece? Half of me says obviously yes, that's what expertise is for, and the other half says no, that's exactly the one place where deferring gets you nothing. Which half does your principle side with, and what does it cost?
