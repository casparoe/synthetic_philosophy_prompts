hi — I work in a tiny unit that vets draft policy papers and ministerial speeches before they go out, and honestly about a third of what we end up flagging is a historical analogy. At the moment the flagging is just whoever's on duty going "hmm, that feels loaded", which isn't great when one line in a speech gets quoted for the next decade. So I want an actual procedure.

What we have to work with: the draft text only, no research time (call it fifteen minutes per review, sometimes five), reviewers who studied politics or law rather than history, and a unit head who signs off with one of four stamps — green (fine as illustration), amber (usable, but the disanalogy has to be named in the same sentence), red (cut), and a fourth one we've started using: strip, meaning kill the historical frame but keep whatever claim it was carrying.

Some distinctions we use informally and that I'd like either sharpened or thrown out: the carrier (the surface story of the episode) versus the payload (what the analogy is actually doing — predicting, legitimating, allocating blame, or just adding colour); analogies that transfer a mechanism versus ones that transfer an outcome; the mirror test, i.e. when the same episode is routinely wheeled out for the opposite lesson (Vietnam both as "don't intervene" and as "don't half-commit"), which makes me think it isn't doing any inferential work at all; and what I call the birth-date problem, that a lot of the famous lessons were coined afterwards by people with an axe to grind about their own record.

So: write me the procedure. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficent answer to each, where it terminates in red versus strip, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is about values or risk appetite rather than facts. Ground it in whatever is genuinely known about analogical reasoning — relevant versus superficial similarity, reference classes and base rates, whether a causal mechanism has actually been stated, the fact that invoking an analogy can itself change what the players then do — but no citations and no reading lists, I need something my team can run off the page.

Then run it on three things on my desk right now:

1. "This is our Munich moment — any concession on tariffs will only whet their appetite." From a speech on a trade deal.
2. A paper arguing that the US and China are caught in a Thucydides trap, so we should assume a high baseline probability of war this decade and plan accordingly.
3. My own favourite, which I suspect definately won't survive: a note saying the current cyber escalation is like July 1914 in that nobody actually wants the big war, so we have far more room to de-escalate than people think.

Give me the margin note my reviewer would write for each, including at least one stamp I won't like — don't soften it. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure can't do. British English please, and keep the jargon light: this is going into a style guide for non-specialists.

cheers
