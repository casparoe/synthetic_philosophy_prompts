I'm in my fourth year, writing on the numbers question — the Taurek-style stuff about whether you should save the five rather then the one — and there's a postdoc down the corridor, Sam, who has been handing me verdicts on cases for about a year now. We keep them in a shared spreadsheet (his idea). He is very much not a utilitarian: every time I try to add harms up across people he says something like "worse for whom?" But he also pretty reliably saves the greater number when everyone on both sides is in the same boat, and after a year of this I still can't tell what he's actually running. Verdicts so far — relationships, ages, and everything else are supposed to be held fixed, since he says obligations to people you actually have a relationship with are a separate matter:

- five strangers vs one stranger, everyone will otherwise die, I can only reach one group: save the five.
- same case, but the one is his brother: save his brother.
- one person will die vs five people will each lose a finger: save the one who'd die, although he admitted he couldn't say why and muttered something like "maybe death is just a bigger deal for the person it happens to."
- five people will each lose a leg vs one person will lose a leg: save the five.
- one person will lose both legs vs five people will each lose one leg: he said "the one losing both legs, I think — but I've never been sure about that one."
- nothing involving chances: he has always dodged probabilities, on the grounds that the spreadsheet only has certain outcomes in it.

The case I want to spring on him at the pub on Thursday is this: I can either (A) remove an independent 30% risk of death from each of five unrelated strangers, or (B) save one unrelated stranger who will certainly die otherwise. Not both; everyone is a stranger; no relationships in play. I'm also going to ask him whether it makes a difference if the five risks aren't independent — five people on one boat with a 30% chance of sinking, versus five separate boats at 30% each.

So: what's he going to say, and more importantly what rules actually fit his track record? I can think of at least three that fit it — "compare what is at stake for an individual first and let the numbers break ties only when those are equal," "minimize total expected harm," and "you may only add losses up across people when the individual losses are the same kind of loss" — and I want to know exactly where they come apart on this case, and whether there's any reading of him on which he has to answer "flip a coin" here. Give me your best guess with reasons for preferring one reading over the others. My own suspicion is that once probabilities come in he has to start multiplying, at which point he's just doing expected value like everyone else and his whole no-aggregation line quietly collapses; but maybe he can say that a certain death is simply a worse prospect for the person who faces it and stop there. Finally, tell me which further case would actually settle it: I was going to try scaling up to a thousand people at 5% each, or making the expected numbers match by using five people at 20% each, but I'm not convinced either one separates the rules cleanly, and I don't want to put a case to him that leaves him exactly as underdetermined as this one does.
