Right, I'm trying to work out what I actually think about nudging by going case by case, and I want the principle behind it if there is one. My verdicts:

- Workplace pension scheme, employees auto-enrolled at a default rate, opt out in two clicks. Fine.
- Same scheme, but the default ratchets: your contribution goes up a point every pay rise unless you say no. Also fine, and I can't properly say why.
- An app that charges you £40 if you log on to a gambling site after midnight when you signed up sober at midday and would never have signed up at 11pm. Not fine, feels like a trap. But that's the same exploitation of present bias as the last one, isn't it?
- Organ donation on an opt-out basis. Fine, even though the whole effect depends on people not getting round to it.
- Council puts the recycling bin by the door and moves the general waste bin down the steps. Fine.
- Same council makes general waste collection available only on request. Not fine — and I can't see how that's a different kind of thing from the one above.
- Supermarket moves the booze to a less prominent aisle. Fine. Supermarket keeps it behind the counter so you have to ask. Not fine.
- My GP texts me a flu jab appointment already made, cancel if you don't want it. Fine. My employer does the same thing through my work calendar, so that declining is visible to my manager. Not fine. Which makes me think I've been measuring the cost of opting out in clicks when it's sometimes measured in audience.
- I install a blocker on my own phone at 2am when I'm cross with myself. Obviously fine, nobody's paternalising anybody.

My working rule has been something like: a nudge is ok if it would still work after you'd told the person plainly what you did and why, and not ok if telling them kills the effect — call it the survives-transparency test. It tracks most of the above but it falls over somewhere I can't locate.

Two more things I can't fit in at all: (1) I'm much more bothered by nudges that only work on people who are exhausted, broke or stressed than by ones that work on everyone, even when the behavioural effect is identical; (2) I worry a default left in place long enough stops being a default, because opting out becomes the sort of thing you'd have to justify, so the nudge quietly becomes a soft mandate. Is that a separate objection or part of the same one, and does it belong in the principle or as a side constraint?

Can you give me a principle, or two rival candidates, with the case that motivates each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which I should give up, and apply it to a case I haven't listed? And please mark which bits are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this — I don't want a contested view dressed up as the consensus.
