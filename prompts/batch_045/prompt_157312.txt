I help run a university effective-altruism group and I've been asked to give this term's ninety-minute intro session on cause prioritization. The room will be quantitatively literate newcomers who have already absorbed the standard three-factor decomposition — importance, tractability, neglectedness — from online write-ups and can recite it. I've read the standard posts and the standard pushback; I don't want the session to be a vocabulary lesson, and I suspect the recitation is exactly what stops people from being able to use the framework.

So plan the session rather than writing it. I care most about three places where I think the usual treatment is wrong or silent. First, neglectedness as normally stated (roughly, how many resources already go here) looks like the wrong quantity: what does the work is whether my next unit of resources causes something that would not otherwise have happened, and a cause can be starved of funding while my dollar merely displaces another funder's, or crowded while one keystone input is the binding constraint. I want that taught, and I want it to fall out of the framework rather than arrive as a bolt-on fourth factor. Second, the framework returns different rankings depending on whether you apply it to a cause, an intervention, or the funding ecosystem as a whole, and I don't know which level to open with. Third, the three factors do not literally multiply: their units don't match and cost enters twice.

Tell me what has to be in place before each of those can be raised and why, the one worked example that should carry the session and what it must have and lack (I've seen the many-people-mopping-one-spill picture and think it teaches the wrong thing), the mistakes this specific audience will make in the order they'll make them with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, one closing question that would show me they can apply the framework rather than restate it, and what I should openly decline to cover in ninety minutes.
