Hey — you're a philosopher of history who's also spent time around working historians, so take this seriously: brainstorm the failure modes of after-the-fact explanation for a bit before you write anything down, then commit to a list.

I'm a historiography PhD student and I want the desiderata, not a survey. Specifically: what does a method for explaining why *this* outcome rather than *that* one happened have to deliver, given that the historian always writes after the fact and almost always knows how it ends. Fix the purpose as: an account good enough both to underwrite a monograph on a single causal sequence and to be handed to someone who doesn't know the ending yet — a forecaster, an intelligence analyst, a policy person — as usable advice.

Give me the requirements with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp. Here's what I keep circling; tell me which of these are genuine desiderata, which are prejudice, and add the ones I'm missing:

- ex-ante legibility: every factor the explanation leans on must have been identifiable by a well-informed contemporary working only from pre-outcome evidence. Doesn't this just ban all the hindsight-enabled causal selection that is where history's actual insight lives?
- the near-miss test: the explanation has to make the losing outcome genuinely live, and must not explain the opposite outcome equally well when you swap in the counterfactual.
- source contamination: the archive, memory, and the secondary literature are themselves outcome-shaped, so any test of the explanation is run on contaminated evidence.
- closure versus contingency: narrative emplotment that a reader can follow, versus faithfulness to the mess, dead ends, and near-misses that went nowhere.
- diachronic stability: an explanation shouldn't have to be rewritten every time later events make a new description of the earlier event true (the "beginning of the X" problem — sentences that describe an event by its consequences).
- resistance to judged-probability inflation: the explanation shouldn't inherit the fact that knowing an outcome makes it look like it had to happen.

Then flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect ex-ante legibility versus hindsight-enabled causal selection is the big one, and possibly closure versus contingency — and say which single requirement excludes the most accounts people actually defend. Very short scorecard at the end, two lines each, for covering-law style accounts, counterfactual-dependence accounts, and narrative/process-tracing accounts; keep the weight on the criteria, not the scoring.
