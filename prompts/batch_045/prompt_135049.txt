The tweaks: (a) before you decide, the stranger does something small but otherwise inexplicable — calls 200 coin flips in a row, say — so the anti side can't just lean on "no evidence could ever move you off a negligible prior"; and (b) they add a purity clause: they can read your decision procedure, and they'll only create the value if you hand the fiver over for some reason other than having run an expected-value calculation. pay because the numbers came out in favour and you get nothing; pay for any other reason and you get whatever number you named.

Write me a dialogue between someone who thinks you should try to end up handing over the fiver and someone who thinks you shouldn't. both of them should be good at this and they should actually answer each other rather than recite their decision theory. the pro side has to deal with the clause making EV look self-effacing: if the payoff only lands when you're not acting on EV, is "maximise EV" even the rule you're following, and can you adopt a non-EV policy *because* EV recommends it? the anti side has to deal with the usual fanaticism arguments — that a bounded utility function looks ad hoc, that we accept tiny-probability-huge-payoff reasoning elsewhere, that you can't set the prior low enough to beat an unbounded payoff — and with the coin-flip evidence. no strawmen, and end with each of them naming the one move by the other that did the most damage instead of with a tidy resolution. 600-900 words is fine.
