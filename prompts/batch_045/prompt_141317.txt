i'm in a philosophy of social science seminar this term (cross-listed with poli sci, 16 people, and we've got Popper's Poverty of Historicism for a week and a half) and I've been asked to run one 75-minute session on it. I don't want to stand at the front and summarise the argument — I want the room to walk into it. So: design me a forecasting game.

The setup I have in mind is a simulated little society with three variables that get reported each round (say grain price, migration into the city, and support for some council). Rounds 1-6 everyone gets the same data series and forecasts round 7; those forecasts get published to the whole room before anything happens; and here's the part I care about: each variable reacts to being predicted differently. One is self-fulfilling (a predicted panic causes the panic), one is self-defeating (a predicted bottleneck gets routed around), and one over-corrects and settles back to a fixed point — a level where the forecast is true precisely because everyone's already adjusted to it. I want them to work out that three-way distinction from their own scoreboard, not from me. Then a period boundary: mid-game, draw an "invention" card (printing press, new crop, whatever) that just invalidates the old series, so extrapolation across the boundary fails even though fitting within the period was going fine. And I want a ledger where they can log conditional forecasts — "if the council holds the subsidy then X" — separately from unconditional ones, and see which kind survives.

Deliverables: the rules and the payoff scheme (no real money, no laptops, just printouts and a board), how the feedback rule actually computes each new round, a minute-by-minute timeline, and how the scoring should be set up so that trend-fitting scores well and long-range unconditional prophecy scores badly. Nobody should hear "Oedipus effect" or "historicism" or "piecemeal social engineering" until the debrief.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific bits of Popper — including the awkward one, which is that a fixed-point equilibrium in my game is arguably a *law* of the simulation, and a smart student will say so. And be honest about what the game can't stage at all: I think the real argument is the one about not being able to predict tomorrow's knowledge without already having it, and I suspect a forecast game can only fake that with the invention card. Say plainly where I'd have to supply the argument myself.

Commit to one design — don't hand me three options to choose between — and tell me why the scoring rule you picked is the right one. Also give me your actual call on what will go wrong the first time I run it: my worry is half the room concludes "history is just random" and the other half concludes "you just need better models."
