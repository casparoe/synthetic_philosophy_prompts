I'm one of four volunteers who decide who gets money out of a small emergency hardship fund at the community centre where I help out. Grants up to about £400, we meet every fortnight, twenty or so aplications a time, and we have to write a sentence explaining each decision — to the applicant, and to whoever takes my place when I stop. I've been doing it three years, I'm the one who trains the new people, and honestly I don't know what I'm handing on.

I've been trying to write down what counts for and what counts against, and I can't, and I don't think it's because I haven't tried hard enough. Here's what we've actually decided, going by gut.

- Family living nearby: normally counts against, they can help. For one woman whose family is who she's hiding from, it counted hard in favour — she needed money to get out of the area.
- Money in the bank: normally against, use it first. For an applicant with £900 saved, which was a deposit on a room in another city where she'd been offered work, it counted for — we topped it up so she wouldn't have to break into it.
- A car: normally against, sell it. For a man on nights at a warehouse with no bus route, it counted for, it's how he keeps the job.
- We've helped them before: normally against. For a man who'd come twice in fifteen years, it counted for — it showed he only asks when it's really bad.
- The application came in three weeks late: normally against. Then we found out she's dyslexic and it took her a month to find someone to read the form to her, and the lateness just stopped counting against. But the dyslexia on its own isn't a reason to give her money either — it doesn't make her needier. I don't know what kind of fact that makes it.
- Which is like: "he was abrupt with our volunteer on the phone" — I don't think that's a reason against at all. Not a weak reason that gets outweighed, just nothing. Whereas "he's got a job" is a real reason against for some applicants and no reason at all for others, and a job that pays so little you're still choosing between food and the meter feels like it isn't the same fact, but maybe I'm kidding myself there.
- The pair I can't reconcile: we gave money to the woman who needed a deposit to get to a confirmed job in another city, and we turned down a man who wanted money for a van so he could take a building job forty miles away. Same shape, opposite decisions. I can't tell you the difference except that his plan felt like a punt.

My flatmate did a philosophy MA and when I told her all this she said something about particularism and "holism", and I genuinely can't tell if she was taking the piss.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that actually gets these verdicts? Not a list of factors to weigh, I can write that myself — I mean something that says why the same fact points one way here and the other way there. Is there such a thing as a default way a fact counts, with exceptions, or does that fall apart as soon as the exceptions have exceptions? Which of my verdicts would you throw out rather than invent a clause for, and which do you think I've got wrong in a way I can't see? Be definite about it, please — don't lay out both sides and leave me to pick, tell me what you think is really going on and why.

Two more things. First, one off tomorrow's list, a real one: single dad, works, asking for £300 for a laptop because his son's school moved everything online and the kid has been doing homework on a phone; £400 in the bank, has a car, and his ex has written in to say he's fine and doesn't need it. Say what your account says about that one even if it isn't the answer I want. Second: what do I actually tell the new volunteers — hand them a list, or tell them to sit in on five meetings and get a feel? If there's no principle in this, how is it that I've got better at it? Or have I.
