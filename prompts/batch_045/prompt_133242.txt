I'm convening a two-session adversarial collaboration for a graduate reading group on population ethics and decision theory, and I need the closing document the two participants should have produced. One of them is a steadfast longtermist: she holds that the expected value of favourably shaping the trajectory of a technologically mature civilisation is astronomically large, that even quite small probabilities of altering it dominate any near-term comparison, and that the fanaticism objection — the instability of expected-value maximisation over heavy-tailed payoff distributions, the Pascal's-mugging family of worries — is a problem for the formal apparatus but not a reason to abandon the practical conclusion. The other is a simulation-deflater: he grants the astronomical-waste arithmetic as a first pass, but argues that once the simulation argument is taken seriously — the familiar trilemma of near-certain extinction before a posthuman stage, near-zero interest in running ancestor simulations, or near-certainty that we are in one — the expected value either collapses, because the future accessible to us is truncated or is not ours to steer, or becomes so dependent on which hypothesis in the mixture is true that no intervention can be said to robustly dominate, and that the honest response to that is to temper rather than amplify. Their first session was mostly them talking past each other: she kept treating the simulation hypothesis as one more branch in a mixture, and mixtures only ever add stakes, while he kept insisting that the simulation argument is distinctive because it attacks the identity and owner of the future rather than its size, so that adding the branch does not simply add value. I want the document that would have made the second session productive.

Write the joint statement itself, not a verdict on who is right and not a dramatised exchange. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — substantive things both can sign, not platitudes, including what they accept about the trilemma's structure, about the arithmetic of astronomical stakes, about the ethical status of simulated minds (they both accept that welfare which supervenes on computational structure is not discounted merely for being implemented on a substrate someone else owns, so please do not spend the document on that), and about the general fanaticism problem and the simulation argument's relation to it; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the longtermist would accept for her own view and once in wording the deflater would accept for his, with a column flagging whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and, where it is verbal, naming the ambiguity that produced it (I suspect one is that the far future can mean the future of the causal lineage we can affect, the future of base reality, or the measure-weighted future of all minds that ever run, and another is that dampen can mean lowering expected value, lowering robustness, or changing the recommended action — but find your own as well); (3) a cruxes section listing the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, with priority ordering and a note on which crux each side thinks is the real one; and (4) for each crux, one concrete case, hypothetical observation, or argument both would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about what it would show, written down in advance and in a form that could actually be checked, plus a closing paragraph on what each side would count as evidence against its own position and whether the two of them can agree on anything that would genuinely settle the matter rather than merely relabel it.

On the cruxes, I have candidates and I want you to use them, sharpen them, or replace them with better ones, saying explicitly when you think one of mine is a pseudo-crux. First: whether the simulations that dominate observer-measure are ancestor simulations run from a base reality outside us, or nested simulations run by successors inside our own simulation — because in the first case steering the real far future scales the number of simulations that get run, which amplifies, while in the second the case for steering base reality looks like it may not even refer to anything we can influence. Second: the runtime-selection question, which I have not seen either of them address — if operators have finite compute budgets and run many simulations, then conditional on being a randomly selected observer-moment one should ask what the expected remaining runtime of one's own simulation is, and whether a longer-running simulation contains more or fewer observer-moments per unit of compute. Third: the re-run possibility, that our world is one of many varied runs whose results are then aggregated, where averaging over runs dilutes our impact to near nothing while copying the best-performing branch forward amplifies it grotesquely. Fourth: whether existential-risk reduction keeps the same sign across the sim and no-sim hypotheses at all, or whether under some hypotheses succeeding risks triggering early termination — the thought that the operators switch the simulation off once the interesting part is over, or intervene when their object of study is threatened with extinction. Fifth: the meta-crux, whether multiplying simulation hypotheses raises the variance of the payoff distribution and so makes expected-value maximisation more fanatical, or raises the weight on model uncertainty and so should make it less confident. For the observation-based cruxes, the natural case both would accept as bearing on them is a discovery of resource-optimised rendering in fundamental physics — resolution or cutoffs that depend on whether anyone is looking, signatures that look like a compute budget rather than a law — or, conversely, evidence that our physics is unusually cheap per observer; record for each what the deflater predicts it would do to the practical recommendation and what the longtermist predicts, including the possibility that the longtermist says it should push further toward longtermism, since a simulation running at all is evidence that some civilisation reached a posthuman stage.

Aim for something a visiting speaker could read in ten minutes, roughly 900 to 1300 words including the tables, with the numbered agreed-claims section first and the disagreements table after it. Keep the document self-contained: state every substantive premise in your own words, and do not cite or allude to specific papers, authors, or titles, since several people in the group will be reading it without access to the literature. If any part of the dispute turns out on inspection to be a clash of priors rather than a question about evidence, say so in those terms rather than manufacturing a test that would not discriminate.
