I run a small reading group — three graduate students and a retired entomologist — and we have just spent a fortnight on the Aunt Hillary material: first the claim that an ant colony could be the sort of thing that has beliefs and desires, and then the harder claim that it could be the sort of thing there is something it is like to be. Every case I have written myself collapses in the same way: I make the colony good at something — choosing a nest site by quorum, pruning a trail network down to the shortest route, holding a fungus garden within a degree of its optimum — and someone says that cleverness was never the question. So I want a case built to a specification, and the specification is meant to foreclose that reply. The point of the case is to show the group that "does the colony have a mind?" is really three questions we habitually run together, and that agreeing about every observable fact of the colony's behaviour settles none of them. Here are the three tests I want the case to separate, named so there is no ambiguity: (A) the pattern test — a system is a mind if attributing states to the whole buys prediction and explanation you cannot get by tracking the parts individually; (B) the thin-cut test — a subject is a local maximum of internal causal integration, found by cutting the system at its weakest coupling, so that what falls out as more strongly and reciprocally intertwined within than without is the mind; (C) the organisation test — a mind must be a self-maintaining, goal-directed organisation with a boundary it acts to preserve, the unit for whose survival and reproduction the behaviour is arranged.

What I need is a matched pair of colonies — same species, same population size, same behavioural repertoire, and equally successful at the same task — that differ in exactly one structural respect. For the baseline member of the pair (a single nest, say) all three tests should return the same verdict: one mind at the level of the colony, none at the level of the individual ant; show that briefly, so we can see the tests are capable of agreeing. For the second member, (A) and (C) should return one mind spanning the whole, while (B) should balk and locate the subject lower down, in sub-colonial units, yielding two rather than one. The disagreement must be generated by the structure alone: no difference in competence, no colony that is worse at its task, no difference in what an outside observer could see the colony achieve, and no test may change its verdict about the individual ants between the two cases. That is the hard part, and the part I keep failing at: the coupling between the two halves has to be thick enough that (A) and (C) genuinely pick out the whole — traffic across it changes what the whole does, whether by carrying a quorum, redirecting brood care, or redistributing food — and simultaneously thin enough that (B) cuts there. Say explicitly what keeps the channel on both sides of that line, in real ant mechanisms (pheromone deposition, a handful of scouts, tandem runs, trophallaxis, polydomous nests joined by trails, supercolonies with muted nestmate recognition — use whatever is real), and say how much the channel can be thickened before the thin-cut test flips. No science fiction: no implants, no engineered or superintelligent ants, nothing field biologists could not set up with a tube, a barrier, or a little paint.

Please give me: first the scenario as a single paragraph of no more than 180 words; then, in one short paragraph each, what each of the three tests says about the second colony and why, with the reasoning shown rather than asserted; then a paragraph on which single structural feature is doing the work and what happens to each verdict if you remove it or thicken it; then the strongest objection available to whichever test comes out of your case looking worst, and the reply you would give. Close with one sentence naming the feature that carries the argument. Keep the whole response under 700 words, and do not pad it with throat-clearing or with generalities about collective intelligence that my group has already heard.
