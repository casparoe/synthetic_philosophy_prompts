hey — I'm putting together the take-home final for my 4th-year philosophy of biology seminar, and I need one question on the units of selection debate. Last year I asked something like "is the gene the unit of selection?" and got twelve near-identical essays back: genes are replicators, organisms are vehicles, groups only in special cases. I want to make that impossible this time.

So: write me one question where memorising the labels gets you a B at most. Here's the setup I want it built around (sharpen it if you like, and add parameters where you need them). An asexual population. Every generation, individuals are randomly assorted into groups of fixed size. A costly "cooperator" allele makes its bearer leave fewer offspring than its group-mates but raises the group's total output. Then the groups dissolve and everybody goes back into one common pool to reproduce. So there are no group lineages — the group exists for a single episode and never reproduces as a group.

The crux I'm after is this: a between-group term in the multilevel Price partition is available however you slice the population, so its presence can't by itself be evidence that selection is acting on groups — and symmetrically, a gene-frequency description is always available too. So the level question has to be a causal question rather than a bookkeeping one. I want students to have to say (a) what the selection-of / selection-for distinction does and doesn't settle here, (b) whether a level has to be a lineage, or a reproducer, for it to be a genuine unit of selection, and whats lost if you drop that requirement, (c) what further empirical or counterfactual information would actually settle the case, and (d) what the "it's only a matter of which description is convenient" answer gets right, and where it runs out.

Deliverables, please:

1. The question as I'd actually hand it out. They've seen the Price equation in class and shouldn't have to rederive it. No "summarise so-and-so's view" — and build it on an invented example rather than a textbook one, I'm sick of meerkats and slime moulds. Assume about 50 minutes of a take-home.
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25: five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and one line on how students typically get each one wrong. Then a short note on what seperates an A from a strong B — especially how to grade the student who answers "it's all just bookkeeping, the levels talk is pragmatic," since I suspect that one is half right and I want to reward the half that is.
