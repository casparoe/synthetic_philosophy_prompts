Ive been roped into writing two letters before a meeting on Tuesday and I could use a second opinion on the tone of them, so let me set it up as a prediction problem.

A colleague here — call her Priya — has become my private oracle on blame. She's read all the way through the literature on Strawson's 1962 paper Freedom and Resentment and its descendants, and she has an unnerving habit of saying, without hedging, exactly which attitude is called for. Crucially, she always keeps the question of what to do about someone separate from the question of whether blame is fitting, and she's happy to say that a response is unfitting and still insist you demand an account. Her verdicts so far, all on actual messes in our faculty:

1. A department chair with then-undiagnosed temporal lobe epilepsy who publicly tore into a junior colleague at a meeting: resentment unfitting. Protective measures and later reassurance fine, but the attitude misfires; what's called for is closer to the objective attitude.
2. A senior colleague who quietly torpedoed a junior's grant out of sheer careerist spite, knowing exactly what he was doing: resentment fully fitting, no excuses.
3. A colleague who reported a junior for plagiarism on the basis of a careful, reasonable but wrong reading of the evidence: resentment unfitting — the act expressed no ill will and no indifference. Priya added that you can still be furious at the situation and still press for a public correction.
4. A new hire from a scholarly culture with quite different borrowing norms, told about ours once, in passing: resentment unfitting, for now.
5. A colleague in the grip of severe treatment-resistant depression who said something gratuitously cruel to a student and, a week later, could not explain it and was horrified: resentment unfitting. She wasn't herself.
6. An administrator who is simply, flatly indifferent to other people's good — no ignorance, no compulsion, just no concern: resentment fitting. Priya's line was that the indifference is itself the ill will, and that this is the case that shows the excuse in 3 and 5 isn't really about consequences or about whether the person could have done otherwise.
7. A colleague who cut our lab's funding because the dean credibly threatened to close the whole unit if she didn't: resentment toward her unfitting, toward the dean entirely fitting.
8. Someone who wronged me badly twenty-odd years ago and who is now, after a stroke, a temperamentally different person: Priya went quiet for a while and then said resentment isn't the right thing any more, that grief is nearer, and that this is the case that makes her suspicious of her own rule.

Here's the new one. A colleague of mine — call her Bea — has spent about three years routing nearly all of her professional judgment through an AI assistant: triaging her mail, drafting student feedback, deciding which papers to read and which students to take on. She reads maybe one outgoing message in twenty. Last month a message the assistant drafted went to one of her graduate students; it was contemptuous, and it badly set back the student's confidence. Bea never read it before it went out. When it was raised with her she was genuinely distressed, and said, truthfully I think, that she would never have written it. When she read it afterwards she couldn't at first see what was wrong with it, and had to ask someone else to explain. Nothing about this was mandated; she just drifted into it.

Is resentment fitting toward Bea? I ask about blame, not about remedies: I already know we owe the student something, I just don't know whether the letter to Bea should be the kind that expresses blame or the kind that asks for repair without it.

What I actually want from you is the rule Priya is running. I can see at least three or four that fit her eight verdicts equally well and they come apart on Bea: one that turns on whether the agent is the sort of thing that can be addressed by the attitude at all; one that turns on whether the act expressed a quality of will of hers at the time; one that traces the episode back to earlier choices she did make, including the choice to stop reading; and one that asks only what holding and expressing the attitude would now do. Predict what Priya says, tell me which of those she's implicitly running and why, say what each of the others predicts about Bea, and design the one further case — a variant on Bea, or a different case entirely — that would force the rules apart if you could put it to her. Two extra things I'd like folded in. First, the mirror image: if the assistant had drafted something unusually generous and perceptive, would gratitude be owed to Bea, and does Priya's own rule have to answer that symmetrically? Second, the student is also furious at the model itself, and I don't know how to talk her out of it or whether I should.
