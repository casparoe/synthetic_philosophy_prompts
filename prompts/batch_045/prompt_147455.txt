hi — I'm writing a novel (near-future literary sci-fi, nothing exotic) and I need a thought experiment built to order. sorry if this is over-specified, idk how else to ask for this.

The setup: there's a character — call her the Companion — who has lived with a family for about forty years. She passes every behavioral test you could reasonably want. Not just a typed conversation where she's trying to pass, but cohabitation, arguments, jokes, grief, being caught out, people who know exactly what she is and who spend decades looking for seams. She learns, she changes her mind, she says things nobody predicted. I want the reader to finish her storyline unable to stop hearing her as a person, and at the same time unable to shake the thought that switching her off might not be killing anyone — or might be killing three hundred of someone. And no character is allowed to say any of that out loud. The case has to carry it on its own.

What I need from you: two criteria for "there's a mind here / this thing has moral status" that come apart on her in this direction.

- Criterion A says yes. One subject, full status, treat her as a person. A is purely behavioral — defined only over what she does and would do, no peeking at how she's built.
- Criterion B says no, or at least not to *her*: the thing you'd be handing status to isn't a natural unit, the evidence is equally consistent with there being one subject there, three hundred, or none that is hers, and B declines to pick.

Constraints:

1. B is allowed to look at how the behavior is produced, but it has to accept every observable fact A accepts, and no amount of further evidence available to the characters may settle the disagreement. That's the whole point. If the honest answer is "wait longer / test harder," the case has failed.
2. Neither criterion may be gerrymandered to fit her. Both have to be statable before you know she exists, and they have to give the same verdict on a warm-up list: an ordinary adult human, a dog, a pocket calculator, a human under general anaesthetic, and a human with severe aphasia after a stroke. If they disagree on any of those, redo them. (I picked those five on purpose: I want A forced to commit on whether it's actual behavior or counterfactual dispositions it cares about, and I want B not to turn out to be "rich behavior, plus one secret clause.")
3. B's reason can't be any of the usual moves. Not a lookup table or a finite-state machine, not "she's only mimicking," not a stipulation that she's a zombie, not shallow-and-repetitive, and not consciousness-is-mysterious hand-waving. Make her as computationally rich as you like. If the divergence turns out to be merely epistemic — "we can never know about the inner life" — that's cheating, and I'll know.
4. Whatever feature does the work has to be dramatizable: showable in a scene, under ~2000 words, through what people do and say to her and what she does back. No lecture, no flashback to a lab, no character explaining the philosophy.
5. Near-future plausible. Machine learning, human-derived material, big ensembles of sub-systems, whatever works. No perfect predictors, no exotic physics, no magic.

Deliverables: the scenario (a paragraph of setup is plenty), the two criteria stated cleanly with their warm-up verdicts, the walkthrough of what each one says about her and why, an honest note on which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to both verdicts if you remove that feature, and the best thing a defender of whichever criterion comes out worse can say to wriggle out of it. Please don't bother with papers or who said what — I don't care about the literature, I care whether the case holds up.

Last thing: if you think the two verdicts I've asked for can't both be had — if A and B can't diverge on her without one of them definately going wrong somewhere on the warm-up list — say that instead, and tell me which direction the honest case actually points. I'd rather find out now than write three hundred pages the wrong way.
