Me and a colleague keep going round in circles on low-impact AI. The pitch is that you can get an agent to avoid side effects — not knocking over the vase on the way to its goal — by penalising it for changing the world much relative to what would have happened if it had just done nothing, and thereby skip the bit where you have to write down what's actually valuable. I'm at about 0.3 that this can work in principle; she's at 0.75.

I want your number on a slightly more specific version, because the plain version has a hole I think everyone notices: charge for every change and the agent becomes scared to act at all, even when acting is harmless or is the whole point. So suppose the penalty only charges for effects the agent can't cheaply undo. Compare the world after the agent has acted and then cleaned up as best it can, with the inaction world, and penalise the remaining gap — effects that a restorer on the same budget as the agent could have put back are free. Call it a restorability-discounted impact penalty.

Give me your credence that a penalty of this shape, trained off an inaction baseline with no information about human values beyond the task reward, produces an agent that avoids the side effects we'd actually want avoided in a messy deployment environment — rather than one that's paralysed, or one that quietly learns to game the penalty. Decompose it: whether "side effect" can be picked out at all without knowing which changes matter; whether inaction is the right baseline to measure from; whether the restorability credit really neutralises over-penalisation or just relocates it somewhere weirder; and whether any of it survives an agent smart enough to optimise against the measure. A number and one line each, then how they combine, then tell me which of those four my 0.3 and her 0.75 are most likely actually disagreeing about, and which one you'd least want to bet on.

Commit to actual numbers rather than "it depends which environment", and write it in British English.
