I'm a third-year engineering student, and this term I'm also taking an upper-level seminar on Hellenistic ethics. We've just finished the Stoic material and are now looking at the dichotomy of control as it reappears in the modern Stoic revival: the "attend only to what is within your control" version that circulates in popular self-help and productivity writing, generally detached from the rest of the Stoic system. I don't want a summary of the view or a short history of the revival. I want a translation exercise, and I want the residue of the translation to be the main thing you give me.

My own coursework this term is state-space control, so that is the vocabulary I actually think in: plant, state, measured output, control input versus exogenous disturbance input, actuator authority, controllability and stabilizability as properties of the (A, B) pair, disturbance rejection versus disturbance feedforward with measurement, regulation versus reference tracking, quadratic cost functions, and model uncertainty. I'm comfortable with all of it, so please don't simplify. Restate the dichotomy of control, and the practical injunction the modern revival extracts from it, in those terms as faithfully as you can — and flag explicitly where one of my terms is doing genuine work and where it is only a metaphor that flatters the Stoics.

Four mappings I want you to test rather than presuppose:

1. The division between what is "up to us" and what is not, read as the controllable subspace versus the rest of the state space. One of my professors keeps insisting that controllability is a property of a model and not of a thing: it depends on the pair (A, B), including where the actuators are placed. If that is right, is the Stoic dichotomy a discovery about which regions of a human life are steerable, or a recommendation about which model of a human life to adopt — and in particular about where to draw the system boundary, so that your own desires and judgments are inside the plant and your body, reputation and possessions are disturbances entering from outside?

2. The popular injunction to withdraw concern from what is not up to you, read as a refusal to model or measure the disturbance channel. In my coursework, discarding a measurable disturbance is straightforwardly a design error: feedforward compensation dominates ignoring it. So is the popular version just bad control advice, or is the translation wrong — because the objective is not output tracking but something else, so that "using" externals while not staking anything on them is compatible with modelling them well?

3. The less popular but more central Stoic claim that the real work is on judgment and assent, i.e. on what you treat as good and bad. In my terms this is the controller rewriting its own cost function, which my framework treats as fixed from outside, supplied by the designer. Does anything in control theory even gesture at this, or is this precisely the part with no counterpart?

4. A reconstruction I've been toying with and want checked: that the dichotomy is really a claim about finite actuator authority — that control effort spent on inputs which reach the outcomes you care about only through weak or noisy coupling is effort wasted, so the dichotomy is an allocation rule rather than a metaphysical boundary. Does the translation support that reading, undermine it, or have no opinion?

Then the residue report, in three columns: what translated cleanly, what translated only with distortion, and what could not be expressed in my vocabulary at all. For the third column I especially want the case of blame and credit: control theory has causal steering but nothing corresponding to "this outcome is attributable to you," and I suspect the dichotomy is doing two jobs at once — one causal and one moral-accounting — of which only the first survives. Tell me whether you think that residue indicts my vocabulary (something control theory cannot say and should be able to) or the original claim (a single distinction quietly doing two unrelated things), and what would settle it.
