I'm writing up my position on the ethics of eating animals for a graduate seminar in practical ethics, and before I defend it I want the package audited rather than argued with. Below are my commitments, stated as flatly as I can, followed by a set of empirical figures I want taken as given. What I want from you is a consistency audit, not a general case for or against meat eating: assume the welfarist framing in (1) throughout, and don't spend time on whether the empirical numbers are realistic.

My commitments:

(1) Welfarism about food. Nothing about a diet matters morally except what it does to the experiences of sentient beings. Species, ecosystems, rural traditions, the naturalness of a practice and the aesthetic character of a landscape carry no independent weight.

(2) Expected value under uncertainty about sentience. I give roughly a one-third credence to insects and other small invertebrates having aversive experiences that matter morally, and I do not discount a large, uncertain welfare effect merely because the probability attached to it is moderate. Large numbers of small candidate harms can outweigh small numbers of large certain ones.

(3) Beef rather than chicken. On the rare occasions when I eat meat, I choose beef over chicken, on the grounds that a steer yields vastly more edible mass than a bird, so beef involves far fewer animal deaths per unit of food.

(4) Crop deaths are real but routinely overstated. Cultivation and harvest kill field animals, and that is a cost of plant foods. But most of the mice, voles and insects killed by farming would never have existed at all had the crops not been planted; and I hold that an animal whose existence is owed to my food choices is not thereby harmed by existing, so long as its life is not on balance bad. I take this to blunt the usual argument that vegetarianism is self-defeating, and I remain mostly vegetarian.

(5) Pessimism about wild animal lives. I accept the standard pessimistic picture: for most small wild animals, lives cut short by starvation, predation, parasitism and exposure are probably net negative, and the sheer numbers make this one of the largest moral problems there is.

(6) Environmentalism in practice. I support habitat conservation, I oppose converting wild land either to farmland or to development, and I buy organic and local where I can, on the view that lower-intensity farming treads more lightly on the land.

(7) No offsetting. I do not think I can neutralise the costs of my diet by donating to animal-welfare charities or by buying welfare-certified products, and I am suspicious of the thought that a diet could be made permissible by compensating for it elsewhere.

(8) Marginalism, uneasily held. I think the question to ask is what changes given the one purchase I actually make, not what the industry average looks like. I am not persuaded by the objection that one consumer makes no difference, but I could not give you a clean account of why not.

(9) Bivalves are free. I eat oysters and mussels without hesitation, even while taking insect sentience seriously enough to weight it in (2).

Stipulated figures, per million calories of food delivered, counting the slaughtered animals, the field animals directly killed by cultivating and harvesting, insect deaths separately, and the standing wild-animal population the land in question sustains:

Chicken: 220 birds slaughtered; 12 small wild vertebrates; 1.2 million insects; about 1.2 hectare-years of land.

Feedlot beef: 1.6 cattle slaughtered; 25 small wild vertebrates; 2.8 million insects; about 8 hectare-years.

Grass-finished beef: 1.6 cattle; 4 small wild vertebrates; 0.3 million insects; about 25 hectare-years of rangeland.

Pulses: none slaughtered; 30 small wild vertebrates; 3.5 million insects; about 0.5 hectare-years.

A hectare-year of cropped or grazed land sustains on the order of 40 to 60 small wild mammals plus comparable numbers of birds, reptiles and amphibians; the same area under pavement or buildings sustains on the order of 2. Organic pulses require about 25 percent more land than conventional ones. For arithmetic I have been tentatively using a per-insect weight of roughly one ten-thousandth of a chicken's lifetime suffering, multiplied by my one-third credence, though I have never checked how much of my view rests on that figure.

The questions I want answered:

Which of the nine actually pull against one another? Take them pair by pair where a conflict is real, and rank the conflicts by severity rather than listing them as equally damning. For each, say which commitment has to give, and say what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set — a fix that resolves one clash but forces me to abandon two other commitments is more expensive than it looks.

Then separate out any pair that only appears to conflict. I would guess there is at least one: tell me why it dissolves, whether it dissolves entirely or only in one direction, and whether the dissolution depends on reading one of my commitments in a specific way.

Where a commitment is too vaguely stated for the question of consistency to have an answer, say so and specify the readings that do and do not generate a conflict, rather than choosing a reading silently.

Finally: is there a single revision that cleans up more than one of these clashes at once, and is any item in my list doing no real work — inert with respect to everything else I have said, or merely restating another commitment? If my beef-over-chicken verdict in (3) cannot survive the numbers I have stipulated, say so plainly and tell me what the least costly replacement verdict is.
