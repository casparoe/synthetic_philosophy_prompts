Hey — I write a roughly weekly column on science and ideas, and I keep going in circles on something that shows up in reader mail everytime I touch anything environmental, so I thought I'd ask someone who does this stuff for a living.

The practice first, since I'm not sure I have the right vocabulary for it. When someone criticizes a practice and it comes out that they're tangled up in it themselves — the researcher funded by an industry who then writes about that industry, the animal-welfare guy who eats meat, the journalist who took the junket — the criticism doesn't get answered, it gets waved off. You're one to talk. And it works: audiences discount it, the critic gets told to disclose or shut up, and the original point just evaporates. My own paper has two different institutional versions of this and I've never understood why we reach for one rather then the other: sometimes the fix is disclosure (write the piece, put the conflict in a line at the bottom, let readers adjust), and sometimes it's recusal (you can't go near this story at all). A friend is now building a browser extension that attaches a computed "complicity score" to every name on a page — roughly, how much this person benefits from the thing they're currently attacking — which is the same practice turned into infrastructure, minus the editorial judgment.

Here's the part I can't square. The same readers who'll bin a criticism because the critic is implicated will treat the insider who did the thing and then turned on it as the most authoritative critic there is: the whistleblower, the ex-lobbyist, the disgraced founder with the tell-all. Complicity is a credential there, not a disqualification. And when everyone is implicated — the "and yet you participate in society" gag — the whole move collapses, which makes me think it can't be about complicity as such. (I'm also told there's something in equity law called unclean hands that points the same way, but I don't know the details.)

So what I want is the implicit theory. What does the practice of withdrawing standing from the hypocrite presuppose? Is it a claim about a normative power you forfeit when you blame something you aren't yourself willing to give up, or is it really an epistemic or sincerity claim wearing standing's clothes — and does the disclosure-versus-recusal split track exactly that difference? Does the hypocrite's own conduct have to have been wrong, or is benefiting enough, or is being unable to opt out enough? Is the whistleblower case genuinely in tension, or does one theory cover both? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several rival accounts of standing would all license what we actually do, so that the practice can't be evidence for any of them? And concretely: if I decided tomorrow that the tu quoque move is never legitimate and blame is just a judgment anyone may make, what would I actually have to change about how I assign pieces, disclose conflicts, and handle reader responses — and what would I lose that the current practice is quietly protecting?
