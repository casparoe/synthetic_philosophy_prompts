BACKGROUND (§1)

Popper wanted to say that a false theory can still be closer to the truth than another false theory, so that science can be said to progress through a series of false theories. The naive version — verisimilitude = true consequences minus false consequences — got torpedoed sometime in the 70s by an impossibility-ish result to the effect that no false theory comes out closer to the truth than any other false theory (or that the subtraction definition collapses in some equally bad way; I've never nailed down the exact statement, which is part of why I'm writing this). The modern family of approaches goes via similarity between possible worlds: pick a metric on world-space, look at the set of worlds your theory allows, and measure how far that set is from the actual world, aggregated somehow (min, average, min-sum, etc).

MY TOY SETUP (§2)

Two atomic facts: R = it rains, C = it's cloudy. Actual world is R∧C. So four worlds: w1 = R∧C (actual), w2 = R∧¬C, w3 = ¬R∧C, w4 = ¬R∧¬C. Distance between worlds = number of atomic facts they differ on, so d(w1,w2) = d(w1,w3) = 1, d(w1,w4) = 2.

I want to compare three measures. "min" = distance to the closest world the theory allows; "avg" = mean distance over all worlds the theory allows; and then my own thing, call it the Q-score. For the Q-score you fix a set Q of yes/no questions (here: does it rain? is it cloudy?), and score a theory per question: 1 if the theory entails the true answer, 0 if it entails a false answer, and b = 1/2 if it's silent (compatible with both answers). Average over questions.

theory | allowed worlds | min | avg | Q-score
R∧C | {w1} | 0 | 0 | 1
R (says nothing about clouds) | {w1,w2} | 0 | 0.5 | 0.75
⊤ (tautology) | all four | 0 | 1 | 0.5
R∧¬C | {w2} | 1 | 1 | 0.5
¬R∧C | {w3} | 1 | 1 | 0.5
"exactly one of R,C" | {w2,w3} | 1 | 1 | 0.5
¬R | {w3,w4} | 1 | 1.5 | 0.25
⊥ | {} | undefined | undefined | 0

The points I want to hang on this: under the min measure the tautology ties the complete truth (which seems like the standard complaint about min — it makes saying nothing maximally truthlike). Under avg, informativeness gets rewarded, but the tautology, the two one-error theories and the xor theory all land on the same number, so false theories aren't differentiated much. Under the Q-score, the tautology sits strictly between informative-true and informative-false, false theories do get differentiated (0.5 vs 0.25), and the contradiction is worst. Silence is exactly middling.

I initially thought this made expected Q-score a proper scoring rule, but I checked and I don't think it does: with b = 1/k for a k-answer question, silence is weakly dominated (assert your modal answer, since its probability is ≥ 1/k), and nothing rewards you for reporting your actual degree of belief rather than just your top guess. So it's a coarse accuracy score, not a scoring rule in the Brier sense. I can't tell if that's a bug I should fix by moving to credences, or if the whole point of verisimilitude talk is that it's about theories-as-claims rather than about credences, in which case coarseness is fine.

THE BIT THAT WORRIES ME (§4)

The Q-score ordering isn't invariant to which questions you put in Q, and it's not just a matter of adding new information. Take three atoms R, C, W with actual world R∧C∧W, and compare the two one-error theories A = R∧C∧¬W and B = ¬R∧C∧W. Under Q = {rain?, cloudy?, wind?} they tie at 2/3. Add the logically redundant question "is it both raining and cloudy?" — its answer is already fixed by the first two — and A gets 3/4 while B gets 1/2. Add "is it both cloudy and windy?" instead and it flips the other way: A gets 1/2, B gets 3/4. So adding a question that carries zero new information flips the ranking, exactly like cloning a candidate flips a Borda count. Which means Q is doing all the work, and there's no canonical Q.

IS THIS ARROW? (§5)

My half-baked claim: the problem of verisimilitude is really an aggregation problem. Questions are like voters, theories are like candidates, and wanting THE truthlikeness ordering is wanting to aggregate per-question verdicts into one global ordering. So the 70s-style impossibility results would be special cases of a social-choice-flavored impossibility rather than facts about some particular definition. But I can't make the mapping precise and I don't know if it's a real theorem or just me pattern-matching on the word "impossibility". Concretely: Arrow wants fixed voters each carrying a full ordering over alternatives, whereas my "voters" are logically interrelated and each one only hands a theory a 3-way verdict (right/wrong/silent), not a ranking. Independence of irrelevant alternatives would have to become something like "the ranking of two theories depends only on questions where they give different verdicts", which I can't even satisfy in my own setup given §4. So maybe the right frame isn't Arrow but clone-independence / positional-rule characterization stuff, or judgment aggregation, or maybe there's no theorem and the right moral is just "there is no privileged question set, deal with it". This is the section I most want torn apart.

DEFLATIONARY WORRY (§6)

Separately: maybe none of this matters. The rationalist-flavored version of me says what you actually want out of a belief is calibrated credences feeding a decision procedure, and "our theory is closer to the truth" is either reducible to expected predictive accuracy or it's an honorific. But the intuition that Newton is closer to the truth than Aristotle, and closer than Ptolemy, feels like it's asserting something over and above "Newton predicts better" — and it's the thing that structural realists lean on against the pessimistic meta-induction. Can the deflationist actually cash that out, or does it secretly need a real verisimilitude measure? If the deflationist wins, is my whole §2–§5 project pointless?
