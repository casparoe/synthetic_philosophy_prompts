I sit on the Clinical Documentation and Accommodations Committee at a large university-affiliated outpatient psychiatry clinic. Our remit is narrow but consequential: we decide what goes into charts, what letters the clinic will sign for employers, insurers and housing authorities, and — because the same committee also reviews our involuntary-hold and medication-over-objection paperwork — how the clinic's own use of diagnostic categories is supposed to hang together. We meet Thursday. I have been asked to write the decision memo on a case that has split us, and I would like you to draft it.

The patient — call her R., 41, four years with the clinic — presented with recurrent panic attacks, avoidance of transit and crowded rooms, and a stretch of roughly fourteen months in which she could not reliably leave her apartment. She has been in twice-weekly therapy and takes sertraline. Her clinician reports marked improvement over the last eighteen months and attributes it to both the medication and a graded exposure protocol. R. does not dispute any of this. She is not asking us to stop treating her, does not want the therapy or the medication discontinued, and does not deny that she suffers or that the treatment helped.

What she is asking for is narrower and, I think, more interesting. She needs documentation now: her employer's occupational health office wants a letter supporting a part-time, remote-work arrangement plus back pay on a short-term disability claim, and the documentation has to state that the limitations arise from a medical condition. R. asks that the letter describe her functional limitations in detail and contain no diagnostic label. She has been reading Szasz and puts her position to us as follows: nothing has ever been found on any test; there is no lesion; illness language asserts a bodily pathology she does not have; the label is not a description of her but something done to her; and it has already injured her, because an earlier chart entry was disclosed in a child-custody proceeding and she believes it counted against her. She has also said, through her clinician, that she would happily accept a letter using the words impairment or disability. Her objection is specifically to illness.

The constraints we are operating under:

- The billing office will not close or reimburse the episode of care without a DSM code, and the payer will not continue reimbursing the therapy without one.
- Our payer contracts are permissive about which code. The resident on our committee points out, with data, that a substantial share of charts in this clinic already carry unspecified or adjustment-disorder codes chosen not because they are the best clinical description but because they are the least stigmatizing code that will be paid. Her question is why we are discovering a conscience in this particular case.
- R. reads everything in her chart, including clinical notes, through our open-notes policy. We cannot quietly record a diagnosis she will not see.
- The occupational health office's intake form has a required field for an underlying medical condition, and our own letter template has the same field. We can decline to fill it in, but we cannot fill it in with something we do not believe.

Two further things came up that I think are where the real disagreement lives. First, the medical director notes that in the past two years two patients referred to us with what looked like textbook panic attacks turned out to have a catecholamine-secreting tumor, and a third had a paroxysmal cardiac arrhythmia; all three stopped having attacks after non-psychiatric treatment. His claim is that this presenting picture is therefore not a mere value judgment dressed in clinical vocabulary — it is a real clinical kind with an organic differential, and declining to name it is not neutrality but a failure of care. R.'s advocate replies that the existence of a differential shows only that some cases are misattributed, and that it does nothing to show that the remainder are diseases. Second, a peer member of the committee says we can set all metaphysics aside and just ask what the classification is for; the resident answers that this is precisely the Szaszian point, because what the classification is for is not a neutral question — among the things it is for are billing, eligibility, and, elsewhere in our own paperwork, detention and treatment over objection.

Please draft the memo I will circulate on Monday, roughly 1,000–1,200 words, addressed to the committee. It should:

1. Give a concrete recommendation, not a menu, on three questions: whether we issue the accommodation letter at all and if so in what form (disease framing, functional-impairment framing, or a refusal with an explanation of what we would need to sign); what, if anything, we record in the chart and under what code, given that R. will read it within a day; and what we tell the billing office, including whether the clinic absorbs the cost of any uncompensated care that follows.
2. Attach dates: what goes out by when, what is provisional, what is reviewed, and by what point the underlying policy question comes back to us.
3. Take seriously the strongest version of whichever position you do not adopt — state it as its best advocate in the room would, and say explicitly what would have to be true for it to win. I would rather circulate a memo that the losing side recognises as fair than one that wins the vote by misdescribing them.
4. Address the background worry directly, because it will be cited against whichever way we go on R.: is it coherent for this clinic to treat mental disorder as a medical category for reimbursement and eligibility while declining to treat it as one when coercion is at stake? If you think yes, say what does the work — what the relevant difference between the two uses is, and whether it survives the fact that the same people, the same chart, and the same code are involved in both. If you think no, say which use has to give and what that costs us.
5. Say plainly where you think the case is genuinely hard rather than resolving everything, and do not lean on authorities, quotations or citations: I need reasoning I can defend out loud when the medical director pushes back, not sources I would have to look up.
