Question 4 (20 marks). Ann (A) and Bob (B) play the following four-node centipede game G. Ann moves at nodes 1 and 3, Bob at nodes 2 and 4. At each node the mover either takes, ending the game with the listed payoffs, or passes. Payoffs are given as (Ann, Bob); each player cares only about his or her own monetary payoff; the tree and the payoffs are common knowledge; and the game ends after node 4 whatever happens.

| node | mover | take | pass leads to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ann | (10, 4) | node 2 |
| 2 | Bob | (8, 20) | node 3 |
| 3 | Ann | (40, 16) | node 4 |
| 4 | Bob | (32, 80) | game ends, (160, 64) |

(a) [5 marks] Give the backward-induction outcome of G, and say whether the backward-induction strategy profile is the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium.
(b) [8 marks] What would Ann and Bob each have to believe about the other for the argument in (a) to go through? Be as precise as you can about how strong that assumption is. Then evaluate this objection: "the argument is self-undermining — it tells Ann to take at node 1 on the ground that Bob would take at node 2, but Ann could face a choice at node 3 only if Bob had passed at node 2, which is exactly what the argument says he would never do; so the argument gives Ann no guidance at node 3, and the same flaw infects the reasoning at every earlier node." Does the objection show that backward induction fails as a predictive claim, as a normative claim, or neither?
(c) [7 marks] It is often reported that in laboratory centipedes players pass at the early nodes and frequently earn more than the backward-induction payoff. Does this refute the claim that taking at node 1 is the rational thing for Ann to do? What would have to be true for passing at node 1 to maximise her expected payoff?

Student's answer.

(a) Working backwards: at node 4 Bob gets 80 by taking and 64 by passing, so he takes. Hence if Ann passes at node 3 she gets 32, against 40 from taking, so she takes. Anticipating that, Bob at node 2 gets 16 by passing against 20 from taking, so he takes. And Ann at node 1 gets 8 by passing against 10 from taking, so she takes. The backward-induction profile therefore has both players taking at every node, and the outcome is (10, 4). Since every choice in the induction is strict, this profile is the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium. This is where subgame perfection earns its keep: G has many Nash equilibria, among them profiles in which Ann passes at node 1, Bob passes at node 2, and Ann then takes at node 3, and Nash equilibrium on its own is consistent with any amount of early passing; it is only the subgame-perfect refinement that rules these out.

(b) For the induction to go through, Ann and Bob must both be payoff-maximisers and this must be common knowledge between them: Ann is rational, Ann believes that Bob is rational, Ann believes that Bob believes that Ann is rational, and so on up the whole infinite hierarchy. That is what makes the argument fragile, and it is the target of the self-undermining objection: if Bob has passed at node 2, the hierarchy has broken, and at node 3 Ann can no longer assume what the argument needs her to assume about Bob. So the objection shows that the argument is unsound as a piece of advice at a node that has actually been reached. It does not follow that Ann should pass at node 3: that depends on what she should now expect at node 4, and if Bob's pass was a one-off lapse then taking still maximises her expected payoff. But nothing in the objection turns on Bob's being irrational; it turns on the fact that the induction's verdict about a later node is what makes the earlier node's reasoning go through, so if the later verdict is unavailable the earlier reasoning collapses too.

(c) The experimental pattern does not refute the claim that taking is rational. It can be explained in any of the ways in which the premises fail: subjects may not be pure money-maximisers, they may doubt whether the game really ends at node 4, or they may be unsure whether the other player is a payoff-maximiser. For passing at node 1 to be better than taking, Ann has to be uncertain in the right way: if she assumes that she would herself take at node 3 should she get there, then passing at node 1 is worth more than the certain 10 as soon as her probability that Bob passes at node 2 exceeds 1/16. So the data are evidence about the players' beliefs and preferences, not a refutation of a mathematical result.
