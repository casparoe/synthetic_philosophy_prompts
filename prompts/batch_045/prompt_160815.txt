I'm in eleventh grade, and a few of us run an after-school reading group — six people, a borrowed seminar room, and a photocopied packet — and the session that derailed us was on a taxonomy of minds that sorts creatures by how much of their trial-and-error they can get done before paying for it. Darwinian creatures have a behavioural repertoire fixed by evolution; their learning happened across generations, paid for in deaths. Skinnerian creatures can learn inside a single lifetime, but only by actually doing things and being rewarded or punished for the outcome, so they must risk their own skin to find out what not to do. Popperian creatures can carry candidate actions around internally and discard the bad ones there, letting their hypotheses die in place of themselves. Gregorian creatures go further: they don't merely arrive with a brain, they get fitted out by the culture around them with designed tools for thinking — above all words and other symbols — that reorganise which thoughts are available to them at all. Our tutor kept insisting that the distinctive thing about the Gregorian step is that the tool has to be designed by somebody. We couldn't settle whether that somebody has to be a mind, or whether a blind, cumulative process could count, and the argument has been spilling out of the room ever since.

So I'd like a short story — around 1,200 to 1,800 words — that moves the argument off the seminar table and into fieldwork. Here is the setting I have in mind, which you should feel free to adjust if you can make it better. A small survey team is stationed above a tidally locked moon, cataloguing a colonial species the crew has nicknamed the weavers: fist-sized, mostly sessile, no central brain, building and tending lattice-like arrays of stretched filament on the rock faces in the fog belt. Over the course of the story the team runs three sequences of observation: one showing that individual weavers can solve a novel problem only after physically attempting the wrong thing and paying for it; one suggesting that some individuals can reject a bad option without performing it, apparently by testing it in advance; and one that doesn't fit — the population's signalling repertoire is changing far faster than any individual lifetime could account for, and the new signals work while being, as far as anyone can tell, unintelligible to the weavers who use them. The discovery I want at the centre: the filament arrays are not just storage or display. Variation in the arrays changes how reliably they install useful new discriminations in the brains of the next cohort of weavers, and the weavers' tending behaviour serves as the arrays' reproduction, so the thinking-tools are undergoing selection of their own, in an environment made of weaver brains, at a speed biology could never manage. The team's two lead researchers should disagree about what this is. One argues it is only the Gregorian story with the dial turned up: culture has always been smarter than the people inside it, and we humans already obey rules whose rationale no one can state. The other proposes a fifth rung — call it, if you like, a Babbagean creature: one whose thinking-tools not only get installed from outside but improve themselves by a process their users cannot follow, so that the creature's competence outruns its comprehension not merely in degree but in kind, and there is no one, anywhere, who understands why the new signal is right. Both of them should be good at their jobs and should actually answer each other.

Please don't have anyone lecture the taxonomy or explain the categories out loud; I want the distinctions to be visible in what the weavers can and cannot do, and in what the humans are able to establish about them from a distance. Assume a reader who knows nothing about any of this and who should still be able to feel the difference between the rungs by the end. Keep the aliens genuinely alien — no councils, no wise elders, no translated dialogue — but keep the human characters recognisable people with professional habits and a temperature. Don't resolve the disagreement. Instead, end by sharpening it: leave the reader with the most precise version of the question of what kind of evidence would actually settle whether this is a new rung on the ladder or just the old one running unusually fast, and what would be at stake for us, as Gregorian creatures, either way. We read these pieces aloud to each other at the end of term, so the prose should hold up when heard rather than skimmed, without being written down to a sixteen-year-old.
