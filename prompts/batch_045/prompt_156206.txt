I'm an undergraduate in a social choice theory course, and we've just finished a week on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. A blog post I found while reading around the subject (it argues against adopting ranked ballots) states the result like this:

"Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved independently that every voting system is manipulable. Take any procedure that collects voters' rankings of three or more candidates and returns a single winner. Unless it is a dictatorship — one fixed voter's favourite always wins, whatever anyone else says — there will always be some election in which some voter does better by submitting a ranking that isn't her real one. So the dream of a strategy-proof voting rule is dead, and the practical lesson is that tactical voting is simply part of democracy; we should stop treating it as a defect. This is really just Arrow's theorem in another costume: the same impossibility, stated as a claim about tactical voting rather than about independence of irrelevant alternatives. And it isn't an artifact of ranked ballots: it covers every procedure you could devise, including approval voting and even procedures that use chance, such as drawing one ballot at random and implementing it."

I can't tell how much of this to trust, and I don't want to repeat it in my essay if it is wrong. Could you go through it and separate what a social choice theorist would accept, what they would call a simplification, and what they would reject — and say whether the post's practical conclusion still stands once the mistakes are corrected? (We defined strategy-proofness as: no voter, at any profile, can obtain a strictly preferred outcome by reporting a ranking other than her true one, holding everyone else's reports fixed.) Two claims I am especially unsure about: the identification with Arrow's theorem, since in class the two were proved separately and Arrow's seemed to concern social welfare functions and IIA; and the remark about chance, since the theorem we proved was for deterministic rules.

The reason it matters practically: we've been asked to determine whether the rule my own department uses for hiring is strategy-proof — five committee members, three shortlisted candidates, the candidate with the fewest first-place votes is dropped, and the other two go to a straight majority vote, with ties broken by a fixed order. I take it the theorem rules out strategy-proofness here, but I cannot produce a preference profile in which anyone actually gains by misreporting. What would such a profile have to look like structurally, and a concrete one if possible? And does the fact that the theorem applies to this rule tell us anything about how often, or how seriously, it is manipulable in real committees?
