I'm in a final-year metaphysics seminar on modality, and for the past four weeks our professor has been ruling, one by one, on a long list of "could that still have been you?" cases. My study group keeps a shared document of her verdicts, partly to study from and partly because we get one seminar before reading week in which we each bring a case of our own and try to catch her out. She's an origin essentialist of some sort, though she's never quite said which version, and that's what I want your help with.

Here is her record, all of it about me. Yes, still me: I am born eleven weeks early and spend three months in an incubator; the zygote is frozen for eleven years and transferred later, so I am born in 2019 rather than 2008; the same zygote is gestated by a surrogate; a single base-pair substitution occurs in one cell of the eight-cell embryo, so my genome differs from its actual sequence at exactly one site; the nuclear material of my mother's egg and my father's sperm is transferred into an enucleated donor egg before fertilization, so a third woman supplies all my mitochondria; a single cell is removed from the eight-cell embryo for preimplantation genetic diagnosis and then discarded — she said explicitly that taking the cell does not divide the embryo into two organisms. No, not me: my mother's egg fertilized by a different man's sperm; my father's sperm fertilizing a different egg of hers. And the verdict she was most emphatic about: my zygote splits at the two-cell stage, both cell lineages develop, and two monozygotic twins are born and grow up — neither of them, she said, would have been me, and she added that she does not think there is a fact of the matter about which one it would have been, because that question is badly posed; fission is simply not something the embryo survives.

My case is the same as that last one with one change. The zygote splits at the two-cell stage, a second gestational sac with a heartbeat is visible at the seven-week scan, and by the tenth week it is gone — reabsorbed, no co-twin is ever born. The survivor is delivered on my actual due date, to my actual parents, with my actual genome, and lives the life I in fact lived, down to this seminar. Would that person have been me? I can see at least three rules her record fits equally well: that fission is unsurvivable no matter how it turns out; that fission is fatal only when it leaves two rival continuers, so that a sole survivor inherits the original's identity; and that once fission has occurred the identity question becomes indeterminate, in which case she should refuse to answer rather than say yes or no. Tell me what each rule predicts about my case, which rule her whole record — including the PGD verdict and the mitochondrial-donation verdict, which I suspect are doing more work than they look like they are — best supports, what she would say if she wanted to be consistent with herself, and, if her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what she would be committing to either way. Then tell me what further case would separate whichever rules you think are still live; I was going to propose the variant where the two blastomeres are separated and then put back together an hour later so that a single embryo develops, but say so if you think something else — a co-twin lost before implantation, or one lost at twenty weeks — discriminates better.

Please give me the prediction, the rule, the closest analogue among her earlier verdicts, and the discriminating case, then finish with a single last line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing one of three labels and nothing else: would have been you, would not have been you, or no fact of the matter.
