I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and I'm teaching the closing session of our department's graduate seminar on conditionals this term. By that point we'll have spent several weeks on the indicative/subjunctive contrast, and my experience is that students leave able to recite the slogan while still quietly treating the two as the same sentence with a tense change applied to it. Rather than close with a summary slide, I'd like to hand out a poem.

What I have in mind is a villanelle: five tercets and a final quatrain, two refrains, nineteen lines. The refrains should be the two members of the standard pair — one indicative, one subjunctive, same episode in the antecedent, same consequent, differing in mood:

A: If Oswald did not kill Kennedy, someone else did.
B: If Oswald had not killed Kennedy, someone else would have.

A few constraints, because I want the form to carry the philosophy rather than decorate it.

1. The refrains must recur word for word; no paraphrase, no progressive decay, no clever variation on the fourth return. If the Oswald sentences genuinely will not scan or will not rhyme, substitute a pair of your own construction that meets the same conditions: a single episode, one consequent, distinguished only by indicative versus past-perfect subjunctive mood, where the indicative is something a speaker with partial evidence could properly assert and the subjunctive is false. Tell me briefly if you substituted.

2. The recurrence should do the work of showing what is stable and what is not. Move the speaker's epistemic position between stanzas — a prosecutor before the rifle is traced, an archivist after the film, a historian asking whether the decade would have gone differently — while the event itself never changes. At one recurrence the refrain should still be true but no longer be something the speaker can offer; I want the reader to feel the difference between a line's truth and its assertibility, and to feel it from the fact that only the surrounding lines moved.

3. Neither refrain may entail, correct, or collapse into the other, and the poem must not adjudicate between them. Let the subjunctive have the last word, but leave the reader seeing that the two lines answer different questions — whom to look for, as against whether the killing would have happened anyway — rather than that one of them was right all along.

4. Somewhere in the non-refrain lines I want the weaker modal to appear: that if Oswald had not killed Kennedy, someone else might have. Use it for what it is — a separate claim that survives when the would-counterfactual fails — not as a repair of the subjunctive refrain and not as a bridge that reconciles the two.

5. Keep the speaker lightly characterised and attached to some actual question, so that each conditional is doing a job for somebody; no theorist's voice, no confessional voice.

6. Accuracy matters more than coverage, and I will be reading this in front of people who know the literature. Nothing in the poem should imply that a subjunctive is just an indicative with a tense adjustment, that a false antecedent is what makes a conditional subjunctive, that the indicative's correctness consists in the speaker's ignorance, or that the contrast is one of subject matter rather than of what the sentence is answerable to. No jargon — no talk of nearby worlds, similarity, or partitions — and no named theorists. A philosopher of language should find nothing here false; a smart undergraduate who has read nothing should still be able to follow it.

And it has to survive as a poem first: real metre, rhymes that don't strain, refrains that still land when they come round the last time.
