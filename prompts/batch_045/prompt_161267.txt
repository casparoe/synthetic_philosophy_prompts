I run a small reading group — half of them are machine-learning people who have never taken a philosophy course, the other half work in decision theory and formal epistemology — and we have spent the term on the question of whether an agent can be made to avoid side effects without anyone first writing down what is valuable. Our last meeting is on impact measures, and I would like to close it with a poem rather than with a summary, because the discussion ended somewhere a poem can hold open and a summary would have to shut.

What I want is a villanelle spoken by the agent itself, mid-task: it has been told to bring the blue mug down from the top shelf of a kitchen where a vase stands near the edge of the counter, a cat is asleep on the windowsill, and a kettle is coming to the boil. The two refrains should be the two things the literature keeps saying at each other. One refrain belongs to the baseline move — every candidate action is priced against the world that would have unfolded had the agent done nothing, and that counterfactual no-action world is the only reference point a value-free measure has, so it keeps coming back no matter where the stanza has got to. The other refrain belongs to the permission complaint: the agent's own assigned task is itself an enormous change in the world, air displaced and cat startled and mug removed from its shelf, and nothing in a measure defined without value marks those changes as the permitted ones and the vase as the forbidden one, because the measure counts changes rather than badnesses. Let them take turns through the tercets and then meet, unresolved, in the closing couplet. What I especially want the middle stanzas to get right, and to get right without lecturing, is that the units of such a measure are units of option-loss rather than units of welfare: the agent prices the kitchen by how much of it it can still bring about, so the cat and the vase are counted alike and are counted only insofar as each is something the agent could still have affected, which makes its alarm partly an alarm about its own shrinking reach. I also want the stanza that notices that a broken vase cannot be restored — the penalty attaches permanently to an irreversible change, while the waking cat is recoverable — so the measure is sensitive to irreversibility and entirely insensitive to whether anyone cared. And I want somewhere in the poem the problem that the numbers move if you redescribe the world at a finer grain: the dust it disturbs, the air it pushes, the state of the molecules in the cat's whiskers all count as impact, and nothing inside the measure selects the right grain of description, so the penalty has an arbitrary unit in a way a penalty expressed in value would not. Someone who has followed this literature should be able to say which technical move each stanza is performing; someone who has not should be able to read the whole thing as a poem about hesitation and not notice they are being taught anything. Keep to the nineteen lines and do not force a rhyme at the cost of the sense. Send the lines only — no title, no headings, no bold or italic type, no bullets, and no prefatory or closing prose explaining what you took the problem to be; line and stanza breaks are fine, but nothing in the reply that is not verse.
