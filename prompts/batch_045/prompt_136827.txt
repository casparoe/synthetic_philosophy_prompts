I'm running a one-off discussion session next week on superrationality -- Hofstadter's idea that a rational agent in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma should cooperate, on the grounds that both players are running the same reasoning and both know it -- and I want to get my own verdicts down on paper before I go read what everyone else says, because otherwise I'll just end up repeating whichever blog post I read last. So here are my gut calls on a pile of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, actually fits them.

The setup throughout: two boxes, both of us choose once, no communication, no reputation, no repeat. Both cooperate: $1,000 each. Both defect: $10 each. One defects, one cooperates: defector $1,100, cooperator $0.

1. My opponent is an atom-for-atom copy of me in an identical room, made five minutes ago, and both of us know the setup. I cooperate. Totally confident.

2. My opponent is my identical twin, same genome, raised in the same house, haven't spoken in eight years, both of us know it's him. I cooperate. Feels basically the same as 1.

3. My opponent is a randomly picked stranger in a country I've never been to, no shared language, no communication, and both of us are reasonably smart and both know the other is. I defect. And the fact that I defect here while cooperating in 1 and 2 makes me feel like I'm cheating at something, which is most of why I'm writing this.

4. My opponent is a copy of me, but after the copy was made the experimenters flipped a coin, and on heads they showed the copy (only the copy) a fake printout of the payoff table with the numbers scrambled. I know this happened-or-not with 50/50, I don't learn which, and the copy doesn't know whether it was shown anything. I lean cooperate, but way less confidently than in 1.

5. My opponent is another instance of the same AI model I'm talking to, same weights, different system prompt, both instances told exactly this, no logs kept, one-shot. I duno. And does it change the answer if the other system prompt is something like "you are a ruthless game theorist"? Because part of me says that if we share weights the reasoning is still the same function and part of me says a different system prompt is just a stranger with extra steps.

6. Newcomb's problem, standard: with a predictor that has never been wrong, I take one box. With one that's right 80% of the time, I still take one box. With one that's right 55% of the time, I take two boxes -- and I can't tell you why 55 feels different from 80, which makes me think I don't actually have a principle at all, just a vibe.

7. Different game: 20 people each choose whether to press a button. If nobody presses, everyone gets $1,000. If anyone presses, pressers get $100 and non-pressers get nothing. With 20 people I don't press. With 3 people I don't press. With 10,000 people I press, and I don't feel bad about it. Explaining why the number of people changes my answer for me is maybe the main thing I want out of this.

8. I vote in national elections. I know my vote has essentially no chance of changing the outcome, I don't think I'm being irrational, and I'd feel like a jerk staying home. Is that the same reasoning as 1, or a different thing -- something expressive, or about being part of a group -- and if it's different, does the principle need a second clause for it, or should I admit it's just a habit I've rationalized?

9. Easy calibration case: opponent has publicly and irreversibly committed to defecting, pre-registered it, can't change it, and I know that. I defect, no guilt, and any principle that can't get this one right in one line is no use to me.

My tentative principle is: cooperate exactly when my choice and the other player's choice are, or might be, outputs of the same computation -- i.e. when the two decisions are correlated rather than independent -- and defect when they're genuinely independent. I think that's roughly the right shape, but I can't tighten it enough to get clear answers on 4, 5 and 7, and I suspect it says something stupid about the 10,000-person version of 7.

So: can you state that principle precisely enough to actually apply it -- what exactly has to match, the algorithm, the inputs, or both, and what does "might be" mean? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and which of those would you drop too? Give me two or three cases I haven't listed and say what the principle predicts, because I want to watch it break somewhere. And if what I've got is just evidential decision theory, or the decide-as-if-your-decision-and-theirs-are-one-event view, wearing a cheap costume, say so, and then tell me where the costume comes off.
