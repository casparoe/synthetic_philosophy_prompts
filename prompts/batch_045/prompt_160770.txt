"Anthropic reasoning comes down to a choice between two assumptions, and those are the only two games in town. The self-sampling assumption says you should treat yourself as a random draw from the observers similar to you — the only real wrinkle there is the 'reference class problem', which is just the question of whether to count aliens or chimpanzees in the pool. This is the assumption behind the doomsday argument, which concludes that we are probably living near the end of the human species, since if humanity survives for millions more years there will be trillions of people and it would be an amazing coincidence for you to be one of the first hundred billion. The self-indication assumption says instead that your own existence is evidence for hypotheses on which there are many observers, so in any dispute you should back the theory that predicts more minds, in proportion to how many more it predicts. That sounds mild, but it has an absurd consequence: the 'presumptuous philosopher'. Suppose two cosmological theories are equally well supported by all the astronomical evidence, but one says the universe will contain a trillion trillion observers and the other says a trillion. SIA says the philosopher should be virtually certain of the first theory without ever looking through a telescope. That's a reductio, so SIA has been refuted, and serious people fall back on SSA. This matters for the AI question: if our descendants build a civilization containing astronomically many digital minds, humans will be a microscopic fraction of all minds, so SSA tells you to be very confident that you are not one of them — you're a random human, so you don't need to worry about waking up and turning out to be an upload."
