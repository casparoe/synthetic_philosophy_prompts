I keep getting stuck on how inconsistent we are about risky new stuff. If a company wants to sell a new prescription drug it has to run big trials and convince a regulator that the thing is safe *and* actually works before anyone can buy it. But if a company wants to sell a new herbal supplement, it just puts it on the shelf, and now the regulator has to prove the product is dangerous before it can be pulled. Same kind of split with food additives: in Europe a new additive generally has to be authorized before it goes into anything, while in the US the company can more or less decide for itself that the ingredient is fine. So sometimes "not proven safe" is a reason to block, sometimes "not proven harmful" is a reason to allow, and I cant see that the difference is just how big the risk is, since supplements and dyes send people to the hospital too.

What does each of those policies actually have to assume in order to make sense? I know the slogans — better safe than sorry on one side, innocent until proven guilty on the other — but I want to know whats underneath: is it something about consent, or about who is imposing on whom, or about whether someone is seeking out a benefit versus just being exposed? I've started calling one version of my worry the "baseline problem": whether precaution tells you to ban or to allow seems to depend entirely on which option you've labelled the status quo, and both the drug and the supplement start off equally absent from the market, so it looks like the principle is empty until somebody just picks a baseline by hand. Is that right, is there a non-arbitrary way to set the baseline, and what would the rule look like if we tried to be consistent and dropped it — do we end up blocking everything new, or waiting for the body count?
