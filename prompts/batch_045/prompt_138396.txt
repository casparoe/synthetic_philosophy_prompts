Hey — I'm putting together a discussion session on constitutional interpretation for a reading group and I've painted myself into a corner, so help me out.

Take District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) and run it as a test case for textualism vs. purposivism. Facts you can take as given, no need to check them: DC had basically outlawed handgun registration and required any firearm kept in the home to be stored unloaded and either disassembled or trigger-locked; Heller, who was licensed to carry a gun at work, couldn't register one to keep at home; the Court struck the scheme down 5-4, Scalia writing the majority, with Stevens and Breyer each writing a dissent. I care about the methodological stuff, not the gun stuff.

What got me stuck is this: the Second Amendment states its own purpose, right there in the text — "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." Call it the written-purpose problem (my label, not a real term of art): when a text contains an explicit statement of why it exists, a textualist has to either treat that statement as doing work — and then explain how a purpose clause limits an operative clause, which starts to sound like the thing textualism was supposed to reject — or treat it as decoration, and then explain why some of the enacted words are allowed to be inert. So:

1. Map the case properly on both theories. Whose understanding is supposed to be authoritative (a reader in 1791, one in 1868, a lawyer, an ordinary citizen?), what each theory says counts as evidence — founding-era sources, the preambles and rights provisions in the state constitutions, post-enactment practice, the Court's much older militia cases — and what each theory predicts the prefatory clause should actually do.

2. Then say what each opinion really did. My read, whcih you should push back on hard if it's wrong, is that "purposivism" isn't one position here at all: Stevens uses purpose as a restraint on the operative clause, while Breyer does something else entirely — weighing whether DC's ban is proportionate to the Amendment's aims — and I don't know what to call that or whether it belongs in the same debate as the other two.

3. Where it breaks. The passage in Scalia's opinion that lists longstanding regulations — carrying in sensitive places, conditions on commercial sales, and so on — and says the list isn't exhaustive has always bugged me. It reads like policy. Is that textualism cheating, purpose leaking back in, or something both theories are just silent about? And be honest about whether method predicted the outcome here, or whether the Justices picked the method to fit.

4. Does the written-purpose problem generalize? Plenty of federal statutes have enacted findings and purpose sections sitting at the very top of the text, in the same document as the operative rules. Is it more defensible for a textualist to ignore those than to ignore the militia clause, or is my problem just constitutional law being weird?

No reading list or footnotes, I've read the opinions — just argue it out. And please write in American English.
