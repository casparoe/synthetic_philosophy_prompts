I'm revising a paper on higher-order evidence and I've managed to build an argument whose conclusion I can't accept and whose joints I can't locate. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

Two stipulations first, so nothing turns on jargon. By *higher-order evidence* (HOE) I mean evidence about the reliability of the process that produced your attitude toward p, as opposed to evidence bearing on whether p. By *pure* HOE I mean HOE that is silent about p except through what it says about that process — the pill case, a peer's disagreement, evidence that you are subject to a bias in a domain. The background theses I'm helping myself to are these:

- **Evidentialism.** Rationality requires proportioning your credence in p to your total evidence.
- **Uniqueness.** For any total evidence E and proposition p, there is exactly one credence in p that E rationally permits.
- **Enkrasia.** It is irrational to be confident that p while also being confident that this is not the response your evidence supports. Call the state of doing exactly that *epistemic akrasia*.

Now the case my argument runs on. Suppose there is a device — call it the **Mirror** — stipulated to be perfectly reliable, which for any proposition reports only whether your current credence in it is the one your total evidence supports. It never says whether you are too high or too low, and it never says anything about the proposition itself. You ask about p; the Mirror says NO.

The argument:

1. **(Defeat)** Pure HOE that your attitude toward p is defective requires you to change that attitude. You may not simply retain it on the strength of the first-order evidence. This is what makes the pill case and the peer-disagreement case work at all.
2. **(Level-Symmetry)** A process's unreliability is not confined to the level at which it was detected. If there is reason to doubt the output of M when it adjudicates p, there is the same reason to doubt the output of M when it adjudicates whether M is reliable and when it adjudicates what credence in p the total evidence supports. Those tasks are harder than the original one, not easier, and they are performed by the same M.
3. **(Iteration)** By (1) you must move from your current credence to some new credence cr′. But cr′ is the output of that same M, now operating on evidence that includes the Mirror's report. So by (2) there is the same reason to doubt cr′ as there was to doubt the original credence.
4. **(No fixed point)** The Mirror is reliable, so it says NO of cr′ as well. Indeed you know in advance that it will: the defect is a feature of M, not of any particular output, and you have no method for singling out a credence you have reason to think is the supported one. So there is no credence in p that acquiring the report permits you to rest with.
5. **(Unsatisfiable demand)** A norm cannot require what cannot be done. If (1)–(4) are right, the Mirror case is one in which rationality requires you to hold a credence you have reason to think is not the supported one while forbidding you to hold any candidate replacement — an impossible demand, hence no demand at all.
6. **(Generality)** Nothing here depends on p or on the Mirror. Every piece of pure HOE has the same shape: it says your response to the evidence is defective without telling you what the correct response would be.
7. So either pure HOE generates no rational requirements whatsoever, or it generates requirements that cannot be satisfied.
8. **(Too much)** The first disjunct is unacceptable. It entails that learning you have been given a drug that wrecks your evidential reasoning gives you no reason at all to revise anything, and that a respected peer's disagreement is normatively null. I don't know how to run a paper on HOE without (1).

So at least one of (1)–(6) is false, or the Mirror case is not the coherent case it looks like.

I should flag two things that make me distrust my own argument without telling me where it breaks. First, my supervisor says (2) is the weak joint, on the grounds that reliability is task-relative: a process can be unreliable about p and perfectly reliable about what p's probability is given the evidence. That strikes me as too quick, because in the Mirror case I'm not told that M is unreliable in some domain — I'm told that my credence is not the one my evidence supports, and that is a claim about precisely the relation I would have to evaluate in order to choose cr′. Second, I have a variant of the case in which the Mirror adds that your errors are always in the direction of overconfidence, and there the argument seems to lose its grip, since you at least know which way to move. I can't tell whether that variant rescues the argument or shows that the original one was only ever pressing on a special feature of directionless HOE.

What I want from you: name the step you reject and commit to it. Explain why that step looked fine — what makes it tempting — and then price the alternatives: giving up Defeat in favour of treating some mismatches as rationally permissible akrasia, giving up Uniqueness for some form of permissivism, giving up Level-Symmetry, restricting Enkrasia, or denying that the Mirror case is coherent. Tell me which of these you would actually pay, what it costs you elsewhere in the epistemology (disagreement, calibration, the pill case), and where you think the real break is. Give me your own view rather than a survey of options, and say how confident you are in it and what would change your mind.
