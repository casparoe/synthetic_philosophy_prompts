I look after the disposition catalogue for a firm that supplies components to offshore wind operators. The database allows exactly three fields per ascription — stimulus, manifestation, standing conditions — and no others; there is no field for "provided nothing interferes", and no free-text notes that procurement officers read. Those officers query the catalogue and treat a recorded ascription as a prediction about behaviour on the platform, so what I record has to be both true and usable: a propensity that is genuine but never expressed in service is, for their purposes, as good as absent, while one that is recorded but not genuine is worse than a blank.

Below are eleven cases where I do not know what to put in the catalogue. Names are anonymised and the ascriptions are quoted as they currently stand; in several cases the wording is what hides the difficulty.

Please do the following. Assign each case to one of four tiers —

A: record as true, no flag
B: record as true, but flagged as condition-sensitive
C: do not record as true
D: not a well-formed disposition ascription, or not gradable on the information given

— and give each case a confidence score from 0 to 10 for the claim that the ascription is true on its intended reading. Within each tier, order the cases by that score. Then give one sentence of justification per case, and tag each with the locus of the discrepancy: in the object, in the surroundings, or in the test rig. Finally, say which cases the familiar taxonomy of finks, masks, antidotes and mimickers does not cover, naming any further category you have to introduce, and identify any pair of cases that your tier scheme cannot separate from one another even though you would treat them differently in practice.

The cases:

1. Insulating sleeves: "shatters under impact above 2 J." Every sample tested has shattered. Nothing unusual about service conditions.

2. The same sleeves once fitted: the bracket's rubber housing absorbs the blow, so an installed sleeve never sees more than 0.5 J. Eight years of field data, no shattering.

3. Polymer clips: "becomes brittle below −20 °C." The enclosure thermostat brings a heater in at −20 °C, so in service the clips never fall below −19 °C. On the bench, with the heater disconnected, brittleness below −20 °C is confirmed.

4. A sealant: "degrades on contact with solvent." On contact, an added blooming agent reaches the surface within seconds and blocks ingress; sealed joints are intact after a year. Scrape the bloom away and the material beneath degrades normally.

5. Glass ampoules: "fractures when dropped from one metre." Every tested unit fractured. It then emerged that the technician, who knew the batch was under review, tapped each ampoule against the bench before releasing it.

6. Coated tablets: "dissolves in water." The lacquer coating is impermeable for roughly the first thirty seconds of immersion, after which it crazes; every tablet has dissolved within two minutes.

7. A steel sample: "is magnetisable by a field of 50 mT." At that field strength the domain structure reorders over a few seconds, and the sample acquires a permeability it did not have beforehand.

8. A single-use squib: "fires on a 10 A pulse." Having fired, it cannot fire again. Spares are verified destructively on a sample basis; the rest are assumed to fire.

9. A batch of tiles: "cracks under thermal shock." Thirty per cent of every batch cracks, and which thirty per cent is not predictable from any measurement available before the test. The catalogue can record only "cracks" or "does not crack".

10. Fire-retardant panels: "ignites at 400 °C." Under slow heating an intumescent char forms near 300 °C and protects the core indefinitely; under rapid heating there is no time for the char to form and the panel ignites at 400 °C.

11. "Resists salt fog for 500 hours." The standard test rack is stainless and renders the sample cathodic; on that rack every sample passes. On the older aluminium rack, forty per cent fail by 200 hours. Nobody can say which rack resembles conditions on the platform.

Please answer in British English.
