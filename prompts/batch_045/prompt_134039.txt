Here's my situation. I work in model validation at a company that does automated underwriting, and for two years now my team, our external ethics review board, and our own data scientists have been having what I'm pretty sure is the *same* meeting, with the same three slides, and nobody has moved an inch. My camp says: there's a standard incompatibility result — you can't have a classifier that's calibrated within groups and also equalises its false-positive and false-negative rates across those groups unless the groups' base rates match or the classifier is perfect — so this is a forced trade-off, and the honest thing to do is pick your criterion, write down the political justification for it, and use the theorem to make the cost visible rather than pretending there's a technically correct answer. Their camp says the theorems are beside the point or actively worse: the results take the population, the feature set, the outcome variable and the existence of the sorting institution as *given*, they get waved around to make a political choice look like a mathematical necessity ("we'd love to be fair, it's provably impossible"), and the real question is upstream — who is in the data at all, what gets counted as a good outcome, whether this decision should be made by a model in the first place. A friend who does philosophy of science sat in on one of these and said, brightly, "oh, you're just answering different questions," and something in me rebels at that: it feels like a way of closing a conversation rather than diagnosing it. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the theorems aren't doing the work everyone thinks they're doing, because both sides cite them as confirmation, and the actual fight is about which parts of the setup are fixed and which are up for grabs.

Two things I've been chewing on that I want you to actually engage with, not nod at. First, a sketch I think I can turn into a proof: in our setting the outcome is only observed when you make the positive decision (you only learn whether someone repays if you lent to them), so each year's training data is generated by last year's model. My claim is that for any non-constant rule and any two groups with different underlying repayment distributions, the retrained sequence can't settle — if a rule equalises its false-negative rates at time t, the labels it generates push the time t+1 model into violating equalised false-positive rates, roughly because equalising one error rate forces you to lend deeper into one group's distribution, which changes what you then learn about that group. If that's right, the impossibility isn't a fact about a decision rule at an instant but a fact about a loop, and the question stops being "which criterion do you pick" and becomes "which year's violation do you want, and who eats it." Second, a smaller observation: every incompatibility result I know fixes a partition of the population in advance. If instead the auditor gets to name the group *after* seeing the classifier, drawing on a reasonably rich feature language, then for any non-constant classifier there's some definable subgroup on which it looks badly unfair; so the whole "pick a criterion" framing apparently rests on a choice nobody in these meetings ever argues about, namely who gets to name the groups.
