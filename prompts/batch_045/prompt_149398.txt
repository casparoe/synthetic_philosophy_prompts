I sit on the grantmaking committee of a small foundation with a fixed five-year payout and a mandate to spend it down. We have been ranking causes with the importance / tractability / neglectedness framework, scoring each cause as the product of the three factors. A colleague now argues that cause selection is really like venture capital: importance is market size, tractability is whether the problem is solvable at all, neglectedness is that no one else is in the space — and in venture capital you do not concentrate everything on the best-evidenced company. You take many shots at power-law returns, you expect most of them to fail, and you treat other investors piling in as confirmation rather than a reason to stay out. From this he draws three conclusions: (i) we should abandon ranking by the ITN product and instead spread many small grants across speculative causes; (ii) we should read crowding as a positive signal; (iii) we should replace multiplication with a lexicographic or maximin rule, since a single near-zero factor (a tractability estimate of 1 percent, say) swamps any importance score.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of market size, of product–market fit, of the exit, of follow-on rounds, of the limited partners, of the fund's fixed life, of the power-law return distribution — and then separate the disanalogies that matter for his three conclusions from the ones that do not. I have in mind the absence of anything like a price signal or a hard market test, feedback lags measured in decades or never, the fact that philanthropic returns are non-rival and not appropriable by the funder, and the fact that a cause can be neglected for reasons that are themselves evidence of intractability. For each of (i), (ii), and (iii), say whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it. Finally: is multiplication even the right functional form, or does a marginal-value-per-dollar framing, in which neglectedness enters only through the price of progress, do the same work? And if the venture analogy fails, what comparison would serve better?
