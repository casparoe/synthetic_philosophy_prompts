I teach a graduate seminar on confirmation and induction, and I would like an accuracy check before I put a summary in front of students. A widely used philosophy-in-brief website summarises Goodman's new riddle of induction roughly as follows (my own condensed paraphrase of the entry, faithful to its claims but not a verbatim quotation):

Goodman's new riddle is really just Hume's problem of induction in a new costume. Hume showed that we have no justification for assuming that the future will resemble the past; Goodman's invented predicate "grue" — a thing is grue if it is green when first examined before some future time t, and blue if examined after t — makes the same point vividly, because every green emerald we have examined is grue as well, so our evidence supports "all emeralds are grue" exactly as strongly as "all emeralds are green." The moral is that no evidence supports any prediction about unexamined cases, and hence that induction cannot be rationally defended at all. Goodman himself escaped by appeal to simplicity: "green" is a shorter and simpler predicate than "grue," and science prefers the simpler hypothesis, which is why we may go on projecting "green" but not "grue." In his terminology a predicate is projectible if it will still be true of the things we examine later, and "green" is more projectible than "grue" because it is better entrenched: the word "green" has been used far more often in English, and the more often an expression has been used, the more entrenched it is. Philosophers of science now draw a wider moral. Any finite body of data is compatible with infinitely many hypotheses that fit it perfectly, and grue-like predicates can be built over any division of the data one likes; so the real lesson is that learning from data always requires an inductive bias, and the "no free lunch" results show that you cannot get one without assuming what you were trying to establish.

I also had a colleague argue, in another connection, that the riddle generalises without loss to any gerrymandered division of the cases, and he offered this variant: call a specimen grell if it is green and its accession number in the museum register is below ten thousand, or blue and its accession number is ten thousand or above; a mineralogist who had seen only low-numbered green specimens would then have for "all specimens are grell" exactly the evidence the rest of us have for "all emeralds are green." My worry is that this variant is too easy — the accession-number division is arbitrary in a way the before/after-t division is not — and I suspect the real work in Goodman's example is done by the clause about what has been examined rather than by the temporal cut.
