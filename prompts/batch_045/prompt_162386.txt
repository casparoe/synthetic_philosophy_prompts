Hi — I'm on the three-person committee that rules on eligibility questions for our professional society's mid-career prize, and I need to circulate a short decision memo before Friday. Whatever we decide is final for this cycle; the bylaws can only be changed by a two-thirds vote at the annual meeting in November.

The bylaw, unamended since 1989: "Applicants must have received the doctorate no more than twelve years before the application deadline." The preamble says the prize exists to support people who have an independent research program but don't yet hold a secure senior post.

The applicant got the PhD thirteen years ago. In between: two years of parental leave and a year out with a serious illness; she started her first tenure-track job four years ago. She is almost exactly the person the preamble describes, and by the text she is ineligible.

Three wrinkles. (1) The grant guidelines, written by a different committee in 2014 and never reconciled with the bylaws, say career stage is what matters, not years since degree. (2) For at least six years we've quietly discounted parental leave without ever writing it down; last year we rejected someone with a nearly identical record, and he has now written to ask why. (3) A separate bylaw says the committee "may not waive or modify any eligibility requirement."

I want the memo to: give a bottom line on this application with a time frame; take a position on whether this is an interpretive question (what the twelve years mean now) or something closer to a dispensation we're not permitted to make — I keep suspecting the no-waiver clause just forces the purposive answer to disguise itself as a reading of the text; and say what test, if any, we should apply to the next case, since we get about two hundred of these a year and I don't want to invent a standard case by case. Also whether we owe last year's applicant anything.

I know theres a huge literature on statutory interpretation and I don't need the tour or a reading list, just the reasoning. Please be explicit about how confident you are in the bottom line and which step a careful textualist colleague of mine would reject, and if you think the interpretation-versus-waiver distinction I'm leaning on is bogus, or that our document can't bear the weight we're putting on it, say that instead of working inside my framing.
