I run a monthly philosophy evening at my town's public library. Typically ten to fourteen adults show up, from their twenties to their eighties, almost none with any formal background in philosophy and no reading between sessions. I have one room, ninety minutes, a whiteboard, index cards, pens, and a few household objects I can bring in a bag. I would like you to design a single self-contained session for that group.

The topic I have been reading about on my own is people who, as a treatment for severe epilepsy, have had the thick bundle of nerve fibres connecting the two halves of the brain surgically cut. What grips me is not that information fails to cross. It is the shape of what follows. Something shown briefly to one side of the visual field is available to one half and not the other; the half that controls speech apparently cannot name what the other half saw, but the silent half can still act on it, choosing or arranging objects correctly with the hand on the other side of the body. And then the speaking half, asked to explain the action, produces a smooth and confident reason that is simply invented. Nobody involved seems to experience a gap.

What I want the group to feel — not to be told — is the force of the question that raises. Is there one person in there now, or two? And a sharper version, which interests me more: does that question have a determinate answer at all, or is it the kind of question that dissolves when you press on it? I suspect most people, hearing the case described, have a confident immediate answer, and I want that confidence to get into trouble during the session rather than in the discussion afterwards.

A few things I need from the design.

First, no lecture up front and no naming of the philosophical issue until the debrief. The experience should come out of the rules, the roles, the incentives, or the constraints on what people are allowed to say — not out of anything I say first. If a participant would have to be told the point in order to play, the design has failed.

Second, I want the core of the activity to be built around a stipulated variant that I made up, because the standard case seems to me to push people toward one answer too quickly. Suppose the surgery is only partial: the detailed traffic — what was seen, what was instructed — does not cross, but a coarse alarm signal does. The speaking half never learns what the silent half saw, yet it reliably registers that something is off: a vague bodily unease, a sense that the room is not quite right, an impulse to check something. It cannot tell whether the feeling is about the task, the temperature of the room, or something it has forgotten. I find that this cuts both ways, and I cannot settle it. It may be the one thing that makes the system genuinely single: a state belonging to nobody in particular but directed at the whole. Or it may be the clearest evidence of the opposite: two streams, plus a third thing registering a collision between them and unable to say whose it is. Please build the rules so that participants can come down on either side, or somewhere else, rather than so that they recover the two readings I just gave. I am not claiming this variant is a real case; treat it as stipulated, and tell me plainly if you think it is incoherent or rests on a false assumption about how any of this works.

Third, I would like the setup to generate confabulation rather than stage it. My thought is that the role doing the explaining should have something to gain by committing to a single confident account and something to lose by hedging or saying they do not know, with the actual state of things revealed only after they have committed. I am open to being told that is the wrong way to do it.

Fourth, I would like a short final phase in which the explaining role keeps going but the people feeding it are swapped, so that the same first-person story has to continue across a discontinuity. My reason is that I am not sure whether being one person at a moment and being the same person over time are even the same question, and I would like the activity to put pressure on that without announcing it.

Fifth, before any discussion, I want each person to write a private answer to the one-or-two question and have the answers tallied on the board, including any answers that say the question itself is broken.

What I need in the response: the materials; the roles and the rules as a participant would hear them; a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no money and cannot be gamed by a group that decides to cooperate; a minute-by-minute outline for the ninety minutes; the debrief, with each stage of it mapped to the moment in the activity it is meant to cash out; and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it in the room. Please also say how to adjust if only eight people come or if sixteen do.

Two last requests. Please write in plain language and gloss any technical term in a sentence when you cannot avoid it; I have been reading about this for a few months on my own and have no training in it. And please flag where you are unsure: which design choice you have least confidence in, what a first run would have to show to make you change it, which part of the activity rests on a contested reading of either the empirical material or the philosophical claim, and what evidence or argument would make you drop or rewrite that part. Finally, be clear about which feature of the issue the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I would have to supply myself in the debrief. Please do not suggest further reading or cite studies, papers, or case names — I cannot look anything up and could not judge it if I could.
