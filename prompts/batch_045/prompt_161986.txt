You are a professional philosopher with fifteen years of experience teaching upper-level philosophy of mind and the philosophical foundations of artificial intelligence, and a departmental reputation for setting finals that cannot be passed by paraphrasing the seminar readings. Think carefully before you write. Begin with three or four sentences of brainstorming: list two or three candidate cruxes you considered for the question, and in a single line each say why you rejected the ones you did not use. Then give me the finished item.

The context: I teach a final-year seminar, "Delegated Cognition: Minds, Agents, and Institutions", and I am writing the take-home final. One section of the exam must cover HCH-style amplification and factored cognition, understood as candidate idealizations of deliberation — the idea that a recursive structure of human-level nodes, each permitted to break its task into subtasks and delegate them, might model what a single agent's deliberation would look like when idealised along the dimension of time, memory, and attention rather than along the dimension of raw intelligence.

What I need from you: one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Constraints on the question.

Students will have 90 minutes and a 1,200-word limit; the question is worth 20 marks. The question must be unanswerable by reciting what the recursive construction is, by stating the definition of decomposition into similar-difficulty subtasks, or by running through the standard checklist of worries that any attentive student has already memorised (the absence of a stable base case, the exponential growth of the tree, the cost of simulating a human, the gap between "this human" and "a human"). Assume students know all of that; the question must bite only for someone who does not know it. The difficulty should therefore come from a distinction or a stipulated device that the students have not seen in exactly this form, introduced inside the question itself, which they must then apply to a case.

To that end, the question should contain at least one piece of apparatus of the following specificity — use one or both of the devices below, or invent something comparable, but do not simply ask for an assessment of the literature:

- A three-operation characterisation of a node's work: it may DECOMPOSE its task into subtasks, SOLVE a task it can answer directly, or MERGE the returned answers of its children into an answer to its own task. Pair this with a distinction between tasks that are locally checkable — where, for any candidate answer, the question "is this answer correct?" can itself be decomposed into subtasks each strictly easier than producing the answer — and tasks that are globally checkable, where settling correctness requires a further act of MERGE that is not easier than producing the answer.
- A condition of deliberative transparency: a process counts as transparent for an agent only if, at each MERGE step, the considerations combined are ones the agent could have weighed for themselves given enough time and without change of standards, and are combined in a way the agent could endorse on reflection. Alongside it, the observation that the boundary of "the agent" in the tree is stipulated rather than discovered: the root is not canonical, since any internal node could be redescribed as the root of a re-cut structure, with the cut fixed only by a depth limit, a token budget, or a time budget.

I would also like the question to embed a false-but-assumed empirical premise that students are told to grant, of roughly this shape: that observed gains from amplification are concentrated in the locally checkable tasks and do not extend to globally checkable ones. Granting this premise should force a choice rather than settle it.

The question must be self-contained. A student with no internet access and no memory of any particular paper, author, or research group should be able to answer it from the apparatus you stipulate and from ordinary philosophical competence. Do not build the question around any named author, paper, year, or quotation, and do not ask for or reward citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. If you mention the historical origins of these ideas at all, do so in a way that no mark depends on.

Constraints on the model answer and scheme.

Give a model answer of roughly 800 words that takes a position rather than surveying options, and that is written in the voice of a very good student working under the stated limits. It should make at least one structural point about the apparatus rather than delivering a verdict on the slogan, and it should register the strongest consideration on the other side without collapsing into fence-sitting.

Then give a marking scheme out of 20, organised around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, where the difference between an A answer and a B answer is a named move rather than a difference of detail or length. For each point, add a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. Finish with three short descriptions of answers that would land in the B range, each labelled with the specific thing it is missing.

One last requirement: the question should be genuinely in the weeds. If a strong reader's first reaction is "I have seen this question before," it has failed. Prefer a question that turns on something small, technical, and newly drawn — a condition, an asymmetry, a re-description — over one that turns on a large familiar theme such as whether the system is conscious, whether it is trustworthy, or whether it is really one agent or many.
