I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I keep watching the same thing happen whenever Hilbert's Hotel comes up in a comment thread: one side says the hotel is perfectly coherent because Cantor gave us a workable theory of completed infinite totalities, the other says the whole setup is a category error because there are no actual infinities, only finite totalities that can always be extended, and after a few rounds somebody declares the disagreement merely verbal and the thread dies. I don't think it's verbal, and I want a case that demonstrates it isn't. Concretely: I'm writing a long post on actual infinities — the diagonal argument and the countable/uncountable split, the hotel and its extensions, and the strict finitist refusal to treat a completed infinite totality as something one can quantify over or manipulate in one go — for an audience that already knows all of that cold, so please don't spend words re-explaining it. A friend I was arguing with is a constructive mathematician and says the finitist position is really just a preference for weaker logic dressed up in metaphysical language; another friend says the Cantorian position is just the observation that a certain formalism is convenient. I want something that is not answerable by either of those remarks.

So build me a scenario to the following specification. (i) Everything that actually happens in it is finite: finitely many steps, finitely many objects at any one moment, and every number that ever appears on a screen, in a ledger, or in anyone's mouth is an ordinary finite integer. (ii) A Cantor-accepter — grant them countable and uncountable infinities, bijections, transfinite ordinals, and the coherence of operating on an entire completed infinite totality at once — and a strict finitist, who treats talk of completed infinities as at best a useful shorthand for indefinitely extensible finite procedures and at worst as incoherent, come apart over something observable and finite: a different prediction about what a machine will do, or a different recommendation about whether to accept a bet at finite odds, or a genuine disagreement about whether some specific finite question has a determinate answer. (iii) The finitist may not escape by declaring the set-up impossible or the question ill-posed on the bare grounds that infinity was mentioned, because the set-up is one they accept on their own terms: no physically completed supertasks, no hotel with infinitely many rooms, no box containing an actual infinity of anything, no perfect predictor or demon or oracle. Any infinity lives in the reasoning available to the characters, not in the furniture of the world. (iv) The finitist's verdict has to be one a thoughtful finitist would actually own — not confusion, not obstinacy, not refusal to answer. One more constraint, because I can see the tempting shortcut: I'd rather you didn't lean on the standard move where a detour through the transfinite settles a finite combinatorial question that weaker arithmetic cannot reach — a Goodstein-style sequence whose termination is provable by transfinite induction up to epsilon-nought but not in Peano arithmetic — as your whole idea. If you use something in that neighbourhood, I want to see the extra machinery that turns unprovability-by-these-means into a divergence in expectation about what will actually happen.

Deliverables: first the scenario itself, one paragraph, realistic, with ordinary people or ordinary machines rather than a laboratory in metaphysics; then, for each of the two positions, the verdict it gives and the reasoning that gets it there, shown rather than asserted; then an explicit note on which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens if you delete it — and if deleting it just collapses you back into the hotel, say so and explain why that is the failure mode here. After that, give the strongest attack available to whichever side comes out looking worse, stated as strongly as you can manage, since I would rather know where the case is weak than publish a scenario that only works because I have strawmanned someone. Finally, a short honest flag on the charge my friends would raise: is the disagreement you have built really about whether there are actual infinities, or has it quietly turned into a dispute about meaning, or about which logic to reason in? If you think your own case is vulnerable to that, tell me, and tell me what a case that wasn't vulnerable would have to look like.
