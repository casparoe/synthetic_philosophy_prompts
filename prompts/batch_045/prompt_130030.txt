I work on scientific realism, and I keep returning to the literature on verisimilitude with the suspicion that it is stuck in a way nobody in it is willing to name.

Here is the shape of the impasse as I see it. After the 1974 results — Tichý, Harris and Miller, independently — showed that Popper's content-based definition has the consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than another, a result Popper himself conceded almost immediately, the field splintered into families that are still with us. The content approaches (Miller's and Kuipers's among them) reconstrue truthlikeness as a function of a truth factor and a content factor. The consequence approaches (Schurz and Weingartner, Gemes, Mortensen) keep Popper's consequence-class framework but restrict the consequences that count to those that are, in some technical sense, "relevant." The likeness approaches, descending from Tichý and Hilpinen and developed especially by Niiniluoto and Oddie, take likeness literally: a proposition's closeness to the truth is a function of how like the actual world the possible worlds are in which it would be true. Half a century on, nobody has converted anybody. Each new measure meets a battery of counterexample cases drawn from what people take to be obvious judgments about closeness, and there is now a secondary literature — including a published impossibility theorem for verisimilitude and an active dispute about whether the three families are compatible with one another at all — whose effect seems to be to establish that no measure can jointly satisfy the adequacy conditions the field has accumulated.

My hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about which measure is correct, because the sides are not trying to define one thing. It looks to me as if there are three different jobs on offer: a logical one, giving a God's-eye ordering of propositions by closeness to the whole truth, answerable to case intuitions of the "nine planets versus nine billion planets" sort; an epistemic one, giving something a scientist could have reason to think her theory has, and that would make the realist's reply to the pessimistic meta-induction — superseded theories were approximately true, retained ones are closer — more than a gesture; and a practical or decision-theoretic one, giving a quantity that tracks expected cognitive value for an inquirer with a particular question-set and a particular array of interests. On this reading the impossibility results merely record that desiderata drawn from one job clash with desiderata drawn from another, and the much-remarked dependence of the likeness measures on a choice of metric, or of the consequence measures on a choice of "relevant" consequences, is not a defect but the visible trace of the fact that the notion is interest-relative all the way down.

A further thought, which may be independent of that hypothesis: the realist needs a diachronic notion — is this sequence of theories approaching the truth? — while everything actually on offer is synchronic, a ranking of rival propositions at a time. So perhaps the programme has spent fifty years answering a question the realist never needed answered, and the interesting arguments were always about convergence, retention, and structure.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and I want you to commit to one. Is this a verbal dispute, or several questions sharing a name? Is there a hidden formal question — whether any measure could satisfy the desiderata together — doing the real work? Is the dependence on a metric or a question-set a discovery about the concept or an artefact of the frameworks that generate it? Is the disagreement, at bottom, about what truthlikeness is for? Argue for one diagnosis against the alternatives, engaging what the competing accounts actually propose rather than describing them, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and say why. I have read the main papers, so please do not spend your words on a bibliography; and if you think my hypothesis is wrong, say so plainly and give me the better one.
