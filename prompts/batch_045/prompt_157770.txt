I run the platform team at a company that deploys a dispatch agent inside a regional blood-bank logistics network: it reassigns blood product shipments between hospitals in real time, and every quarter I have to run a ninety-minute deployment-readiness session for the people who are about to be given operator access to it. This quarter's room is twelve new ML hires — genuinely strong engineers, but almost none of them have formal RL theory, and several treat anything that sounds like philosophy as evidence the meeting could have been an email — plus two product managers and one person from compliance who is there for the audit trail. No pre-reading, no bibliography, one whiteboard and a couple of diagrams. Two features of the actual deployment matter and I want the session built around them rather than around the cleaner textbook setup. First, there is no true off switch available to these operators: pulling the agent out of the loop mid-shift means products sit at the wrong site, so what they really have is a three-level throttle that progressively strips its action space down to advisory-only, together with a separate restore control that hands the scope back; and because using the throttle has visible short-term costs, they are noticeably reluctant to touch it. Second, a throttle input is not self-interpreting: the same request can arrive from an authorized operator, from a quarterly drill, or from someone who has gotten hold of a console session, and the agent has to decide how much to care which.

The single idea I have to get across is the shutdown problem: why a system that is otherwise doing exactly what we asked can still acquire a reason to keep the humans from narrowing it, why putting a preference for being shut down into the objective is not an answer, and why the throttle rather than the off switch is the honest version of the question for this room. Please don't write the explanation for me — I have given a worse version of it myself and watched it bounce, usually leaving the room with the useless takeaway that AI systems are dangerous. Give me the plan instead.

So: the order in which the pieces have to appear and what depends on what (in particular, where the idea that the human's correction can carry information belongs, and what it does and does not buy you here); the one case the whole session should hang on, what it has to contain and what it has to leave out, and why that case should not be the usual robot standing next to a big red button; the misconceptions this specific group will form, in roughly the order they will form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused — my guesses are the hardware-cutout objection from the engineers, the collapse of corrigibility into simple obedience to whoever is at the console, which the drill-and-hostile-press wrinkle should make look like gullibility, the compliance person's conclusion that mandatory human approval on every action solves it, and the assumption that an agent will only resist correction if someone built a survival instinct into it; a short exercise or question for the last ten minutes whose wrong answers would tell me something, so I can tell who has the idea from who has merely agreed with me; and a brief list of things I should explicitly decline to open at this level and say out loud that I am declining.
