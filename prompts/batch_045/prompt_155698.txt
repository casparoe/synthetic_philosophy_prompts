I'm writing a short chapter — the kind where you have to say something definite in three pages — on the old complaint that the things we call pleasures have nothing much in common: the itch scratched, the proof finished, the cold drink on a hot day, the friend's good news. I have a working grip on the standard moves on both sides of that complaint. What I keep tripping over is a pattern in the cases themselves that I can't put a name to, and I'd rather have it identified precisely than be handed a label.

Here is the pattern, in the cases I keep returning to.

Ask me what the pleasure of the cold drink and the pleasure of scratching the itch have in common, and I have an answer: both feel like a pressure being removed. Now add the pleasure of finishing a difficult proof. It feels like neither of the first two. What connects it, when I'm honest, is not a felt quality but what it does — in all three I want the thing to keep going and would rearrange my afternoon to get more of it. Now add the pleasure at a friend's good news. That one is not a relief, and it isn't much like the proof either; what links it to the proof is that in both there is something I am pleased *about*, whereas with the drink and the itch there is not. So the linking is pairwise and local: each new case gets attached to some earlier case, and the attaching relation changes as I go — felt similarity at one step, effects on what I do at the next, having an object at the next. By the end of the chain the itch and the good news have, as far as I can tell from the inside, nothing at all in common; and yet nobody hesitates over calling both of them pleasures, and nobody hesitates over ranking them against each other when a decision forces the ranking.

Two further features of the same cases, which any account of the pattern has to accommodate. First, comparisons inside one kind are easy and stable — I can say that today's proof was more satisfying than Tuesday's and mean something sharp — while comparisons across kinds go slack: asked whether the drink was more pleasant than the good news, I have no answer, and it feels like the question isn't intelligible rather than merely hard. Second, the slackness doesn't stop me trading them off; I skip the drink to finish the proof, and the trade isn't a guess. So the unity is strong enough locally to support pairwise comparison and substitution in choice, and not strong enough to support one global ordering.

The nearest structures I can think of, described rather than named, are a set of cases each of which overlaps its neighbours while no single feature is present in all of them, and a term with one central case that the others inherit from by their relation to it. Mine looks like neither exactly: no single central case, and the links are not all of one kind. So:

- Does this configuration have an established name and a literature of its own, or is it a case that the existing notions each capture only partly? If partly, I want the differences laid out — what each nearby notion gets right about my cases, where exactly it stops fitting, and which one I should adapt.
- If nothing established fits, coin a term and give me a definition tight enough to apply to these cases and to exclude the near-misses, plus the boundary cases where it goes either way and the reason it goes that way.
- Tell me which of three readings the pattern actually favours, and why, rather than surveying all three: (a) there is one feature present in every case and introspection simply cannot isolate it; (b) the unity lives in the relations among the cases rather than in a feature of them, so the word is not being used in one and the same sense throughout; (c) the theory that well-being consists in feeling good has quietly switched from talking about how things feel to talking about something else that does happen to be shared.
- What does the pattern do to the working assumption that pleasures can be added up? If the unity is local and relational, does more pleasure makes for a better life retain determinate content, or does it hold inside a kind and break exactly where it is needed — across kinds, and across persons?
- What would settle it? Name the observation, or the kind of argument, that would discriminate between the readings.
