Context: I am a philosophy MA student in a graduate seminar on defeat, disagreement and rational requirements. We have each been asked to write a one-page philosophical profile of our own position so the rest of the seminar can attack it. Here is mine, and I would like to know whether it hangs together before I hand it in.

(1) Instance-level defeat. Higher-order evidence that my reasoning on a particular occasion was unreliable should move my first-order credence, even when my first-order evidence has not changed and I have learned nothing new about the subject matter. Case: I work through a long insurance-liability calculation and end up 0.9 confident that the claim should be denied. I then learn that I did the calculation at four in the morning after a night without sleep, and that calculations done in that condition are wrong about thirty percent of the time. I should lower my confidence, even though I have already seen everything the calculation turned on and going through it again would tell me exactly what it told me the first time.

(2) No defeat from generic fallibility. Learning only that I am a human being, that human reasoning is subject to motivated reasoning and confirmation bias, and that I have been badly wrong before should not lower my confidence, either across the board or in any particular case. Rationality cannot be self-undermining in that way. So there has to be a principled line between higher-order evidence that defeats and higher-order evidence that does not, and I want that line to fall somewhere near the distinction between evidence about this occasion of reasoning and evidence about my reliability in general.

(3) Enkrasia as an irreducible requirement. If my total evidence supports the claim that it would be irrational for me to be 0.9 confident that P, then it is irrational for me to be 0.9 confident that P. I do not think this is just evidentialism in different clothing: I want to say that there are hard cases where matching my total evidence and satisfying the no-akrasia requirement come apart, and that the requirement is what settles them.

(4) The re-run case. Suppose I receive the higher-order evidence in (1), become less confident, and then go back and redo the calculation slowly and carefully, in good conditions, with someone checking each step, and I get the same answer, and I know all of this. I think the higher-order evidence still retains some force: I should end up more confident than I was immediately after the defeat, but less confident than I was before I ever heard about the four-in-the-morning effect. My reason is that if I was unreliable the first time through, that gives me some reason to doubt my own judgment that the second pass was careful and correct.

(5) Disagreement. When an epistemic peer who has seen all of my evidence and all of my arguments disagrees with me, I should move a substantial part of the way toward their view, roughly halfway. But when someone disagrees with me while having seen strictly less of the evidence than I have, and knowing that I have seen more, I should move barely at all.
