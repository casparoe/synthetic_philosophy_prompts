Here's one answer, came in right at the word limit:

STUDENT ANSWER: Laïcité and multiculturalism are usually presented as opposites, but I think the contrast gets overstated. Both are attempts to answer one question - how a liberal state should handle religious and cultural difference - and my claim is that they share more than their defenders like to admit, though they do come apart on one real point about what equality requires.

Start with laïcité. It gets translated as secularism and then read as state hostility to religion, which is a mistake. Historically it is a principle about the state: the state does not recognise, fund or organise religious worship, and in exchange it guarantees freedom of conscience. That is what the 1905 separation law is about - institutions, not private belief - and the Republic has carried on funding religious schools under contract and paying chaplains in the army and in prisons. So laïcité is compatible with a pretty thick religious presence in civil society. What it rules out is the state taking sides.

Which brings us to the policy cases everyone argues about. The 2004 law bars pupils in state primary and secondary schools from wearing conspicuous religious symbols; the 2010 law bars concealing the face in public space generally, which is aimed at the niqab and the burqa. These get run together but they differ in scope and in justification. The school law is about the school as a protected space where children should not have to signal a religious identity or be pressured by other pupils. The 2010 law is about public space, and was defended partly on identification and security grounds and partly in terms of living together, which is more or less the reasoning the European Court of Human Rights accepted when it upheld it. And since the 1905 settlement already required neutrality from state agents, extending it first to pupils in schools and then to anyone at all in public space is a straightforward application of one principle - a matter of degree rather than a difference in kind.

The multiculturalist tradition starts from the opposite worry, that a state insisting on identical treatment will end up imposing the majority's cultural terms. Taylor captures this with his contrast between a politics of equal dignity and a politics of recognition: the claim is not that the state should be neutral about culture but that neutrality is unavailable, because the state always already supports some cultural options over others. Kymlicka turns this into a specifically liberal argument. Cultural membership matters because culture is the context of choice within which individuals form and revise their aims, so the state has reason to protect cultural structures - not because groups have rights as such, but because individuals need them. That is why Kymlicka argues that external protections - the restrictions a group places on its own members to preserve the integrity of the culture against internal dissent and exit - can be legitimate, above all for national minorities, while internal restrictions, which protect a minority against the pressures of the wider society, are the ones liberals should be suspicious of. And because the argument rests on autonomy rather than on the value of cultures as such, the same reasoning extends to immigrant groups, who on this view need cultural protections most of all.

Where the two positions genuinely diverge is the view of the person. French republicanism, at least in the version used to defend the 2004 law, draws on something close to the communitarian critique of liberalism: individuals are not unencumbered choosers but are constituted by their cultural membership, and the state therefore has a duty to protect the integrity of the national culture. On that picture visible religious difference in schools is communautarisme, the assertion of a group identity against the common culture, and the state is entitled - obliged, even - to keep it out of the institutions where citizenship gets formed. Anglo-American multiculturalists reject exactly that. For them the individual is prior to her ends and must be free to revise them, which is why they read headscarf bans as straightforwardly illiberal.

My conclusion is that the gap is narrower than it looks. Both sides appeal to individual autonomy and equality; they disagree about which arrangements actually secure them. The French worry is that accommodation entrenches the power of group leaders and leaves the weakest members of a group - usually women - worse off. The multiculturalist worry is that banning religious dress removes the option from the very women the ban claims to help, the familiar paradox of protecting women by restricting what they may wear. Which of these is right depends on empirical questions about what such bans actually do to the girls affected. So the two are best seen as variations inside one liberal framework, responding to different institutional histories.
