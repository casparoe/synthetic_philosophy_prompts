ok so you're a labor lawyer who also somehow got a PhD in political philosophy — or at least play one on tv. take a breath, do a bit of unstructured brainstorming before you commit to anything, and then be precise. Hohfeld-precise. I'd rather get ten sharply distinguished readings than three fuzzy ones, and I'd rather you didn't cite cases or statutes at all: I can't check them and I don't care, I care about the shape of the claim.

I'm on the organizing committee for a grad-student-and-adjunct union at a public university in a state with basically no public-sector bargaining statute, so we're writing our own rules from scratch. And "the right to work" has been driving me up a wall. Two weeks ago at a statehouse hearing both sides chanted it at each other. Our side meant something like: everybody deserves a job with decent conditions, and nobody gets locked out of a trade by a boss's blacklist or a licensing board. The other side meant: nobody should have to join or pay a union to keep the job they already have. Then a law prof got up and said the two sides were actually asserting the same Hohfeldian thing, and somebody in the back yelled "right to work for less," and that was the end of the useful discussion.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making — at minimum: a claim-right to be provided with a job; a negative right not to be prevented from working, and separately by whom (state licensing, employer blacklists and non-competes, a union's closed shop or hiring-hall dispatch); a right to contract your own labor on whatever terms you'll accept, including terms worse than the union's; a right not to be made to subsidize somebody else's political speech; a right to equal consideration in hiring; the human-rights-declaration reading where it comes bundled with free choice of employment and just conditions of work; and the meta-question of whether it's waivable or something you can't sign away even if you want to. For each one: who owes the duty, what exactly the correlative duty is, whether the reading is trivial, substantive-and-defensible, or actually false, and which reading (if any) would really support what each side at that hearing was trying to do with it. Especially: is the law prof right that they collapse into one thing?

Then two cases I actually have to decide. (1) We're considering a members-only structure: no exclusive representation, no agency fee, it bargains only for dues payers — but we'd also run a hiring hall for adjunct course assignments, dispatch by seniority, members before non-members. (2) A member wants to opt out of the grievance procedure and represent herself in a discipline case, and seperately wants her dues cut by whatever share goes to the union's legislative lobbying. Which readings of "right to work" do these violate, if any, which readings are compatible with them, and is the violation (where there is one) a reason for us to stop or just a reason to say we never accepted that reading?
