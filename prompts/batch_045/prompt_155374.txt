I'm presenting a twenty-minute paper next month at a one-day invited workshop on truth, publics, and democratic institutions, and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not another draft of the argument.

The paper, in brief. I reconstruct Hannah Arendt's account of organized lying in her 1971 essay Lying in Politics as a claim about the infrastructure of political judgment rather than about deception. Her target, on my reading, is what she calls defactualization: the replacement of the factual texture in which people orient themselves by a purpose-built pseudo-reality whose success condition is not that anyone believes it but that the shared factual frame loses its grip. Her asymmetry claim is that the liar holds a structural advantage over the truth-teller, because a lie can be cut to fit what its audience wishes to hear while facts are stubborn, unaccommodating, and cannot be tailored to anyone's wishes.

My extension, which is what they will actually argue with. I treat the public record — the stock of claims that strangers can cite to one another as shared premises without further argument — as a scarce resource that somebody pays to maintain. I distinguish two failure modes: propositional error, a false claim sitting in the record; and what I call frame capture, the consumption of the record's finite capacity by a claim's vocabulary, which is incurred in the very act of refuting it. I call the cost of refutation the rebuttal tax, and I argue that it makes political lying structurally like a denial-of-service attack rather than a contest of ideas: a speaker who can issue claims at trivial cost can force the record's custodians to spend unbounded effort, and this works independently of how credible the claim is. Hence a prediction I call believability-independence: the optimally defactualizing claim is one that nobody believes, because implausible claims still have to be answered and answering them spends capacity. If that is right, the standard repertoire — detect, flag, correct — targets propositional error while the damage is in frame capture, and what needs defending is the institutional fact-finder whose output is not immediately usable in political combat, rather than the fact-check.

The room, about twenty-five people and forty minutes of questions. Two historians of twentieth-century political thought, one of them an Arendt scholar who is sympathetic but impatient with the recent habit of recruiting Arendt for commentary on American presidents, the other broadly skeptical that a mid-century theorist of totalitarianism has anything to say about liberal democracies. Three political communication and political behavior researchers who run survey experiments on elite cues, corrections, and misinformation; they care about identification, effect sizes, durability, and the replication record. Two practitioners: an editor who runs a fact-checking desk and a trust-and-safety analyst; both care about what to do on Monday morning and about the harm metrics their organizations report.

What I want from you: eight or so objections, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to the subgroup it comes from and phrased in that subgroup's own vocabulary rather than translated into mine. For each, a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, and one line on where the reply is soft. Also: what I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything; the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive the session; any objection that is really a misreading I should correct in one sentence instead of answering; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
