My friend works in disability advocacy, and we spent last night arguing past each other, largely because neither of us could say what the disagreement was even about. The pattern I think I noticed is this.

Take a slur a group has substantially taken back. We argued about *queer*, but the same seems true of *crip*, and of a couple of other disability terms she uses about herself daily. Outsiders can use it without trouble in kind-modifying or group-level positions — queer politics, the queer community, crip theory, a queer reading of the film. Let the same outsider use the same word to classify an individual — she is queer, he is a crip — and many hearers, including hearers who use that word in the first sort of construction about themselves constantly, hear a slur again. It does not seem to track who is speaking, how sympathetic the remark is, or who is listening; I watched my friend's own colleague get the first half of a sentence right and the second half wrong.

Is there an established concept for a reclaimed slur's being reclaimed in some grammatical or semantic positions and not others, stably enough that the offense is predictable in advance? If so, I want it together with what it says the offending use is doing that the acceptable one is not. If not, please coin a term and define it. Either way I want the carving more than the label: is the distinction really syntactic, or is it about what the utterance does — classifying a particular person rather than modifying a kind, presenting the membership as essential to that person rather than as one feature among others? Where are the edges: plural self-descriptions such as queers, uses that echo the target's own self-application, uses about public figures? And how does the phenomenon bear on whether the derogation is part of the word's meaning or something the utterance does, which was the claim my friend's whole case rested on and which neither of us had earned? I assume this is not merely the general point that offense is context-sensitive, and not merely the familiar account of reclamation as a group's own historical process — but tell me if I am wrong to assume that, and what the nearest concepts are if neither fits.

I should add that I have no library access and don't want a reading list or references; the distinctions matter to me more than who coined them. Please answer in American English.
