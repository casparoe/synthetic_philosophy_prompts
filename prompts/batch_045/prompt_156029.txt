I have spent the last several months deciding whether to take the predictive genetic test for Huntington's disease. My mother was diagnosed last spring, so my prior is very close to a coin flip, and the result would tell me nothing I could act on medically. While trying to think about this properly I started reading on the value of information, and I keep circling around Good's theorem, or rather around the result I take it to state: for an agent whose preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms and whose utility function will not itself be altered by what she learns, a costless piece of information cannot have negative expected value, since she can always decline to let it change what she does and act exactly as she would have acted without it. I find that result both unimpeachable and, in my situation, close to useless. So I want to stop arguing with it in the abstract and instead start from the verdicts I actually have about particular cases, and see what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here is where I find myself.

1. Taking the Huntington's test itself: worth doing, before the decisions I have coming up about children, about a mortgage, about whether to stay in a job whose pension vests in twelve years.

2. A whole-genome scan returning several hundred variants of uncertain significance and lifetime risk estimates for dozens of conditions, free, through a research study I've been invited into: not worth doing, even though some of the returned results would be actionable.

3. Asking the surgeon who would do a procedure on me how many of them he has performed and what his complication rate is: worth asking, even on the assumption that the answer may well be unflattering.

4. Reading the last fifty pages of the novel I am two hundred pages into: not worth doing, and this feels like the clearest case on my list even though nothing turns on it and no decision is at stake.

5. Being told my own score on an IQ test I have already sat: not worth being told.

6. Looking at my students' teaching evaluations before I finalize their grades: not worth doing, and I would go further and say it would be a reason to distrust the grades I then produced.

7. Letting my employer, or any insurer, come to know my genetic result: not worth it, even though it is the very same information I have just said I want for myself.

8. Asking my mother's neurologist to walk me through her early clinical notes: worth it, I think, even though I can foresee that I will then read my own ordinary twitches as symptoms for years.

Two of these pairs I cannot get comfortable with. In 3 and 6 alike the information is free, relevant to something I am going to do anyway, and impossible for me to pretend I have not seen; yet I want the surgeon's numbers and I do not want the evaluations. And 1 and 2 are harder than they look: the scan in 2 contains strictly more actionable content than the test in 1, and my reason for refusing it is not that the information is useless but that I do not trust myself to hold several hundred weak signals at their proper weight rather than converting them into dread. That is, I take the theorem's escape clause — that one can always ignore the information — to be doing far more work than it appears to do, because ignoring is a thing I can resolve to do and then fail to do.

The closest I have come to a principle is this: costless information is worth acquiring just in case its expected effect on what I do runs through a channel I endorse — it should move me by revising my beliefs about matters I already count as reasons for that action — and not through a channel I do not endorse: altering what I want, degrading an experience that is itself part of what I was choosing, leaking into a judgment I am obliged to make only on specified grounds, or becoming visible to someone who will then use it against me. The obvious trouble is that this is close to empty. Stated in terms of channels I endorse, it threatens to ratify whatever the information does in fact do, and so to restate my verdicts rather than explain them. What I need is a version that tells me, before I have the information, which channel it will travel by; otherwise I have a slogan and not a principle.

So I would like the following: a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough to be applied, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on my eight cases; a plain statement of which of my verdicts it refuses to accommodate and whether you think I should abandon those verdicts or abandon the principle; and what it says about two cases I have not listed — a friend in my exact position who is certain that no result would change her mind about having children, and the difference, if the principle recognizes one, between being told my own result in a clinic and having it appear in a patient-portal message on a Sunday night. If the honest answer is that there is no single principle here and that what I need is a taxonomy of the ways in which the theorem's antecedents fail in ordinary life, say that instead, and give me the taxonomy.
