I help run a small reading series — one evening a month, in the back room of a secondhand bookshop — where half the audience comes for the poems and half comes for the puzzles, and I would like to read something of my own at the next one. I left school at seventeen and have no mathematics beyond what I have picked up on my own since, so what I need from you is not an explanation of the problem. I think I understand the problem. What I do not trust is my ability to turn it into a poem that someone who works on probability for a living would not wince at.

The material is this. Draw a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle in it, and ask how likely a randomly chosen chord of the circle is to be longer than a side of the triangle. The answer depends on what picking at random is taken to consist in. The three usual ways of spelling it out are: choose a radius, choose a point on it uniformly, and draw the chord across that point (answer 1/2); let the chord's midpoint fall wherever it falls in the disc, like a raindrop on a skylight (answer 1/4); choose two points independently on the rim and join them (answer 1/3). When I first met this I did the obvious scrupulous thing and treated the length itself as what one should be even about — every length from nothing up to the diameter equally likely — which produces a fourth number, 1 − √3/2, about 0.134, that I have never seen listed anywhere. I would like that fourth attempt in the poem, spoken in the voice of someone being as fair as they know how and landing where nobody else lands.

Then there is the thing I want the poem to turn on, which as far as I can tell is not in any of the books. Leave the three procedures exactly as they are and change only the question: inscribe a square instead of a triangle and ask whether the chord is longer than a side of the square (that side being √2 times the radius). Now the rim-points procedure and the raindrop procedure both give exactly 1/2 — they stop disagreeing — while the point-on-a-radius procedure gives 1/√2, about 0.707. The three-way split collapses into a two-way one, and it collapses because of the shape that was drawn, not because of anything about chords. I worked this out on the back of an envelope and could not find another inscribed regular polygon at which any two of the three coincide.

What I want is a sestina: six unrhymed six-line stanzas on the usual rotation of six end-words, then a three-line envoi in which all six words come back, two to a line, in whatever order serves you. I want that form for a reason worth honouring — a sestina keeps the same six words and changes nothing but their order, and the poem means something different each time, which is the whole difficulty: same circle, same question, same handful of nouns, different arrangement, different number. My candidate end-words are chord, circle, middle, rim, random, evenly; if a different six does the work better, use yours.

Give the first two stanzas to the naive picture, in which the question looks like an ordinary question with one answer. Let the middle stanzas give each procedure a physical body — a spinning pointer, rain on a skylight, two pins stuck into the rim — and put my even-about-the-length attempt in among them. Make the turn the swap of the triangle for the square, and let the envoi be the place where the numbers have to be said in one breath. The poem should not resolve into all answers being equally good, and it should not resolve into probability being merely subjective. What it should land on, I think, is that the rule telling you to spread your confidence evenly does not survive your changing which quantity you spread it over. One further constraint: I am told there is a serious argument that one of the procedures is the only unbiased one, because it is the only one that behaves the same if you move the circle or change its size — the one that yields 1/2 on the triangle. Do not have the poem deny that, and do not have it announce a winner either; leave the door open.

No symbols, no jargon, nothing explained before or after — the poem has to stand by itself for someone who has never heard of the problem, and it should not be a joke poem with a number as the punchline. If any of my figures are wrong, say so in one sentence and write to the corrected ones.
