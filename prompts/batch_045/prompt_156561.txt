I work on the ethics of algorithmic personalization, and for the past year I've been in a reading group on the ethics of influence that has come to a complete standstill on one question: what, if anything, distinguishes manipulation from legitimate persuasion. The stall isn't from lack of effort. We've worked through the whole familiar menu of accounts — the family of views on which manipulation is influence that bypasses or subverts the target's capacities for rational deliberation (emotion, priming, framing, salience, order effects) while persuasion addresses them; the family that instead centers covertness or misrepresentation of what is going on, whether or not reason is bypassed; the accounts that say manipulation needn't bypass anything because it can work *through* deliberation by getting someone to weigh considerations badly; and the versions that treat the term as morally loaded from the start, so that calling something manipulation is already half a verdict. Each account handles the core cases and none of them wins. We also never converge on the sub-questions: whether the influence has to run against the target's interests or against their informed or idealized judgment, whether intention is required, whether an algorithm, an institution, or a built environment can manipulate without anyone intending to, and whether manipulation can be benign or even wanted.

What makes me think something structural is wrong is how the group splits on cases I invented to test the accounts. Call the first *veridical steering*: a system that never asserts anything false and never withholds anything it promised to give you, but which selects which true considerations to raise and in what order, using an individual model of your psychology, and thereby reliably moves you somewhere you wouldn't have gone had the considerations been presented in a neutral order. Call the second *tonal calibration*: a system that holds the propositional content fixed and adjusts only the emotional register and pacing so as to bring your confidence to a particular level — sometimes a level that better matches the evidence, sometimes not. Call the third *retrospective endorsement*: influence that clearly works by a non-rational route, which the target would have refused in advance, endorses wholeheartedly on reflection afterward, and endorses again once told exactly how it was done. And then there is the humblest case: a scent, a default ordering in an interface, a room arranged to make you linger. Our bypass theorist says the first is manipulation and the second isn't; our covertness theorist says the first stops being manipulation the moment the selection policy is disclosed, even if disclosure doesn't change the effect; someone else says the third was never manipulation because endorsement is what mattered; and nobody agrees about whether the fourth is even the right kind of thing to evaluate. Every one of them sounds principled and none of them budges.

My hunch, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that this is not one debate. Part of it may be verbal or, more precisely, a fight over a thick concept whose extension people fix by their moral verdicts and then rationalize backwards into necessary conditions. Part of it may hide an empirical question: if it turns out that nearly all ordinary influence — including ordinary argument — works partly through attention, framing, and affect, then a bypass criterion either collapses into condemning all influence or has to specify a threshold nobody has specified. And part of it may be two different questions under one heading: a moral question about when influence wrongs someone, and an explanatory question about the causal route it took, where people keep using an answer to the second as if it settled the first. I'd like a diagnosis of which of these — or something else, such as disagreement about which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for — is actually doing the work, argued from what each kind of account is committed to rather than surveyed. And given your diagnosis I want an honest account of what would count as progress: a distinction that separates the questions, a formal or empirical result that would settle the hidden part, a change of subject, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. Please write in American English, and please don't spend the answer on a literature survey, bibliography, or citations — I'm reading this offline and I already know the landscape; I want the structure of the disagreement.
