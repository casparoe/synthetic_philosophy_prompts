I'm giving a paper next month at a workshop on responsible engineering, and I want the Q&A to go well, not just the talk. My thesis is that an artifact's politics lies neither in its designer's intentions nor in the social relations the artifact is said to require, but in the asymmetry of *exit costs*: the gap between what it costs a person to go along with the artifact's default behavior and what it costs them to route around it, together with who bears which of those costs. On this account something is political to the degree that its default is cheap to accept and expensive to refuse, and it stops being political when the two costs are evened up — which makes politics a design variable rather than a fate. My corollary, and the part I expect to fight over, is that the remedy is symmetry of exit costs, not the procedural remedies this literature usually reaches for: stakeholder consultation, participatory design, ethics review boards.

The room will be roughly forty people, and I'd rather be prepared for *this* audience than for the one I'd get in a philosophy department. Most of them are mechanical and software engineering faculty who teach the required engineering-ethics module and who mostly understand ethics as professional codes, case studies about whistleblowing, and the conviction that tools are neutral and that politics enters only at the stage of regulation and use; they will hear my cost talk as an attempt to make them legally responsible for user laziness. Three or four are STS people, at least one a social constructivist who thinks Langdon Winner's 1980 paper settled the matter and that an artifact's politics is a property of the network that stabilizes it rather than of any feature anyone designed — and who may also point out that the empirical case behind the famous low-overpasses example has been disputed. Two are philosophers of technology in the pragmatist line who will want to know why I am not simply re-describing instrumentalization theory in the vocabulary of costs.

Draft my prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased in the idiom of the group it comes from, with a ninety-second reply and one sentence marking where that reply is soft. Tell me which objection I should concede before anyone raises it, and which single reply the paper's survival depends on. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and flag any reply that is true but that I should swallow anyway because it would lose this room. Close with two or three lines on what would genuinely make me abandon the asymmetry account — the argument or finding that would show exit-cost symmetry is either unengineerable or not the thing that does the normative work I'm claiming it does.
