I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next week I'm giving a paper at a joint philosophy–cognitive neuroscience workshop. I'd like a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence of the thesis or any reading.

My thesis: Block's access/phenomenal distinction has been drawn in the wrong place, because 'access' as it is normally glossed — poised for use in reasoning, report, and the rational control of action — conflates two things that the global neuronal workspace literature already keeps apart. The first is global broadcast: the all-or-none, sustained, long-range fronto-parietal amplification that makes a content available to many domain-general consumer systems at once, with its familiar ignition and P3b signature. The second is uptake: some consumer actually using the content, in report, encoding, reasoning, or control. My claim is that phenomenal consciousness tracks broadcast, not uptake. Broadcast is a single parallel event with a capacity of roughly one content at a time; uptake is serial and capacity-limited. So the classic partial-report cases are not overflow beyond the workspace — the whole array is broadcast, but only a few items can be consumed — and, conversely, highly automatized, cognitively impenetrable routines can show uptake without broadcast. The testable consequence is that manipulations which spare ignition while disabling consumption should leave phenomenology intact, and vice versa.

The room: two labs in the global neuronal workspace tradition, who operationalize conscious access as reportability and think in terms of masking and the attentional blink; a postdoc who runs no-report paradigms; the philosophy side, who take A- versus P-consciousness as fixed background and will want to know whether I have only renamed the problem; and one psychologist who holds that frontal ignition indexes report and attention rather than consciousness itself, and that the relevant signature is posterior.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased as that person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Please include how they'd press the empirical worry that any readout I appeal to is itself a consumer; the philosophers' version of the worry that if nothing can use the content, calling it 'poised' or 'available' is just relabelling phenomenality; and the challenge of why broadcast needs a bottleneck at all rather than just recurrent processing within sensory cortex. Also include the question I'm hoping nobody asks: a workspace that still broadcasts but whose consumer systems are all lesioned or disconnected — is that subject fully conscious, and if so am I in a better position than the overflow theorist I'm criticizing?

For each objection, mark which claim of mine it attacks as settled empirical consensus and which is genuinely contested among people who work on this, so I don't present a contested piece of the theory as established.
