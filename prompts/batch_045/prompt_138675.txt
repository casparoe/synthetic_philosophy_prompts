I'm working on a feature about the "whose values" question in AI alignment — the version of it that isn't really a technical problem, where even if you could perfectly recieve and implement whatever target you specified, you'd still have to say *which* values go in the target. My editor says the piece needs an actual argument in it rather than a tour of the options, so I want you to properly compare two families of answer and come down on one side.

Call the first aggregation: the different value systems in the population are inputs to a single social choice and get traded off against each other — Harsanyi-flavoured expected utility over a distribution of value systems, or the more alignment-native variant where you imagine everyone's values extrapolated and then let them bargain or vote, roughly the coherent extrapolated volition idea. Call the second neutrality: the system should act only on reasons that no reasonable comprehensive doctrine could reasonably reject — the Rawlsian public reason move, so the AI doesn't take a stand on contested questions of the good life and stays hands-off where doctrines disagree. Please don't just describe them side by side: I want to know where they actually come apart, and I'd like you to walk through two test cases to do it. First, content moderation decisions on a global platform, where there's a genuinely plural audience and something coercion-like is happening. Second, a personal AI assistant advising an individual user about something deeply personal that everyone in the user's own community would call sinful — because it occurs to me that liberal neutrality was designed as a constraint on *state* power, and an AI's power is often advisory, infrastructural, or subtly nudgy rather than coercive, so it's not obvious the analogy even holds in the second case and I'd like you to say whether that breaks neutrality as an answer here.

Three more wrinkles I keep getting stuck on, which I'd like you to fold in rather than treat as footnotes. (1) Real people aren't coherent comprehensive doctrines: they're internally fragmented, they change their minds, their revealed preferences and their stated ideals conflict. So the choice between the two frameworks arguably has to be made twice — once between persons and once within a person — and I suspect neutrality needs more coherence than actual humans have while aggregation has an easy time of it only because it doesn't care, at the cost of seeming to disenfranchise someone's settled self-conception in favour of their sub-personal factions. (2) Temporal constituency: if the system is around for centuries, aggregating the values of currently living humans looks like a dead-hand rule, but neutrality gives you no guidance at all about which generation's doctrines count as the reasonable ones. (3) If the aligned system is itself a possible moral patient, is it inside or outside the constituency — and which framework strains more under that.

Give me a verdict on which family is in better shape as an answer to "whose values", and what the strongest thing the losing one gets right is that the winner can't absorb. And be explicit about the epistemic status of what you're telling me: where your reasoning is most likely to fail, what argument or consideration would flip you to the other side, and which parts of your answer rest on contested empirical guesses about AI capabilities or on contested metaethical claims rather than on anything settled. No reading lists or citations — I can't use them in the piece anyway, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.
