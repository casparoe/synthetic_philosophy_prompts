I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of human enhancement, and next week I'm running the discussion section of our department's "Bioethics on Screen" module, where I've chosen Andrew Niccol's Gattaca (1997) to anchor a session on the therapy/enhancement distinction. I'd like you to write as a philosopher who works professionally on the ethics of enhancement and who also takes film seriously as a text rather than as a syllabus of examples. Please begin by brainstorming for a paragraph or two — the distinction's main formulations (normal-function and biostatistical accounts, opportunity-range and welfarist accounts, and the familiar worries that the line is value-laden, unstable, or unable to bear the normative weight put on it), and the places in Gattaca where each of those formulations seems to be illustrated, tested, or quietly violated — before settling on the reading you want to defend. Then give me the reading itself: structured, concrete, and with scenes I can put in front of students.

Here is where I want the reading to be genuinely in the weeds rather than a restatement of the standard debate. First, a worry I can't shake: the technology at the centre of the film's world is not enhancement in the sense the literature usually discusses. Vincent isn't denied a treatment; he is sorted, and the sorting happens before he exists, through embryo profiling and selection, while his cardiac condition apparently has no treatment available to him at all. So is Gattaca even a film about enhancement, or is it a film about genetic discrimination, meritocracy, and what I'd call genetic fatalism — the treating of a probability profile as a destiny — that only looks like a film about enhancement? Second, I've been developing something I call the ratchet objection: once selection for favoured traits is common, the baseline against which "normal functioning" and hence "therapy" is defined ratchets upward, so that interventions which were enhancements get reclassified as restorations of normal function, and the distinction starts to look conventional rather than natural. I think the film dramatizes this — the natural conception that used to be unremarkable is relabelled "in-valid" and "degenerate," the valid/in-valid cut sits near the top of the distribution rather than at any clinical threshold, and even a valid like Irene Cassini finds her own cardiac risk caps the career she wants — but I'm not sure whether the film is self-aware about it or whether it actually needs a stable, pre-social baseline of normal functioning in order for its discrimination critique to have anything to condemn. Tell me which, and where you'd look in the film for the evidence. Third, the ending troubles me for teaching purposes: Vincent reaches Titan only by borrowing Jerome Eugene Morrow's genotype, Dr. Lamar falsifies the final test partly because his own selected son is "not all that they promised," and Jerome immolates himself with his medal. I read that as the film conceding that the merit/luck contrast is less tidy than its tagline about the human spirit suggests — but does the ending vindicate the enhancement sceptic, or is it fully compatible with a defence of enhancement, on the grounds that what is wrong in Gattaca is discrimination and fatalism, not the modification of human traits?

Please be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of a distinction, and don't stretch the distinction until it fits the film; say plainly where the film resists the reading and where a critic has to admit the lens is being imposed. My supervisor's view is that using Gattaca to teach the therapy/enhancement distinction is a category mistake, since the film is really about determinism and the human spirit, and I want the reading to answer that objection rather than talk past it. Please don't give me citations, bibliographies, or pointers to further reading — I have those; I want the argument and the scenes. Close by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the philosophical debate that the papers on the distinction don't.
