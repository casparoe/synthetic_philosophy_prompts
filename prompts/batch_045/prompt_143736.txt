I run a weekly two-hour seminar on philosophy of physics, about 14 people, mixed: half have taken a thermodynamics course, the other half are philosophy majors who get twitchy around equations. We sit around one big table, no lab, no laptops. Materials I can get hold of: several decks of cards, index cards, pennies, poker chips, dice, rubber bands, cardboard, shoeboxes, a bag of mixed beads.

I want a hands-on activity for Maxwell's demon where two things land in the gut rather than on the whiteboard:

1. the bottleneck isn't the looking, it's the forgetting — that getting the information can in principle be done for free, and what you can't do for free is get the demon's memory back to a blank state so the whole cycle can repeat;
2. the demon's notebook isn't bookkeeping standing outside the physics, it's another physical system in the room, and once you count it the books balance again.

Ideally they spend the first hour convinced they've beaten the second law and then find the bill. I definitly don't want to stand up front and draw boxes.

What I'd like from you:

- Rules for a cards-and-chips game, with the payout calibrated so that the seperation gain and the memory-clearing cost cancel exactly. My thought: each molecule that ends up on the right side earns 1 chip, each bit the demon has to erase costs 1 chip, so a perfect sort of 32 molecules uses 32 bits and nets exactly zero. Is that the right calibration, or is the honest payout per molecule smaller than the price of a bit (a real engine can't extract all of it)? And should the payout per molecule be flat, or does it need to be a curve, since I have a feeling the free energy of separation is logarithmic in how pure the chambers are? Does the game break if I cheat and use a flat rate?
- A mechanism that forces the demon to actually use its memory, so it can't peek and sort in one motion. My idea is a holding chute: look at the card, write a bit, drop the card in the chute, and only at the end of the batch read the oldest bit and route that card. Does that work, or is there a cleaner way to make the record load-bearing?
- The escapes my students will try, and what to do with each. I can predict four: (a) never erase, just keep buying more notebooks; (b) clear a slot for free by re-matching a record to the card it refers to and undoing that bit of the sort; (c) copy the record onto a second tape and declare the cost avoided; (d) don't measure every molecule, just act when a fast one happens to come by, and claim the information was free. I want these to fail instructively rather than be blocked by fiat, so for each: what rule makes it come out to zero, and what's the one-sentence debrief line?
- A trapdoor round: shoebox, cardboard, rubber bands, beads. Ask each group to build a sorter with no memory at all; they will build a one-way flap. I want them to run into the reason it fails — that a flap light and loose enough for a molecule to push open is also light and loose enough to be jiggling open by itself, and that stiffening the rubber band cures one and causes the other. Give me a dice rule or a tuning procedure that makes that tradeoff show up in what they do.
- A debrief mapping specific moments onto the ideas, plus the honest part: where the card game is only an analogy, the fact that the exact price per bit is something I'll have to assert rather than demonstrate, and anything else I shoud flag.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it. I'm most worried about the student who says the whole thing is circular because I set the payouts using the second law, and about groups finishing at wildly different speeds. Is groups of four or five, one demon and one banker each, the right shape?

One last thing: a couple of them will have read somewhere that what really matters is how much the memory actually tells you about the gas, so that a fuzzy or partial record still buys you something proportional to it. Is there a variant — noisy bits, or a demon that records the sign of a fluctuation instead of every molecule — that lets them feel that in the same session, or am I overloading it?
