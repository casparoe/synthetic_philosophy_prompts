I've been reading about AI safety stuff on my own for a couple of years now — no academic background, I work in logistics, taught myself some probability off the internet — and I keep coming back to one distinction: worst-case guarantees versus average-case guarantees. The way I've got it in my head: a worst-case guarantee says the system stays safe no matter what situation or input it lands in, and an average-case guarantee says it's safe in expectation across some spread of deployments you think you'll actually face. And as I understand it, engineering outside AI mostly does something in between: planes get certified for the worst weather inside a design envelope, not for every physically possible storm, and there's a whole apparatus for arguing about where the envelope stops.

Some things I've been chewing on that I want you to actually engage with rather than politely set aside:

1. Tail dominance. My worry is that the whole distinction collapses at the bad end. If there's any nonzero chance of an outcome that's catastrophically bad, then the average is just that probability times that badness, so an average-case guarantee is only reassuring if you already know something about the far tail — which is exactly the part nobody can measure. Is that right? Does it mean average-case guarantee is more or less an empty slogan once the stakes get existential, or is there a version of it that survives this?

2. Seatbelt guarantees (my term, no idea if there's a real name for it). Instead of safe in expectation over the deployments we expect, how about guarantees shaped like this: there's some cheap condition P that you can actually monitor at runtime, and when P holds you get something close to worst-case strength, and when P fails you at least get a loud and slow failure instead of a silent fast one? It seems to me like this sidesteps the whole fight about who picked the distribution. Is it coherent, is it secretly just average-case reasoning with extra steps, and if it is coherent, what's the hard part of it?

3. Whoever writes down the deployment distribution is doing politics. An average-case guarantee is only as good as the process that decides which scenarios count as normal and who gets to declare a scary scenario out of scope. This feels like the most underrated part of the whole topic to me, but I might be overindexing on it because it's the one bit I can get my head around.

4. The adversary's allowance. Worst-case reasoning usually imagines an unbounded adversary, which for something as flexible as a general model is so strong it risks being vacuous. But actual bad actors have budgets, deadlines and limited information, and so do accidental failure modes. Is there a useful notion of a bounded adversary here, given that the AI system itself might be the thing that lowers everyone else's costs?
