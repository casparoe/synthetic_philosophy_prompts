I have no academic background in this -- I do scheduling for a freight company and read about this stuff at night -- and I've been following the argument about whether DNA is literally a code, or literally contains information, and it looks totally stuck to me. Its been going on for decades, nobody seems to budge, and every thread I read ends in the same place. I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict, and I'd like it in American English please. Also: please don't give me reading lists or author names to chase -- I'm not going to be able to look anything up, so argue from what the two sides actually say.

Here's how it looks to me from the outside. One side is basically the working molecular biology / textbook way of talking: there are four symbols, they come in triplets, each triplet maps to one of the amino acids, the mapping is many-to-one and could have been different, the cell "reads" the sequence and "translates" it, you can change one letter and get a different protein, you can compute how much information a sequence carries in bits, and you can treat genomes as strings and do statistics on them like they were text. Their point is that this isn't decoration: it predicts, it lets you design, and the mapping being arbitrary is exactly what a code looks like.

The other side says all of that is metaphor inherited from the early days plus from the cybernetics people, and that literally speaking nothing in the cell is about anything, nothing in DNA is correct or incorrect, nobody reads anything, the ribosome just does what its shape makes it do, the "information" is just a measure of how surprising a sequence is and has nothing to do with meaning, and the "genetic program" way of talking has actively misled people about development, because the sequence doesn't contain a plan for an organism -- the cell's existing machinery and the environment do most of that work. And then, usually last, someone says "fine, it's a metaphor, but a useful one," and nothing follows from that and the thread dies.

Some wrinkles I've come up with on my own that I never see addressed, and that make me think the fight is being had in the wrong place:

1. Half of what DNA does isn't code-like at all. When DNA gets copied, that's base pairing -- chemistry, like a mold or a plaster cast. Nobody calls a mold a code. The only part that looks code-like is the triplets-to-amino-acids part. So maybe "DNA is a code" is true of the translation system and false of the molecule, and a lot of the arguing is about which thing is the subject of the sentence.

2. The stuff that does the decoding -- the adapters and the enzymes that load them -- is itself built by the system that contains the code. So the "rulebook" that says which triplet stands for which amino acid isn't written down anywhere outside the system, and nobody ever stipulated it. It's like a dictionary written entirely in the language it defines. Is a code nobody stipulated still a code, or does that make it something else -- a habit, a regularity, a lock and key?

3. A code needs a notion of getting it wrong. Biology seems to only offer "unusual" and "selected against," and those feel like different things. If "error" just collapses into "statistically rare and expensive," then the normativity that a real code needs seems to have gone missing, and I don't know if that's a flaw in the code claim or just a fact about biology.

4. "Code for whom?" feels like three different questions stuffed into one: a code the cell uses, a code that exists because evolution set it up, and a code for us in a lab who can write any sequence we want and get a predictable protein out. Those could come apart.

My hunch is that this isn't a verbal dispute but isn't one disagreement either: the two sides are each right about a different object, and the thing that would actually be substantive is a question further down, about whether biology is allowed to have functions and norms that aren't just statistics about what got selected. Is that right? Or is there a real, single, substantive disagreement hiding inside -- and could you tell me what it is, using what each side actually claims?

And then the part I care most about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? I have one idea -- if somebody built a second, independent triplet system from scratch with a different mapping, and it worked, would that settle whether the mapping is arbitrary or chemically forced, or would both sides just absorb it? Is there any distinction, result, or change of question that would actually end this, or should I expect it to go on forever, and why?
