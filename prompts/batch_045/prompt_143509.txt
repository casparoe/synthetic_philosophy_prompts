I help run a small reading group on ethics and technology — mostly working game developers, two philosophers, and a QA lead — and we have now spent two sessions going in circles about whether anything that happens to a character inside a video game can matter morally in its own right, or whether every argument on the table is secretly about the players and the people who build the games. Everyone keeps winning by pointing at one vivid case, so I want the specification written down before we argue any further. What I am asking for is the list of requirements that any adequate account of the moral standing of in-game characters would have to satisfy, where adequate is fixed by a purpose: the account has to be good enough for a studio's internal ethics review to use when deciding whether to ship a mechanic, how to tune it, and whether to keep a cancelled build's simulation servers running.

The cases I want the requirements anchored to, at minimum: a procedurally generated village of a few hundred NPCs whose needs, moods and relationships are simulated in full only while the player is nearby and are approximated by aggregate statistics once the player walks away, so the villagers seem to gain and lose standing as a function of the render budget; a colony-management game cancelled three weeks before release whose servers ran the simulation for fourteen months during testing, with logs recording famines that no player ever saw; the reload case, in which you murder the blacksmith, restore the save, and befriend him instead, which raises both the headcount and the identity question about branches; a companion character whose dialogue is produced on the fly by a live language model conditioned on the same game state, who produces unscripted grief-talk at the death of an in-game spouse, to be contrasted with the same character reduced to a script that emits a single Ouch token when damaged; an otherwise identical build in which the sensitivity parameter is set to zero, and a fan patch released a week after launch that removes the famine mechanic and is installed by most players; and a version in which each villager's need-simulation is replaced by a precomputed table of outputs that is indistinguishable from the outside, or in which the whole thing runs distributed across a million volunteer machines so that no single device instantiates anyone.

Please give me a numbered list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, a one-line statement of why it is a requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp, and a classification of it as non-negotiable, a genuine but defeasible desideratum, or a demand only one side of our dispute makes. Where a requirement is easy to appear to satisfy while evading it, say what the most tempting dodge looks like. I also want the following distinctions kept apart rather than collapsed, each with the case that shows why collapsing it is a mistake: a character's mattering directly versus the wrong consisting in what the mechanic does to players, developers or the culture; having standing at all versus how much; being a patient who can be wronged versus being an agent who can be responsible; and runs that actually execute versus runs that merely could. One member insists the whole question is whether there is a real pattern of suffering to be found there, in the sense that adopting the intentional stance toward the villager predicts it successfully in a way the physical stance and the design stance do not, and that this is what should settle it; someone else says that is only a fact about us. If that belongs on the list, put it there and say what it would rule in and out. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which of them has to give. Finish with a short stress test: describe three familiar families of position functionally — the view that only suffering realized in the right kind of biological or causal structure counts, the view that any functionally organized state of the right sort counts whatever it is made of, and the view that nothing in a game can matter directly and only effects on persons do — and, for each, name the single requirement it has the hardest time meeting. Keep the weight on the criteria and their cases rather than on the scorecard.
