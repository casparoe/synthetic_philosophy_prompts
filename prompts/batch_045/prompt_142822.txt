My dad finished chemo in March and ever since I've kept a note on my phone where, the night before each scan, I write down a number for "still clear at the next one". I always write about 0.7. Not because I think 0.7 — if you made me bet I'd take 0.85 on either side, happily — but because 0.85 feels like a hostage I'd be handing to the future, and 0.7 feels survivable whichever way it lands. My partner says a number I wouldn't bet on is just a superstition with decimal points, and honestly its hard to argue with. So I've been reading up on accuracy-first epistemology to work out whether he's right, and I keep coming back to something I don't see discussed much: the argument that your true credence is the report that maximises expected accuracy assumes you're an expected-utility maximiser. Once you let risk-weighting in (Buchak's version — the weight on an outcome is a risk function r applied to the probability of doing at least that well, so a risk-averse forecaster has r(q) < q and the worst-ranked outcome gets more weight than its probability), strict propriety stops doing the job it's supposed to. I scratched it out for the quadratic score: if your credence is c and the optimum stays on the same side of a half, the report that maximises risk-weighted expected accuracy gives the more likely outcome its risk-weighted probability — r(c) on the likely side — which under risk aversion drifts toward a half. So my 0.7 could be optimal and still be a worse forecast by the lights of my own credence. That upsets me more than it should, and I want a poem out of it.

Write me a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six plus a three-line envoi, unrhymed, on these six end-words — half, true, score, worst, weight, one. (half = the number I keep drifting to; true = accuracy as the only epistemic good, so hoping has no epistemic vote; score = the strictly proper scoring rule; worst = the outcome risk aversion loads weight onto; weight = the stuff that gets moved around; one = credences summing to one.) The rotation has to do real work: the same six words permuted stanza after stanza and nothing new allowed in — that's the partition staying fixed while the weights get redistributed, updating without new worlds. The envoi is where all six come back together, two to a line: the rule scoring the whole distribution at once instead of one report. Put the turn in the last stanza or the envoi, at the moment "true" and "score" come apart.

Write it in the voice of the 0.7 I actually write down, addressing the 0.85 I believe, and let the envoi be the only place the 0.85 answers back. Get the framework right: no pragmatic reasons for belief, you can't beat your true credence in expectation, the hedge costs expected accuracy, and the risk-weighted optimum isn't your credence. Someone who knows this literature should find it correct rather than just moody; someone who doesn't should still get a poem.

Commit to a reading — is my 0.7 a failing, am I leaving accuracy on the table, or is it what accuracy looks like once you stop assuming the forecaster is an EU machine? Don't sit on the fence. Then give me three or four lines on where the poem cheats: which contested premise it helps itself to, and where it goes past what the sketch above actually establishes.
