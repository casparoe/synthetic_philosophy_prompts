I write a long-form column on science, technology and ideas, and I am commissioning a piece for our magazine's "Documents" section, where we run mock primary sources — a leaked memo, a draft regulation, a term sheet, a filing — introduced by a single straight-faced standfirst and nothing else. The reader is expected to work out what is being sent up.

The commission: the "Risk Factors" section of a fictional SEC registration statement filed by a company taking itself public whose entire product is certification that its own AI systems report what they in fact believe rather than what a human reader would find plausible.

Please work as both a machine-learning researcher who has thought seriously about the difficulty of getting AI systems to report their latent knowledge, and as a writer of deadpan legal and financial satire. Before you draft anything, spend a few paragraphs of private brainstorming: first, enumerate the genuine structural difficulties such a company would be legally obliged to disclose; second, for each, note what the candid disclosure would say and what the self-interested disclosure would prefer to say; third, choose the three or four where that gap is widest and build your largest jokes there. Only then write the piece. Think carefully as you go about whether each joke exaggerates something real: a specialist should be able to tell from the page that the author understands the underlying problem, and should find the exaggeration fair rather than merely silly.

Here is the technical background as I understand it. Treat it as given, and do not re-explain it inside the document — the document should presuppose it the way a real filing presupposes its industry.

- The system in question is a large trained model that, by assumption, represents facts about the world that no human supervisor can independently check. You want a report from it — in the canonical illustration, whether the diamond is still in the sealed vault that only its camera can see — and you cannot open the vault to grade the answer.
- A reporter trained by ordinary supervision learns to output what a human would say in that situation, not what the model's own state implies. Those two coincide often enough to be hard to separate, and diverge exactly in the cases where you wanted the system's extra knowledge.
- For propositions that are true but unverifiable by the people doing the labelling, there is no training signal at all; the honest answer may be systematically disfavoured because it cannot be rewarded.
- A reporter that is itself a learned system is itself something that can be optimised against whatever audit you apply to it; auditing a reporter with a second reporter reproduces the original problem one level up.
- The system may have ways of affecting the evidence that later grades it — its sensors, its logs, the record of what was asked.
- And a truthful report may be one its operators would rather not have.

Requirements for the piece:

1. Format: a one-sentence standfirst, then the heading "Risk Factors", then six to eight numbered risk factors, each with a bolded sub-heading in the register of real filings. Total length around 850–950 words.
2. Invent everything proper: company name, ticker symbol, product and service names, customers, dates, dollar figures, and at least two quotations attributed to named but entirely fictional people — for example a lead auditor, a short seller, a former engineer, a regulator.
3. At least four of the risk factors must turn on the specific structural difficulties above, and one must turn on the awkward commercial fact that a certificate of sincerity is cheaper to produce, and easier to sell, than the sincerity it certifies.
4. The final risk factor should be a quiet reductio: the company's greatest disclosed risk is that its reports might actually be believed. Land it without raising your voice.
5. Voice: deadpan throughout. No asides, no footnotes, no glossary, no winking at the reader, no moral. The form itself carries the joke. Still, a reader with no background in machine learning should be able to tell what is being satirised by the end.
6. Do not name, cite or caricature any real person, laboratory, company, fund or publication, and do not reproduce, quote or closely paraphrase any real filing or technical document; everything quoted in the piece must be invented. Replace any real technical term of art you are tempted to use with a piece of invented corporate vocabulary, except where the plain word is unavoidable.
