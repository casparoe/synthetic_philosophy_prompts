I write about AI and the sciences of the mind for a general-readership magazine, and I keep tripping over one sentence I can't seem to make honest: the claim that a language model trained only on text doesnt really mean anything by its words, because it was never hooked up to the things its words are about. My editor cuts the paragraph every time I try to cash that out, which is fair, because when I look at it I realize I don't know what "hooked up" is doing. So take the standard Twin Earth setup: Oscar and Twin Oscar are indistinguishable from the inside, the rivers and lakes hold H2O on one planet and XYZ on the other, and the conclusion is supposed to be that their word picks out different stuff and hence means something different even though nothing internal to them differs. What I can never get clean is which thesis the case actually establishes. That reference is fixed by the environment rather than by whatever description the speaker associates with the word? That meaning isn't in the head? That no complete story about a speaker's intrinsic physical and functional states settles what she means? That we lack the armchair authority over our own thoughts we thought we had? Those look like four separate claims to me, and I want to know which ones follow from the bare case, which need an extra premise bolted on, and which are just riding the others' coat-tails. It would also help to know whether the argument needs words like "water" to behave like rigid designators rather than like shorthand for a cluster of superficial properties.

The variant I keep springing on people at dinner parties (sorry) is this: forget two planets. One colony, and across two centuries the water supply is very slowly contaminated with XYZ, a fraction of a percent a year, nobody notices, nobody's experience changes, and at the end everyone is drinking XYZ while using the same word and taking themselves to be talking about the same stuff their grandparents talked about. Does the reference of their word shift, and if so when, and does the answer change if there's one chemist in the colony who could in principle detect the change but never actually runs the test? My vague sense is that the gradual version is doing philosophical work the original doesn't, because it forces you to say something about reference over time and about how much causal contact with the stuff a speaker needs, but I can't say exactly what work.

And then the bit I actually need for the piece. For a system that has only ever seen text, is there even a Twin Earth to run? Two training corpora, one written by Earthlings and one by Twin Earthlings, would presumably be indistinguishable to the training procedure, and you'd get the same model out the other end either way. So does the token it emits for water refer to H2O, to XYZ, to whatever actually caused the texts, or to nothing at all — and is that a question externalism has an answer to, or one where it has nothing to say because there's no use and no causal chain from world to word except through other speakers? Walk me through it at a level I could paraphrase accurately for readers without embarrassing myself, and be explicit about your own confidence: how sure are you of the main things you're claiming, and which single point are you least sure of?
