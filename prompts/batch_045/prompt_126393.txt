I'm a research scientist in earth system modeling — eight years in, mostly on convective and boundary-layer parameterizations in a global model — and I've been dragged into an argument I can't finish. At a conference dinner an economist colleague defended what I've since learned is called the F-twist: the view, associated with Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, that the descriptive falsity of a model's assumptions is not a legitimate objection to it, and may even be a mark of its power, so long as the model predicts well over the domain it is meant to cover. I've since tried to work out what exactly is being claimed, and I've ended up with four candidate theses that I think get run together: (i) an appraisal thesis — only predictive or explanatory performance over the target domain bears on whether a model is a good one, and the truth of its internal assumptions is irrelevant to that appraisal; (ii) an abstraction-sign thesis — the more general and powerful a theory, the less its assumptions resemble the concrete situation, so unrealism is a symptom of depth rather than of carelessness; (iii) an as-if thesis — the entities in the model need not actually do what the model says they do, it is enough that they behave as if they did; (iv) a tractability thesis — unrealistic assumptions are chosen because they make the model computable, and the honest criterion is the trade-off between tractability, generality, and fit, not realism as such. I suspect these are genuinely different claims, that some are much easier to defend than others, and that the fight is really about which one you need — but I can't get traction from inside my own practice.
