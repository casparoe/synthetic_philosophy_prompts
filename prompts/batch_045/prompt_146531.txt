I write historical fiction — four novels now, all built out of documentary material: assize records, parish registers, a shipping company's ledgers, one memoir published in 1897 by a man nobody has heard of. Because my material is archival, most of what I need is simply not there. The archive is nearly silent about the poor, about women, about anyone who could not write, and it is silent about interiority even for the people it records at length. So I invent constantly. Over four books I have developed, without ever writing it down, a set of verdicts about when inventing is permissible, and I have begun to suspect they do not add up to a coherent view. I would like the principle, if there is one.

My tentative rule has two clauses: (i) invent into silences, never against records — where the archive speaks I follow it, where it is silent I am free; and (ii) what I invent should be the sort of thing the archive could not have recorded anyway — thoughts, speech, domestic life — rather than the sort of thing it would have recorded had it happened: a payment, a signature, a death, a name on a roll.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A real, named backbench MP whose votes survive in the printed division lists. I wrote the scene in which he decides to change his vote, and gave him reasons: ambition, a humiliating interview with his patron, a memory of his father's bankruptcy. Verdict: fine. It is where the novel lives, and I would do it again.

2. The same man, earlier draft: I had him take two hundred pounds from a railway interest to break with his party. There is no record of any such payment, and no whisper of it in the newspapers of the day. Verdict: not fine; I cut it. Yet this too was invented, unrecorded, and about the same man — the cut felt obvious, which is strange, because (1) felt obvious in the other direction.

3. In the same man's house, a housemaid: name invented, a childhood in a Norfolk village invented, a dead brother invented, a way of speaking invented, and roughly a third of the book's pages given to her. Verdict: fine, and more than fine — I feel I would be shirking something if I let the house hold only the people the census recorded.

4. A real woman, named, whose adult correspondence survives: forty letters, spirited and funny. Nothing at all is recorded about her first twenty years. In an early draft I wrote her childhood — a violent father, a schooling cut short. Verdict: not fine; I removed it and let her arrive on the page at twenty-one. But (3) gave me an entire invented childhood, and no one, including me, objected. The only difference I can locate is that in (3) the child was invented too. That looks like a technicality rather than a moral difference, and it troubles me.

5. I took two sentences from that obscure 1897 memoir and gave them, unchanged, to my invented housemaid to say. Verdict: fine — better than fine; they are the best thing she says. But when I considered putting one real woman's recorded letter-words into the mouth of another real woman, in a scene between them, I judged it not fine. In both cases recorded words end up spoken by someone who never spoke them; in one case the speaker never existed. So my verdicts seem to track something about the speaker rather than about the words.

6. I invented that a real, named woman did not burn a letter. A tiny physical detail; the archive is silent. But it settles, for the reader, whether a long recorded correspondence was reciprocated, and that is the book's central question. Verdict: not fine; I rewrote around it. Meanwhile I freely invented that the same woman breakfasted on porridge, took snuff, and disliked her brother-in-law. Those are also inventions about a real person, also unrecorded, and arguably also interpretive. My clause (i) says silence is silence, so it cannot distinguish them, and clause (ii) cannot either.

7. I wrote a diary for the maid and set it into the book beneath a heading that looked like an archival shelfmark, with no note. Verdict: not fine; I added a note. The identical diary, introduced inside the fiction as something the maid wrote and the narrator found: fine. Not one word changed — only who presented it.

8. Three obscure real clerks at the shipping firm, merged into one character. With an invented name: fine. With one of their real names: not fine. The character has the same invented thoughts and does the same invented deeds either way, and I cannot fully explain the difference.

9. A shameful, entirely undocumented act, attached to a lightly renamed character who is recognizably a real person with living relatives. Verdict: not fine, and the strength of my verdict here is out of all proportion to my rule. On my rule, a renamed fictional character is not a record at all, so (8) and (9) should both be easy permissions. They are not.

10. The case I keep returning to. A bill of sale lists eight people by first name only, sold with a cargo. I want to give two of them names, families, and the middle third of my novel. My verdict: fine — and I feel strongly that the archive should not set the terms of who gets a life. But a friend argues that inventing a life for them repeats what the bill of sale did: making a person legible on terms not their own. She thinks the honest novel would leave the gap and say so. I do not know which of us is right.

11. A whole novel of refusals — the record does not say, repeated at every turn. Verdict: fine and even admirable in small doses, but as a general policy it feels like a way of keeping my hands clean while the book stays about the people the archive happened to like. That suggests inventing into a silence can be an obligation and not merely a permission, which neither of my clauses expresses.

12. A minor real figure who died in 1971, with grandchildren living. Inventing an undocumented act for him feels worse to me than the same invention for the 1832 MP. If that is right, then something other than silence and recordability is doing the work — harm, or the expectations of readers who can still check.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures this pattern? Please state it precisely, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you would overturn; and say what it predicts about cases I have not settled — a novel narrated by an invented archivist who presents fabricated documents as real inside the fiction; depicting a documented atrocity through invented witnesses; inventing interiority for a real person who left an enormous archive as against almost none (does the density of the record constrain me, or license me?); one invented character made to stand for an entire unrecorded class; and whether the genre's contract with the reader — historical novel, roman à clef, documentary fiction — ought to matter at all, given that readers' expectations seem to be driving several of my verdicts whether I intend them to or not. Please answer in American English.
