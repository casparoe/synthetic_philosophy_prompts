**The fourth exemption**

Everyone knows the move. Aumann says agents with a common prior can't agree to disagree; Milgrom–Stokey says they can't even trade, because your willingness to trade reveals that you know something I don't, and once I update on that I don't want the trade. So the person on the other side of your fill is one of three things: different prior, hedging some outside exposure, or a noise trader charging you a fee. That's the whole epistemology of markets in three lines — disagreement is evidence of error somewhere, volume is a tax, and the price is our best available community posterior.

I want to add a fourth item to that list and then argue it breaks the third line.

Call a question *price-sensitive* if the market's own price is among the causes of the thing being predicted. Bank runs. Whether a norm catches on. Whether a lab's model gets described inmentalistic terms by the people who use it. Whether a startup makes its round. Whether a peg holds. Contrast: the mass of a particle, whether it rains in Bethesda on Tuesday.

The standard no-trade results assume in the background that the state is exogenous to trading: nature picks, then we trade, and allocations are functions of the state, never the reverse. That assumption is load-bearing in a really specific way — it's what makes a bet zero-sum in expectation over the common prior. If trades can move the state, a bet stops being a transfer conditional on how the world is and becomes partly a lever on how the world goes.

And then there's a trade that's expected-profit-maximising for someone who agrees with the market about everything. Say the contract pays $1 if the bank fails, and depositors are watching the price. It's at 20. In a book thin enough, I can walk it to 65 for a few thousand and hold it there until the run is underway. I don't think failure is more likely than the market does. I think my buying *is* the likelihood. I share the prior, I'm not hedging anything off-market, I'm not confused. I'm not betting on the world, I'm buying it. The theorem has nothing to say about me.

So: on price-sensitive questions, volume doesn't even weakly indicate disagreement, and the price isn't an estimate but a control input.

Here's the part I actually care about. The case for market epistemology is basically empirical — these things calibrate. But calibration is precisely the observation that can't separate:

- H_info: prices track an exogenous state because they aggregate dispersed private information.
- H_perform: prices track an endogenous state because they're one of its strongest causes, and collective action works.

Both predict calibration. Every "the market called it again" post is evidence for the disjunction, not the first conjunct. And it bites hardest where we most want it: AI timelines, institutional collapse, norm change, will anyone care about X — the most price-sensitive questions there are. The questions where markets are cleanest are the ones where nobody needs them. Futarchy is the limiting case, where the collapse of the two hypotheses is the design goal rather than the bug.

If I had a market and an IRB I'd run paired markets on matched sets of price-sensitive and price-insensitive questions, one lit and one dark (prices not displayed, settle on a hidden book's median). If lit and dark calibrate alike, information wins. If lit beats dark on the sensitive set and ties on the rest, the gap is performativity and its size is a price-elasticity of reality. My guess is you'd see the gap and it'd be bigger than anyone posting market screenshots wants it to be.

Upshot I'd defend in public: quoting a price on a reflexive question isn't reporting a probability, it's an intervention, and it deserves the etiquette of leaking an early result. Which means a decent chunk of our local practice — the market says 12%, so I'll act as if 12% — is on those questions not deference to a better guesser but membership in a causal loop. Sometimes that's what you want. You should know which one you're doing.
