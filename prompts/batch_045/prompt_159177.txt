I'm an assistant collections officer at a mid-sized city museum in the north of England and I've been drafted onto our "collections ethics" working group, which mostly means sitting in meetings where we try to write down a policy for what we give back and what we keep. Everyone argues about the huge national cases, but I keep feeling our own little policy has reasoned its way into a position nobody ever stated out loud. Here's roughly what we do: human remains — ancestral remains, hair samples, plaster life-casts, that sort of thing — go back basically on request, no proof needed about how we got them, and we don't ask what the recipients intend to do with them. Objects taken during a punitive military expedition are "returnable", but only to a recognised national government or its national museum, only if we're satisfied about storage and climate control, and preferably with a rotating-loan or long-term-partnership deal so the thing spends part of its life back here. Objects bought in good faith at auction in the 1920s-50s from colonial officers' estates — sometimes the same kind of object, sometimes from the same expedition — are not returnable at all; for those we offer a high-res 3D scan, a co-curated display and a very honest label. Anything where the maker seems to have sold the object to a European trader for money we treat as an ordinary market transaction and don't discuss. And the group has just floated a fifth idea: a "custodianship rating" — if we judge the requesting country's national museum can't care for the object properly, we hold it "on their behalf" indefinitely, which is quietly how we justify keeping nearly everything.

What confuses me is the shape of the thing. We hand back a person but not a bronze. We hand back the looted one but not the purchased twin of the looted one. We'll deal with a state but not with the descendants of the court the object was taken from, even when they're the ones asking and the state's own claim looks shaky. And holding something "on behalf of" the very people who are asking for it back seems to dissolve into just keeping it. One colleague says we're secretly committed to the view that the wrong was in the violence of the taking and nowhere else; another says we're committed to thinking culture belongs to a nation rather than a people; a third says we just think we're better at looking after objects than anybody else, and the rest is decoration.

So: what does a policy shaped like this actually presuppose — about ownership and whether the claim is to title or to custody or to the story or to the ancestors; about who can hold a claim at all and why a state rather than a community or a lineage; about whether the wrong is located in the original transfer, in the violence, or in the ongoing holding; and about whether a good-faith purchase or a willing seller could make a removal legitimate? Which of those commitments does the practice strictly require and which is it merely comfortable with? Where do its parts pull against each other — especially the "on their behalf" clause against the state-only rule, and the remains rule against the objects rule? And if I wanted to take the rival view seriously for a change — say, that what's at issue is the living relationship between a community and its things rather than property at all — which parts of the policy would have to change and which could stay exactly as they are?
