Background, so we're starting from the same place: the case this corner of the literature runs on is the digger wasp. It paralyses a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect, comes back out and pulls the cricket in. If you move the cricket a few inches while it's inside doing the inspection, it comes out, drags the cricket back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again — and you can apparently keep that going indefinitely. The question people draw from it is whether any of our own behaviour has that shape: rigid, unresponsive to the fact that the environment has changed, running the same subroutine while the point of the subroutine has quietly evaporated. And then, the further question, whether mechanically rigid behaviour is the same thing as unfree behaviour, or whether that's a slide.

Three distinctions I want you to keep separate and tell me where the film sits on each:
1. Insensitivity to changed circumstances — the wasp's problem: the world moved, the behaviour didn't update.
2. Insensitivity to reasons — you can see the reasons perfectly well and they don't move you.
3. Insensitivity to counterfactual point — here's the test I've been toying with, and I want you to tell me if it's any good: an agent is sphexish with respect to some action iff it would still perform that action in the nearest nearby world in which that action's point has been deleted.
