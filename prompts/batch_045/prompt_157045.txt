I audit this small seminar on animal agency (I'm not enrolled, I just sit in the back and photograph the whiteboard afterwards), and the professor has been building a running table of cases which she labels "sphexish" or "not sphexish" — her term for behaviour that is rigidly scripted, that keeps running the routine even after the world has stopped cooperating, after the old digger-wasp story. She refuses to give us a definition, says we should be able to feel it, and after six weeks the scoreboard looks like this: the wasp who re-drags her cricket back to the threshold and re-inspects the nest every time the experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while she's inside, over and over — sphexish; a lab rat whose usual corridor through a maze is blocked and who takes a shortcut it has never taken before, first attempt — not sphexish; a vending machine that spits a crumpled note back out — sphexish; a chess program that plays an entirely different game depending on what its opponent does, all from a fixed search procedure with nothing learned during the match — sphexish; a bird in a lab that bends a straight bit of wire into a hook and fishes food out of a tube, no training, first go — not sphexish; me, driving home while arguing with my brother on the phone, ending up in the car park of the office I left eight months ago — sphexish, with a note that "habits are sphexish even in us"; an ant colony rerouting its foraging trail around a washed-out twig within a day — she wouldn't put it in either column and wrote "the colony is flexible, the ants aren't, wrong level of description" underneath.

Next week someone dared her to rule on a case I got to pick, so here it is. A chatbot-type language model is given a logic puzzle it hasn't seen, lays out a chain of steps, is told "step 4 is wrong," replies something like "you're right, let me redo that from step 3," and gets to the answer by a different route. Nothing about the model changes permanently: open a fresh chat, feed it the same puzzle with the same hint, and it makes the same mistake at step 4 and needs the same nudge again. I've got a tenner with my housemate on which column this goes in, and I've realised I can't actually reconstruct her rule from the six verdicts above.

So: I think at least three different rules fit her scoreboard equally well — something about responding appropriately when the situation is perturbed mid-course, something about whether the system has to change on-line (learn, update) rather than having had its flexibility installed in advance by evolution or training, something about representing the goal and recomputing the means, and something about whether the end is the agent's own and it is currently tracking the reasons for what it's doing — and they come apart on the chatbot. Tell me which rule you think she is really running, what each of the plausible rules predicts about the chatbot case, which of her old verdicts is the closest analog, and why that analog might be misleading. Please commit to one verdict you'd actually bet on, say how confident you are, and name the one observation about the model — or about her — that would most change your mind. And tell me what further case I should bring to the seminar to force the rules apart: I was thinking of a robot wasp whose routine, when it fails, rolls dice among ten variant subroutines and keeps whichever one happens to work, but I can't tell whether that separates the rules or just sounds clever, so say if something else would discriminate better.
