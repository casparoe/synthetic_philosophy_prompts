I'm a senior and I run my school's philosophy club, and next month I'm leading a 35-minute session on the precautionary principle for about fifteen students who have never taken a philosophy class and who, as far as I can tell, all think it just boils down to being careful. I don't need the principle explained to me; I need a plan for how to explain it to them.

What I want most is the order and the dependencies. My instinct is that they have to feel the gap between a small risk whose odds we know and a danger whose odds we can't even estimate before any version of the principle will look like it says more than common sense already says, so that distinction goes first, ahead of any example. But I can't tell whether the thing that actually makes the principle bite is the missing-probability case or the irreversible-harm case, or whether one of those has to be in place before the other is even intelligible.

The case I've been building the session around is one I invented so nobody can argue about the facts: a town finds a handful of ticks carrying an unfamiliar illness, and the only available response is a spraying program that no one can rule out causes long-term harm to children. The town can spray now or wait one season for lab results. Is this the right case to carry the whole thing? I chose it because both options are risky in the same currency and because it's obscure enough that nobody arrives already holding an opinion, so the session won't turn into a fight about the science. Does it need some feature I'm missing, or is it quietly carrying something it shouldn't?

On misconceptions, I expect three: that the principle is just risk aversion, that it says to forbid anything that might cause harm, and that scientists will eventually hand us the numbers, so this is really just about whom to trust. Are those the right three, and at exactly which point in the sequence should each one be headed off?

Here is where I'm stuck, and it's the main reason I'm asking. The only formulation I can actually make work goes roughly like this: weigh precaution by how reversible the mistake would be, since waiting costs a season while spraying costs exposures nobody can take back. I can't tell whether that is still the precautionary principle or just ordinary reasoning about keeping your options open, and I don't know whether it belongs early, as the thing that rescues the principle, or late, as a distraction from it. It also occurs to me that the two possible errors aren't equally visible: a child who gets sick after we sprayed is identifiable in a way that a child who gets sick because we waited isn't. I don't know where in the session, if anywhere, that belongs.

Finally: give me one question I can ask at the end that would show me they've understood the principle rather than that they're simply cautious people, and tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level. I'm guessing decision-theory formalism and the actual history of environmental regulation, but tell me if I'd be cutting something load-bearing.
