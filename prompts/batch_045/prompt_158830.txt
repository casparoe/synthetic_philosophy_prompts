And here is the answer I have to mark (I've left her typos in):

"Veritism says the only thing that is fundamentally epistemically good is having credences close to the truth. Everything else — coherence, fitting your evidence, being open-minded — is good only as a means to that. The accuracy-first program then argues for probabilism as follows. Take some way of measuring how far a set of credences is from the truth, a scoring rule. The dominance argument says: if your credences violate the probability calculus, then there is some alternative set of credences that is more accurate than yours no matter what the world is like — the alternative does better at every possible world. So being incoherent is a sure-fire way of being less accurate than you could have been, and since accuracy is what matters, incoherence is irrational. The expected-accuracy argument is the same point from the inside: relative to your own current credences, your expected inaccuracy is minimised by having credences that are probabilistic, so from where you sit you have most reason to be coherent. Both arguments require that inaccuracy be measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, and that is what pins down the quadratic (Brier) score as the correct measure, so the two arguments stand or fall together with that choice.

The same reasoning tells us how to update. Conditionalization — setting your new credence in H equal to your old credence in H given E — is the update rule that minimises inaccuracy: it is dominant, in the sense that at every possible world an agent who conditionalizes ends up more accurate than one who updates any other way. So the accuracy framework gives us Bayesian updating as well as Bayesian coherence, and it does this without appealing to anything pragmatic like betting, which is why its defenders call it a non-pragmatic vindication.

It also settles the permissivism debate. If accuracy is the only fundamental epistemic value, then two agents with exactly the same evidence cannot both be rational while having different credences in the same proposition: one of them is further from the truth, and being further from the truth is the only way to be epistemically worse off. So uniqueness follows and permissivism is dead.

As for partition-local accuracy: the objector thinks that by refusing to aggregate across subject-matters she escapes the argument for probabilism. She doesn't. For one thing the proposal is self-undermining, because if your credences are probabilistic within each subject-matter then they are automatically probabilistic overall — the probability calculus just is a set of constraints on each assignment, and once each part satisfies them the whole does. And even setting that aside, the dominance argument is a world-by-world argument: the alternative credence function beats yours at every world, so no matter which subject-matter you are being scored on, and no matter what the facts turn out to be, you could have done better. Localism therefore gives the incoherent agent no shelter at all, and the accuracy-first case for probabilism is untouched by it."
