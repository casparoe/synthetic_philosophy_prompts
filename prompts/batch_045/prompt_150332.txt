I'm in the last year of a PhD in Near Eastern archaeology, and next week I'm giving a twenty-minute paper (plus ten for questions) at a joint seminar. The room will be mostly archaeologists who work on the Neolithic in the Levant and Anatolia — people who think in stratigraphy, faunal assemblages, site surveys and radiocarbon ranges, and who get visibly uncomfortable with grand narratives about the course of human history — plus one economic historian who will want a mechanism you could in principle put numbers on, and a political theorist who comes to everything and always ends up asking what the normative word in your title is supposed to mean.

My thesis, in brief, is that the standard framing — the affluent-forager picture on one side, the famous worst-mistake line on the other — is asking the wrong question, because it imagines a chooser standing at the transition. What I argue instead is a ratchet. Move one: what made domestication stick wasn't calories per head but storability — grain can be stockpiled, counted, taxed and raided in a way that meat and tubers mostly can't, and storage is what turns a surplus into something expropriable rather than merely perishable. Move two: once a settlement has a granary it needs a wall, once it has a wall it needs people to watch it, and those people get fed out of the granary, so the arrangement can't be dismantled even if everyone in the village would be better off without it. Move three: what actually got lost at the transition, on my account, isn't leisure hours but exit. A forager can walk away from a would-be big man, and that option is doing a lot of the work in keeping camps level; a household with a ripening field and a stored harvest has vastly more costly exit, and once exit is expensive, hierarchy is cheap. So inequality never needs anyone to prefer it — it needs surplus to become legible and exit to become costly. I call this a legibility ratchet in the paper, but I'm not married to the name.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the argument. Give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from this particular room, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way each of those three constituencies would phrase them — I want the archaeologists' version in their own vocabulary, along the lines of asking where my assemblage-level evidence is, and not a generic philosopher's objection about whether wellbeing is measurable. For each one: how much damage it does to the thesis as I've stated it, a reply I could genuinely deliver in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that reply is soft or rests on something I haven't shown. Then tell me what I should concede in the first two slides so it doesn't get conceded for me during question period, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive at all, and — importantly — the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. Two extra wrinkles: the person who runs the seminar wrote their dissertation on egalitarianism in early farming villages and is attached to the idea that foraging-style leveling carried over for a long time after domestication, so assume at least one objection comes from that direction aimed at move three; and I know somebody will bring up the big monumental ritual sites that seem to predate full-blown agriculture, so tell me where that one hurts and where it doesn't.
