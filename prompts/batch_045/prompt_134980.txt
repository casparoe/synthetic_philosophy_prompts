Our causal-inference reading group — two biostatisticians, two people trained in the graphical/diagrammatic tradition, and a philosopher of science — has spent about a year going in circles on whether Simpson's paradox is a solved problem, and I want a structural diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Please do not give me a literature survey or a list of interpretations; I want to know what kind of standoff this is and what, if anything, would end it.

The positions, as they actually get stated in the room:

(a) Dissolved. The reversal is a symptom of conflating conditioning with intervening. Draw the diagram, classify the third variable as confounder, mediator or collider, and the rule is mechanical — adjust for the first, refuse to adjust for the others — after which the surprise is a fact about us, not about the numbers.

(b) Not dissolved. The diagram never settles the estimand. Marginal or conditional, which target population, which effect measure, individual-level or population-averaged: these are substantive choices, and they are not read off a graph. The paradox is the visible edge of an unresolved question about what a trial result is supposed to be a number *of*.

(c) Nothing to explain. It is a fact about weighted averages; there never was a paradox, only the bad habit of hearing 'the effect' as a definite description.

(d) A referee on our internal write-up proposed what we have started calling the Decision-Relevance Criterion: report the effect at the finest partition whose cells are distinguishable by whoever has to act on the number at the moment of acting, and treat every coarser number as an aggregation of those rather than as a competitor to them.

Three tables are doing all the work in our arguments, and I suspect they are doing different work than we think. All are hypothetical.

Table 1. Two antibiotics for suspected sepsis; severity is recorded at admission, before the choice. Among severe patients: new drug 180/600 dead (30%), standard 70/200 (35%). Among non-severe patients: new drug 15/300 (5%), standard 63/900 (7%). Pooled: new drug 195/800 (24.4%), standard 133/1100 (12.1%). The new drug is better in both strata and much worse overall; 75% of new-drug patients were severe against 18% of standard-drug patients.

Table 2. A randomised trial, 1:1, 2000 per arm. An immune-response marker is measured on day 7, after treatment, and is caused by the drug (90% positive on drug, 20% on placebo). Mortality: marker-positive, 180/1800 (10%) on drug versus 20/400 (5%) on placebo; marker-negative, 100/200 (50%) versus 720/1600 (45%). Pooled: 280/2000 (14%) versus 740/2000 (37%). The drug looks harmful in both strata and strongly beneficial overall, with randomisation guaranteeing no confounding.

Table 3. A randomised trial, balanced within strata of a pre-treatment biomarker (stratum sizes identical in both arms). Stratum A: 900/1600 dead on treatment versus 480/1600 on control (56.25% versus 30%). Stratum B: 300/2200 versus 110/2200 (13.64% versus 5%). The stratum-specific odds ratios are exactly 3.0 in both strata. Pooled: 1200/3800 (31.58%) versus 590/3800 (15.53%), a marginal odds ratio of about 2.51. The pooled risk difference, 16.05 points, is exactly the case-mix-weighted average of the two stratum risk differences, but the pooled odds ratio is not any average of the stratum odds ratios; and on a difference scale there is no anomaly at all, whereas on the odds scale the two analyses answer different questions.

I have convinced myself of one small thing: if the stratum-specific risks all point the same way and the strata have the same composition in both arms, no sign reversal is possible for any monotone effect measure, because both pooled risks are then averages with common non-negative weights. So a genuine reversal must be bought either with an imbalance in stratum composition or with conditioning on something downstream of treatment — and it disappears entirely if you are willing to work on a collapsible scale.

My hypothesis is that this is where the real content is: the three tables are one arithmetic event, and what differs across them is only the decision problem silently attached to the number — treat this patient, set policy for this population, license a mechanistic claim about a pathway — so the standoff is four groups answering different questions under one name, with the word 'effect' doing the hiding. The counter-hypothesis I cannot shake is that the Decision-Relevance Criterion, which settles the decision question cleanly for Tables 1 and 2, is silent or unstable exactly where Table 3 bites: it tells you which number to hand over, not which number is true, and it inherits whatever arbitrariness lives in the choice of effect scale.

So: is this verbal, substantive, or a nest of different questions? Is there a residual empirical or formal core — a question about what makes an estimand well-posed, or about which variables are legitimate adjusters, or about why the reversal strikes us as paradoxical — that the decision framing leaves untouched? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharp criterion theorem relating reversal to case-mix imbalance and link function, a reporting convention that forces both estimands into every table, a formal separation of individual-level from population-level quantities, a change of question, or nothing at all? If nothing, say why, and say it as a claim about this dispute rather than about philosophical disputes in general.
