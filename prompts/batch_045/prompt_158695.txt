So I've somehow ended up being invited to give one of the keynote-ish talks at a small invite-only workshop next month on historical analogies for transformative AI. About forty people: roughly half ML researchers, the sort who are sympathetic to safety stuff but allergic to anything that sounds like policy entrepreneurship; maybe six or seven tech-policy and economics people who actually model things; two or three early modern historians who work on print culture; and a handful of programme officers from foundations that fund AI policy work. I'm a novelist — I got invited because my last book is set about thirty years after general AI shows up and someone on the organising commitee liked it — and I dont have the vocabulary for this room, which is exactly why I need the prep.

The slot is twenty minutes plus fifteen for questions, and here's the argument as it currently stands. One: people pick analogies for their policy upshot, not for their similarity. The bomb licenses export controls and state monopoly, the Industrial Revolution licenses redistribution and labour-market adjustment, and the printing press usually gets wheeled in as the cheerful add-on about information wanting to be free, which is true and does no work. Two: the actually useful thing in the press case is a lag. When a technology massively cheapens the production of something that used to be scarce *and certified*, the institutions doing the certifying keep operating for decades after they've quietly lost the capacity to certify, and most of the damage lands inside that gap rather than at the moment of arrival — I've been calling it the authority lag, for want of a better name. Three: so applied to AI, the binding constraint over the next decade isn't capability or compute, it's whether the institutions that certify epistemic goods — universities, journals, professional credentials, courts' treatment of evidence, the whole chain that turns a claim into something anyone can cite — still have the capacity they're currently claiming. And the bit I think is genuinely new, and that I'm most nervous about: these institutions can't repair themselves the ordinary way, because the asset and the product are the same thing. A credentialling body can't announce 'our certification is degraded' without destroying the credibility it would need to do the repairing, so the reform either has to be silent or it isn't reform.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view. Give me the seven or eight objections I'm actually going to get from this room, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each group would phrase them — I want the ML people's version and the historians' version separately where they come apart, because I suspect the economists will say something like 'that's just a credence-goods market with adjustment costs, we have models for that, what is the history buying us' while the historians say something like 'the press didn't break the certifiers, states and churches adapted, licensing and censorship held for a couple of centuries, and you're generalising from one case whose causes are still fought over.' For each objection: how much damage it does, what I should concede up front (I'm happy to concede a lot, I don't need to win), the one or two things I have to hold or the talk was pointless, and where my reply is soft — a one- or two-minute answer I could actually say out loud, plus a line on which part of it is bluff. And please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks; my guess is it's either 'what would have had to happen in the 1500s for you to admit the analogy was wrong' or something about why a novelist is standing up there at all, but you tell me.
