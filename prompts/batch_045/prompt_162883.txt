I am a novelist, and in six weeks I am giving the closing keynote at a three-day conference on consciousness and the arts. My title is *What Novelists Know That Introspection Forgets*. I get forty minutes to speak and twenty minutes of questions afterward, with no slides and no one to hide behind. I would like the prep sheet.

The thesis I am defending is illusionism in a specifically literary key: phenomenal character is not a property that introspection discloses, but the residue of an act of composition, and the act is the one I perform at a desk. It has three moving parts.

- The dictation error: we remember introspection as taking dictation from an inner object, when it is in fact drafting — selecting a coarse, contrastive, communicable predicate under pressure of time, memory and imagined audience, then reading the draft back as a discovery.
- Grain-matching: the felt fineness of grain of an experience tracks the grain of the vocabulary currently at hand rather than the grain of the stimulus, so that a subject supplied with three new colour words reports three new regions of phenomenal space.
- The aura argument: my own migraine aura is by any physiological account a low-dimensional, stereotyped, geometrically crude disturbance — an expanding zigzag arc with a blind region trailing behind it — yet I have filled two decades of notebooks about it, and every entry reports a qualitative depth that no description exhausts. A competent stylist can manufacture inexhaustibility at will, including for states known independently to be thin or fictional. So inexhaustibility is evidence about the describing, not about the described.

Here is the argument they will be aiming at:

1. Introspective reports are composed under the constraints of a public vocabulary whose grain is fixed by practical and social need.
2. The reporter then reads the output of that composition as a reading-off of a property present in the experience: the dictation error.
3. If 1 and 2 hold, then the datum that phenomenal realists take as their starting point — introspection's apparent revelation of a determinate, fine-grained, inexhaustible what-it-is-like — has an explanation that never mentions phenomenal properties.
4. Grain-matching and the aura argument show that this explanation is not merely available but independently supported.
5. By inference to the best explanation, there are no phenomenal properties; there is only the seeming, and it is produced.

The room holds about two hundred people, but the questions will come from four groups.

First and largest: analytic philosophers of mind, nearly all of them phenomenal realists. One or two run the knowledge argument as their home defence and think the ability-hypothesis reply fails; two are Russellian monists of different stripes, convinced that the intrinsic-nature move is precisely what my premises should push me toward; one holds that illusionism is verificationism with better press and will enjoy saying so. What they care about is whether I have changed the subject — description, reportability and acquaintance are three different things, and they will want to know which one my premises are actually about.

Second: six or eight vision scientists and psychophysicists. They will want operationalisation, controls and effect sizes, and they will note that my evidence is a lifetime of notebooks. The friendlier ones work on change blindness, inattentional blindness and postdictive report, and are sympathetic to richness illusions; but they will insist that their findings concern scene representation, not phenomenal consciousness, and that I am over-extrapolating from a paradigm.

Third: the literary scholars and creative-writing faculty who invited me. One is sure that moving from texts to metaphysics is a category error. Another believes the entire enterprise of literary interiority depends on there being something inner for prose to be faithful to, and will hear my talk as an act of bad faith toward my own characters.

Fourth: a small medical-humanities contingent drawn by the migraine material, at least one of whom will say that pain and aura are exactly the cases where patients insist their reports are dictated rather than composed.

What I want from you is this:

- Eight to ten objections, in the order I will actually get them, each phrased the way that group would phrase it. Give me two orderings — how likely each is and how much damage it does — and tell me where they come apart.
- For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft.
- What I should concede in the first five minutes, before anyone asks, and which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the hour.
- The weakest step in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the form the attack on it will take.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks. My hunch is that the worst question will come from the literary side rather than the philosophy side; tell me whether you think that is self-flattering.

One constraint on the replies. I am a novelist: I can hold a metaphor under pressure, but I will lose any exchange conducted in the vocabulary of properties, acquaintance, constitution, grounding and inference to the best explanation. Where an objection turns on a distinction I would have to deploy correctly in front of two hundred people, say so plainly and give me the sentence to say instead — the one that buys me the distinction without claiming it.
