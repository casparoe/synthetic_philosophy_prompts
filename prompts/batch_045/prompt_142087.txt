I have been stuck for weeks on an argument a colleague sketched after a talk, and I can no longer tell whether it is a reductio of cluelessness or a reductio of nothing. He writes it out like this.

(1) A maximizing consequentialist ranks acts by expected value; where an act's expected value is not even approximately determinate, the theory returns no verdict, and there is nothing else in the theory to rank them by.
(2) For any act of mine, the value of its effects over the next few years is roughly estimable, but the value of its effects over the centuries ahead is not: the sign of the difference my act makes to the far future looks as likely negative as positive, and the spread of the plausible magnitudes dwarfs any near-term term.
(3) The far future can contain vastly more of what matters than the next few years — vastly more lives, vastly more suffering, vastly more of everything the theory counts. So the far-future term is no rounding error: if it is nonzero at all, it dominates the near-term term.
(4) Hence for any two acts whose near-term values differ modestly — the career, the donation, the vote — consequentialism returns no verdict. Call this cluelessness.
(5) But the "near term" in (2) is arbitrary, and cluelessness is contagious backwards, as he calls it: whatever difference my act makes over the next five years is itself a node whose downstream consequences over the following fifty are equally intractable, and those fifty over five hundred, and so on. There is no cutoff at which the chain stops generating cluelessness, and intractability does not accumulate gradually — one unrankable term in the sum is enough to return no verdict.
(6) So the argument does not stop at long-term acts: consequentialism returns no verdict on any pair of acts at any temporal scale, including the near-term comparisons (2) conceded were fine.
(7) And whether to accept (1)–(6) is itself a choice with centuries-scale effects, so by its own lights the argument cannot recommend believing it, acting on it, or even refraining.

The conclusion is unacceptable and (7) suggests the argument eats itself rather than the view, but every step looks fine in isolation. I have a candidate diagnosis that I cannot make precise: (3) may be sliding from how much value the far future contains to how much difference my act makes to it, since the difference an act makes may decay and diffuse as the causal chain lengthens even while the total value in play grows. If that is the crack, I want to know what it costs me — in particular whether cluelessness then survives for the cases that motivated it (existential-risk mitigation, institutional design, any act aimed at centuries-out outcomes) or whether it evaporates there too. If the crack is somewhere else, I would rather give up (5) than (2). Where does this go wrong, and what does the least costly rejection commit me to?
