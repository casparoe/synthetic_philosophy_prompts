Long one, sorry. I'm a few weeks into a feature on why some countries end up rich and others don't, and I've worked myself into a position I can't tell is one position or five positions duct-taped together. I cover science and ideas, so I'm fine with methods talk, but I'm not an economist. I'm not looking for citations or paper names, I've read plenty of them — I want to know whether the thing I actually believe hangs together.

Heres roughly where I've landed:

1. The institutionalist story is basically right. The big driver is whether a country's rules are inclusive or extractive, and the arrow runs from institutions to prosperity, not the other way round.

2. Geography matters hardly at all directly. I dont buy latitude, tropics or disease ecology as direct causes in a world with trade and modern medicine. At most geography shaped which institutions got set up back when it mattered.

3. What actually convinces me is the quasi-experimental stuff: the historical cases where history handed out institutions more or less by accident. The settler-mortality instrument, the division of Korea, Botswana as the odd one out. Those are the ones where I can see an identification story rather than a narrative.

4. Culture explanations I want nothing to do with. Unfalsifiable, and mostly a polite way of blaming poor countries for being poor. Where culture tracks institutions, I think it's downstream.

5. I also dont think the micro stuff — RCTs on deworming or microlending or whatever — tells us anything about national wealth. Different question, different scale. Part of why I like the historical cases is that theyre at the right scale.

6. I've been using a rule of thumb I made up, which I've started calling the one-rerun rule: I'll only take a causal claim about national prosperity seriously if I can point to a case where the alleged cause changed while everything else stayed roughly put, AND I can say what I'd have expected to observe if the cause had been absent. I like it because when you only get one run of history it's the only thing I've found that separates a causal claim from a just-so story. It's also what finished off culture for me — I could never name the rerun.

7. But I still want a payoff that isn't just "history is complicated." I want to be able to say something like: don't try to engineer growth, but do clear the worst institutional blockages, and when a reform window opens, what you put through it matters. My editor wants a takeaway and I'd like to give her one without lying.

So: does this hold up? The worry I keep circling is number 5 — a friend said if I'm going to demand identification I can't then throw out the RCTs, and I dont think I have a good answer. I also have a vague sense that 2 and 3 are doing something circular, because the settler-mortality thing seems to need geography to have had no direct effects after colonisation either, which is my contested claim and not something the instrument independently shows me. And 4 plus 7 feel like they might not fit, but I can't put my finger on why. I genuinely can't tell which of these are real problems and which are just me being nervous the week before I file.

Can you audit it: which pairs actually conflict, which only look like they do, and rank the real ones by how bad they are. For each one, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me.
