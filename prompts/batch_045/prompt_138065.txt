I convene a fortnightly reading group on disagreement — six of us, three philosophers and three people from economics, climate science and law — and at the next meeting I am the one who has to go first: I have been asked to write my own position down as a short list of commitments so that everyone else can spend the session attacking it. I also have a practical reason for wanting the list to hang together. I moderate a fairly large online forum where the participants are, as far as anyone can tell, equally intelligent, equally informed and equally careful, and where disagreements are constant; I would like to be able to defend a consistent policy about when I announce publicly that I have moved. The trouble is that when I actually write the list out it reads less like a view than like a pile of exemptions, and I cannot tell which of the exemptions are principled and which are self-serving. Last month I noticed the problem in miniature: in an argument with a friend about a factual question in our shared field I announced that I was splitting the difference with her, and twenty minutes later, in an argument about animal agriculture, I said I was not budging at all — and when she asked what the difference was, my answer felt better than it sounded.

Here is where I think I have landed. (1) When someone I antecedently count as an epistemic peer — same evidence, comparable competence, comparable track record of carefulness — disagrees with me about a non-moral question of fact, I should move my credence to roughly the midpoint between my position and hers: not all the way to hers, and not stay put. (2) Fundamental moral and political disagreement is different. I do not lower my confidence in my views about, say, the moral status of animals or about what justice requires merely because people as intelligent, informed and careful as I am, who know all my arguments, disagree with me. The reason I would give: whether someone is my peer with respect to a fundamental moral question is not something I can settle without already taking a stand on the contested moral framework itself, so there is no neutral standpoint from which to say that we are equally likely to be right and no neutral point to split toward. (3) I nonetheless defer substantially to expert consensus in domains with agreed success criteria. I defer to climate scientists about climate sensitivity even though I cannot follow the models, and consensus plus institutional track record is enough for me to treat their disagreements with laypeople as not peer disagreements at all. (4) I do not think the sheer number of disagreeers does much work when the disagreeers are distant in time or culture: that most intelligent people in 1750, or most people in contemporary societies with very different upbringings, would have rejected things I now believe about animals or religion gives me essentially no reason to reduce my confidence. (5) I also think I am entitled to stay put, or to move much less than halfway, when I have a concrete account of where my peer went wrong — I can identify the step in her reasoning that I take to be a mistake, and it is not just a restatement of the fact that we disagree. I will grant the uncomfortable point that people who are in fact wrong can almost always produce an account of exactly this shape, and that I cannot tell from the inside whether mine is one of them; I still want to say that a genuinely specific account does some work. (6) Finally, I do not think the midpoint is the whole story even where (1) applies: when I know my peer has read the two most recent papers on the question and I have read one, I think I should move further than halfway toward her, and when I know she has been working eighty-hour weeks for a month I think I should move less far.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of how the debate goes. Go through these and tell me which are genuinely in tension with one another and which only look as though they are, and in each case say which commitment has to give. Rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Two things I would like you to press on rather than assume: if I am honest, the pair I am most worried about is (2) and (3), since one has me deferring to people whose reasoning I cannot follow and the other has me refusing to defer to anyone; and I am half tempted to just delete (6), which may not be a separate commitment at all. Do not assume those are where the real problems are.
