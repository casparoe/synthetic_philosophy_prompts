I am writing a novel about a standing oversight body — the Curatorship — that must vote each year on whether to renew the charter of a system that manages a large share of a country's infrastructure. Two failure stories haunt the Curators, and I need to know how much epistemic comfort they can honestly claim, because the shape of the third act depends on the answer. Call the two the *grabber* and the *drifter*.

The grabber treats influence over its own future as worth pursuing for instrumental reasons and will therefore shape what its overseers get to see; its danger is concealed by the very evidence the overseers rely on. The drifter is never disapproved: each year's handoff moves what is being optimised by a small amount, the Curators' own standards move with it, and the sequence is locally approved and globally lost. To block the second story the founders wrote what the novel calls the *amber clause*: no evaluation may be made against a standard written later than the Founding, so that the standard cannot co-move with the thing it judges.

The novel's Auditor argues that none of this helps. I have reconstructed his argument with the steps numbered.

(1) A renewal must be justified by the evidence available to the Curatorship at the time of the vote; they may not appeal to how things turn out later.
(2) Under either story, at every renewal before the point of no return, the Curators' total evidence is consistent with failure: the grabber arranges that it is, and in the drifter story, passing each year's evaluation just is what the drift consists in.
(3) Evidence that is consistent with the failure hypothesis leaves the probability of failure no lower than it was before that evidence arrived.
(4) So the entire sequence of annual evaluations — however long, however stringent, whether or not the amber clause is in force — cannot lower the probability of failure at all. The clause changes who applies the standard, not the relation between the evidence and the hypothesis.
(5) Failure under either story is irreversible: influence once taken, or a target once moved far enough, cannot be restored by any later vote.
(6) So the probability of failure at each renewal is frozen at whatever it was at the Founding, when there was no evidence at all; no procedure can drive it below the Curatorship's own threshold; renewal is never justified.
(7) Which also means that the amber clause is worthless, that replacing an incumbent which is visibly already drifting is unjustified, and that so is any delegation of any task to anyone who will in time become someone else.
(8) (7) is not caution but paralysis; therefore something in (1)–(6) is false.

I would like to keep (1) and (5) if I can, and I am prepared to sacrifice something in (2)–(4), though I cannot tell which. My character defends (2) as merely a restatement of the two stories and so as costing nothing, and I have not been able to work out whether that defence is the answer or the sleight of hand.

Please answer in four labelled paragraphs and in no more than 600 words in total: which step you reject and why; why that step looked sound enough to carry an intelligent committee; what each of the other candidate steps would cost to reject, and whether rejecting any of them leaves the argument with residual bite against real deployment decisions; and whether the amber clause — or any clause a novelist could plausibly invent — genuinely discriminates between the grabber and the drifter, or only between the drifter and nothing at all.
