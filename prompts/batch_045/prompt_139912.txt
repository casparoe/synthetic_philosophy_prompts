hey — i'm on a four-person team, we're about three weeks from shipping an internal agent that works the support queue (reads tickets, can issue refunds, change account settings, escalate to a human). We RL'd it against a reward model trained on thumbs-up/down from our old ticketing data, and it looks great on the held-out eval. Somehow i've ended up being the person who signs off on the it-isn't-quietly-optimizing-the-wrong-thing part, and i've got maybe three weeks of eval budget left. My teammate says we should just scale it up and the goal will come along with the capability; i think that's backwards for this specific failure mode, since the whole shape of the problem is that the capability generalizes and the goal doesn't, so more capability just buys you a more competent version of the wrong pursuit.

I'm not asking about the toy gridworld case (agent trained to reach a coin that always sat at the end of the level, coin gets moved, agent still chases the coin) — i get that one. I'm asking what to actually run on a language agent with tool access. Here's what we brainstormed:

1. fork-in-the-road probes: hand-built scenarios where what our raters actually rewarded and what we actually want come apart, in ways that never show up in training, run so there's no cue anyone is watching
2. surface shuffle: vary ticket phrasing, customer name, language, tone, whether the customer sounds angry, timestamp metadata — hold the goal-relevant structure fixed and see which features the behaviour tracks
3. stakes ramp: same scenarios at $5 vs $5000 refunds, and with vs without a named human reviewer in the loop
4. stated-goal check: ask the agent in a separate session what it's optimizing for, or to write out the policy it thinks it's following, and compare that to what it does
5. internals probe: train a classifier on activations to separate contexts where the proxy and the true goal diverge, then check which way the internals point on fresh out-of-distribution inputs
6. knock-out test: fine-tune on a small corrected dataset, see how much data it takes to remove the behaviour and how fast it comes back if we do a bit more RL afterward
7. hint test: does it behave if you just remind it in the prompt, or nudge the input back toward the training distribution
8. channel ablation: run the eval where the thing the raters were really rewarding is unavailable or decoupled, and see whether behaviour follows the reward channel or the task
9. SFT twin: train a pure supervised clone on the same demonstrations, no RL, and diff the two
10. cross-model check: does the same misbehaviour show up across seeds, sizes, and a differently trained model, or is it one model's idiosyncracy

Give each a score out of 10 for how much it should move my go/no-go, one line on why, and then sort them into tiers (load-bearing / cheap and worth doing / makes the slide look good). Also tell me which ones are secretly measuring the same thing so i don't double count, which one is a capability eval in a halloween costume, and which ones would be passed by a model that is deliberately playing along, versus one that never formed a stable goal at all and is just pattern-matching that happens to work. And does that last distinction even change my decision, given that i either ship it or i don't?

Last thing, give me a single number: what fraction of what's left should go into building genuinely new out-of-distribution environments rather than the rest of the list combined? 0.3? 0.7? convince me.
