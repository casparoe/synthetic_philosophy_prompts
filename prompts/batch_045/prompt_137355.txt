Setting up a reading group on group minds and I want the spec before I let anyone near the views. Take the Ant Fugue style case: a colony, call it Aunt Hillary, where the pitch is that the ants stand to the colony roughly as neurons stand to a brain, the pheromone trails and antennal contacts are the signals, and so the colony itself is the thing that has the mind. I dont care yet whether that is true. What I want is the standard of proof: what would a theory of colony-level mentality have to deliver before I am entitled to say the colony believes the good nest site is to the north *literally*, rather than as a convenient compression of a few hundred ants are laying trails northward?

Fixing the purpose so adequacy is relative to something: the theory has to be good enough (a) to settle whether colony-level state attributions are explanations or just a handy façon de parler, and (b) to be portable — I want to run whatever test it proposes on other candidate group minds, e.g. a firm with written decision procedures, a server farm of fine-tuned models routing queries to each other, a slime mould spreading over a nutrient map. If a criterion only works for things made of insects, say so and say whether that is a point in its favour.

Write me the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it. Things I already think belong on the list, which you should re-cut, merge or throw out, plus whatever I am missing:

- House-hunting: scouts visit nest sites and recruit others, and the colony ends up moving to the better one even though no single ant has compared the two. So colony-level states have to be able to carry information no individual member has.
- The colony can be wrong: a trail to a food source that has since been eaten away persists, and foragers keep following it. So the theory cannot just be tracking reliable covariance or information transfer; it has to make room for misrepresentation.
- Fission: split the colony down the middle, both halves survive and go on foraging. Which one is Aunt Hillary, which one has the memories, and does the answer depend on organisation or on which ants you happen to have scooped up?
- The swap case I made up: take two nests with different trail networks and swap the entire workforces between them overnight. Does the mind follow the ants or the nest? Whatever the theory says, it should say it for a reason and not by stipulation.
- No double counting: the colony's belief and the chemistry of ten thousand ants cannot both be the whole cause of the move. Say how the colony-level cause relates to the ant-level one.
- The redundancy worry: if every attribution of a colony belief has an equally good ant-by-ant redescription that predicts the same behaviour and supports the same counterfactuals, is the belief real, or is it a compression of our ignorance? I want this turned into a testable requirement rather than a shrug.
- Gradual replacement: swap ants one at a time for tiny robots that follow the same signalling rules. If mentality survives the slow swap, does it survive the fast one and the all-robot endpoint? If it doesn't, where exactly is the cut-off — and a two-ant colony either has a mind or it doesn't, so be ready for the sorites.
- The slave-making colonies: some species run their nest with workers of a different species that they raid as pupae. If the colony's states are carried by aliens, genetic relatedness and shared selection can't be what makes the colony a subject. Is that a genuine requirement or a prejudice I am smuggling in?
- Memory without a rememberer: no individual ant lasts long, so whatever persists is in trails, nest architecture and the distribution of the workers. Is requiring a persisting remembering subject a real desideratum or just individualism in disguise?
- Too many minds: any overlapping subset of a colony is a functioning system of some sort. The theory has to stop the count from exploding without drawing an arbitrary boundary.

Also decide, and argue, whether the list is a list for intentionality only — beliefs, desires, responsiveness to reasons — or whether phenomenal consciousness belongs on it too, since I suspect demanding consciousness is one camp's prejudice rather than a requirement, but I might be wrong about which camp.

Then: mark each requirement non-negotiable or negotiable, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — my guess is that determinate belief attribution and no sharp threshold in ant number are already in trouble together, and that mentality being intrinsic to the colony while also being checkable by us without a translation manual for the pheromones might be the other one, but you find the real collisions. Say which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidates, and which one does almost none.

Very short last section, half a page at most: score four or five stock positions against the list — as-if or intentional-stance, realiser functionalism about colony states, the superorganism or unit-of-selection view, plain eliminativism about anything above the ants — but keep the weight on the criteria, since that's where I think the actual thinking is.
