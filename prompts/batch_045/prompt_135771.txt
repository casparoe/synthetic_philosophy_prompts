I want you to do a piece of applied exegesis: reconstruct how Wittgenstein, working from the remarks eventually published as *On Certainty*, would have responded to a phenomenon that could not have arisen in the form I am about to describe, and be explicit about where the reconstruction stops being supported by the text.

Call the phenomenon hinge laundering. An institution — a land registry, a statistical agency, a liturgical calendar, the maintainers of a widely used database or classification scheme — introduces a claim into its routine output not as something anyone asserts or argues for, but as background: a cutoff date, a category boundary, a transliteration convention, a default value. Nobody holds it in the sense of having weighed and accepted it; it is simply what the forms, the tables, and the training of new staff presuppose. Two generations later it has become basic for a large and varied set of practices. It is what disputes around the institution presuppose rather than what they are about, and anyone who called it into question would be treated not as mistaken but as failing to grasp what the institution is doing. From surviving paperwork it then emerges that the claim entered the practice through a documented accident — a mistranslation, an arbitrary decision by one clerk, an unrechecked miscalculation — and that, had the accident not occurred, some different and equally arbitrary claim would now occupy the same place. Assume throughout that nothing has broken: coordination around the claim still succeeds, the practice is in good working order, and the discovery concerns provenance rather than truth.

Please address the following.

1. Draw, as carefully as you can, the distinction a Wittgensteinian needs between an ordinary empirical proposition and a hinge or world-picture proposition, and say whether the laundered claim is one or the other — both before the paperwork surfaces and after. Be explicit that the discovery does not show the claim to be false.

2. Provenance. Hinges are, on his account, themselves unfounded: they are not held on the strength of reasons and are not the sort of thing that could be. If that were the whole story, learning that a hinge originated in a clerical error would be a non-event. Is that where he would land, or does anything in the material on inherited world-pictures, on what is passed on in schooling and upbringing, or on the difference between the hard rock and the shifting sand of the riverbed give him resources for treating accidentally acquired hinges differently from ones with a long history? Which way would you expect him to go, and what in the text pushes each way?

3. The reformers. Suppose a movement forms to correct the claim, publicize the paperwork, and hold an inquiry. Given what he says about argument, persuasion, and the way a world-picture changes, would he expect publicizing the accident to accomplish anything? If not, what sort of thing would he expect to move a community's hinges, and would he regard that as something one can plan?

4. Where the reformers stand. Identify the hinges that the demand that arbitrary origins discredit a practice, or that a documented error is a reason to stop, itself rests on — and say whether he would treat that demand as exempt from the treatment he gives everyone else. Compare the case with G. E. Moore's gesture of holding up his hands as a proof of an external world.

5. Limits. Mark which parts of your answer *On Certainty* genuinely supports, which require going beyond it, and where the text is silent or pulls in two directions. If you think he would decline the question altogether, on the grounds that whether to keep or discard the claim is not a philosophical question, say so and explain why that refusal is the most faithful answer available.

Do not summarize *On Certainty* as a whole; go straight to the case. Keep the whole reply under 700 words.
