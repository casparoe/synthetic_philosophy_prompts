You are a decision theorist who works at the boundary of social choice and welfare economics. Please think carefully, and before your main answer spend a few lines separating the distinct questions that get bundled together under the phrase "interpersonal utility comparison," and say which of them the setup below actually settles.

Context: I help allocate a small grant fund across projects whose beneficiaries are different people, and we wanted our comparisons to be respectable rather than vibes. Expected utility theory, as I understand it, pins down each person's utility function only up to a positive affine transformation, so a gain for one person looked incommensurable with a gain for another. Our fix was to renormalize everybody onto a single scale: we use the same two anchors for every beneficiary, 0 at a stipulated reference life (income at 60% of the national median, in full health) and 1 at another (income at twice the median, in full health), on the assumption that the distance between those two anchors is the same size for everyone. That gives each person a comparable number; where outcomes are uncertain we then convert to willingness-to-pay so everything sits in one currency.

What I want resolved: (1) Philosophers still write as if interpersonal comparison were an open problem after this normalization, but the scale looks to me fully determinate now — what residual problem are they pointing at? (2) Our ranking is dominated by a handful of projects that help small numbers of very intense beneficiaries. I want to cap sensitivity to intensity, but I can't find a principled place to put the cap. Where should it go, and how do I defend it in the note we publish alongside the allocation?
