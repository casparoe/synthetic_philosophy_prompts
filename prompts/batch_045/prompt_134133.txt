so I'm in this little forecasting meetup — ten of us, fake money, mostly long-run political questions. Right now we've got an open market on whether the EU still exists in something like its current form in 2100 (say: same broad treaty setup, at least twenty members, still making law that binds them). People are miles apart, and one guy — call him M. — keeps ending the argument with the same move: he says reading the news is cargo-culting, that the outside view is just an actuarial table, that an insurer doesn't ask whether *this* house burns, it asks what fraction of houses burn, so we should pick the reference class (he wants "large multi-ethnic polities," basically empires), read off how long those tend to last, and stop pretending the details matter. Don't worry about whatever number he's using, the number is meaningless.

What I can't tell is how much work the insurance picture is doing here. So: audit it. Give me the correspondence table — the insured, the pool, the table, the underwriter, the premium, the medical exam — and then where the mapping breaks. The bits I keep circling:

insurers get thousands of near-independent cases out of a process that barely changes; we've got maybe two or three dozen polities ever, and they're not independent of each other — they conquer each other, copy each other, and sometimes fall *because* a neighbour fell. Does the analogy survive without independence, or does that sink the read-off-the-table move?

the cases we forecast aren't a random draw from the class either. Someone put this question on the board because something looked shaky, and the EU is a weird animal — no army of its own, members can walk out, it's mostly a legal order. In insurance that's adverse selection, and insurers answer it by underwriting harder, i.e. by looking at the specifics more carefully, not by ignoring them. Am I right that the analogy argues against M. on this point specifically?

and the forecast is public. The people inside the case can read it, and if elites know the base rate they can react to it — reform early, or panic and overcorrect and trigger the thing they feared — so the rate isn't stable under being known. Insurance has no version of that, or does it?

Then the class question: is "empires" even right, or should it be federations, customs unions, international organisations? Because it feels like the EU answer just *is* whichever of those you pick. I've been using "narrowest class whose rate is still stable enough to be worth quoting" as the tiebreaker, but that feels like it pushes the problem back one step.

Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion M. wants — details are noise, quote the base rate — and which is pedantry? Does the conclusion survive in any form?
