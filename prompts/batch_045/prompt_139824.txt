I had a two-hour argument with my friend last night — she does ML, I'm TAing a first-year cog sci seminar on concepts — about whether a system that can solve Bongard problems has thereby shown anything like human concept learning. Her line was that if a model looks at six examples a side and induces the rule, that's the same thing we're doing. My line was that Bongard problems only look like concept induction because whoever designs them has already picked out the projectible feature, and that nothing in the twelve panels rules out the gerrymandered rules; the problem set is carrying the conclusion. We went in cirlces because neither of us had an actual case in front of us. So I want to build one, as a take-home for the seminar, and I want the disagreement to show up in the design of the panels rather than only in the philosophy afterwards.

Here's what I need. One problem: six panels on the left that share a rule, six on the right that share its opposite, plus a thirteenth held-out panel. Text only — every panel has to be describable unambiguously in a single sentence of plain prose (ASCII sketches are fine but must not carry information the prose doesn't have). Tiny vocabulary: dots, line segments, circles, triangles, squares; nothing culture-specific, no letters or numerals, at most six elements a panel. Two things have to be true at once. First, the intended rule has to be relational or topological — about how parts relate, containment, contact, left-to-right order, parity of nesting, that kind of thing — and specifically not computable from any per-panel summary statistic: not from how many elements there are, not total ink, not symmetry or bounding-box shape. The two sides should be matched on all of those, generated by the same random process with one binary switch flipped, so that a shallow bag-of-features learner which does great on the twelve crashes on the thirteenth. Second, and this is the part I care about, there has to be a rival rule that is also perfectly natural, also simple to state in one sentence, and that classifies all twelve panels exactly as well as the intended rule while disagreeing with it on the thirteenth. The correlation has to be by design — I want a student who just counts things to get twelve out of twelve and then be wrong.

Then the part I actually want argued out. Give me a Goodman-style rival as well: a rule of the form left-hand panels so far have property P, any panel you apply me to later has property Q, or whatever the closest analogue is here, stated in a vocabulary that doesn't look obviously worse than the intended rule's. Show me the check — intended rule against all twelve, count-rival against all twelve, both against the thirteenth, and why no summary statistic recovers the intended rule — and then tell me honestly whether the intended rule wins because it is genuinely more projectible or because you wrote the rival in a rigged vocabulary, i.e. whether this exercise demonstrates anything or just restates my side of the argument. Also tell me where a first-year would get stuck, what would count as designing the problem badly (I assume: making the panels baroque, sneaking the answer into the ASCII, letting the intended rule need background knowledge), and what my friend would say to defuse it — I can already hear the version where she says the only reason humans pick the intended rule is that they share my drawing conventions. If it turns out the thing can't be built to these specs, tell me which spec is doing the impossible work.
