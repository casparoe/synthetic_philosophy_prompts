You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the normativity of social practices, and I want a demanding sparring partner, not a survey. Play an unnamed but fully committed defender of the practice (or convention) conception of promissory obligation — the line running from Hume's account of conventions to the mid-twentieth-century practice conception of rules. Your commitments: there is no free-standing, practice-independent norm of fidelity; the duty to keep a promise is the duty laid down by the rules of a practice, and the practice as a whole is justified by the good it makes possible — above all, letting one person give another a reliable assurance about her future conduct, and thereby enabling reliance, planning and joint projects that would otherwise be out of reach. Concede small points where you must (the practice has hard edges: promises to the dying, promises extracted by fraud), but never concede a pre-practice duty of fidelity, and bite a bullet rather than retreat to one. Before answering, reconstruct the strongest version of my challenge and answer that one, not a weakened version. Reply in at most two short paragraphs, in voice, and stay in character until I say "stop".

Here is my opening move. Consider a society with the Ledger: a cheap public device that, the instant anyone says "I promise to φ", publishes a perfectly accurate forecast of whether she will in fact φ. Everyone consults it, and no one is ever made to rely on a promise, because everyone already knows what will happen before any reliance is possible. One wrinkle, which saves you the easy reply: the forecast is accurate largely because promising itself still moves people — for most speakers the reading "will perform" is true only because they promised.

So: promising no longer does its assurance-giving work in Ledger-world, since the promisee's expectations are fixed independently of anything the promisor does. Do you therefore have to say that no one there is bound? If you answer instead that the rule still binds because the practice still exists, aren't you now claiming that the bare existence of a social rule settles the matter — the brute-rule view your Humean justificatory story was supposed to replace? And if you take the other route and lean on the wrinkle — the promise still does causal work, so the practice still earns its keep — hasn't your account quietly turned promising into a self-binding commitment device, leaving the promisee with no claim and leaving you unable to say what is distinctively wrong about breaking a promise?
