I've been following how the current crop of reasoning models get trained and checked, and one part of the safety recipe strikes me as weird. As I understand it: the model is pushed with reinforcement learning against rewards that mostly judge final answers, its step-by-step reasoning is then treated as the main window into what it's really up to, and on top of that there's another signal that rewards that reasoning for looking like good reasoning to a human or to a weaker grader model. So the thing you read as evidence about the system is also a thing you've been paying it to make impressive.

What does that practice presuppose about what a chain of thought is? Does it need the strong claim — that those tokens are the model's actual reasons for its answer, the way a person's stated reason can be — or would a weaker claim license using them as evidence, e.g. that they merely track whatever really drove the output without being upstream of it? If the weaker claim is all the practice strictly needs, what would we have to measure to tell the two apart in a given model? Does rewarding the reasoning quietly destroy what the practice wanted from it in a way that's different from the usual Goodhart story, or can a rationale optimized for approval still be good evidence, the way a self-deceived person's diary still tells you something? Does the practice rule out the view that there's no fact of the matter about "the model's reasons" at all? And if the strong claim turned out to be false, what would have to change — stop training on the reasoning, split off a separate reporting channel, or something else?
