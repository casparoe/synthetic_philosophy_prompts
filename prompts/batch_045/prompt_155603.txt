I'm a final-year undergraduate in a seminar on the philosophy of mind, and the paper I'm writing defends a claim about pain that I would like stress-tested before I commit another three weeks to it. The claim is that what makes a pain bad for the person who has it is entirely a matter of the pain's felt unpleasantness, its hedonic character, and not at all a matter of its sensory-discriminative profile, its location, intensity, and sensory quality, nor of what it moves the subject to do or attend to. Two stipulations: the target is prudential badness for the subject, not moral badness or badness simpliciter; and unpleasantness is for me a qualitative feature of the experience, not something to be identified with a desire that the pain stop, with an evaluative judgment, or with any motivational or attentional effect.

My main evidence is a clinical phenomenon our seminar touched on only briefly. Some patients, typically after damage to the insula or after cingulotomy, and sometimes under morphine analgesia, come apart in a specific way: they locate a stimulus correctly, rate its intensity accurately, and describe its sensory quality in the vocabulary ordinary people use, but they report that the pain does not bother them. They may smile or keep talking during stimulation, and they show none of the usual urgency about getting it to stop. I read this as a dissociation in which the hedonic dimension drops out while the sensory dimension is preserved, and I argue that what has gone missing in these patients is precisely the badness of the pain, which is why asymbolia supports my view against the rival views that identify pain's essence with an imperative to protect the body, with an evaluation of one's bodily condition as bad, or with a desire to be rid of the experience.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure, and I want it ranked rather than listed.

First, the defeaters I should concede rather than the talking points of people who already reject my framework. Which considerations would a defender of my own view have to treat as counting against it, as opposed to considerations I can wave off as resting on a rival theory of pain I do not accept? Please separate these clearly, and say for each whether it would be fatal, serious but survivable at the cost of a revision, or merely awkward.

Second, a problem I can see coming and cannot solve. If I define unpleasantness as whatever property makes an experience bad for its subject, my thesis becomes analytic and predicts nothing. If I define it as a distinctive felt quality, I owe an account of what that quality is, and I am exposed to the worry that a quality could be present without registering as bad, or that its connection to badness is brute. Tell me which horn I should take, what each horn costs me, and whether there is a third option I have missed.

Third, two hypothetical dissociations that I invented to probe the view, neither of which I take to be an actual reported syndrome, so please treat them as stipulations and do not go looking for them.

The first I call demand analgesia. Subjects whose pain is unchanged along the sensory dimension and, by their own report, unchanged along the hedonic dimension: they still call the pain unpleasant, they give the same ratings on unpleasantness scales, and they withdraw when asked to. But the pain no longer interrupts anything. They finish the sentence they were speaking, they do not yank the hand away unbidden, and afterwards they deny that anything had to stop. On my view, I take it, their pain is exactly as bad as it was before, and only its practical grip has gone. Is that the right verdict, is the stipulated profile even coherent on my account, and would it count against me, or only against the imperative and desire views?

The second is the mirror image. Subjects who show the entire motivational and attentional signature of severe pain, withdrawal, grimacing, inability to concentrate on anything else, and who nonetheless deny that the pain is unpleasant and insist, when asked carefully, that it is not bad for them. Which should I trust here, the report or the behavior, and does my theory have principled resources for answering, or only the stipulation that first-person authority is basic?

Fourth, empirical and methodological exposure. My case rests on the reports of asymbolic patients being authoritative about their own condition. What would undermine that? I can think of two threats: that asymbolia turns out to involve a global blunting of motivation rather than a selective loss affecting pain, and that the reports are unreliable in a way I have not considered, perhaps because patients are applying a learned label rather than describing anything they feel. Say how much each would cost me, and whether there are further threats of the same shape.

Fifth, overgeneralization. If unpleasantness is the bad-maker, then nausea, itch, grief, shame, and dread are bad in exactly the way pain is, and a pain that the subject wants to keep, the burn a runner is glad to feel or a grief one would not give up, is still bad for the subject while wanted. Are these genuine liabilities, or are they consequences I should simply accept? Rank them.

Finally, three things I would find most useful. Tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not, especially about cases where the dimensions come apart, about pain nobody notices, and about subjects with no concept of unpleasantness. Tell me honestly whether my two stipulations have insulated the thesis into unfalsifiability, and if they have, whether that is a problem in a domain where the rival views may be equally insulated. And tell me whether the case I would personally find most persuasive is actually available: a case in which the unpleasantness is fully present and the pain is nonetheless not bad for the subject, or a case that forces a choice between trusting the report and trusting the behavior. If no such case can be constructed, I would rather know that now.
