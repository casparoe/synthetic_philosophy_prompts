I work as the planning officer for a small internal drainage board in eastern England. Our job is to keep water off low-lying farmland and the villages on it: about forty miles of raised embankment, a dozen pumping stations, and several hundred culverts and sluices. Once a year we put forward a shortlist of the schemes we want funded — rebanking this reach, replacing that pump — and every scheme has to be justified with a benefit–cost ratio: how likely the asset is to fail over some period, multiplied by what the flooding would cost, against what the work would cost. The numbers go into a spreadsheet supplied by our funding body, which will not normally pay for anything whose ratio is below one, and the spreadsheet wants a single number for the probability.

The difficulty is that we hardly ever have anything to count. A pumping station of a given age and type failing in a given year is not something anyone here has seen even once, and the few failures we have had turned on causes nobody predicted. For years we filled the box in anyway, because it had to be filled, and the more honest among us put in a round number and shrugged. Recently I started asking our engineers and operators for a range instead: the lowest failure chance they would take seriously over the next ten years, and the highest. Two things have happened since, and I do not know what to make of either.

The first is that the ranges go into the spreadsheet and come out as their midpoints. Someone has to type a number into the box, the midpoint is the only thing that looks defensible to type, and within a week everyone is talking as though the midpoint had been the estimate all along. The range seems to buy us nothing except a few honest conversations in the room where it was written down.

The second is worse, and it is what I most want help with. Sometimes we learn something and the range gets wider rather than narrower. A reach of embankment that the team put at between two and ten per cent chance of breaching in ten years was surveyed; the survey found animal burrows in a place nobody had looked; and the same team, given the survey, widened it to between one and forty per cent — the bottom moved down because the burrows are now a known and fixable thing, and the top moved up because a failure mechanism nobody had considered turned out to be live. My colleagues say this is nonsense: finding things out is supposed to make you less unsure, not more. I think the widening is right, and that a single probability could never have recorded it. But I cannot say what should follow from it, and it makes the shortlist impossible to rank.

So please write me a procedure my team could actually run, once a year, on each asset, in about half a day, with three or four people in a room, none of whom has any statistics beyond school.

In order, I want it to cover: what question to put to the room to get the two ends of a range, and what the ends are meant to mean in terms someone untrained can act on (I have wondered whether to put it as a bet — the lowest odds at which you would put money on failure, and the highest at which you would take money against it — and I would like to be told whether that is a good idea or a trap); how to combine ranges from people who disagree, including when one person's range sits entirely above another's; what to do when a decision is due and two schemes' ranges overlap, or when one scheme's range straddles the break-even point; how to think about several assets failing together, since what would hurt us most is one storm taking out three reaches at once; what single number, if any, we should give the spreadsheet, and how to stop that number being mistaken for the estimate; and how to handle the widening case when it arises.

Where the procedure cannot decide, I want that marked, along with whether the remaining question is one that more survey or monitoring could settle, or one about what we actually care about, which is for the board to argue over. I also want it to say when the right output is that we have no idea at all — when the honest range is essentially zero to one — and what we are then supposed to do with that asset, because we have several.

Then please run the procedure all the way through on three cases.

1. A mile of embankment protecting a village of about two hundred houses. The team's range for a breach in ten years is two to ten per cent. Flooding there has been costed at around four million pounds. The rebanking would cost £400,000.
2. Two pumping stations of the same age and type, of which we can afford to replace only one this year. Replacing the first would cost £60,000 and the second £400,000, being the larger installation. The first: a ten to thirty per cent range for failure in ten years, and a modest consequence — a few fields under water for a day, £80,000. The second: five to twenty-five per cent, and a consequence of a road and eleven properties, £900,000.
3. The embankment with the burrows, whose range widened from two to ten per cent to one to forty per cent after the survey. The board had more or less decided to do this one. A further survey would cost £15,000 and take eight weeks; the funding deadline is in six.

I would like at least one of these to come out somewhere I do not want it to, and I would rather you did not soften it if it does.

Alongside the procedure, tell me how confident you are in the parts where you are recommending something that people who think about this properly disagree about, and single out the one point in what you have written that you are least sure of. Please write in British English, and keep the procedure usable by someone who will not read anything else on the subject — gloss any term of art in ordinary words the first time you use it.
