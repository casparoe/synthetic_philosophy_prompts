I run a little discussion seminar on the side — this term's theme is how much you can get with nobody understanding anything — and I need one of my scripted dialogues on the strange inversion stuff. Setup: two people, both of them genuinely good at this, stuck on a delayed train home from a workshop. One is a philosopher of biology roughly in the Dennett mold, and she thinks Darwin and Turing each pulled the same trick: take something that looks for all the world like it must have a mind behind it, and show how a blind, stepwise process with no comprehension anywhere in it can build it up. The other works in machine learning and is more or less on board with the Darwin half and dubious about the Turing half, for a reason I want the first one to actually have to deal with rather than steamroll. Nobody should be a strawman, and they shouldn't just converge by the last stop — leave the disagreement standing but sharper and more specific than it was when they sat down.

The thing I want them fighting over is a distinction Ive been kicking around in my own notes, between what I'd call sealed and transmissible competence. Sealed competence: the system reliably produces the right result, but there's no way to get the know-how out of it and into somebody who lacks it. Transmissible: it carries along whatever it takes to bring a novice up to speed. My hunch, which the skeptic gets to run with, is that this is where the two inversions come apart. Selection pumps out sealed competences — nothing in the lineage, and often nothing in the animal, has any idea why the trick works, and a genome can't teach anybody anything. A big language model trained on text that was written by creatures who did know what they were doing is different: the comprehension isn't eliminated, it's parked outside the system in the corpus. Have her press that hard, and have the inversionist come back with the strongest version of the reply I'd of guessed she'd make, which is something like: the corpus is just more environment, and we don't say a bird's wings borrowed their aerodynamic understanding from the air.

Then let the argument roll through a few stops, in whatever order feels natural, with them interrupting each other and circling back rather than marching through a list. Darwin's actual move, where he grants that it seems absurd to suppose something as intricate as an eye could be assembled by blind sifting and then shows that the appearance of design is just what you'd predict from a design-free process — and someone should notice that this only works because we're looking at the finished article and reading the function off it backwards. Turing's parallel move, swapping the question of whether a machine can think for something you can actually test, plus the part I love, his suggestion that instead of writing the adult mind you build something childlike and educate it — and the skeptic should catch that this is an awkward thing for Turing's own side, since teaching is exactly what sealed competence can't do. Then the old Lovelace-style objection, that a machine can only ever do what we've told it to do, with the inversionist giving the Turing-ish answer about programs surprising their own makers, and the skeptic replying that surprise isn't comprehension and that the surprise is itself the output of a search somebody designed. Somewhere in there the skeptic should get a real objection to my sealed/transmissible distinction instead of a wave of the hand — maybe that teachability is a social achievement rather than a mark of comprehension, and that a solitary animal could understand a great deal without ever initiating anyone — and the inversionist should answer it without just repeating herself. And I want them onto what a genuinely comprehension-free machine competence would even have to look like if the corpus point is right: does it have to be trained on nothing human, is self-play or synthetic data the relevant contrast, or does that just push the borrowing one step further out?

Close with each of them naming one concrete, checkable thing that would count against their own side — not a reading list, and please don't have them cite papers at each other, since half the seminar won't have read any. Give them names, let them enjoy the argument a bit, aim for something like 1000 to 1400 words. And keep the whole thing as one continuous piece of prose: no scene headings, no bold, no italics, no bullet points, no tables, no neat summary at the end, just the conversation with names in plain text where you need them.
