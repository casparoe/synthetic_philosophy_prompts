I run a 90-minute professional-development workshop for about eighteen public-sector data scientists and policy staff who commission and operate risk-scoring tools; they have solid applied statistics but no formal training in ethics or fair machine learning. I want them to run into the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness themselves before I name them: the fact that when the groups being scored differ in their base rates, a score can be well calibrated within each group, or it can equalise error rates across groups, or it can produce equal selection rates, but not all three at once. Design a runnable activity for this group — materials, roles, rules, and a scoring scheme with no real stakes — in which participants build a decision rule from a small dataset I can hand out (two groups, a score, an observed outcome) and discover for themselves that tightening one criterion loosens another. Two constraints should be built into the rules rather than announced: their rule has to rank people in score order, so they cannot reorder individuals to massage the group-level numbers, and in a second round the same score is redeployed for a different decision, at a different threshold and over a somewhat different population, so that it becomes visible that the trade-off attaches to the score itself and not merely to where the cut-off is drawn. Include a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the competing criteria, an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and a frank statement of what the exercise cannot reproduce — above all why the tension is not an artefact of bad data, measurement error, or prejudiced modellers — since that part I will have to supply myself. No lecture up front, and the terms calibration, error-rate balance, and demographic parity should not be used until afterwards. Please write the answer in British English.
