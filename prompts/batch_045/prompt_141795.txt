I am giving the after-dinner speech at the tenth-anniversary dinner of a small grant-making foundation whose charter contains a deliberately self-applicable amendment rule: any provision, including the amendment rule itself, can be changed by a two-thirds vote of the members, and an amendment that touches the amendment rule takes effect only if it also names the procedure by which it could in turn be repealed — the drafters nicknamed this the escalator. Twice in ten years motions have been filed to amend the amending rule, once to entrench the mission statement behind a higher threshold and once to open everything to a simple majority; both were withdrawn before a vote and are still being argued about in the room, and roughly half the audience are the members who fought over them while half are staff, donors and family who were not there for any of it.

I need a five-minute speech that is honest under those conditions, and honesty is constrained here: I cannot say what the foundation really stands for, as if some fixed purpose sat behind the rules and survived their amendment, and I cannot treat the founders' intentions as authoritative, since the charter deliberately gives them no special standing; I also cannot flatter either faction or pretend the matter is settled. So I want the self-amendment point to carry the structure of the speech rather than decorate it. What can honestly be praised about a thing whose identity and purposes are, by its own rules, up for grabs, if not a stated purpose and not a record of outcomes? I would like the speech to answer that, and to include one short passage near the end that states the condition under which the rest of what I have said should be withdrawn — so that the toast is answerable to the same kind of rule as the institution it toasts. One of the drafters is certain to ask afterwards whether the members could validly repeal the amending power altogether; I would rather the speech have already said something about that in passing than leave it as a heckle. It should be plain enough for the non-lawyers and exact enough that the people who wrote the escalator cannot accuse me of begging the question.
