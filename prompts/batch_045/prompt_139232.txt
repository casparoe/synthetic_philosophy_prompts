I am giving a paper to our joint metaphysics and philosophy of physics seminar next month — the relativity group attends, and I have fifteen minutes of questions afterwards. The paper is on Gödel's 1949 argument from rotating universes, and my thesis has two parts.

The first is a reconstruction. I argue that the argument is best read not as a proof that time is unreal but as a modal argument against the a priority of becoming: because the field equations admit a stationary, non-expanding solution with swirling dust and a negative cosmological constant tuned to the dust density — a solution with no global time function and with closed causal curves — the objective lapse of time cannot be something a world has merely in virtue of being governed by the laws it is governed by. Whether time passes is, on this reading, an accident of which solution one is in.

The second is a defence against the objection I expect most: that his world is nothing like ours. We observe no global rotation, the universe expands, the cosmological constant we measure has the wrong sign, and in any case the model is a contrived toy that no working relativist would treat as physically admissible. My reply is that this concedes the modality the argument needs, since the argument was never about our solution. I then add an argument of my own, which I call the diary argument: an observer in such a world can hand her earlier self a record of her own future, so that one and the same event is both anticipated and remembered, and so both future and past, by stages of a single subject. Either that event becomes twice, or becoming was never a fact about events at all, but only about where a subject is located.

The room will be: Halloran, a relativist who works on global causal structure, will speak of stable causality, chronology-violating regions and the fine tuning, and whose position is that it is a solution, so what; Vance, a B-theorist who holds that the relativity of simultaneity already delivered the conclusion and will want to know what rotation adds; Okonkwo, a presentist who takes passage to be primitive and not an order-theoretic notion, and who will say that closed curves are a problem for temporal order, not for time's passing; a colleague sympathetic to Gödel's idealism who will ask whether my modest modal conclusion does not eviscerate it; and several graduate students who will press the modal premise — what notion of physical possibility is in play, and whether being an exact solution is enough.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this room, not for the readership I usually argue with: the seven or eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that person would phrase it; for each, a reply I can give in about two minutes, what I should concede outright, what I must not give up, and where the reply is softest. Tell me which concession to make in the talk itself, before anyone stands up, and which single question I should hope nobody asks.
