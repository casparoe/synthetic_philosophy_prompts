I'm writing a short "where I stand" paper on the direction of time for a philosophy of physics workshop, and before I let it out of the house I'd like the position itself audited rather than the literature surveyed back at me. Here is the package, more or less in the order I'd present it.

A. There is no primitive direction of time. Every asymmetry that matters to us — memory and records, causation, counterfactual dependence, deliberation, the epistemic asymmetry between inference to the past and prediction of the future — is to be derived from a single asymmetry in the physical picture, namely the entropy gradient. The derivation has three inputs: fundamental dynamics that are, near enough, time-reversal invariant; the Past Hypothesis, that the universe's earliest macrostate was of extraordinarily low entropy; and a statistical postulate distributing probability uniformly, by the natural measure, over the microstates compatible with a given macrostate.

B. I follow the Albert–Loewer line and treat the Past Hypothesis as a law of nature rather than as a brute initial condition, on the grounds that a mere initial condition looks too accidental to carry the explanatory weight we want from it. More generally I am a best-system Humean about laws: lawhood is a matter of figuring in the simplest, strongest description of the mosaic. I have been assuming these two views support each other, and that the combination is simply how one runs A.

C. I want no part of the Price-style move of accounting for some of what looks like an objective asymmetry by appeal to our standpoint as agents who deliberate about the future and not the past. If the package in A cannot do the work unaided, I would rather amend the physics than add a perspectival ingredient.

D. I am an interventionist about causation, roughly in Woodward's sense: causal claims are claims about how one variable would respond to interventions on another. I also hold that the asymmetry of causation is a real, objective feature of the world, and not something we project onto a world that does not contain it.

E. On the cosmological side, I take the expansion of the universe to be an arrow in its own right and not merely an echo of the thermodynamic one.

F. I accept a strong principle of typicality: other things being equal, we should expect to be typical members of the class of observers there are. I also expect the universe to have an unbounded future — eternal expansion, heat death — which means that over infinite time there will be fluctuations of every size, including short-lived observers whose apparent memories of a past that never happened are as coherent as mine are. I do not accept that we are probably such observers, and I think the argument for that conclusion goes wrong somewhere.

G. One verdict on a case, which may or may not belong with the rest: suppose there could be observers in a region whose entropy gradient runs the other way from ours, so that they remember what we call the future and deliberate about what we call the past. I want to say that they, and not we, are the ones who are wrong about which way time goes.

What I want is an audit. Work through the items — pairwise where two are involved, in threes where three are — and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the real ones by how much damage they do to the package rather than listing them flat, and for each say what would have to be given up, what else in the set that revision would cost, and whether some cheaper reformulation of the item would serve instead. Flag anything internal to a single item as well as anything that holds between items, and if two of the tensions turn out to be the same tension in different vocabulary, say that. Finally, is there one revision that would settle more than one of them at once, and what else would it commit me to? I have no settled view about which item I would abandon, so please don't read one off the order in which I've written them.
