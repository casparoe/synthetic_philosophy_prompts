I am writing a novel a large part of which is set inside a persistent online world, and one subplot turns on a tribunal convened in that world to decide what, if anything, actually happened during a raid. One of my characters — a retired logician brought in to advise the tribunal on whether its accusations are even capable of being true — has to reason about this out loud, in front of other characters, and I want his reasoning to be technically right rather than merely plausible-sounding. I also want it to be recognizably in the spirit of Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions, as he originally worked it out, rather than of some later or rival treatment of definite descriptions.

So I need you to tell me what Russell himself would say about the material below, if he were handed it and asked to adjudicate. Please reason from the theory itself; I do not need citations, reading suggestions, or any secondary literature.

The world is invented, so nothing in it is meant to be recognizable.

Meridian is hosted on servers and persists between sessions. A player controls one body at a time, and bodies remain in the world when their player logs off. The company that runs Meridian keeps a small number of frozen, read-only snapshots of the entire world at past moments; these can be visited but not altered. There are offices written into the code — Warden of the North Gate, Keeper of the Lantern Road — which may be held by players or may be vacant. The world also contains a great deal of player-authored lore, pilgrimage practice, and song about figures who were never encoded anywhere. Separately, a generative system occasionally produces unscripted figures in ruined chapels, and different players report mutually incompatible encounters with them.

Here are the utterances the tribunal has to rule on:

1. "The cathedral that Wren built is gone." Wren's cathedral was destroyed in a permitted raid last winter; nothing of it remains in the live world, but three snapshots contain exact copies of it.
2. "The cathedral that Wren built was the finest thing in Meridian."
3. "The Warden of the North Gate is corrupt." The office is still in the code; the gate was sealed four years ago and the office has been vacant ever since, though players still gossip about Wardens and several former holders are alive in the world.
4. "The Silent King is cruel." There is no such character, no throne, and no quest anywhere in the servers; what there is consists of songs, prayers, a pilgrimage route, and a large body of fan-written lore — plus the unscripted chapel figures, whom some players identify as him.
5. "The woman who carried the lantern across the Ash Wastes is standing in front of you." Exactly one body did the carrying; its memories were then copied into the body now standing in front of the speaker, while the original remains standing, inert and empty, on the far side of the wastes.
6. "Wren's cathedral does not exist."

For each utterance, I would like: the verdict Russell would give — true, false, or in need of scope disambiguation before it has a truth value; the logical form he would assign, in whatever mix of notation and plain English a bright non-specialist reader could follow, with the scope-relevant ambiguities made explicit where they matter; and his account of why the character who said it took herself to have said something true, and what he would say she really succeeded in conveying.

Then three further things. First, rank the six utterances from the one his descriptive apparatus handles most comfortably to the one that would give him the most trouble, with a single sentence of justification for each placement. Second, a paragraph on where he would have to concede that the difficulty is not one the theory of descriptions can settle at all — tense and time, uniqueness across copies, reference to figures internal to a fiction, descriptions that carry a hidden relativity to the speaker or the hearer. Third, whether he would grant virtual objects any special ontological category, or whether "the cathedral in Meridian" would receive exactly the treatment he gives to "the cathedral in Chartres," and what he would say to a character who insists that Meridian's things are somehow less real and therefore need a different logic.

Finally, since some of this will end up as speech, mark which of the verdicts a character could state in two or three lines of dialogue without the apparatus showing, and which could only ever be narrated.
