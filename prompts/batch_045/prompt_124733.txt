I build and maintain the ontology and schema for a biodiversity/biobanking data platform — SKOS vocabularies, a pile of OWL classes, Postgres underneath, dbt models on top, plus a binary classifier whose whole job is to stamp incoming specimen records ORGANISM or NOT_ORGANISM, because our funders wanted one flag and now we're stuck with it. So I live in SQL, entity resolution, polythetic vs. monotone class definitions, required vs. nullable fields, soft deletes, migrations, and precision/recall tradeoffs; my philosophy background is one freshman survey I mostly slept through. Please translate into my vocabulary rather than making me learn yours, and don't send me papers, citations, or a reading list — I'm working offline and I don't need sources, I need the translation itself. Here's what I want: take the main accounts of what life is and restate each one as literally as you can as a type predicate in my stack. For each: what are the required fields and which are nullable, is it a hard boolean or a score against a threshold, is the definition monotone (necessary-and-sufficient conditions) or polythetic (a weighted cluster where you can be missing some traits), what would the unit tests and the test suite look like, what does it do with a record whose parent pointer is null, and — since our lineage table is really a DAG and not a tree once horizontal transfer is in the picture — what data structure does the account assume? The accounts I keep running into, in my words: (1) the astrobiology working definition, roughly self-sustaining chemistry that can undergo Darwinian evolution, with that embarassing consequence that a lone sexually reproducing individual fails its own test; (2) autopoiesis / organizational closure, the self-producing-and-self-distinguishing one; (3) replication- or information-first views where the real object is a gene lineage and organisms are what genes build; (4) metabolism- or dissipation-first views where life is a far-from-equilibrium process that eats gradients; (5) the deflationary one — no essence, just family resemblance over a cluster, and maybe not even a real property at all.

Then run them against three cases we actually have to classify. (a) The Chimney Fields: mineral structures in a flooded iron mine that hold internal pH and redox gradients, accrete, and bud off daughter structures — but the daughter's shape is set by local flow and chemistry, nothing gets copied, so there's growth and no heritable variation. (b) The tenant: a synthetic cell with its own genome and its own membrane, but translation and ATP both come from the helper cell line it lives inside. (c) Pando, the clonal aspen: one genome, tens of thousands of stems, connected underground, and our entity-resolution step genuinely cannot say whether that's one record or forty thousand — plus, as a side check, prions and freeze-dried spores, because our own intake rule is a two-year rule (dry it down, leave it in a drawer, rehydrate, and if it restarts it gets a canonical ORGANISM record, otherwise it's demoted to SAMPLE) and I've noticed that rule admits prions while rejecting things everyone agrees are alive. Tell me what each of the five says about each case, and translate my two-year rule back into a philosophical position — is it secretly one of the five, and is it cheating?
