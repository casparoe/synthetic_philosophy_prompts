I'm the junior student member of the Discipline Review Panel at my high school — two students, two teachers, and an assistant principal, who votes on appeals of any suspension longer than three days. The principal has never once overruled us, so whatever the panel writes down is, in practice, what happens to the kid. Ours is the last appeal of the term and we vote Tuesday.

The appeal is from a sophomore I'll call Devon, who was given five days out of school for defiance after the fourth incident of exactly the same shape. A teacher asks him to hand over his phone; he slides it into his pocket, says the same three words, stands, and walks out; he ends up in the same second-floor stairwell every time and sits on the same step until someone comes for him. He has never raised his voice, never run, never gone anywhere else, and has never once explained himself afterward beyond "I don't know." He has now had a warning, a lunch detention, a one-day suspension, and a three-day suspension, and the behavior did not change at all. His written appeal argues that this proves he cannot help it: if leaving were something he chose, he says, the first suspension would have ended it, and each new consequence is just one more trigger that produces the identical result. His mother adds that the school is in no position to blame anyone for being triggered, since the whole building runs on triggers — point charts, the demerit ladder, automatic Friday detention, the "gotcha" announcements over the loudspeaker — and that punishing a reflex while running a conditioning regime is hypocrisy.

What has me stuck is an old story I ran into while reading around for my biology elective: a digger wasp drags its paralyzed prey to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out, and hauls the prey in; if someone nudges the prey a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp drags it back to the doorway and then goes in to inspect again, and can be made to repeat the whole cycle indefinitely. Somewhere I saw this sort of rigidity given a name — something like "sphexishness" — and used as a worry about us: that human behavior only looks flexible because nobody has found the perturbation that reveals it as a fixed routine, and that if that were true there'd be nothing left for blame to attach to. I don't care whether the wasp story is literally accurate; take it as a fable. My own thought, which may be wrong, is that the fable shows less than it seems to: it shows that the routine is tuned to some range of situations and the experimenter stepped outside that range, and any finite system, however smart, will have some such range. So "mechanical" isn't yet the issue; the issue is how many features of the situation the response actually varies with, and whether those include the reasons we appeal to when we punish. That suggests a question we could actually ask about Devon instead of speculating about determinism: does his behavior change when the reason changes — say, if a teacher needs the phone for the morning announcements rather than to confiscate it, or if a friend's parent is trying to reach him, or if the request comes from a teacher he trusts.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the other four members before Tuesday: 600–800 words, memo style (To / From / Re, then a few short sections), recommending one of three dispositions — uphold the five-day suspension; reduce it to two days plus a written contract reviewed in three weeks; or overturn the discipline and replace it with a support plan and no record in his file. It has to say what we should concretely do with Devon on Monday morning, and it has to engage seriously the strongest consideration on whichever side loses — for me that's his mother's charge that we can't run a school on triggers and then blame a boy for being triggered, but use whichever objection you think is genuinely strongest. Please don't just assert that he could have done otherwise, since that is the exact claim his appeal denies, and don't advise us to defer to the counselor or wait for an evaluation; we already did that and the report was inconclusive. End the memo with a final line reading exactly \answer{uphold}, \answer{modify}, or \answer{overturn}.
