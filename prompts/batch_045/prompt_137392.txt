The question I set was as follows:

"Question 4. The Kestrel-9 class consists of unmanned feeder vessels operated from a shore control centre (SCC), where a single watchkeeper may be responsible for up to seven vessels at once. Each vessel runs two separately certified software systems: a collision-avoidance system trained to comply with the international navigation rules, and a route-and-fuel optimiser that tightens margins in order to save bunker costs. Under a 'lineage certification' regime, the classification society certifies the vendor's development process and update pipeline rather than any fixed build: the models are retrained weekly on fleet-wide telemetry and the vendor pushes updated parameters over the air, with no human reviewing or signing off on the individual values in force. In heavy traffic, and under a pressure to keep to schedule, the two systems interact so that the vessel holds a close-quarters course and then turns late; there is a collision with a fishing vessel and two of its crew are killed. Investigation shows that the behaviour cannot be traced to any single line of code, any single specification, or any decision by a named individual: it emerged from the interaction of two modules each certified as safe in isolation, in a build that no one ever reviewed. The relevant statute makes the shipping company strictly liable for damage caused by its vessels, subject to a contractual indemnity from the vendor that is capped at a fixed sum, and it also imposes on the SCC watchkeeper a duty of continuous oversight whose breach is a criminal negligence offence.

(a) State precisely what the responsibility gap is claimed to be, and distinguish it from the retribution gap (the mismatch between our punitive practices and the availability of an agent who is a fitting target of them) and from Elish's moral crumple zone (the deflection of responsibility onto the nearest human operator, who had limited control over the automated system, in a way that protects the integrity of the technological system). (b) Is there a responsibility gap in this case? (c) One proposed reform is to remove the indemnity cap and make the vendor jointly and severally liable with the shipping company. Would that close whatever gap you have identified? (d) If no natural person is blameworthy, what, if anything, should the criminal law do?"

The answer I have to grade reads as follows:

"Candidate 2214. (a) The responsibility gap arises with learning systems because the behaviour that causes the harm cannot be traced back to a decision that any human agent made or could have made at the time of the deployment. The programmer makes a real causal contribution, but the epistemic and control conditions on blame are not satisfied by anyone: the designer does not know, and in a learning system cannot know, what the system will do, and the operator does not know what it is doing. This is not merely an evidential problem that a better investigation would solve; even a complete causal account of what happened leaves us without an agent who satisfies the conditions for blameworthiness. The retribution gap is a separate problem: it arises when something has gone wrong and our punitive practices have no fitting object, which can happen whether or not anyone is blameworthy. The moral crumple zone is a third thing again: the tendency of blame to migrate to the human nearest the machine even where that human's control was nominal, so that the technological system's integrity is preserved at the operator's expense.

(b) This case is harder than the standard one, because the harm does not come from a single opaque system but from the interaction of two modules each of which was certified in isolation, and because no one reviewed the build that was actually in force. So the problem here is not simply that there are many hands: with many hands, responsibility is spread but there are still agents with the relevant knowledge and control, whereas here there seems to be no one who had the behaviour of the system in view at all. Consider the watchkeeper. She is legally the person with a duty of continuous oversight, but she was monitoring seven vessels; the literature on meaningful human control gives us the vocabulary to say what is missing, since she could not track the reasons for the manoeuvre the system was about to make, and the confirmation prompts she received were rubber stamps that she was under time pressure to accept. Her control was nominal. But Elish's analysis of the moral crumple zone gives us the answer to where responsibility goes: responsibility in a socio-technical system has to land on some human being, and the lesson of Elish is that the human in the loop is the one who carries it. The watchkeeper is therefore the responsible party for the collision.

(c) The reform would close the gap. The gap just consists in there being no one who is held to account for the outcome; once the indemnity cap is removed and the vendor is jointly and severally liable, there is a party who bears the cost of the collision, and a party who bears the cost of an outcome is the party who is responsible for it in the only sense that matters. The vendor is thus morally responsible for the deaths. This also resolves the retribution gap, because the vendor can be punished in the way in which firms are punished, and it is the vendor whose design decisions produced the vessel's behaviour.

(d) If no natural person is blameworthy, the criminal law should not pretend otherwise: it should either abandon the punitive response here or accept that no punishment is warranted. There are things the state can still do that look like punishment — grounding the fleet, deleting the model weights, withdrawing the lineage certification — but these are expressive and regulatory measures and do not satisfy the retributive demand, which is precisely why Danaher treats the retribution gap as a genuine problem rather than a puzzle about bookkeeping."
