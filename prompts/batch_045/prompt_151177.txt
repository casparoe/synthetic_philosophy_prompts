I should say at once that this is not a hypothetical for me. Last month I was asked to be one of the first three candidates of a residents' association that has just registered as a political party to contest seats on our town council. I have a fortnight to give an answer, and what is stopping me is not the workload but an argument I read some years ago in Simone Weil and have never managed to get out from under.

As I carry it about with me, the argument runs in three steps. First: a political party is not a group of people who happen to agree with one another; it is a machine, and what it manufactures is collective opinion. It exists in order to bring it about that its members think, and say, one thing. So its first business, whatever it declares its business to be, is the maintenance of its own single voice. Second: because it must survive in order to speak at all, its own preservation and growth become in practice its supreme end; and this substitution is not a corruption into which some parties fall but something written into the thing itself. Third: a person outside a party can attend to what is true and to what is just, whereas a person inside one cannot do so at the point of conflict without damaging the party. So the party does not merely fail to serve the public good; it renders the public good unthinkable to its own members. Weil, as I read her, drew the conclusion that parties ought therefore to be abolished rather than reformed.

I find this hard to shake, and I have made it harder to shake by extending it. It seems to me that the argument does not really depend on elections at all. Any body that has to present a single public position — a campaign group, a trade union, a professional association, a church, a university senate — inherits the same structure: it must maintain its voice, therefore it must maintain itself, therefore there will always be some point at which it prefers its own credibility to the truth. I watched this happen in a professional body I sat on for six years, and it is the one part of the argument for which I have first-hand evidence. But the extension worries me, because I can no longer think of a case that would refute the view. Any organisation that fails to corrupt itself in this way I am now inclined to reclassify as not really an organisation of the relevant kind. So before I say yes or no, I want to know what the view actually risks.

Two features of my own case complicate it further. The association has no whip, and the only commitments in its programme are three specific pledges about a bypass and a building; it has also written into its constitution that it will dissolve at the end of a single four-year term. One person has told me that this is precisely the sort of arrangement Weil's argument predicts will decay. Another has told me that it is precisely the sort of arrangement which shows that her argument is really about a historically specific kind of party — the mass, ideological, disciplined party of her own time — and not about parties as such.

Please play this straight. What would have to be true, or to happen, for the view I have described to be false? I should like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and aimed: which of the three steps does each one strike at, and does any of them leave the rest of the argument standing? I am particularly interested in what Weil's own defenders would concede as counting against her, rather than only what her critics allege. And please tell me honestly whether, in the form I now hold it, the view has been qualified into something that risks nothing at all — and if it has, whether that is a genuine defect in a claim of this kind, or whether its defenders can reasonably reply that it is not. If you think the four-year self-dissolving association is a useful test, say what outcome would tell against the view and what outcome would not, and why.
