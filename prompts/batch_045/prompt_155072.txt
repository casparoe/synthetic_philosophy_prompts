I want you to analyze the decipherment of Linear B as a case of radical interpretation, and then tell me where Davidson's framework stops being the right tool.

The case, as I have it — please take these facts as given rather than as claims to be checked:

- From 1900 Arthur Evans excavated clay tablets at Knossos, and later finds came from Pylos, Mycenae and elsewhere: a script of roughly eighty-seven syllabic signs, plus a hundred or so non-phonetic ideograms depicting commodities (vessels, grain, animals, people), written alongside numerals. It is the earliest attested Greek, and the tablets are almost entirely palace administration: inventories, allocations, rations.
- There was no bilingual inscription. Unlike Egyptian hieroglyphs and the Rosetta Stone, no crib.
- Evans and most of his contemporaries assumed the language behind the tablets was non-Greek. Alice Kober's tabulation of sign-groups that share a stem and vary in their endings established that the language was inflected, and she herself inclined away from Greek.
- Michael Ventris, an architect working largely alone, built a grid of possible consonant-vowel values out of purely distributional constraints — sign frequencies, position within words, substitution classes, stem-and-ending alternations — and then, in a circulated note of 1 June 1952 titled "Are the Knossos and Pylos tablets written in Greek?", proposed that the language was Greek. He described the result as, in his own words, "a difficult and archaic Greek, but Greek nevertheless," and turned to the Cambridge philologist John Chadwick that July for help with the historical linguistics.
- The nearest thing to a test: the tablet now catalogued PY Ta 641, found by Carl Blegen at Pylos in June 1952 and first published after Ventris's proposal, carries recognizable ideograms of vessels whose accompanying sign-groups the grid read as their Greek names — a tripod ideogram beside syllables of the Greek word for tripod.
- It did not settle quickly. The decipherment was disputed through the 1950s and into the 1960s, notably by the classicist A. J. Beattie, and reached general acceptance among Anglophone scholars only by the 1980s.
- Linear A, which shares many signs with Linear B and was found in the same excavations, remains unread: smaller corpus, unknown language.

The theory, as I am using it: radical interpretation as the construction of a theory of meaning from evidence, under the principle of charity (maximize agreement, assume the speaker is mostly right about the world), holism of belief and meaning, triangulation (two creatures jointly responding to a shared world as what fixes content), the passing theory the interpreter improvises for an occasion of utterance, the denial that there could be a language we could recognize as one and yet not interpret, and interpretive indeterminacy.

What I want is a two-way analysis.

First, do the mapping carefully. Who or what occupies the role of the speaker, and who of the interpreter, when the "speaker" is a dead scribal administration and the "utterances" are warehouse records? What is the evidence base — the corpus, its distributional structure, the ideograms, the archaeological and administrative context? If triangulation is supposed to be constitutive of content, what, if anything, plays its role here: the objects the ideograms depict, the excavated palace, the bureaucracy inferred from the tablets, or nothing at all? And if charity is supposed to do the work of getting interpretation off the ground, say concretely what does that work instead — my suspicion is that it is a pair of substantive priors rather than a principle of rationality: a genre-and-function prior (these are the stock records of a palace bureaucracy, so sign-groups adjacent to an ideogram and numerals name the thing and its quantity) and a structural prior about what kind of language fits a syllabary of this shape with these inflectional alternations. Say whether that suspicion is right, and what charity would even maximize given that inventories contain few or no assertions about the world whose truth we could assess.

Second, say what the theory illuminates. Does holism explain why the grid had to be solved as a system rather than sign by sign? Does the framework predict, better than its rivals do, that distributional analysis alone cannot reach meaning without a risky substantive hypothesis — here, the identification of the language as Greek, made against the field's assumption? Where would indeterminacy be expected to bite, and does it bite there (which sign values and which words stayed contested longest)?

Third, say where it breaks or falls silent. There is no interlocutor, no assent or dissent to observe, no shared scene of utterance, no passing theory for an occasion, no propositional attitudes to be charitable about, and no speaker whose beliefs the interpreter is trying to recover — the target is a record-keeping practice, not a mind. Does Davidson's apparatus have anything to say about an interpretation that is confirmed by prediction onto tablets excavated after it was formed, or about a consensus that took three decades to form? And is that lag evidence for underdetermination in the Quinean sense, or merely the sociology of a small field?

Then give me the verdict on the theory. Does this case vindicate the claim that there are no untranslatable languages, refute it, or pass it by — given that the failure of Linear A looks like a poverty-of-evidence problem rather than a conceptual-scheme problem? If the episode shows that interpretation can get off the ground without charity, does that undermine the route Davidson took to indeterminacy, or only restate it? And does a competing account — interpretation as inference to the best explanation over a hypothesis space of (language identity, sign values), or as an intentional stance adopted toward an institution rather than a person — cover more of the episode than Davidson does, and if so, what exactly does it cover that Davidson misses?

Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and if a judgment of yours would turn on a fact I have not supplied, name that fact instead of hedging. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the case vindicates Davidson's picture, breaks it, or falls outside its scope, and which single feature of the episode drives that verdict — and then give the analysis, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
