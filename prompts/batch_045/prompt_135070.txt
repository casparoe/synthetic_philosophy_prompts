So I let myself get talked into doing a forty-minute slot at a writers' festival in the spring: a room of about thirty novelists and short-story people, a flipchart, no slides, and definately no equations (I think I can get away with a little grid of boxes and arrows, and that's it). The topic I stupidly proposed is inverse reinforcement learning, and what it might mean to infer what a person wants from what they do. Bit of context: I'm a novelist, not a philosopher or a CS person, and the reason I said yes is that my new book has a subplot where a woman's adult son reads her old diaries and tries to work out what she actually wanted out of her life — and I got interested in what it would even be to get that right. So I understand the idea at an eager-amateur level and I've read around a bit, but what I need is a plan, not an explanation. I can muddle through the explanation if I know the order it has to come in.

Before you start: you're someone who has taught inverse reinforcement learning and preference inference to both engineers and humanities people, and who has thought hard about what it would mean to read a person's wants off their behaviour. Take your time. Brainstom two or three different ways the session could be sequenced and tell me in passing why you dropped the losers, then give me the one you'd actually hand me. And don't explain inverse RL to me — plan how I explain it to them.

Here's what I need the plan to settle.

The order, and the reasons for it. What has to be in place before I can even say the word "reward" out loud? My instinct is that the forward problem has to land first — there's an agent, there's a situation, the agent does things over time, we get to see what it did but not what it was optimizing — and that if I open with "so we're trying to recover their reward function" everyone hears "the thing that makes them happy" and the rest of the session is me untangling that. Tell me if that's right, and what else in the sequence is genuinely load-bearing rather than just conventional.

One example to carry the whole forty minutes. I want to build it on something from fiction, because that's the room — possibly my diaries subplot, possibly something flatter like a character who does the same odd thing every Tuesday for twenty years — but I worry a novel example quietly smuggles in the very thing I'm trying to show, and that the audience will read it as being about people rather than about a formal setup. The two examples I keep circling are a self-driving car learning from a human driver, and a lab animal in a maze; if either of those is a trap, say why. Then give me the example you'd actually use and spell out what it has to have and what it has to lack to carry the whole session — including the features it must NOT have, since I suspect most of my candidate stories have exactly those.

The misconceptions, and where in the sequence each one gets headed off. Here's my list; tell me which are real, which are me misunderstanding, and add the ones I've missed:

- "Oh, that's just what novelists do — reading motivation off behaviour." I think I want to open with this and then break it somewhere in the middle, but I don't know where the break goes or what does the breaking.
- "So it assumes people are rational." I half-know the technical version — the behaviour gets modelled as roughly optimal with respect to some reward, so irrationality shows up as noise rather than as a refutation — but I don't know how to say that without starting a forty-minute fight about whether people are irrational, which would eat the session.
- "Surely more data would fix it." I think the worry here is structural rather than about sample size: a lot of different reward functions explain the same behaviour, and there's some degenerate one that explains all of it and predicts nothing. I don't trust my grip on why, so tell me what I'd actually be claiming and where it belongs.
- "So the reward it recovers is what the person would say they want." This one matters for my book, where the diaries say one thing and the behaviour says another. Does the setup have any way of adjudicating that, or is it blind to the difference between what's avowed, the quantity the behaviour is tracking, and what's actually good for her? I need to know whether I'm making a real distinction or a sentimental one.
- Someone will say "isn't this just copying what the person does?" I think the difference is that you take the inferred thing and re-run it in a situation nobody demonstrated, but I'm not certain I can make that feel like a difference to a room of novelists rather than a technicality.

Also: where do I concede things? I'd rather give something away in the middle than look like I'm defending a machine at the end, but I don't know which concession buys the most credibility with this crowd. Say plainly what I should refuse to explain at this level (I'm guessing the actual maths of the inference, anything with a partition function in it, and whatever the formalism for the Bayesian version is) and which one thing I should flag out loud as genuinely unsettled rather than papering over.

And one check at the end: a single question, or a tiny thing they can do in their heads and shout out, that would show me they'd got the actual idea rather than "computers can now guess what you want." Ideally it would also show me whether they've got the bit about the example being carried into a new situation, since that's the bit I care about for the book.

Oh — and I've started calling one specific mistake "the biographer's error": reading a whole life as if it were the maximizing of a single thing. I'd love to use that phrase in the session, but only if it's a real distinction and not a cute name for something I've half-understood. Tell me if it earns its place, and if so exactly where.
