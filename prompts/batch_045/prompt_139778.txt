I was appointed last spring to the nine-member Urban Wildlife Commission of a mid-sized city, which advises the city council on management of the park system — I am the lay member (I teach high-school biology and used to volunteer at the municipal animal shelter, which I suspect is why I was asked). We vote on Tuesday on what to do about the white-tailed deer herd in the Mill Creek greenway corridor. As near as I can tell the commission is split three-three, with three of us genuinely undecided, so the memo I circulate beforehand could actually decide the outcome, and I would like it to be good enough that commissioners on both sides can argue with it productively rather than dismiss it.

The staff report puts the herd at roughly three times the density the habitat can sustain: browse damage in the restoration plantings, a rising number of deer–vehicle collisions on the two roads that run through the corridor, and a sharp increase in resident complaints about ticks. It lays out three options: (A) a managed cull by contracted sharpshooters over two winters, reaching target density by the end of the second season; (B) a five-year program of dart-delivered immunocontraception, several times the per-animal cost of the cull and slower, because sterilized adults go on browsing and the population does not fall below target for at least three years; (C) no direct population intervention, with expanded fencing, planting of unpalatable species, and continued monitoring. Please take staff's figures at face value rather than relitigating them — the disagreement I need help with is not empirical. Two things complicate the vote. First, a coalition of animal-advocacy groups has petitioned us to adopt what they call the Reciprocity Condition: that the city may not use a population-control method on a wild species that it would refuse to use on a comparably sized population of companion animals of comparable sentience, unless it can name a difference in the animals' capacities — rather than in our feelings about them — that justifies the difference. They pair this with a parity complaint: the city spends, per animal, something like two orders of magnitude more on shelter cats and dogs, and uses only non-lethal methods on them, which they say shows our deer policy is explained by species membership and not by anything morally relevant. Second, one of our commissioners has argued that the coalition's own premises cut against them: if deer count equally by sentience and what matters is total welfare, then preventing births prevents lives that would on balance have gone well, whereas removing animals who already exist ends lives that would have continued to go well — so the option that "kills no one" may not be the welfare-superior one. Another commissioner replies that deer lives in this corridor may well be net negative once winter starvation, road mortality, and disease are counted, in which case the ranking flips. Nobody on the commission can settle which empirical picture is right, and I do not think a decision should quietly assume one.

What I want is a decision memo, about 900 to 1,100 words, that I can circulate to the other commissioners under my own name: a recommendation up front — approve one of the three options, reject the lot, or approve with conditions — followed by the reasons, given in terms commissioners who disagree about whether the point is animal welfare, ecological integrity, or public health can all engage with. The recommendation needs to be concrete: which option, under what conditions, on what timeline, with what measurable thresholds and what would trigger reassessment or a change of course. It should prefer, where it can, a decision that stays defensible whether deer lives in the corridor turn out to be net positive or net negative, and it should say explicitly which considerations it is treating as decisive and which as merely supporting. It must also take a position on the Reciprocity Condition itself — recommend that we adopt it, reject it, or adopt some narrower version — and explain why, rather than conceding the deer-versus-dog asymmetry and moving on. Finally, give the strongest version of the case for whichever option you do not recommend, including the considerations that tell against the option you do: I would rather hand commissioners a memo that has already absorbed the best argument on the other side than one that gets torn apart in the first ten minutes of the meeting.
