I chair the serious-incident review panel at a mid-sized hospital: we investigate maybe two or three serious incidents a month, the actual investigation is done by a facilitator who is a ward nurse or a pharmacist doing this on top of their real job, they get one 90-minute meeting, and the report goes to a lay board that wants a single line saying "root cause" plus a table of actions with owners and dates. Our method is basically the five whys, and it keeps degenerating in three ways I can't referee: (1) facilitators stop as soon as they hit something they can hang an action on — usually "staff didn't follow the protocol" or "training was inadequate" — because that's what the board will sign off; (2) other facilitators won't stop, and go training budget, then trust deficit, then how commissioning works, either never terminating or stopping wherever it sounds systemic enough that it's nobody's fault; (3) some chains obviously go in a circle — staff didn't escalate because they didn't recognise deterioration, because escalation isn't practised, because staff don't escalate — and nobody can tell me whether that circle is a defect in the investigation or just an accurate picture of how a system behaves.

I did a philosophy minor years ago and half-remember the regress problem: a reason needs a reason needs a reason, and the options are that it stops somewhere that doesn't need support (foundationalism), that it's mutual support all the way down with the whole web holding itself up (coherentism), or that it never stops and the requirement is just that you can always produce another, non-repeating reason (infinitism, which my brother, who finished the degree, insists is a real position and not a joke). It feels like it maps onto exactly our problem, except the textbook versions are about justifying beliefs and our chains are about causes, and I think we slide between the two without noticing. So: write me a procedure my facilitators can actually run — one page, no theory vocabulary in it — for deciding when a chain of reasons is deep enough to stop. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the points where the procedure just says stop, and, very importantly, the points where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a question about what this hospital is willing to act on rather than a question about the incident. Be explicit about which distinctions you had to keep for the procedure to work at all, which you threw away as unusable by a non-specialist, and where the procedure will misfire.

Specific things I want handled: a chain that's beautifully consistent internally but never touches anything outside the report (my sense is that this is the coherentist version of the old complaint that a coherent story can float free of reality — we see it when a team interviews only managers and never looks at the actual drug chart); a chain whose next step would be something nobody in the hospital can change, like national procurement rules or the building's layout, which is where our funders tell us to stop and which feels like it bottoms out for the wrong reason; a chain that would have to name a specific senior clinician's behaviour, where the evidence is good and the politics are awful; and the case where the honest answer is that nobody can tell whether the proposed action will fix anything. I've also been toying with replacing fixed depth altogether: have someone from a different panel spend ten minutes at whatever node the facilitator stopped at just asking "and why is that a reason?", and see whether the facilitator can produce one more supporting reason that isn't a repeat of an earlier one. My brother says that's infinitism with a stopwatch. Is it, and is that a bug or a feature?

Then run the finished procedure on three of ours and show me what the output would look like: (a) a patient given ten times the intended opioid dose, where the vials for two strengths look nearly identical and were chosen because they were the cheapest; (b) a patient who deteriorated overnight on a ward and wasn't escalated, where the chain keeps circling through training and escalation culture, and where I suspect the honest finding is that the proposed action — another training module — is untestable, which I want to see the procedure say out loud even though the board will hate it; (c) a near-miss where the facilitator stopped at "agency nurse unfamiliar with the ward", and the panel suspects the real story is that the rota has been short for eight months and everyone knows it. Happy to be told that parts of this just can't be turned into a checklist.
