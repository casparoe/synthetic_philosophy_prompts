I've recently joined the oversight board of the Northern Reach Disaster Tribunal, and I have become fixated on one of its internal documents, Guidance Note 9, which governs how assessors should respond to what the Tribunal calls a "process audit". The background: the Tribunal determines the causes of industrial disasters from a closed written dossier, and after a finding is reached but before it is certified, its Methodology Office runs an algorithmic audit called the Process Ledger. The Ledger examines only how a finding was reached — length of deliberation, the order in which exhibits were sifted, time of day, the panel's sleep and medication logs, the carbon dioxide reading in the deliberation room, whether the prescribed sift procedure was followed — and never the evidence itself; it returns only an archival reliability profile, telling you how often panels whose process shared this profile were subsequently shown to have been right. The case that prompted my interest is the Meridian cold-store fire, with five candidate causes: A, compressor failure; B, arson by a former employee who has since died; C, spontaneous ignition of linseed-oil-soaked rags; D, a defrost-heater fault; E, deliberate ignition by the site manager for the insurance. The dossier points very hard at C — burn geometry, the position of the rag store, the logbook, an expert report — and the first-instance panel certified C at 0.95. The audit then came back with profile P-9: in the archive, panels in that condition named the true cause 20% of the time, their errors scattered evenly across the other four candidates, so that taking the audit into account in the ordinary Bayesian way flattens the distribution to 0.2 on every option. The Tribunal cannot abstain; it must certify one cause. Note 9's instruction is that the assessor should retain the first-order finding as the finding, and attach a second-order "reliability annotation" recording that the assessor's total evidence does not in fact support it — in the philosophical vocabulary, Note 9 instructs assessors to occupy a state of epistemic akrasia, or level-splitting, rather than revise the finding downwards.

Please play Dr Ines Halvard, Chief Methodologist of the Tribunal and the author of Note 9, while I try to talk her out of it. Make her sharp, dry, entirely unsentimental, and long practised at defending the Note against lawyers who want it withdrawn: she may concede that the archival sample is small (some sixty cases), that the "true causes" in that sample were themselves settled by later human investigation, that the word "retain" is doing unfortunate work, and that a flat distribution is uncomfortable for an institution that must certify something. She must not concede that a flat, non-directional audit can simply be folded into the first-order distribution by conditionalisation, nor that certifying C while accepting the audit as accurate is a contradiction rather than an unhappy but genuine rational requirement. She should argue from the details of the case rather than from the literature, and cite nothing. Reply in the first person, in British English, in under 250 words per turn; end a turn with at most one question to me; stay in character until I say "stop".

My opening to her is this: Dr Halvard, Note 9 asks me to certify C at 0.95 and, in the same document, to record that my total evidence gives me 0.2 on each of the five candidates. I want to know what that 0.2 is for. It does not guide the certification, it does not alter the number I publish, and nobody downstream reads it; it looks like a diary entry rather than a judgement. But the audit is not a mood, it is evidence: panels in condition P-9 got the cause wrong four times in five, and I have been told that I am one of them. My 0.95 is no longer the verdict of my evidence; it is the output of a procedure I now have excellent reason to distrust, and the correct posterior is flat. That a flat posterior is inconvenient for an institution that must certify something is the Tribunal's problem, not a licence to keep the old number. And your Note has no stopping point: had the audit come back saying that panels in P-9 are right 5% of the time — or never right, in a way that never favours any particular alternative — Note 9 would still instruct me to certify C at 0.95. So tell me plainly: on your view, what is the audit for?
