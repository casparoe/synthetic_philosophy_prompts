Our reading group is doing a semester on moral responsibility and this week was Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — we read one of his papers in seminar, and I also grabbed a pop-philosophy book that has a chapter on it because I wanted something plainer to hold on to. The chapter summarizes the whole thing like this:

"Strawson's Basic Argument shows that nobody is ever truly morally responsible for anything, and it manages this without assuming determinism. It runs in three steps. (1) You do what you do because of the way you are — your character, values, desires. (2) So to be morally responsible for what you do, you have to be morally responsible for the way you are; and since your whole personality feeds into any action, that means being responsible for your entire personality. (3) But you can't be responsible for the way you are. The only way to be responsible for it would be to have brought it about, and to bring it about you'd already have had to be a certain way, which you'd also have had to bring about, and so on forever. True moral responsibility would require self-creation, which is impossible. And notice the argument never mentions causation by earlier events: that's why it works whether determinism is true or false, why it's stronger than the old incompatibilist argument from determinism, and why it's finally just an outright refutation of compatibilism. It's really the same point as the manipulation cases — if neuroscientists had secretly fixed all your values you wouldn't be responsible, and that intuition generalizes, since your values were fixed by something or other. Strawson's father had already gone halfway there: in the famous paper on reactive attitudes, P.F. Strawson argued that praise and blame just consist in attitudes like gratitude and resentment, so once the Basic Argument convinces you that nobody is responsible, you can let those attitudes lapse and take up the objective attitude instead, treating people as things to be managed. And the practical upshot follows straight from the conclusion: no blame is ever deserved, so punishment can only be justified by deterrence or quarantine and never by desert — and by symmetry nobody deserves credit for anything they achieve either, so praise is really just a motivational tool."

Does such an agent slip past the Basic Argument, or does Strawson's premise bite anyway because an uncaused agent still hasn't *made* itself the way it is?
