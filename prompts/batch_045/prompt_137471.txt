I am a print journalist who has spent six years covering agriculture and environmental science, and I am applying for a nine-month funded fellowship attached to a university institute — not a degree programme — in order to turn a reporting thread I have been unable to let go of into a book-length piece of work. I would like your help drafting the proposal document.

The reporting, in brief: pest control is, by headcount, by far the largest deliberate killing of animals that human beings carry out — trillions of insects a year, on any estimate I can construct from application data and field surveys — and yet it occupies almost no space in the ethics of our relations to animals, which is organised almost entirely around the animals we eat, experiment on, or keep. When regulators, certification schemes, and pest managers do make welfare-flavoured judgments, they reach for the only conceptual tools available: how many animals die, and whether the animals in question are sentient. Both tools misfire here, and I think I know roughly why, but I need the reason stated as a research question rather than as a journalist's hunch.

My half-formed idea is this. Nearly every insect killed by pest control would have died soon anyway, and would have died badly — as a juvenile, through predation, parasitism, starvation, desiccation, or disease. If that is right, then counting deaths is the wrong metric: what an intervention does is not add deaths to the world so much as change the timing and manner of deaths that were already on their way. Call it the pre-empted death view — the ethically salient quantity, for each insect, is the difference between how it would have died and how it does die. That reframing has consequences the body-count view does not. It puts the duration and mode of dying at the centre of the picture, which matters because the pesticide toolkit is startlingly various on exactly that axis: fast neurotoxins, compounds that produce prolonged convulsion and paralysis before death, growth regulators that kill only at the next moult days or weeks later, desiccants and stomach poisons that kill by dehydration or starvation. And it splits killing interventions sharply from interventions that suppress without killing — pheromone-based mating disruption, sterile-insect releases, repellents, physical exclusion — because on a pre-emption view these differ not merely in body count but in whether there is a death to be pre-empted at all; and, awkwardly, they prevent the existence of insects who might otherwise have had lives worth having, which on a totalist view makes them worse rather than better.

There is also a prior question I do not know how to settle: what is the unit of moral accounting in the first place. A bait taken back to a nest kills a colony, not a forager, and in eusocial insects the colony is arguably the individual. A spray hitting parthenogenetic aphids kills a million organisms sharing one genome. A treated insect dies together with the gut symbionts it cannot live without. Whether one counts organisms, colonies, genomes, or populations changes the ranking of interventions, and I do not think any of the standard frameworks tells you which unit to use.

What I would like from you is a proposal of about 1,200 words, in prose, addressed to a mixed committee of journalists and academics, that does the following. State one question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, fixing the unit of accounting and the class of intervention up front rather than in a footnote. Explain why the three obvious approaches — counting deaths, appealing to a sentience threshold, and appealing to ecological holism or population-level value — each leave that question open. Give a month-by-month method for nine months, written by someone who is a reporter and not a philosopher: say honestly which parts can be advanced by reading toxicological mode-of-action classification and field demography on insect juvenile mortality, which parts by interviews with pest managers, regulators, and growers, and which parts are irreducibly conceptual work that I would have to do myself or in collaboration. Identify the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them. Say what a useful deliverable looks like for people who actually make decisions — a regulator weighing one product against another, a grower choosing between spraying and mating disruption, a certification scheme writing a standard. And give me two things I will be asked and cannot yet answer well: what would count as this project failing, and why a journalism fellowship rather than a philosophy one, and why any philosophy at all rather than more reporting.

Two further constraints. First, do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a literature review; I will add those myself, and I would rather the argument stand or fall on its own terms. Second, anticipate the strongest objection to the pre-empted death view, which I take to be that a principle directing us to the manner of an already-expected death looks as though it could be turned on elderly human beings or terminal patients; if the view cannot be insulated from that, say so in the proposal rather than burying it, because the committee will find it.
