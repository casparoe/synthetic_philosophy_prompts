I belong to an interdisciplinary reading group on the physics of information — two philosophers of physics, a condensed-matter experimentalist, a computer architect, and a convener who has an unnerving way of closing discussions with the sentence *information is physical*. Over the last three sessions I have heard him use that one line to do three quite different pieces of work, and I no longer think all three can be the same claim.

The case that matters most to me is the one I'll call the annalist demon. Take the usual Szilard-style setup: a gas in a vessel at temperature T, partitioned by a shutter, with a controller that measures whether a molecule is on the left or the right and opens or closes the shutter so as to sort fast molecules to one side, converting information into work. The familiar exorcism we read about is that the controller must store its measurement outcomes, that a finite memory must eventually be reset to a standard state, and that this reset is logically irreversible and so carries a thermodynamic price — on the order of kT ln 2 per bit cleared. One member of our group then proposed a variant: give the controller an indefinitely extendable tape, let it write each outcome onto fresh tape it has never touched, and never erase anything. There is no reset step, so, he argued, there is no dissipation, and the second law appears to be beaten by a demon that simply keeps records. Our convener's reply was that information is physical: every mark on the tape is a physical state, the tape must be manufactured and must run out, and the erased-bit cost is merely deferred to whoever clears or replaces it, so the accounting is safe either way. The proponent replied that copying onto tape known to be blank is in principle reversible in the way reversible computation is — the operation can be undone by comparing — so writing need not dissipate, and that an externalized memory is not part of the working substance anyway.

The same slogan was then used twice more. In one session it was offered as support for the claim that Shannon entropy and thermodynamic entropy are the same quantity, from which it was said to follow that a disk full of ordered zeros is thermodynamically colder than a disk full of random bits, and that wiping a drive measurably warms the room. In another it was offered as support for the claim that since every physical system registers information about its surroundings, every physical system computes, so the universe is literally a computer; and it was extended to the assertion that you cannot get net work out of information, so the laboratory feedback devices that apparently convert measurement records into extracted work must be getting their energy from somewhere ordinary and are being misdescribed when people say the work comes from the information.

What I want is a disambiguation rather than a defence of one reading. Please lay out, as a numbered menu ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the audacious, the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing — the claim that information must always be realized in a physical substrate; that there is no such thing as a free abstract bit; that logical irreversibility entails thermodynamic dissipation; that Shannon and thermodynamic entropy are one and the same; that every physical process is a computation; that the world's fundamental furniture is information rather than matter; and whatever else belongs on such a list. For each entry, give a formulation sharp enough that someone could coherently deny it, then say whether you take it to be trivial, true and substantive, contested, or straightforwardly false, and say which of the three uses above it would actually license if it were true. I am especially interested in which readings do real work in the annalist case and which merely sound relevant.

Then, in a few closing paragraphs, give me something I can say at the next meeting. On the annalist demon specifically: is the deferred-cost reply the right one, or is the better reply that an endless supply of blank tape is itself a low-entropy resource whose preparation costs at least as much as the work extracted, or is the right point rather about cyclicity — that a system which never returns to its initial state has not been shown to violate anything — or about an information-theoretic bound on how much work a single record can underwrite before the books balance? If more than one of these is correct, say how they relate and which one actually answers the member's argument rather than changing the subject. And please flag which of the two further uses survives on the defensible readings, and which collapses once the slogan is precisified.
