my flatmate's a med student and every time well-being comes up he says "well-being is basically just health, isn't it" and then uses that to settle the whole experience machine debate. The version of the argument I keep getting: health isn't a feeling -- you can have something silently wrong with you and feel fine, and you can feel awful from something harmless -- so if welfare were just a matter of how things feel from the inside, the machine would obviously be the best life available; since we balk at plugging in, welfare must have experience-independent, objective components, and out pops some kind of objective-list theory. He also says refusing to plug in is no different from refusing pain relief on the grounds that it "only masks the symptom".

I think the analogy is carrying more than he thinks, and I want a proper audit rather than another argument at the kitchen table.

First the mapping: what plays the patient's self-report, the biomarker, the symptom, the incidental finding on a scan, the placebo, the "feels fine, is dying" case, the doctor's authority? Lay it out explicitly. Then the breaks, and which of them actually matter for the job he's using it for. The ones I can't get straight on my own:

- Health gets measured against something like a functional standard for the organism (and even that isn't purely statistical -- the whole "is infertility a disease" fight shows the line is partly drawn by what we disvalue). Is there anything analogous for a life, or does the analogy just borrow health's agreed standard and quietly hand it to well-being?
- A lot of health's importance looks instrumental -- being healthy is good because of what it lets you do and feel. If it's instrumental, the analogy seems to prove the hedonist's point instead of his. If it's partly constitutive, which part, and why?
- The inverse case: a body that is genuinely in great shape, but whose owner's experience is unrelenting pain and exhaustion. Under the health reading they're flourishing; under an experience reading they're not. Which way does the analogy push, and is that a cost?

Now the bit I made up to stress-test it: the resort version of the machine. Same as Nozick's, except the pod also keeps your real body genuinely healthy -- it moves you, feeds you, does the physio, so the biomarkers are excellent. The health complaint against plugging in is now fully answered. Does his analogy have to say the resort is fine? And if he wants to still say no, the extra ingredient he needs looks like a "contact with reality" requirement -- which health, as such, doesn't seem to have any room for. Say whether that's the real load-bearing intuition all along, and whether the analogy was smuggling it in.

End by telling me whether the conclusion survives -- that the machine refutes experience-based theories and supports an objective list -- or whether it survives only in a weaker form, and if there's a better analogy for what welfare is supposed to be, what is it.
