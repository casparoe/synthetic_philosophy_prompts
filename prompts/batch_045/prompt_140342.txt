I am trying to work out how much of expected-utility maximisation is genuinely delivered by the incoherence arguments, and how much is delivered by substantive assumptions that get carried in unnoticed along the way. I would like a number, and I would like the reasoning behind it laid out where I can poke at it.

What is your credence in the following proposition?

P: The coherence-style arguments for expected-utility maximisation — money pumps, Dutch books, and exploitability or dynamic-inconsistency arguments generally — suffice, taken on their own and without leaning on any substantive assumption that is not itself already a requirement of coherence, to establish the full expected-utility representation as a requirement of rationality for a single agent choosing under risk: completeness and transitivity of preference, continuity, dominance, and in particular independence.

So that the proposition has something like a truth value, take it to be resolved by the settled verdict of the literature twenty years from now: count it true if the dominant view is that independence itself is derivable from considerations of coherence, exploitability or dynamic consistency alone; count it false if the dominant view is that independence needs support from something extra — a separability assumption, a consequentialist constraint on how preferences over sequences relate to preferences over outcomes, or an argument from the value of planning — which a critic could coherently reject without thereby counting as irrational.

To make one of the sub-questions precise, let me introduce two stipulations. Call an agent weakly sequentially exploitable if there exists a finite sequence of trades with a willing counterparty such that at every step the agent strictly prefers, by her own lights at that step, the bundle on offer to the bundle she currently holds, while the bundle she ends up with is one she ranks strictly below the bundle she started with, or strictly below a bundle she could have secured simply by refusing the first trade. Call an agent plan-exploitable if, in addition, she would at the outset accept the plan of undergoing the whole sequence rather than refusing it outright. The money-pump argument against cyclic preferences delivers weak sequential exploitability; the interesting question is what exactly the dynamic arguments against violations of independence deliver, and whether what they deliver is a defect of reasoning at all.

Please decompose your credence over the sub-questions you think actually carry the weight. The cuts I would propose, as candidates you may replace, are:

1. That the arguments establish even the structural axioms from coherence alone, rather than from an implicit assumption that the agent's preferences are defined over a rich enough domain to include composite and plan-level objects.
2. That every violation of independence yields weak sequential exploitability, as opposed to yielding only plan-exploitability together with the claim that the agent ought to evaluate sequences separably across steps — the worry being that the separability premise is very close to what is in dispute.
3. That weak sequential exploitability is a mark of irrationality rather than merely of imprudence, or merely of having non-separable preferences over sequences: that a bookie's ability to extract from you reveals a defect in your reasoning and not only in your bank balance.
4. That the resolute chooser is in worse shape than her critics say — that an agent who adopts a plan she knows she will later want to abandon, and sticks to it, is properly described as incoherent or as rationally defective, rather than as coherent-but-exploitable, or as fully rational.
5. That the class of decision rules left standing by these arguments is exactly expected-utility maximisation, rather than a broader family of non-expected-utility rules that are not stepwise exploitable, so that coherence constrains the shape of rational choice without pinning it down.
6. That avoiding sure loss, or avoiding being exploitable by a well-informed counterparty, is constitutive of practical rationality at all, for an agent who is indifferent to money and unconcerned at being predicted.

For each term, give a number and one line of justification, then show how you combine them and say whether a simple product is appropriate here. Then tell me which single term you are least confident in and what argument or result would move that term most; and, if the terms do not multiply to your headline number, say why not.
