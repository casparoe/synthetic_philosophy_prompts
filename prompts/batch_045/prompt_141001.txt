I ended up helping out at a joint philosophy/CS thing my department runs every spring — a public "imitation game" evening. Undergrads from a first-year class get course credit to sit at a terminal and interrogate a hidden interlocutor for twenty minutes over typed chat; the interlocutor is either our department's fine-tuned open-weights model or a human we hired through a temp agency. I was mostly there to hand out lanyards and reboot the terminals between rounds. This year the model fooled 7 of the 12 interrogators, and afterwards two of the organizers — Marco, who built the fine-tune, and Dana, a philosophy PhD student who helped design the protocol — got into it about what we're allowed to claim in the write-up for the university newsletter. I've typed up roughly what they said and I honestly can't tell who's winning.

(1) Marco: look at the numbers. Around chance-level identification was the null result in Turing's original set-up, and the whole point of that set-up was to replace the unanswerable question "can machines think?" with an operational one. We got 7 out of 12. If your response is "sure, but it doesn't really think," you owe me the question you're asking instead — and every replacement I've heard ("does it understand," "is there anyone home," "is it just pattern-matching") just smuggles back the intuition the test was built to get us out of. (2) Dana: two problems. First the comparison class: our human confederates were told to be 'neutral and concise' and were typing in a noisy room with undergrads, so the bar was depressed-human, not human. Second, and more important, the thing that passed was trained on essentially all of human text. Fluency bought from storage is exactly the output you'd get with nobody home, so the inference from output to source — which is the only reason we cared about the output — is broken. (3) Marco: then cash out "nobody home" in something I could in principle detect, or it isn't a claim, it's a mood. And we run on stored material too; most of what any of us say is recomposed fragments of things we've heard. Give me a line between recombining what you've ingested and speaking for yourself that doesn't bottom out in yet more behavior and I'll take this seriously. (4) Dana: agreed, it's hard to draw in the abstract — so don't, change the experiment instead. Append a cross-examination phase: same interlocutor, but after ten minutes the interrogator switches and requires the subject to (a) say in its own words what the interrogator meant by one of their questions, (b) flag which of its own earlier answers it's least confident in and say why, and (c) keep the conversation going after the interrogator inverts a rule the subject itself stated earlier ("from now on answer as if you'd argued the opposite"). Chatting is the cheap part; tracking what the other person is after, owning the weak spots in your own performance, and re-entering under reversed rules are the expensive parts, and they're what the chat phase was supposed to be evidence for. (5) Marco: that's the Turing test with extra steps, and they're exactly the steps you'd pick if you already knew which system you wanted to fail. Also, plenty of humans would flunk your cross-examination phase — the inarticulate, the exhausted, second-language speakers, people with certain cognitive disabilities. A bar that excludes humans we happily count as minded isn't a test for mind, it's a ranking of how glib you are. (6) Dana: fair, and I'll grant that failing it can't be decisive — because what matters is the shape of the failure. A person who loses the thread notices, says "wait, you've lost me," asks for the question again, or refuses and explains why. So the datum isn't pass/fail, it's how it comes apart. And here's a second guard: run the identical protocol on something you already know has nothing going on inside it — a plain retrieval system over the same corpus, or this model with its record of the conversation wiped after every turn. If the protocol can't tell that thing from the candidate, it isn't measuring anything. (7) Marco: now you've quietly changed projects. A negative control is what you demand when you want a mechanism rather than a behavior: you want to see the generator and check it's the right kind of generator. Maybe that's right, but notice the price — we have no mechanism-level assurance for each other either. I take you to be conscious because you're like me in the respects I can observe, plus the one case I know from the inside. That's inference to the best explanation from behavior all the way down. So either the test is enough or nothing short of a finished theory of mind is, and nobody has one. (8) Dana: then let's at least write up honestly. "Indistinguishable from a human interlocutor in a twenty-minute typed exchange with unpaid amateur interrogators" I'll sign. "Passed the Turing test, therefore it thinks" is a slogan with our whole argument hidden inside it.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall? I'd like each of the eight moves rated from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a line or two of justification, including where someone concedes too much or too little. And please tell me what both of them are missing: is there a version of Dana's cross-examination or negative-control demand that survives Marco's "glibness" and "we have no mechanism for each other either" objections, or does the whole exchange show the behavioral criterion can't be patched from inside? Don't just tell me it depends what we mean by "thinking" — say which moves actually survive.
