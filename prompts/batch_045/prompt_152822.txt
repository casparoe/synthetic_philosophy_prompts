The setup. I want to separate the philosophical question from the sociological one, so set aside what you actually know about the real literature (including the familiar worry that experimental subject pools are WEIRD — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) and take the following as stipulated. Call it the layered-divergence picture:

Across a genuinely representative sample of the world's societies — small-scale and large, literate and oral, market-integrated and not — moral judgments have a stable layered structure. At the first layer there is near-universal agreement about which kinds of consideration are morally relevant at all: harm to others, reciprocal fairness, loyalty to local groups, deference to legitimate authority, bodily and spiritual purity, and liberty from interference. No sampled society treats any of these as morally irrelevant, and none adds a kind of consideration that is not recognizably a specification of one of them. At the second layer there is sharp, systematic, lawful disagreement about how these considerations are to be weighted against one another when they conflict: how much loyalty is owed for a given unit of prevented harm, how much liberty for a given unit of purity, and so on. This disagreement is not noise. It correlates with ecological and economic variables (pathogen load, dependence on collective action for subsistence, degree of market integration); it is stable within a society across generations; and it survives changes in how the questions are asked.

Two arguments I want you to place inside the tree rather than paraphrase. The first I will call the continuity argument: the space of weightings looks like a continuous surface with no distinguished point, and between any two societies there is a chain of small weight-shifts, each of which is the sort of adjustment competent agents make in ordinary life without supposing they were previously mistaken about a matter of fact — so either there is a true weighting that nobody has any reason to think they are close to, or the question of which weighting is correct has no answer, and the second option better explains both the phenomenology and the epistemology. The second I will call the calibration reply: the weights are not arbitrary but track the local optimum of a single objective function — the expected value of some impartial good under local ecological constraints — so the variation is contextual application of one standard rather than disagreement, in the way that recommended calorie intake or the sensible level of trust in strangers varies with environment under one standard of what is good.

The proposition I want a credence in. Define robust moral realism as the conjunction of two claims: there are stance-independent moral facts about how the relevant considerations are to be weighted; and human beings in ordinary circumstances are capable of getting at least some of these right. Give me your credence that robust moral realism, so defined, is true, conditional on the layered-divergence picture being the whole of your evidence about moral diversity, plus ordinary background knowledge.

The deliverable:
