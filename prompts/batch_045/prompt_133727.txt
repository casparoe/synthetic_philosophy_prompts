I am a software engineer with no philosophy background. My vocabulary for this stuff is "bias," "prior," and "circular," so please define any term of art you use in plain terms.

I help maintain a public knowledge base of claims about the pre-modern past, edited by volunteers and used by teachers and journalists. Our editorial rulebook is inherited and nobody has ever written down what it assumes. It says, roughly:

R1. Every claim carries a tier: Established, Probable, Disputed, or Rejected.

R2. Each ancient source has a reliability weight, set by its track record — how often its independently checkable statements have held up — measured against a "gold set" of claims the editors already treat as settled.

R3. Interest asymmetry: a statement that damages the cause of the source, or of the group the source speaks for, can be carried by a single source of any weight. A statement that flatters that cause needs three independent sources, or one source plus physical corroboration.

R4. Silence: if a well-informed contemporary who had every reason to mention something says nothing about it, drop the claim one tier. The same rulebook also says that a gap in the record is never evidence that something did not happen, because most writing and most objects are simply lost.

R5. Anachronism veto: if a claim requires a technology, a word, an institution, or a practice that is not attested before a certain date, reject it no matter how many sources repeat it — a source cannot report what it had no way of knowing. Our editors describe R5 with the slogan be Thucydides, not Herodotus, invoking the old contrast between Herodotus passing along what he was told and Thucydides restricting himself to what he witnessed or could check with witnesses.

What I want from you:

1. What view of testimony, evidence, and knowledge of the past does this rulebook as a whole presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory in terms I could hand back to my co-editors.
2. Which rules strictly require that theory, and which merely sit well with it? Where is the rulebook philosophically underdetermined — where would two quite different theories both license the same rule?
3. Where do the rules pull against each other? I suspect at least three places: the gold set in R2 is built out of claims established by these very sources, so the weights look circular; R4 contains two clauses that seem to contradict one another; and R3 treats bias as a property of a source rather than of a topic, so we end up discounting a writer's astronomy for his sins in politics.
4. R5 is what we use against submissions from people pushing sunken continents, ancient visitors from space, or the claim that the conventional chronology of antiquity is itself a later fabrication. They reply that R5 simply reasserts the chronology they are disputing. Our standing answer is that R5 assumes no dates, only that a report cannot outrun what the reporter could have known. Is that a real distinction or a dodge? And if we deleted R5, would anything left in the rulebook still let us reject those claims?
5. My instinct as an engineer is to replace the whole thing with scoring: put a number on each source's reliability and on how likely the report would be if the claim were false, combine, update. Tell me concretely what that would change in our practice and what it cannot capture — especially what happens to the "independent" sources in R3 when their independence is really just different copyists working from one lost text.
