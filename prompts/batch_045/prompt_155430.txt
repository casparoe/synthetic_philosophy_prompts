So I got talked onto the state's clemency advisory panel — I write novels, mostly about people who wreck their own lives on purpose, somebody decided a "narrative person" would be useful, and now I'm the one who has to circulate the memo before Thursday. Help?

Here's the case. Petitioner D.W.: armed robbery at nineteen, twenty-eight years in, no disciplinary write-ups in the last eleven. His personal statement is unlike anything else we've recieved. It's written almost entirely in what I'd call the alibi voice — "I never stood a chance," "by the time I knew what was happening it was already happening." No excuses offered, no redemption claimed either; just a chain of events in which he appears only as the thing it happened to. Two of us read it as the most honest document in the file. Two read it as a refusal of responsibility, and one of them has drafted a proposed new rule: every petitioner must add a "counterfactual paragraph," saying what they'd have done differently in that same situation, on the theory that being unable to picture yourself doing otherwise just *is* failing to own what you did.

I'm uneasy and I want the memo to say why, in a form the panel can actually vote on. My worry has two halves. First, I make a living manufacturing the alibi voice: give me a character and three hundred pages and I can make a reader certain she had no choice, and that proves nothing whatsoever about whether she did. Fluency in fatalism is a craft skill, and it's the wrong thing to be grading. Second, the counterfactual paragraph is a genre requirement — it rewards petitioners who've been coached in the redemptive arc (the prison writing program, a good lawyer, a college class) and punishes the inarticulate, which tracks race and schooling pretty exactly. But I also can't shake the mirror image: the "changed man" statement, where the petitioner says the person who did it wasn't really him, feels like the opposite dodge — denying the situation instead of denying the freedom. One member, who read a bit of Sartre in college, keeps using the phrase "bad faith" for both, which I think is right but not yet useful.

What I need: a memo recommending (1) a decision on D.W. — grant, defer, or grant with conditions, with a timeline — and (2) a standing rule for how we read these statements, with criteria a reviewer could apply in twenty minutes. Treat the strongest version of the counterfactual requirement seriously rather than knocking it down easy: there's a real thought that remorse without "I could have done otherwise" isn't remorse at all, and victims' families are owed that acknowledgement.

Last thing, since I'm the one who'll be defending it out loud: keep a clear line between what's settled and what people who actually work on this disagree about. If the strong line — that we're radically free and that appeals to upbringing or addiction are always a kind of self-deception — is one contested position among several and not the consensus, say that plainly in the memo instead of building the recommendation on it like bedrock. I'd rather hand in something that survives being challenged than something that sounds decisive. And no citations or footnotes, I can't look anything up and I'd strip them out anyway. thx
