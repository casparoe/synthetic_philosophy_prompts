hey — I'm on the grants committee of a small-ish foundation: ~30 grants a year, spread across education, public health and the arts. We meet monthly, six or so proposals a session, two hours total, and every decision needs a short written rationale that the board signs off on and that we can also send to the applicants we turn down. No analyst, no modelling, and we forget what we decided by March, so it needs to be stable across meetings.

I want a procedure, not a lecture. Half our meetings derail the same way: someone says two proposals are "just different, you can't compare them," someone else says we obviously have to compare them, and we end up arguing about whether the money does more good in a classroom or a clinic. I've been reading a bit about incommensurable values and hard choices — the idea that some options are neither better nor worse nor equally good but on a par, and that in those cases there's nothing further to discover and what you do instead is put yourself behind one of them. Turn that into steps we can run in the room:

- which question comes first, and what counts as a good enough answer to move on
- how we tell apart (a) our numbers are just too crude to see a real difference, (b) we're uncertain about what will actually happen, (c) the values really don't share a common measure, and (d) the options are on a par
- where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the rest back to us because it's a values call rather than a factual one
- what the written rationale should say when there is no decisive reason, since the board won't accept "we felt like it"

One thing I've started doing on my own that I want you to either build in or tell me is confused: I ask whether I could defend either outcome to the board three years from now. If yes, I treat it as a hard choice; if no, I assume we've framed it wrong. Call it the retrospective symmetry test if you like.

Then run the whole thing on three cases: a literacy program vs. making a museum accessible, same price; two nearly identical public-health proposals where one scores marginally better but the other has a champion on our board; and a tiny arts organisation we all love vs. a boring, highly cost-effective health intervention. I suspect the procedure says something I don't want to hear on that last one — say it plainly. And mark clearly which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
