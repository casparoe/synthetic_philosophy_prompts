I'm the lay member of the Emerging Genetics Policy Group at the Merrivale Fertility Service, part of a publicly funded hospital trust; we advise the trust board on which embryo-testing practices the service may offer and on what conditions. We have now deadlocked twice on a case that has become urgent, and the chair has asked me to draft the decision memo the board will vote on this month, which I've agreed to do largely because I can't make up my own mind and would rather defend a document than a hunch.

The immediate trigger is a request from two patients, Rachel Okonjo and Daniel Ferris, who have five euploid blastocysts in storage and a transfer date in three weeks. They ask for two things. First, that the lab not transfer whichever embryo carries the highest polygenic liability score for bipolar disorder: Daniel's older brother has a severe, treatment-resistant form of the illness, Daniel describes his own childhood as having been organised around it, and he says he could not go through that again as a parent. Second, among the remaining embryos, that the lab transfer the one with the highest polygenic score for educational attainment — they are both teachers, they expect to provide a book-filled, well-resourced home, and they say plainly that if avoiding a disabling psychiatric condition is acceptable, then improving a child's prospects is the very same act, differing only in degree. Rachel adds a point that bothered several of us: whichever embryo is transferred, the child who results would not otherwise have existed, so no one is made worse off by the choice, and it therefore isn't obvious what the objection to either request is supposed to be. Two policy proposals are on the table. The first, which the clinical director calls the treatment test, would permit a score to influence transfer order only where the predicted condition is one this service would itself treat or try to prevent if the child were born with it; this grants the first request and refuses the second. The second proposal, backed by two of our scientific members, is a no-maximising rule: the lab may use a fixed panel of scores to order embryos from lower to higher aggregate health risk, may never use scores to maximise any trait, and may not disclose the numbers or the ordering to patients, who are simply offered transfers in the lab's recommended sequence. Its backers argue this captures whatever is defensible about risk reduction while refusing to turn embryo selection into a ranking of children, and that withholding the numbers also withholds a false precision the scores do not have. Our legal officer replies that this is paternalistic, that patients who want the numbers will get them from a private clinic abroad where they will receive no counselling and no caveats at all, and that a rule we cannot enforce mainly exports the practice to people wealthier than our patients. Three things complicate both proposals. Our lab director has told us the score panels were trained mostly on European-ancestry samples, so for embryos of partly Nigerian ancestry like these the predictions are much weaker and the ranking could in principle be worse than a random choice; that siblings share most of the variants these scores aggregate, so differences between embryos from the same two parents are small compared with differences between unrelated people and the confidence intervals largely overlap; and that a score for educational attainment partly tracks the environment these particular parents are likely to provide rather than anything intrinsic to the embryo. And two members raise the expressive objection: permitting the first request while refusing the second looks like an official judgement that a life shaped by bipolar disorder is a worse life, and the medical/social line is unstable anyway, since the traits in the second request predict health outcomes as strongly as many things we call medical.

Please draft the memo I will circulate, addressed to the trust board: roughly 900 to 1,100 words, with a clear bottom line rather than a survey. It should recommend one of the two proposals or a specified hybrid, state the conditions the service should attach, and give a time frame for review; it should include an interim determination on Rachel and Daniel's own case, since their transfer date will not wait for policy, with reasons they could be told to their faces; it should say what if anything the lab must disclose to patients about accuracy before offering any of this, and whether the service should offer polygenic scoring to mixed-ancestry embryos at all given what the lab has told us; and it should give the strongest version of the argument for whichever option it rejects, not a version trimmed to be easy to answer. Take a position on whether the no-difference point Rachel makes really does neutralise the objections, or whether it instead removes the point of the whole exercise. Do not cite literature or name authors; the board will not follow references, and I need the argument to stand on its own.
