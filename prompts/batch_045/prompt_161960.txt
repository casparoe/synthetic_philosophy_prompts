I organise a Saturday-afternoon meetup for my local rationality community — usually 12 to 16 people, mostly programmers and data scientists with two or three philosophy undergrads mixed in, one room, 90 minutes, nothing but a whiteboard, and a props budget of index cards and poker chips. Last month we ran a calibration exercise and it went well, so they are used to writing down numbers and being scored on them. For next month I want them to run into the Sleeping Beauty problem themselves: no lecture up front, the name not spoken until the debrief, and I would rather they discover that a question they were all confident about has a real disagreement inside it than hear me tell them that camps called halfers and thirders exist. Three things I want them to come away seeing: that the dispute is not really about the coin but about what counts as an equally relevant possibility or centre; that when the number of decisions you get to make depends on the outcome, a naive one-half price can be systematically exploited; and that it is a further, non-obvious step from this price loses chips to this credence is wrong.

Here is my sketch, which I think is broken in places and want you to fix or replace. Phase 1: everyone plays Beauty at once. I announce a fair coin, heads means one awakening, tails means two, the amnesia procedure wipes each session. At each awakening every participant posts a single sealed number — the price in chips at which they would buy a ticket paying 100 chips if the coin landed heads — and I, as the house, fill the order at every awakening with all purchases standing, and I am free to take either side of whatever price they post. Settle in chips across several runs and keep a leaderboard. Phase 2, the part I am least sure about: keep the fair coin but make the tails awakenings an unknown number — I roll a die behind a screen at the start of each experiment and wake them that many times on tails without ever telling them the number, so the count the possible centres and divide heuristic has nothing to count and the one-half answer and the counting answer come dramatically apart. I am genuinely not certain what the correct price is in that variant, and I half suspect the construction begs the question by building the outcome-dependent number of bets into the payoff rather than testing it — tell me honestly if so and what to do instead. Phase 3: flip the roles, so each pair writes down a strategy that extracts chips from someone who always posts 50, and then checks whether the same strategy still works against someone who posts 33. We obviously cannot put anyone to sleep, so I need a way to stage awakenings with cards or envelopes that makes the information loss explicit rather than merely asserted.
