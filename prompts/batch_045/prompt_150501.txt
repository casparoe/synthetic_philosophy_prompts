I'm retired now — most of my working life was comparative physiology, measuring things in animals, and I've drifted into a philosophy reading group by accident. We got onto the moral status of animals last week and I've been chewing on one corner of it since.

Here's the claim I want a number on, stated as precisely as I can: suppose we had to rank animals by how much moral consideration they're owed, and we were allowed exactly one physical measurement of the nervous system to rank them by. My claim is that the number of neurons in the associative forebrain (or in the bird equivalent of it) is a better thing to rank by than whole-brain mass or volume. Not the stronger claim that brain size is morally irrelevant — I can see the argument that mass is a decent proxy for the thing that matters — just that neuron count beats it. Count as settled, for my purposes, if you can tell me which of the two measurements tracks whatever feature is actually doing the moral work, and how much of the disagreement between me and my group is really about which feature that is.

My own credence in the neuron-count claim is about 0.6. One person in the group put it at 0.9 and another at 0.3, and I can't tell whether they disagree about the neuroscience or about the philosophy, which is what I want the decomposition for.

So: give me your number, but decomposed. Here are the sub-questions I think it hangs on, though add or replace as you see fit — I'd rather have your structure than mine:

1. Is moral status in animals a single scalar thing that can be ranked on one scale at all, or is it several partly independent things (capacity for suffering, agency, future-directedness, being the subject of a life, being in relationships) that don't collapse into one number?
2. Conditional on there being a scale, does it come down to amount or richness of valenced experience at a time, or does it also respond to structural/cognitive features that aren't about how experience feels?
3. Conditional on it being experience-driven, does the capacity scale with something countable and roughly additive in cells, or is it a system-level property — a matter of architecture, recurrence, integration, global dynamics — that isn't linear in cell number at all?
4. Conditional on countable: is the right currency neuron number, or synapse number, packing density, conduction velocity and metabolic rate, or something like information-processing capacity per unit time?
5. The empirical bit: how well does each candidate actually track the behavioural and anatomical evidence we'd independently take as signs of rich experience, across the cases that make this hard — corvids and parrots versus big-brained mammals, cetaceans, elephants, cephalopods, insects?
6. Shape of the curve: smooth scaling, or a threshold where the property switches on? This is what decides whether insects and lab-grown neural tissue are anywhere near the scale at all.

Two cases I invented to isolate the variable, which I'd like you to actually use rather than wave at:

(a) Species M has the same neuron number, the same cell types and the same wiring diagram as a cognitively normal large-brained animal, but its neurons are physically miniaturized and packed tighter, so its brain is a tenth of the mass. Same behaviour, same everything else. Does an individual of M matter less? If you say no, then mass as such is doing zero work and I want to know what you think is doing it instead; if you say yes, I want to know what exactly was lost in the shrinking, since by stipulation nothing about connectivity was.

(b) A piece of cortical tissue grown in a dish, which we can grow to any neuron number we like — ten thousand cells up to tens of millions. If neuron count is the currency, there ought to be a defensible answer about where along that gradient moral consideration begins. Give me a number for where, or tell me why the question is malformed.

One hedge: I may be misremembering the neuroanatomy. I recall a group that counts brain cells reporting that elephants have roughly three times as many neurons as we do but that almost all of them sit in the cerebellum, and that crows and parrots pack far more neurons into a gram of forebrain than primates do. If I've got that wrong, correct it and reason from whatever the truth is — the argument shape is what I care about, not the exact figures. And please don't give me references or a reading list; I can't chase them up and I don't want them.

Then combine the terms into one final number, tell me which single sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and tell me where my 0.6 and my friend's 0.9 most likely come apart. Last thing, and I care about this as much as the number: what would change your mind — which argument or finding would move you off it — and where is your own reasoning most likely to be failing?
