I work as a researcher at a small grant-making foundation, and our programme team is drafting an internal strategy note on where to place long-run bets on moral progress. A disagreement has crystallised around the expanding-circle picture, and I would like that picture audited rather than simply applied.

Here is the work it is being asked to do. One of our trustees argues as follows. In the 1981 book of that name, Singer describes the circle of altruism as having broadened from the family and tribe to the nation and race, and holds that the only defensible stopping place is the point at which all beings capable of pleasure or pain are included. Our trustee infers that moral progress just is the outward movement of that boundary; that the urgent work at any given time is therefore work at the frontier, on candidates whose membership is still contested; and that once a group is inside, what remains is administrative — enforcement, compliance, measurement. On that basis he wants our portfolio tilted strongly toward (a) securing moral status for future digital minds, and away from (b) work on the treatment of beings already counted, farmed animals above all.

I have several reservations, and I cannot tell which are real disanalogies and which are complaints about the figure's cosmetics.

First, a circle has a single radius, so it presupposes one metric of distance from a centre. But the expansions people cite seem to run along different axes — similarity of kind, spatial distance, temporal distance, relatedness, and now substrate or architecture — and it is not obvious that these are commensurable, or that there is one boundary moving outward rather than several boundaries moving independently, some of them in opposite directions.

Second, the figure draws a sharp inside/outside line, whereas actual practice looks graded: farmed animals are arguably inside the circle and yet are given a small fraction of the weight given to a human. The frontier looks more like a gradient than a perimeter, and the picture seems to have no way to say this.

Third, a colleague raises what she calls the dilution problem: if what there is to spread is finite, then a widening circle can be progress when measured radially — the boundary moves — while being stagnation or regression when measured by the weight accorded to each included being, or by how they actually fare. It looks to me as though the last two centuries widened the circle while enormously intensifying what is done to beings inside it, industrial animal agriculture being the clearest case; and the figure has no vocabulary for that at all, because it only tracks where the boundary is.

Fourth, the limit case. If the telos is impartial equal consideration, the radius goes to infinity, the centre loses its privilege and the boundary degenerates into a straight line: the figure stops being a circle exactly when the process it describes succeeds. My colleague says this shows the metaphor was only ever heuristic. Our trustee says it shows that full impartiality is incoherent. I do not trust either inference.

There are also two things I may have wrong about the source, and I would rather be corrected than continue on them. I have been summarising the book to myself as claiming that reason is the engine of expansion and feeling is what has to be overcome, so that the circle is essentially an argument from rationality; my colleague says that inverts it, and that reason universalises altruistic impulses rather than replacing them, so the picture depends on there being sentiments to extend. Relatedly, I am not sure whether the circle tracks motivation — whom we are disposed to help — or status — whose interests count — and our portfolio question is about status, not about anyone's dispositions.

What I am asking for: an explicit table of correspondences (centre, radius, boundary, inside, expansion, and whatever plays the role of the driver of expansion); then a separation of the disanalogies that bear on our (a)-versus-(b) choice from the ones that are merely cosmetic; then a verdict on whether the trustee's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the figure was carrying it the whole time; and, if there is one, a better representational device — something that registers both the width and the weight of concern, and contraction as well as expansion.
