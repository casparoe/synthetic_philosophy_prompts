I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the sequences days and I follow the alignment writing fairly closely, but my day job is in flight controls — I spend my working hours on state-space models, LQR cost functions, setpoint regulation, controllability Gramians, gain and phase margins, and disturbance rejection. At a conference dinner last month I tried to explain to two colleagues what people mean by impact measures and side-effect avoidance, and I botched it badly: I ended up saying something like "you penalize the agent for changing the world," one of them reasonably asked where the penalty term comes from, and I couldn't answer without either helping myself to a full specification of what is valuable or retreating to hand-waving about reasonable versus unreasonable changes. I'd like a version I could actually present to a room of controls engineers with no background in any of this.

So: please restate the low-impact problem in the vocabulary I actually use — reference trajectories and setpoints, cost-to-go, control-effort penalties, reachable sets and controllability, robustness margins, disturbance rejection, the distinction between transient excursion and steady-state offset — as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. Concretely, I want translations of roughly five things: (1) the inaction baseline, and why choosing it is said to smuggle in value; (2) impact measures that compare reachable-set volume, or the agent's own future ability to achieve other goals, before and after its action — the empowerment, relative-reachability, and attainable-utility flavours of approach; (3) offsetting or attenuation, meaning penalizing only changes the agent cannot undo; (4) interruptibility and corrigibility; (5) the pessimistic claim that none of this can be defined without something close to a complete specification of value. For each, say what maps cleanly onto my vocabulary, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in it at all. One hard constraint: don't let the translation quietly assume the cost function is known. In my world the cost is given and the interesting object is the controller, whereas the whole difficulty here seems to be that the cost is precisely what we don't have, so if a step of your translation presupposes a known cost, flag that as smuggled rather than passing it off as translation.

Then the part I actually care about: whether the residue tells us something. Is avoid-side-effects just minimum-effort regulation to a reference trajectory with the setpoint deleted, and is the research program best described as control theory with the cost function removed? I have a candidate fix I've been turning over, and I want it stress-tested rather than affirmed. Instead of penalizing the total divergence between the actual world trajectory and the no-agent counterfactual, penalize the rate at which that divergence grows — how hard the agent is currently bending the trajectory, not how far it ends up bent. In my vocabulary that is roughly a penalty on the derivative of the error, a D-term or a jerk penalty, and it's attractive to me because it seems to require no account of which end states are good, only a notion of how fast the world is being pushed around. Tell me what this becomes in control terms, whether it genuinely escapes the you-need-a-full-value-specification objection or merely relocates that objection into the choice of metric, norm, and time window, and what its characteristic failure mode is. My own worry is that it simply licenses slow-motion catastrophe — arbitrarily large total change performed gently — but I can't tell whether that is a real objection or the same bad intuition that makes people think gradual takeovers are benign. If it fails, I want the diagnosis in my own terms; if a nearby variant survives, I want that variant instead. Finally, say explicitly which parts of the alignment researchers' disagreement have no counterpart in control vocabulary at all, and whether that is because my vocabulary is missing something or because the disagreement is thinner than it sounds.
