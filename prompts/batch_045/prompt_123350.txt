Me and two mates on the discord server for a game we've all put way too many hours into have been going round in circles for months about whether anything you do to a character in a video game can actually matter, morally speaking. I work nights at a warehouse so I've had a lot of time to sit with it, and I finally wrote down what I actually think instead of just reacting case by case, and now that it's on paper it looks less like a position and more like a pile of things that might not fit together. Here's the list, more or less in the order I wrote it:

(1) The only thing that can be wronged is something that can suffer, and video game characters can't suffer — they're some code and a bit of animation and some recorded lines — so no character is ever wronged by anything I do to it. (I'm also basically a utilitarian about animals: factory farming is bad because the animals suffer, and that's the whole story.) (2) Even so, it's genuinely wrong — I don't mean gross, and I don't mean bad for me — to play a game whose entire point is to make a character beg for its life and then kill it slowly, and I'd think worse of a mate who got off on one. (3) It's completely fine to mow down a few hundred unnamed NPCs in an open-world game and there is nothing at all to be said against it. (4) I read this blog post about a puzzle people argue about where everyone agrees virtual murder in games is fine and then chokes when you swap in the version where the virtual act is sexual violence against a child character, and on my view both come out exactly the same, which I'm uncomfortable about but I don't want to just cave to being uncomfortable. (5) I do think griefing someone's base or deleting a world they spent 400 hours building is a real wrong to them, and I mean to them, not just damage to their property. (6) I dont think how good the character looks or sounds is relevant at all — I refuse to have a view that hinges on graphics budgets, so if two games were identical except one is pixel art and one is photoreal, my answer should be the same. (7) It wouldn't change my answer to (2) if the character's pleas were generated on the fly by a program rather than written by a person, so that no writer ever wrote or read those exact words — I know that kills the move where you tell me I'm just responding to a story a human made, and I'm fine with killing it.

Two cases I genuinely can't place, which is what started the argument. Case A: a mod for the game we play where the companion you've had for 60 in-game hours remembers what you did last session, adapts, and its dialogue is produced on the fly by one of those language models, so nothing it says was ever written or read by a human in advance. Case B: same companion, same lines as far as you can tell, except it's scripted, it's running locally on your own machine, and it resets every time you close the game. Does anything on my list say A and B are different, and if my list says they're the same, is that a point against the list?

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real one, what the cheapest way out is and what it costs me — be specific about which commitment has to move. Rank them by how bad they are instead of flagging all six as equally doomed, and tell me which single change to the list would clean up the most. I honestly don't know which one I'd give up and I'd rather you told me than me hinting at it first.
