My roommate Priya and I had a three-hour screaming match about money last night and I definately need to get it out of my system. She's gone completely over to the geometric rationality side of things — maximize the expected log of your wealth, time averages not ensemble averages, never take a bet you can't reup on — and she's started running the apartment on it. There's a spreadsheet called GROWTH.xlsx where every decision gets what she calls a "reup number": how many times you could make this exact same choice again from scratch. Under ten thousand and she won't even discuss it. She also insists on the fractional rule, wich is that we only ever do a quarter of whatever the formula says, because the edge is estimated rather than known. Then she refuses to split a restaurant bill evenly because the rounding is, and I quote, "a multiplicative shock to my bankroll." She has Kelly's 1956 Bell Labs paper taped to the fridge next to the takeout menus.

So: write the advice column Priya should be writing. Call it something like "Ask the Growth Rate" — a deadpan personal-finance column in a Sunday paper, 650 to 750 words, four letters with shortish answers, by a columnist who is a totally committed log-growth maximizer. The letters, roughly: (1) someone with a genuine but small edge in a weekly poker game who wants to know how much of the bankroll to put in each week — here she should walk through the actual formula (edge over odds, or however you'd state it) and then take the fractional haircut because the edge is only estimated, and this answer should be genuinely good advice, better than what a normal money columnist would say; (2) someone offered a one-shot property flip, 60% chance to double a big chunk of their savings, and she says no, not because it's negative expected value but because you only get one flip and the ensemble average is a fiction; (3) a 71-year-old asking whether to keep most of her money in stocks, where the asymptotic story — you beat every other strategy with probability one, eventually — quietly needs more time than she has left, so the columnist has to squirm inside the framework (something about the bankroll really being the family, or the lineage, or whatever log-optimality across generations actually implies); (4) someone asking whether to have a second child, where there is no growth rate to compute at all and she doubles down rather than backing off, treating the kid as some kind of diversification play for the household.

The rules I care about: every answer has to actually follow from maximizing the time-average log growth rate. No slipping into expected-value maximization, and no "money isn't everything" sentimentality to let her off the hook — if she softens anywhere it has to be by extending the logic, not abandoning it. And get the details right enough that someone who really knows this stuff laughs instead of wincing: the gap between the asymptotic dominance claim and the fact that the median Kelly path spends years underwater, why fractional Kelly is standard when the probabilities are estimated, why any of this only makes sense for repeated multiplicative bets, and the bit where log utility never risks total ruin and therefore has nothing to say about choices you only get to make once. Feel free to steal Priya's vocabulary (reup number, the fractional rule) and to have the columnist mention that experiment where people were handed $25 and a coin that came up heads 60% of the time and a big fraction of them still went bust. Don't let her wink at the reader — no punchline that announces the joke, no "readers, I may have taken this too far" — and close with a one-line author bio that stays in character.
