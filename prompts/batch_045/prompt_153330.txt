A friend has built a version of the presumptuous philosopher argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, and I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs. Here is his chain, as precisely as I can state it.

(1) Identity lottery. Before you learn which observer you are, you should treat your place as a random draw from the observers a hypothesis makes possible. So the hypothesis-relative likelihood of finding yourself to be this observer is proportional to how many observers the hypothesis contains. This is the self-indication factor, and it is what distinguishes the self-indication assumption from the self-sampling assumption, on which your existence is not evidence about the size of the world at all.

(2) Copies are cheap. Where two hypotheses posit the same kinds of thing and differ only in how many instances of those kinds are realized, the prior ratio between them is bounded by a constant however large the ratio of instance counts gets. A cosmos with ten times as many observers is not appreciably less probable a priori than one with a tenth as many: positing more of the same is not positing a new kind of thing, and simplicity penalties track kinds, not headcounts.

(3) Unbounded space. For every N, the hypothesis that the cosmos contains 10N observers of the kinds we already see is available, and none of these hypotheses is excluded by the penalty in (2).

(4) Therefore, by (1) through (3), for every finite N your posterior credence that the total number of observers exceeds N goes to one: every finite-population cosmology receives zero credence, and if a genuinely infinite hypothesis is in the space the posterior fails to normalize altogether.

(5) But your astronomical evidence has only finite strength, while the boost in (4) is unbounded. So no finite body of observation could ever move you: cosmology could never learn from evidence how populous the world is, and you should be certain that the cosmos is vastly fuller than it looks, whatever telescopes show.

(6) That is absurd. So at least one of (1) through (3) is false.

Two things I want. First, name the step and explain why it looked fine; then price the alternatives, including the ones I would rather not pay. Concretely: if I drop (1) in favour of the self-sampling assumption, do I get the doomsday argument back, and with it the result that the first two people should have concluded, purely from being the first two, that they were almost certainly going to remain childless? If I reject (2), how fast would priors have to fall in population size, and is any such rate anything other than a dial tuned to block this argument? If I reject (3), what bounds the hypothesis space, and what happens to cosmological theories that posit infinitely many observers?

Second, my friend has a fallback I want tested. Restrict the hypothesis space to cosmologies consistent with current astronomy, which caps N at some finite value. The argument then no longer diverges; it says only that the population sits at the top of the range our evidence permits. Does that really follow, and is it any less absurd? It seems to commit him to expecting observers, or at least biospheres, on nearly every habitable body we next examine, and to treating the apparent silence as a coincidence we should be astonished by. Does the truncation do more work than he thinks, or is that consequence genuinely his to swallow?

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than (1), but I want to know what I am buying.
