I read a fair bit of cross-cultural moral psychology for fun — the WEIRD-samples complaint, the economic games run in small-scale societies, that sort of thing — and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. (I'm not in the field, so sorry if this is bread-and-butter stuff.)

When a study finds a big difference — undergrads say the guy who kept most of the money did something wrong and deserves to be punished even by a bystander who lost nothing; people in some of the small-scale societies studied are much less bothered, and say in effect that it's his business — the finding gets reported in at least three different shapes, and which shape you pick seems to decide whether it tells you anything about whether morality is objective.

(a) They disagree about a moral claim: same act, same description, opposite verdicts. Genuine moral disagreement.
(b) They aren't judging the same thing. Different concept of what the act even is, or the question got translated but the construct didn't.
(c) They agree about the reasons and disagree about the facts: whether the ancestors really retaliate, whether the curse really makes people sick, whether anyone was actually harmed. Fix the facts and the verdicts come back together.

And then (d), the case I can't place: everybody agrees on the facts and on what happened, but they disagree about who is *in* the moral community at all — dead relatives, unborn children, people in the next valley are or aren't the kind of thing you owe anything to. That's not (a), not (b), and not (c), because nobody has a false empirical belief.

Two more things that make me think this isn't just the familiar point about verbal disputes: in (c), the disputed facts are often themselves morally loaded, so "they only disagree about non-moral facts" doesn't sit right; and the very same study gets filed under (a) by people drawing a debunking conclusion and under (b) or (c) by people resisting it, with no agreed test for who's right.

Is there a name for this? Not for the big relativism conclusion — for the sorting itself: putting a cross-cultural difference into one of these boxes, and the suspicion that the sorting is doing all the work. Does it have an established concept or literature? If the nearest things are the argument from disagreement, mere verbal disputes, or something about concepts not being shared across cultures, tell me which fits best, exactly how mine differs from each, and where the boundary cases land — especially (d) and the morally-loaded-fact version of (c). If there's nothing established, coin a term and define it tightly enough that I could actually sort a case with it. A bare label is no use to me; I want the carving and the edges.
