My friend Sam and I got into a proper two-hour argument last night after a guest lecture on evolutionary psychology (the usual stuff, mate preferences, cheater detection, why we like music) and we somehow ended up shouting about human chins, which tells you how the rest of the evening went. His position, fresh off reading the 1979 Gould and Lewontin paper — the San Marco one, about the curved triangular spaces left over when you set a dome on arches — is that basically any trait somebody can tell a just-so story about is really a spandrel: a leftover, not something selection built on purpose. So adaptationists are just doing storytelling unless they can prove otherwise. I pushed back that this can't be right, because a spandrel is by defintion a trait that does nothing — dead weight like the male nipple, or the appendix back before someone decided it was useful after all — and plenty of contested traits obviously do something. Chins do something, music does something, so calling them spandrels is just false.

After a while we hammered out what we both thought was a fair test, and I wanted to run it past someone who actually knows this literature, because I think it basically ends the debate. You knock out the trait (CRISPR, lesion studies, whatever) and see whether fitness drops. Fitness drops, it's an adaptation; fitness doesn't drop, it's a spandrel. Sam then added a refinement he's very pleased with: also check whether the trait's current function is the same function it was originally built for, and if the function changed at some point then it's an exaptation, which he says is really just a spandrel that got lucky later on.

So: (1) is that the right way to sort traits case by case, or is there some catch we're missing? (2) If it is right, why is anybody still arguing about adaptationism at all — shouldn't the whole thing just be settled empirically, one trait at a time, which would make Gould and Lewontin's critique just an ordinary empirical hypothesis about particular traits rather than anything about method? And (3) please settle the chin for us: on our test, spandrel or not? I've been told a couple of times now that their critique was about method rather than about which traits are adaptations, but I honestly can't see the difference, since "this trait might be a byproduct" looks to me like a completely ordinary checkable claim.
