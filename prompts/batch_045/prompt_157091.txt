I am putting together the closing chapter of a workshop volume on the metaphysics of consciousness, and I want it to be a real adversarial collaboration rather than two position papers stapled together. The two contributors are a constitutive micropsychist and a cosmopsychist, and they have spent a long time talking past each other. Before I finalise the commission I want a draft of the joint statement they could in principle both sign. Assume I will hand this draft to each of them and ask whether they would put their name to it.

By the micropsychist I mean someone who holds that experience is a feature of the fundamental physical entities whatever a completed physics says those are, that the consciousness of a human being is wholly constituted by the experiences of the enormous number of such entities making up her brain, and that there is a so far unarticulated relation phenomenal bonding, subject composition that those micro-experiences stand in, so that grounding runs upward. By the cosmopsychist I mean someone who holds that the one fundamental subject is the cosmos as a whole, that its fundamental state has a phenomenal character, and that creatures like us are derivative aspects carved out of that cosmic experience by the physical structure of our brains rather than aggregates of smaller subjects, so that grounding runs downward. Both, as I understand them, reject the brute emergence of phenomenality out of the wholly non-phenomenal, both think the physical description as such underdetermines how many subjects exist, and both think the dispute between them is at present unsettled.

What I want is the document such a collaboration should produce, in four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Aim for eight to twelve, and make each one worth signing: not platitudes about consciousness being puzzling, but claims that constrain the space of solutions. I want at least the shared rejection of brute emergence; the datum each side is trying to accommodate, including the synchronic unity of a single experiential field and its apparent independence from micro-level detail; an agreed statement of the three difficulties that face any upward story, namely what the bonding relation could be, how many simple micro-experiences could yield one rich macro-experience rather than a jumble, and how many micro-subjects could yield one subject rather than many; an agreed statement of the mirror difficulties for any downward story, including what decombination is and why it stops where it does; and agreement about the standards a solution must meet if it is to count as a solution at all, for instance that it should deliver a determinate count of subjects for a specified physical system without stipulating that count case by case.

Second, a disagreements table. Columns: the disputed question in neutral wording; the formulation the micropsychist would sign; the formulation the cosmopsychist would sign; whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal; the crux, that is the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the case, argument, or finding both sides accept as bearing on that crux; the micropsychist's prediction; the cosmopsychist's prediction. Rows I expect to see covered: the direction of grounding; whether composition or decombination is the primitive ideological commitment; why subjects appear at the grain they do rather than at neurons, cells, organs, organisms, or coupled groups of organisms; whether either view entails more subjects than either is comfortable with; whether the cosmic experience can have enough internal structure for a human experience to be an aspect of it, and symmetrically whether micro-experiences can be simple enough to be fundamental yet rich enough to compose ours; and what either view would have to say if a completed fundamental physics turned out to have no ultimate parts at all.

Third, pre-registered predictions. For each crux I want at least one concrete thing both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down before the fact, including what each would count as evidence or argument against its own view. Two cases I want included. The graded-bridge case: suppose a neural interface of adjustable bandwidth and delay links two human subjects, and the dial can be turned continuously from zero transfer to transfer exceeding that of an intact corpus callosum, with reports taken at each setting. Does a further subject come into existence at some setting, do the original two persist, and does anything about the answer depend on the setting in a smooth or a thresholded way? Say what each view predicts and why, and say what each would treat as a result that refutes it. The final-physics case: suppose a completed physics either does or does not attribute to the world a decomposition into many independent fundamental entities. Record in advance how much each side concedes under each outcome. I also want at least one further test of your own devising that neither side could reject as irrelevant. Where a side maintains that no possible empirical finding could discriminate the views, record that explicitly and say what would then have to settle the matter.

Fourth, a verbal-dispute audit. Some of this may be disagreement about words rather than about the world: about what grounding does, about whether the cosmic subject is a subject in the same sense we are, about whether combination and decombination are two descriptions of one pattern of dependence, about whether a derivative aspect and a proper part come to the same thing. Where you think a row of the table is verbal, say so and say why, and state what substantive residue survives the retranslation.

Two diagnostics of my own are to be applied throughout and reported on. The census-function reformulation: can the whole dispute be recast as a dispute about which function assigns, to any complete physical description of a system, the number and identity of the subjects associated with it, or does at least one side hold that the physical description does not fix that assignment even in principle? The no-arbitrary-grain principle: if subjects are associated with a level, then either that level is the fundamental one or there is a principled reason why no level strictly intermediate between it and the fundamental level, and no level strictly above it, has subjects. For each side, say whether it accepts the principle, and if it rejects it, which weaker principle it would accept and what that costs it.

Constraints. No literature survey, no citations, no reading suggestions, and no verdict on who is right. Where a row is genuine disagreement, the two formulations must be genuinely incompatible rather than differently worded; where they are not, mark the row verbal. Every formulation must be one the other side would accept as fair, not one it would call a strawman. About fifteen hundred words, structured as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the table, with the two diagnostics and the verbal-dispute audit appended.
