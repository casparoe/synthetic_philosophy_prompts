I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been working through the question of whether artifacts have politics — Langdon Winner's 1980 Daedalus essay of that name, and the stronger determinist claim that technical development outruns political control. I already know the canonical illustrations (parkway overpasses built too low for buses, and the rest), so please don't spend the answer rehearsing them.

The case I want analyzed is the Clipper chip. Here are the facts as I understand them; correct me if any are off. In 1993 the US government promoted a tamper-resistant encryption chipset for voice and data, built around the classified Skipjack cipher, in which each device's key was deposited with the government at the factory and could be recovered by law enforcement through a law-enforcement access field transmitted with each message. It was pushed as a standard, drew organized opposition from the EFF and EPIC and from working cryptographers, lost credibility after Matt Blaze showed in 1994 that the escrow mechanism could be defeated while the encryption still worked, was overtaken by freely available software cryptography, and was dead by 1996.

The lens I want applied is not Winner's but a three-part test I've been using: a technical arrangement is political when (i) it settles who may do what to whom, (ii) the settling presents itself as a constraint of the engineering rather than as a choice someone made, and (iii) undoing it would cost more than making it did, with the extra cost falling on parties who had no hand in the decision.

What I'd like: run Clipper through that test and say which conditions it satisfies cleanly and which only under strain; say where the test misdescribes the case or goes silent rather than forcing an answer (I suspect condition (iii) behaves oddly here, because the artifact lost — there was no entrenched arrangement to undo); and then say what the case shows about the test itself: confirmation, anomaly, or a sign that I designed it for artifacts that succeed. Separately: does this episode support a determinist reading of technology, or does it show a design decision being identified, contested, and reversed by the people around it? Finally, if someone in science and technology studies would discard one of my three conditions outright, tell me which and why.
