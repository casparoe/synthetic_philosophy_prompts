I'm in a first-year ethics seminar and also volunteering with a student human-rights campaign on campus, and the two keep colliding. Last week we were arguing about whether to add our name to a petition condemning a customary practice — arranged child marriage, in a country none of us are from — and one of the campaign organizers, who's normally the most fired-up person in the room, shut the whole thing down with "that's their culture." Everyone went quiet and we tabled the motion, and I've been annoyed about it since because I cant tell whether she said something deep or something empty. I also can't tell what she thinks follows from it: that the practice isn't wrong, that it might be wrong but we shouldn't say so publicly, that we shouldn't try to stop it, or that we shouldn't even look into it.

What I want is a precisification menu. Give me the distinct precise claims hiding in "that's their culture," from the boring descriptive one (it's widely accepted there, and the people doing it take themselves to have a reason) all the way up to full-blown relativism (moral truth is indexed to a framework, so there is no verdict available from outside it), and include the ones that aren't really about the practice at all but about us: epistemic humility about local facts we probably don't have, the standing or hypocrisy point (our own society does comparably bad things, so we've got no standing to blame), and the non-interference point (even if it's wrong, outside coercion to stop it is illegitimate or counterproductive). For each reading: state the thesis precisely, mark whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a substantive but shaky one, or something probably false — and, the part I actually care about, say whether it would justify what the organizer used it for, i.e. dropping the campaign, as opposed to weaker moves like changing the wording, handing the campaign to local groups, or keeping it while investigating more.

Three wrinkles I want handled. First, our TA insisted on seperating judging the practice from judging the people who participate in it, and "they did something wrong" from "they're blameworthy for it," and I suspect that cuts across some readings in a way that matters — some readings might get you "don't blame them" without getting you "don't criticize the practice." Show me where, if so. Second, one of the people pushing hardest for the petition is from that country's diaspora here, and she says "their culture" is being used against her: the people with power over the practice are a subset of the community, and there are local reformers who are asking for exactly this kind of outside criticism. Does that fact defeat any particular reading on the menu? My guess is it wrecks the descriptive reading's rhetorical force but doesn't touch the relativist one, though I'm not confident. Third, my professor floated a test I half-remember: ask yourself whether you'd still think it wrong if your own community did it, and if yes, you were never really a relativist whatever you say. Is that test any good, or does a relativist have a reply?

Format: one table first, with exactly these columns — reading_name; precise_claim (one or two sentences, no slogans); status (platitude / substantive-and-defensible / substantive-but-shaky / false); licenses_dropping_the_campaign (yes / partly / no); strongest_objection; extra_fact_needed_to_apply_it_here (the empirical thing you'd have to know, e.g. about the practice or about who in that community wants what). Order the rows weakest claim to strongest. Then, after the table, a short JSON object with keys verdict_on_the_slogan, confidence_0_to_100, most_likely_to_be_wrong, would_change_my_mind, and one_question_for_the_organizer. Be explicit about confidence and about which row you're least sure of, and tell me the one argument or consideration that would flip your verdict — I'm bringing this to office hours and I'd rather know where it's likely to break than leave with something that just sounds good.
