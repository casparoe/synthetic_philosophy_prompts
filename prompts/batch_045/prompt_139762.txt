Hey — I host a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher who's spent years on Sen's liberal paradox. Her line is that the paradox isn't really about rights at all: she wants to keep something close to Sen's minimal liberalism (each person decisive within their own sphere — e.g. whether *they* read the book) and instead weaken the Pareto principle so it only bites when people's agreement rests on preferences about their own life, not on preferences about what other people should do. She also claims the fix scales up to bigger groups, and that it doesn't quietly turn rights into tie-breakers that get outvoted.

Can you draft me 7–8 interview questions that actually push on this instead of letting her restate it? I'd want at least:

- one on whether excluding other-regarding preferences from Pareto just begs the question — in real liberal disputes (pornography, religious dress, blasphemy) the moralised preference *is* the thing being excluded, so the restriction looks like a political conclusion dressed up as a technical fix;
- one on whether the cycle comes back in through conditional or paternalistic preferences — the prude who'd rather read the book himself *if* the other guy is going to read it — and whether she can block meddling preferences without blocking ordinary paternalistic ones;
- one on whether a right that only settles what happens in your own sphere, and loses whenever it clashes with someone else's self-regarding preference, is still a constraint in anything like the usual side-constraint sense, or just a strong default;
- one on a concrete case, so we can hear whether the formal result has any bite — say three people and one book, or a case where two people's spheres genuinely overlap (a shared wall, a loud stereo);
- one on why she rejects the reading Sen himself leaned toward, that the paradox is mainly a reductio of welfarism and of treating Pareto as sacrosanct, not a problem about rights.

She's very good at deflecting with "that's an artifact of the formalism, not a moral problem," so for each question give me the answer she's most likely to give plus a follow-up that makes that deflection harder, and flag anywhere you think she could slip out of it.
