I sit on the small committee that is rewriting our hospital's policy for allocating a single remaining dose of a scarce drug when more patients need it than can be treated, and our last two meetings have consisted almost entirely of two pictures being asserted at each other, with nobody ever laying out what is supposed to map onto what. Our clinical director says the patients' claims are weights on a pair of scales: each person's stake in surviving is one equal weight, so five weights plainly outweigh one, and a policy that saves the single patient is just a sentimental refusal to do arithmetic. Our one philosopher member answers with a broadly Taurekian move — the numbers don't aggregate at all — and substitutes a different picture: since no individual's claim is stronger than any other's, the fair thing is a lottery, each person getting an equal chance of being the one who is saved, with the bare fact that there happen to be five on one side carrying no weight whatsoever. We have to produce a draft by the end of the month, and I've realised the disagreement is not about a principle but about which analogy is running the room.

So I'd like an audit of both analogies in the same terms. For each one, give me the correspondence table — what plays the patient, the weight, the balance point, the person reading the scale; or in the lottery, what plays the ticket, the prize, the draw, and the authority conducting it — then the list of disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the work the analogy is doing here and those that are merely cosmetic. My own suspicion is that the scales quietly import something the case lacks: a single point at which the resultant of the weights is felt, a single subject for whom being outweighed is a matter of degree rather than a matter of losing everything, and a convention that a cancelled weight leaves no residue. And my suspicion about the lottery is that it imports a different thing: that the currency to be equalised is chances rather than outcomes, so it can look perfectly respectful of each person while predictably leaving far more people dead. But I genuinely can't tell whether either suspicion is fatal to the analogy or just rhetorical.

Three cases I invented to press on this, which I'd like used as tests rather than set aside. First: the five each stand to lose a finger and the one stands to lose her life — does the scale picture now say save the one, and if so, has it stopped being an argument about numbers and become an argument about magnitudes, and does that matter for how it was originally being used? Second, a fusion case I made up specifically to probe whether the separateness of subjects is doing the hidden work: five bodies that share a single stream of consciousness, such that any one of the five deaths ends that same stream. If your verdict about the scales changes here while the arithmetic stays the same, what was the analogy tracking? Third, the iterated version: the very same five-versus-one choice, which you know in advance will recur a hundred times with fresh people each round and no way to coordinate across rounds. Equal chances per round and five-saved-per-round come spectacularly apart in what actually happens over the sequence; does that divergence count against the lottery, or does the lottery's verdict simply fail to aggregate in the same way it refuses to aggregate across persons?

Finally, the part I care most about: do these analogies support the conclusions they are wheeled in for? Do the scales really get you that the numbers count, or only that no one counts for more than anyone else — and does the lottery really get you that the numbers don't count, or only that no one's claim is stronger? Is one of them in fact the position the other side retreats to when pressed, rather than a genuine rival? And is there a better analogy available — or is part of the trouble that any picture built from a single distributor handing out a single indivisible good already rigs the question in favour of there being one thing to be maximised? I'll say where I stand so you can aim at it: I find the lottery intuitively right in the one-off case, close to indefensible in the iterated one, and I have no account of how both of those can be true.
