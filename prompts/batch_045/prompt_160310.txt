I write a column on science and ideas, and for the last couple of years I have been covering consciousness research — the brain-scan stories that claim to decode or predict what someone is seeing, and the eyewitness-memory beat, where the interesting finding is always that a confident first-person report can be completely wrong about when something happened. I have been asked to run a ninety-minute hands-on session at a small science-and-ideas festival later this spring: about twenty adults in a hotel conference room, mixed backgrounds, a couple of working vision scientists, two or three people who have read Dennett's Consciousness Explained and will announce that they have, and a lot of curious non-specialists. I want to build the session around the colour phi phenomenon and the puzzle it generates about the timing of experience. If you are not familiar with it: you show a dot of one colour at one location on a screen, then a fraction of a second later a dot of a different colour at a different location; at the right spacing and timing viewers report a single dot moving between the two positions and changing colour partway along its path, and they report the colour change as having occurred before the second dot was ever presented — which on the face of it is impossible, since the colour of the second dot is information that did not exist yet. The two candidate stories are the ones Dennett labelled with the names of two kinds of historical revision: either the experience really did unfold one way and the record of it was then rewritten after the fact, or the content was inserted late, after the second stimulus, so that the earlier part of the experience was never there to be revised. My understanding of the philosophical point is that no first-person method can tell these apart, because from the subject's side they are supposed to be indistinguishable, and that the interesting position is not one of the two but the suspicion that the question has no determinate answer at all. But I do not want the session to be a talk about that. I want participants to collide with it.

So: design the session. Concretely, I want a runnable activity or game for twenty people, ninety minutes, using only what a conference room and my laptop can supply — a projector or a shared screen, index cards, blindfolds or paper cups, phone timers, masks or sticky notes; no tachistoscope, no proper timing hardware, and I should assume the illusion will be temperamental when projected, so I need a fallback if it fails to land for half the room. Rules, materials, roles, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The idea has to show up in what happens rather than in what I say afterwards: nobody should hear either of the two labels, or the phrase timing of experience, until after the activity is over. My own thought is that the cleanest way in is to make them live through an institutional analogue first. Something like this: a control room and an audience, where a real event is being relayed with a short broadcast delay, and an editor can either let the feed go out and then silently rewrite the tape, or hold the feed and compose the tape from notes before anyone downstream sees it. Teams of participants are the downstream audience and have to bet, from the record alone, on which regime the control room is running; score them, let them discover that they are at chance, and only then reveal that the two regimes are the two hypotheses and that their own reports about the dot are in exactly the same position. If you think that particular staging is the wrong one, say so and give me a better one — but whatever you choose should include (a) an actual first-person encounter with the illusion, not just a description of it, (b) some real, if tiny, stakes, so that people are trying to be right rather than being agreeable, and (c) a moment where someone proposes settling the question from the third-person side — a perfect neural recording, a scanner, a slow-motion replay — and runs into why that route does not simply dissolve the problem either.

Then the debrief, which is where I expect to need the most help. Map the specific moments in the activity onto the specific parts of the idea: what each stage was supposed to make them feel, and what a participant who says 'I just know what I saw and when I saw it' has actually failed to appreciate. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I am especially worried about the people who have read the book and will name the distinction in the first ten minutes, about the vision scientist who will move the discussion to lateral inhibition and neural latencies and thereby make it a different and easier question, and about the participant who insists that with practice and attention they could introspect the difference. And be frank about what the activity cannot do. My sense is that it can make the methodological point vivid — that the two stories are indistinguishable from the inside — but that it cannot reproduce the thing that actually frightens people, which is not 'I cannot tell which happened' but 'there may be no fact of the matter about when I became conscious of anything,' and that the analogy with the control room is quietly misleading precisely because in my simulation somebody in the control room does know, whereas the radical claim is that nobody does, not even the subject. Where the analogy breaks, I will have to supply the rest by talking, so tell me where those seams are and what the two or three sentences I say there should be. What I want people to carry out of the room is a felt version of this: that the question of when you became conscious of something may not be answerable from the inside, and that this should change how they read both a confident eyewitness and a headline about a machine reading experience off a brain scan.
