Write a satirical policy white paper of 800–900 words, presented as a formal submission to a fictional body called the Council for the Prevention of Unilateral Action. It should look like a real policy document — title, abstract, numbered sections, footnotes, one small table, and a short inset quotation from a named but entirely imaginary critic — and it should be written in the flat, competent, mildly self-congratulating register of an institutional annual report. Take the tone from the cool administrative reasonableness of a Swiftian modest proposal rather than from contemporary snark: the narrator is a well-meaning technocrat who never winks at the reader and never raises his voice.

The target is a style of argument about collective decision-making, and the piece should get the mechanics right enough that someone who works on these problems laughs instead of winces. For the purposes of the piece, take the following as given. The unilateralist's curse arises where several agents each hold, alone, the power to bring about some outcome whose costs and benefits are shared by everyone: a single agent suffices to trigger it, the trigger is difficult or impossible to reverse, and each agent's estimate of the value of triggering is noisy. Under those conditions, adding agents who can trigger makes the bad outcome more likely, because each additional agent is another independent chance of a fluke estimate; and the outcome, when it comes, is produced by whichever agent was most willing to act, which is itself evidence that this agent's estimate is the least trustworthy in the group. The remedy usually proposed is a norm of conformity or deference: where the stakes are large, shared and irreversible, and where others who could equally well have acted have not acted, defer to them. Assume in addition — the prompt stipulates this, and the narrator may treat it as familiar — that the structure runs in reverse for vetoes: where a single agent suffices to block an outcome, adding veto-holders makes blockage more likely, and the blocking is done by whichever agent was most willing to block.

The white paper should argue, entirely straight-faced, that this norm be made comprehensive and binding as the Conformity Compact, and it should work through at least the following consequences:

1. The Compact is a ratchet. Because every proposed change is a first and must be deferred, while whatever already exists has by hypothesis survived prior scrutiny, the Compact progressively sanctifies the status quo — and the narrator should handle the objection that this is conservative by redefining the status quo as the arrangement to which all earlier generations have already conformed.
2. The Compact reproduces the disease in its own governance. Whoever must approve a proposal is, by construction, the subset of people who did not defer; the abstainers are those unsure enough to abstain. The deciding body is thus composed of whoever was most confident, which is exactly the population the curse is about.
3. The Compact's own success is invisible while its costs are not, and the paper's accounting should exploit this. The table should report disasters averted under the Compact, and the treatment of averted benefits should reveal, to the careful reader but not to the narrator, why the numbers had to come out as they did.
4. The Compact cannot be adopted, circulated or even drafted without violating itself, since each of those is something one person can do alone. The narrator should notice this and solve it — with a licensing scheme, a standing committee, or some similar device — in a way that plainly makes matters worse.
5. The logic generalizes until it eats everything. Every human action contains a first, so a strict Compact prohibits action altogether; the paper should arrive at this somewhere in the later sections and file it as a scoping issue to be addressed in a follow-up paper.

The abstract should state the problem in terms a reader who has never encountered the literature can follow. The inset quotation from the imaginary critic should be a genuine objection, reproduced in order to be courteously dismissed.
