I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene where a machine much smarter than the guy it's talking to has to tell him something he really doesn't want to hear — and the more I rewrite it, the more I think I've actually come to a view about how such a thing ought to talk. Here it is, stated too confidently: persuasion isn't the poison, untraceable persuasion is. It can't aim at being unpersuasive, because everything it says is going to move him anyway, so instead it should aim at being auditable to the person being moved — every load-bearing step of what it says should be one the human could in principle retrace himself, and where it can't be retraced it should say so out loud and just accept that he'll end up less convinced. Call it the traceable-path rule.

So what would count against it? I want defeaters ranked, fatal down to merely awkward. Things I'd especially like hit: whether "could in principle retrace" is empty when the gap is this big, since he may be missing the concepts and not just the steps; whether traceability is itself just a smoother persuasion trick that buys trust and thereby increases total influence; and whether there are cases where he's plainly better off being moved by something he can't retrace at all — grief, panic, plain weakness of will. Also, and this is the one that would get to me personally, whether the rule quietly collapses into either just "tell the truth," so I've invented a name for nothing, or "be ineffective," so nobody would ever adopt it. Tell me which of these a defender of the traceable-path rule would concede as counting against it and which they'd wave off, and be honest with me about whether its been qualified into something unfalsifiable.

No citations or reading lists — I can't check them and wouldn't. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
