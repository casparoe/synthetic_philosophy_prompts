I have come to philosophy late, after a career in the physical sciences, and before I take these views to a reading group I would like to know whether they form a coherent set. Roughly where I have landed:

(1) On the inefficacy question I accept the threshold argument for ordinary food purchases. A retailer reorders against fixed batch sizes and fixed contract periods, and the effect of removing one household's demand is absorbed at the next contract renewal rather than avoided; on my own back-of-envelope calculation, my household abstaining from factory-farmed chicken for the rest of my life makes an expected long-run difference of a few tenths of one bird.

(2) I nonetheless hold that I ought not to buy it, I have arranged our purchasing accordingly, and I would press the same on others.

(3) I reject universalisation arguments of the what-if-everyone-did-that form: that a population-wide version of my act would be monstrous is not, as I see it, a reason bearing on my act.

(4) I vote, and think myself rationally required to, on straightforward expected-utility grounds: the chance of being pivotal is minute, the stakes are enormous, and minute multiplied by enormous is not negligible.

(5) I hold a strict welfarism about moral reasons: a consideration counts as a moral reason only if it is a consideration about the welfare or illfare of some individual creature. I accordingly set no store by alleged free-standing reasons of complicity, expression, integrity or clean hands.

(6) On my own emissions I run the same inefficacy argument and reach the opposite practical conclusion: I take myself to be under no obligation to curb my flights or my heating, though I do think states and firms are obliged to decarbonise, and I vote accordingly.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and for each real conflict what would have to be given up. Please rank them by severity, tell me which single revision buys the most coherence, and say plainly if one of the six is simply unsupported by the others.
