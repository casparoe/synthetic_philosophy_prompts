I'm writing a term-paper proposal (one semester, roughly 6000 words) for a course at the philosophy/game-theory boundary, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a proposal that would actually be fundable.

The idea: textbook presentations of the strategy-stealing argument — Nash's argument for Hex, and the parallel arguments for Chomp and for strong positional games — are usually compressed into the slogan that the first player can help herself to the second player's winning strategy. I suspect that slogan hides two separable assumptions. One is structural: the game has to be one where determinacy plus the absence of draws applies, both players face the same menu of moves, and an extra stone of your own on the board is never a liability — a monotonicity condition that plainly fails in a game like Go, where an extra stone can cost you points, and that is doing real work in the proof. The other is an assumption about the agent: to run the argument you must be able to play as though your extra move were not there, maintaining a counterfactual description of the position, translating the stolen strategy's prescriptions into legal moves in the actual position, and doing so for the whole remainder of the game. Nothing in the proof guarantees that a player of bounded memory or bounded computational power can do this, or that attempting it cannot leave her worse off than she would have been had she never made the extra move.

The question I want stated precisely: can strategy-stealing fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the structure of the game — that is, for a game where the classical argument is perfectly valid, is there a specification of the players' cognitive or computational capacities under which no usable stealing strategy exists, so that the first-mover advantage the classical proof establishes is in principle unavailable to those players? And if so, can the monotonicity condition be rewritten so that it is indexed to a class of agents (finite-state strategies, bounded-memory players, players who can simulate the opponent only a bounded number of moves ahead) rather than to the game alone?

What I need in the proposal: the question put in a form that has a determinate answer; an account of why the standard statement leaves it open, showing exactly where the capacity assumption sits in the proof and in enough detail that a reader could check it; the method and why it beats the obvious alternatives (cataloguing known cases where an extra move hurts, or importing general results about bounded rationality in games), including the smallest result that would still be worth writing up; the range of possible conclusions and what would decide between them; what would count as the project failing; and an honest verdict on what makes this too big for a semester and where you would cut. Please skip the literature review and any citations — I'll supply references myself.
