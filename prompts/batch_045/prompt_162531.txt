Hey — you're a political philosopher who has also taught a ton (like, years of running your own seminars, not just lecturing), and you're good at designing in-class simulations. I need your help with one session. Please think carefully and brainstorm at least five or six possible mechanics before you commit to one; tell me in a line each why you dropped the ones you dropped, and then give me the one you'd actually run, in full detail.

The setting: a 3rd/4th-year undergraduate seminar on distributive justice, 16 students, one 80-minute slot (I can split it into two 40-minute halves with a break). Its the session right after we've done utilitarianism and luck egalitarianism, so they have some vocabulary but they haven't met prioritarianism yet. Materials: poker chips, index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard, and roughly zero budget — so no real money changing hands. If there's a payoff it has to be points, raffle tickets, or something equally silly.

What I want them to come away seeing: that "equality is intrinsically valuable" and "benefits to the worse off matter more" are two different doctrines; that the first one has a genuinely absurd consequence they can feel in their gut rather than just hear about; and that the second one — the one they'll want to retreat to — still has an uncomfortable consequence of its own, just a milder one. And I want it to show up in what they do, not in what I say afterwards: no mini-lecture up front, and nobody should hear the phrase "levelling-down objection" until the debrief.

The rough shape I have in mind — improve it, don't just fill it in. They invent the scoring rule for how good a distribution of chips is, and they get to revise it as we go. Round one: secret, unequal endowments, and a chance to move chips around, but transfers are lossy — a fraction of every chip moved evaporates in transit, and I can dial the leak rate up or down between rounds. Round two: there's a box on the table, and any chip put in the box is destroyed, it helps nobody, it just lowers the spread. I want them to discover that the rule they invented ten minutes earlier is telling them to feed the box. Round three: they rebuild the rule around weighting the worse off instead of rewarding equality, and then they hit a case where the weight tells them to hand the worst-off student one chip at a cost of ten chips to the better-off students — still worth it on their own rule, and they have to sit with that. Final wrinkle if there's time: allocations are made before anyone knows which position in the distribution they'll end up occupying, so priority has to attach either to persons or to positions, and they have to say which and why.

Deliverables: a materials list; the exact rules and the scoring rules as I should write them on the board — I need real numbers (endowments, leak rates, weights), not "an appropriate amount"; a minute-by-minute timeline across the 80 minutes; and a debrief that maps each moment they'll have lived through onto the relevant bit of doctrine — equality as an intrinsic value, priority as a weight on benefits, the pure levelling-down case where nobody gains versus the "huge sacrifice for a trivial gain" case that survives once you switch to priority, and the ex-ante/ex-post wrinkle. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I'm especially worried about someone treating the destroy-box as a joke, about the arithmetic eating the session, and about the "worst off" role landing badly on a student who's actually broke.

Also be honest about what the simulation can't do.
