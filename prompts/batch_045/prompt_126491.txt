I've been forecasting seriously for about a decade — calibration training, prediction logs, the usual habits — and I've lately tried to write down the epistemology I'm actually running on rather than the one I'd defend on request. It is mostly a reductionist line about chance plus a fairly hard-nosed outside-view practice, but when I lay the pieces out together I can't tell which ones are load-bearing and which are colliding. Here is the set.

(1) Reductionism about chance. To say that the chance of a kind of event is p is to say something about the pattern of actual outcomes in some class or sequence of events, and nothing more; there is no further primitive propensity fact over and above how things actually go.

(2) Realism about single-case chance, plus the principal principle. I am willing to assert that this patient's chance of surviving this surgery is 0.83 and mean something determinately true about that one surgery, not a remark about a class it happens to belong to. I also take it as a constraint on rationality that an agent who knows the chance of E and has no inadmissible evidence sets credence in E equal to that chance. This is not idle for me: chance is the thing I take my credences to be tracking, and calibration, measured over my logged forecasting record, is my main evidence that I am tracking it.

(3) No privileged reference class. Every event lies in indefinitely many classes with different frequencies, and I do not think one of them is the objectively correct one; I reject appeals to natural kinds or to the causally homogeneous class as ways of singling out the one real frequency, and I think relevance is question- and interest-relative.

(4) Narrowest-adequate-class rule as my working rule of direct inference: among the classes for which I have statistically reliable frequency data, use the narrowest one.

(5) Outside-view default, with suspicion of narrowing. Default to the base rate for the broad class and treat any move to a narrower subclass as something to be suspicious of, since subclass-shopping is where overfitting and motivated reasoning get in. I also hold, and this is the part that looks flatly inconsistent with the rest of (5), that deliberately setting aside evidence one possesses is irrational, and that one should condition on one's total evidence.

(6) Precision. I reject imprecise credence: somewhere between 0.2 and 0.6 is either laziness or a particular number in disguise. Decision theory needs one number, and if I cannot produce one I should go get more evidence rather than report an interval.

(7) A candidate repair for the (1)/(2)/(3) cluster that I have been toying with, call it chance relativism: there is no such thing as the chance of a single case, only the chance of the case relative to a class; all such relativized chances are equally real; and the principal principle becomes a conditional instruction — when you know that the chance of E relative to class C is p, and nothing makes some other class more relevant to your question, set credence in E to p.

Two case verdicts, since they are the part I trust least and they seem to me to pull in opposite directions:

(8) A friend who I think is genuinely unusually capable is starting a company. I forecast the base rate for seed-stage companies in that sector — low single digits — and decline to adjust upward for her, even though I have real evidence about her.

(9) For the next run of a well-characterized process at work — same machine, same input spec, the post-maintenance protocol in place — I put 0.99 on success, where the base rate across all runs of all machines is about 0.9, and I feel no pull at all toward 0.9.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely collide and which only look like they do, ordered by how much damage the collision does rather than all flagged equally. In (8) and (9) I appear to ignore specifics in one case and ignore base rates in the other, and I cannot tell whether that is an inconsistency or the rules working as intended. In (4) and (5) I seem to have two different instructions for choosing a class, and I suspect they do not agree, but I also suspect the disagreement is really about something I have not stated. (7) is the piece I most want stress-tested: I think it buys me consistency at some cost I have not priced, and I would like the bill stated precisely, including whether some variant of it is survivable. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision costs me downstream — I would rather give up something small than rebuild the whole picture, but I do not know which of these is the small one.
