Setting: a 90-minute session of a sixteen-person undergraduate reading group in decision theory. They can handle Bayes' theorem and expected value; none has encountered anthropic reasoning, and no one should hear the name doomsday argument or the phrase reference class until the debrief.

Design an activity in which participants discover, by committing to real though trivial stakes, that a single fact about their own position in a sequence can move their confidence about how long the sequence is — and then discover that how much it moves depends on a choice nobody asked them to make.

Before the session you will have selected, by a procedure the group can audit but whose outcome stays sealed, between a short world (total population 200) and a long world (total population 200,000). Each participant receives an identity card bearing only a serial number drawn from the actual world's population, plus a dossier situating them in several nested candidate classes — a member of Homo sapiens; a member of a species that has built radio telescopes; a being capable of updating on evidence — whose totals differ across the two worlds by different ratios, so that the same serial number licenses different inferences depending on which class the participant treats herself as having been sampled from. Each participant commits in writing to a class and to a numerical credence that the world is short, and is scored by a strictly proper scoring rule against the sealed outcome. Beyond stating the rules, the facilitator says nothing.

Please supply: materials and setup; how the draw and the sealed choice are made auditable; the exact scoring rule with a table showing how it scores a few representative class-and-credence pairs; a debrief mapping what participants did onto the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions and onto the work the reference class was doing unnoticed; what predictably goes wrong (someone will insist the prior is one-half and one draw tells them nothing; someone will choose whichever class scores best; a low draw in the long world will make the exercise look broken, and you should say how to handle that); and, honestly, what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular that the argument's own prior is not a coin flip handed in from outside, and that the real hypothesis space is not two discrete totals. Finally, state in one sentence what participants should come away seeing: that the live disagreement is not about the arithmetic but about which sampling assumption governs, and that no datum of the kind they were given settles it.
