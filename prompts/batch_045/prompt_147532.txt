I'm a final-year PhD student giving a job talk next month on the third chapter of my dissertation, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The chapter argues for a position I call frame-relative accuracy. Start from the standard accuracy-first setup: a space of possibilities, a credence function over an algebra on it, and inaccuracy measured by a strictly proper rule. My addition is that accuracy is always assessed relative to a frame, a partition of the possibility space into the distinctions the agent is treating as live, so that the inaccuracy of a credence function at a world, relative to a frame, is the squared-distance score of its marginal distribution over that frame's cells against the indicator of the cell containing the world. I then say that a body of evidence settles a family of admissible frames rather than a unique one, where a frame is admissible when it respects every distinction the evidence supports and draws no distinction the evidence gives no support for. Because evidence can support two distinctions separately without supporting their common refinement — it can tell you something about color and something about size and nothing about how they correlate — admissible frames are typically incomparable with one another, and the refinement that cross-classifies them is not automatically admissible. Total inaccuracy is then the average of frame-relative inaccuracy over the admissible family under a weighting that the evidence constrains but does not fix; I impose on that weighting only that it be a probability measure on the family and that it not increase as frames get finer. The result I advertise as the main lemma is that, for any admissible weighting, two credence functions agreeing on the marginals of every admissible frame are exactly tied in expected inaccuracy, while any difference on those marginals makes an accuracy difference. So permissiveness is real but structured, and its size is a function of how much discrimination the evidence supports: thin evidence, coarse frames, wide ties; rich evidence, fine frames, near uniqueness. I keep probabilism and the usual accuracy-based vindication of conditionalization intact, I am not proposing an imprecise-credence or credal-set framework, and I am not making a semantic claim about the word rational. The novelty is supposed to be that the locus of permissiveness is the frame rather than the attitude, that uniqueness holds relative to a frame, and that this predicts the tightness of the link between strength of evidence and size of the permissible set.

The room is a departmental colloquium of about thirty people, and I want the objections as these people in particular would raise them. The chair is a formal epistemologist and a convinced uniqueness theorist: she holds that permissivism is a confusion generated by conflating the attitude of credence with the evidence, and I expect her to grant me the whole framework and then ask what follows from it that a uniqueness theorist should care about, or to say that I have simply relocated the permissivism one level up. Two other formal epistemologists are sympathetic to accuracy-first methods but think its vindications are all conditional on the choice of scoring rule, and one of them works on imprecise probabilities and will want to know why I am not just describing a credal set with extra machinery. There are two philosophers of science present: one works on verisimilitude and the epistemic value of deliberately false but highly informative idealised models, the other on model selection and coarse-graining in multiscale modelling; the first will press on whether accuracy is the right currency for theory choice at all, the second on how a frame differs from a choice of scale or level of description, and on what my apparatus would tell someone choosing between models. There is also a decision theorist who regards epistemic utility theory as decision theory with a gerrymandered consequence space and who will press on why dominance rather than some other decision rule, why separability, and whether averaging over frames is not simply expected utility with respect to a probability over frames, in which case the view collapses into an ordinary hierarchical Bayesian model. Finally there is a social epistemologist working on peer disagreement who thinks the whole permissivism debate has been conducted at a level of abstraction that gives no guidance in any actual case of disagreement. Nobody in the room works on vagueness or on the metaphysics of indeterminacy, which is where I think the most interesting applications lie, so I have to make that motivation land without a friendly specialist to help me.

What I want is the following: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this specific room, ordered by the product of how likely they are and how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, each phrased the way that particular objector would phrase it and not in my vocabulary; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, since the chair runs a brisk fifteen-minute question period, together with an honest note on where the reply is soft or question-begging; an indication of which two objections I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk itself, before anyone raises them, in order to buy credibility for the rest; and a judgment about which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour, with the reason it is load-bearing rather than merely central. My own guess is that the load-bearing reply is the one explaining why the common refinement of two admissible frames is not automatically admissible, since if grain is free the ties in my main lemma disappear and I am back to uniqueness, but tell me if you think the real weight sits somewhere else. Please also mark which objections I should answer by conceding the point and shrinking the claim rather than by replying, and say what someone in that room who starts out hostile would count as a genuine win for me rather than a rhetorical escape. My advisor, who is an impermissivist and thinks the project is permissivism smuggled in through the back door, says the question period will turn entirely on whether the weightings over frames are themselves rationally constrained; include that as an objection and tell me how seriously to take her. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks: I have a private worry that someone will connect the admissibility of frames to the classic problem of which predicates or partitions are projectible, since my apparatus seems to have no principled way of excluding a gerrymandered frame, and I would rather hear it now from you than for the first time from the floor.
