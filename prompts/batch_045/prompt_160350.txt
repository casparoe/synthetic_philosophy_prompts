Retired physicist here, got dragged into a philosophy reading group by a neighbour and am now hopelessly hooked. I've been talked into presenting Arrival as a case study for a session on Leibniz and the best-of-all-possible-worlds business, my idea being that Louise, once she can see the whole of her life at once, is in something like Leibniz's position: comparing complete histories rather than moments, so the grief isn't a cost she discounts but part of what makes that whole worth choosing. Three things I'd like help with.

One: I want to lean on his incompossibility point rather than the crude "add up the total happiness" version — the thought being that these goods simply cannot all be fitted into a single world, so it isnt that God, or Louise, settles for less, it's that there is no arrangement containing all of them. Am I getting that right, and does it actually do work for the film, or am I just decorating a mood?

Two, and this is where I keep tripping: I dont see how "of all possible worlds, this one is the best" gets you anywhere near "so don't try to change anything." The claim looks like it's about which world exists, not about whether my local intervention might be one of the things that happens in it. Louise declining to warn someone reads like a separate fatalist step smuggled in after the optimality claim. Does the film notice the gap, or does it slide from "the whole is best" to "nothing I do makes a difference"?

Three: where is Arrival actually doing philosophy rather than leaning on block-universe atmosphere, and does the Leibniz reading survive if you drop the physics of it? And what do I say to the member who'll insist this is just amor fati with aliens and has nothing to do with any theodicy?
