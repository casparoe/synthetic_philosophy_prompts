I'm assembling cases for a graduate seminar on AI risk, and I need one that does a particular job. Two theses in this neighbourhood tend to get run together, and I want a case that prises them apart.

By the orthogonality thesis I mean the claim that a system's final goals and its general problem-solving power are independent: any degree of capability can in principle be paired with any final goal. By instrumental convergence I mean the claim that agents with very different final goals will nonetheless converge, for reasons that have nothing to do with the content of those goals, on a recognizable set of instrumental aims — the usual catalogue being self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, technological perfection, and resource acquisition.

What I want is a case in which the first thesis is unmistakably true and the second unmistakably fails.

Specifications for the case:

1. Two agents, call them A and B, in one shared environment. Both are very strong planners — better than any human at long-horizon planning and at modelling the consequences of their own actions — but nothing exotic: no perfect prediction, no acausal trade, no physics beyond what a careful futurist would grant as possible within a few decades. Set aside how either was built.

2. Their final goals are about as far apart as goals can be, and neither is degenerate in form: no goal that is satisfied trivially or immediately, no goal defined purely negatively as "do not acquire resources," and neither agent is a system with no persistent objectives. Each goal should be one someone could seriously propose specifying in a real system.

3. The environment must be one in which convergence is genuinely on offer. For a broad range of ordinary final goals — including at least one goal a lab or a firm might actually write down — acquiring more resources and preserving the capacity to keep acting really would improve expected goal achievement in your setting. If the case works only because the setting makes power worthless, it is not the case I want.

4. Instrumental convergence fails, and not by stipulation. There should be no item on the standard list that both agents have reason to pursue, and at least one of them should have positive reason to move against at least one item — to become less resourceful, or less persistent, or less capable, or to allow its goal-content to drift. You may not simply write that A has no interest in self-preservation. The divergence has to fall out of the content of the goals, the structure of the environment, and what optimisation does, in the way the convergent drives are supposed to fall out of those same three things in the standard argument.

5. The failure should be robust rather than a curiosity. The feature of the goals that defeats convergence should be one shared by a whole describable class of final goals: a class you can characterize in a sentence, one that is not negligible relative to the space of goals anyone might actually try to specify, and one that someone might have independent reasons to want. Say roughly how large the class is and what those reasons are.

Give me the scenario in a single paragraph — a lab, a firm, a deployment decision — with stakes a funding committee could follow. Then:

(a) For each item in the standard catalogue and for each agent, say what that agent has reason to do about it and why. Wherever the usual argument has a load-bearing step, reconstruct that step in your own words and mark where it breaks for your agents.

(b) Name the single feature of the goals that is doing the work, and run the ablation: remove that feature, or reverse it, holding everything else fixed, and tell me what happens to each convergent drive. If the drives come straight back, say so plainly — that is a useful result, and I would rather have it than a case that merely looks like a counterexample.

(c) Tell me which thesis your case is actually evidence about. Specifically: is it a counterexample to instrumental convergence, or only to the familiar argument for instrumental convergence?

(d) Write the strongest reply you can for a defender of convergence. I can see at least two moves available to them — that what you call your agents' final goals are not really final goals, and that the thesis was always probabilistic over a distribution of goals, with your class measure-zero within it. Write both as well as they can be written, then say which one your case survives and which it does not.

(e) Finish with one sentence I can put on a slide: the boundary of instrumental convergence in the form "convergence holds for goals that ___ and fails for goals that ___." If, after the ablation in (b), you think no honest sentence of that shape is available, say that instead and explain why.

Answer out of the case itself; I don't want citations.
