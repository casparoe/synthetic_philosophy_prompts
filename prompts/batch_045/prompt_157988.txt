I am a philosopher serving on the ethics advisory board of a fertility clinic, and I have been asked to help with a decision the clinic's counselors now make several times a week and say they are unequipped for. For each cycle the lab returns reports on a handful of embryos: polygenic risk scores for common late-onset conditions and, in the larger package, scores for height and for predicted educational attainment. A counselor then has about thirty minutes to help prospective parents decide which embryo to transfer first, which to keep frozen, and which to allow to lapse.

The clinic's own proposal is a single aggregate welfare score per embryo, ranked. I think that collapses distinctions that matter, and I want something better. Please write a decision procedure a counselor can actually run in those thirty minutes, in the spirit of the principle of procreative beneficence but explicit about what that principle does not settle. I need: the questions in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how the procedure handles the fact that the choice is conditional on a fixed set of embryos, so that no available option leaves a resulting person worse off than they would otherwise have been and harm-based objections therefore run out, with the procedure saying this plainly rather than smuggling harm back in; a rule for traits whose expected benefit exists only because other people will discriminate in the child's favour, which I take to differ in kind from selecting against a disease, and I want to know whether your procedure can genuinely tell the difference or only mimic telling it; guidance on the two-embryo transfer case, whether to take the two highest-ranked or to hedge; and the stopping points at which the counselor must hand the decision to the couple or to the medical director because it is a question of values rather than of fact.

Then run the procedure on three cases: an embryo with elevated type 2 diabetes risk against one with a substantially lower chance of implanting; a request to select in favour of a socially rewarded trait that carries no health benefit; and a couple who want every embryo excluded that carries a variant for an untreatable condition which would not affect the person's life until after their fifties. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer I will dislike.

Two constraints: the scores were trained largely on European-ancestry samples and predict little for anyone else, and the counselors have biology training but no philosophy, so any distinction you ask them to draw must be one someone could draw who could not defend it. Close by telling me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and singling out the one step you are least confident about.
