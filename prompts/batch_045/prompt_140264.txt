Here is the answer (Student 14). It reads well and hits the obvious points, which is exactly why I'm suspicious of it.

---

**(a)** Endurantism is the view that a persisting object is wholly present at every time at which it exists: the whole of Socrates is there at noon and the whole of him is there again at six. Perdurantism is the view that a persisting object is not wholly present at any one time; it is spread out through time, and it is present at a time only in the sense that one of its temporal parts is there. Perdurantism is basically the eternalist idea — that all times are equally real, and that past and future times are just as real as the present one — applied to objects rather than to times. That is why a presentist, who thinks only the present is real, has to be an endurantist.

An intrinsic property is one a thing has in virtue of the way it is in itself, not in virtue of how it stands to other things; the test is that perfect duplicates share all their intrinsic properties. Now the argument. Take Socrates, bent at noon and straight at six. Being bent and being straight look intrinsic, and they are incompatible. If Socrates is wholly present at noon and wholly present at six, then one and the same object both has and lacks the very same intrinsic property, which cannot be. The argument needs the property to be intrinsic: if what changes is instead a relational property like being taller than Theaetetus, the endurantist has no problem at all, since a single object can perfectly well stand in different relations to different things at different times. So the endurantist must either say that bent and straight are not really intrinsic but are relations to times, or give up. The perdurantist, by contrast, says that the temporal part at noon is bent and the temporal part at six is straight, while the whole object has neither property and instead stands in the relation is-bent-at to noon. Note that because the perdurantist needs a distinct part for each time, perdurantism is committed to time's being composed of durationless instants; if there were no instants there would be no part corresponding to a single time, and the solution would collapse.

**(b)** The relativizing reply says that, strictly, no object ever has a shape full stop. The real property is the relational one being-bent-at-noon, which Socrates has timelessly, at every time, in just the way he has being-taller-than-Theaetetus. Nothing both has and lacks a property, so there is no contradiction. What it costs: on this view no object ever changes intrinsically, and every temporary property turns out to be a relation to a time. That threatens to collapse the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction altogether, because even the paradigm intrinsic properties like shape now count as extrinsic. It also seems to get the explanation backwards: whether I am bent should depend on what shape I have, not on what time it is.

**(c)** Facet theory is not perdurantism, because perdurantism just is the doctrine of temporal parts, and the whole point of facet theory is that the facets are not parts of the object — they are numerically distinct items. So it is a form of endurantism with some extra machinery bolted on. It also handles the argument handily: the temporary property is still had intrinsically by something, namely the facet, without being turned into a relation, so nothing has incompatible intrinsic properties. That shows that the argument from temporary intrinsics is neutral in the dispute between endurantism and perdurantism. It shows that we need *some* story about temporary properties, not that the perdurantist's story is better; facet theory shows that an endurantist-friendly story does the same work for free. So the argument carries no real dialectical force for or against either view.
