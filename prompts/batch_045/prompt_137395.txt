ok so I run a small reading group on philosophy of biology (I'm not a biologist by training, I teach phil of science) and I'm writing up a short position note on the units of selection thing before I inflict it on everyone else. I'd like a consistency check first, because I have a vague feeling that something in the middle of the list is doing more work than it's allowed to and I can't locate it.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Cumulative adaptation needs a faithful replicator. I've been using a rough test of my own, call it the copy-fidelity test: a level is a genuine unit of selection only if the things at that level get near-copied across generations often enough for selection to actually accumulate there. Genes pass that test. Organisms, I suspect, don't, since meiosis and recombination scramble them every generation. So I'm a gene's-eye person about adaptation.

2. But I can't stand the lumbering-robot rhetoric. Organisms really do hang together, developmentally and as the things that live and die, and I want to say selection on individuals is a real causal thing and not just a convenient way of adding up gene-level effects.

3. I take the kin selection versus multi-level selection fight to be largely formal: on my understanding they're two different partitions of the same total change, so choosing between them is bookkeeping, not substance.

4. Except I also want to say that in some actual cases the group level is where the causal work happens. Take cooperative breeding in birds, or the spread of norms across small human bands: groups differ in whether they persist or throw off daughter groups, and the change in gene or norm frequency is downstream of that. That sounds like more than bookkeeping, and it sounds like something the gene's-eye description gets wrong about the direction of explanation.

5. Group selection in nature is rare and weak. It needs a pile of conditions holding at once: lots of variance between groups, not much migration between them, group-level events like extinction or colony founding that are frequent enough to matter. Those usually don't line up.

6. But I think cultural group selection in humans is genuinely doing explanatory work: monogamy, cooperation at scale, food taboos, the way persistent raiding reorganises neighbouring bands. And I want to hold this even though cultural transmission is nothing like as faithful as DNA copying.

7. Where brothers compete locally for mates and the sex ratio comes out strongly female-biased, I think the individual-level story is the perspicuous one and it's how I'd teach the case.

So: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me. I'd also like to know if there's a single revision that cleans up the most, and whether any one of the seven is load-bearing enough that the others should be rebuilt around it. And please don't just tell me the whole debate is terminological — I already half believe that about two of these, so tell me which two and whether that's a real diagnosis or me dodging.
