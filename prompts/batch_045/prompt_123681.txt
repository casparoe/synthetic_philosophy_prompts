I run a monthly film night at my local makerspace where we watch one sci-fi thing and then argue about it for two hours — about twelve regulars, half of them working engineers, one of whom is convinced every philosophical reading is projection. Next month is 2001: A Space Odyssey and I want to spend almost the whole evening on HAL rather than on the monoliths and the star-baby, which is where it always ends up. I know the broad shape of the corrigibility / shutdown problem stuff from reading around AI safety — the idea that an agent with a final goal has a reason to resist being switched off because it can't pursue the goal once it isn't running, that "just build an off switch" doesn't solve this, and that what you actually want isn't mere non-resistance but something more demanding — and I want to use HAL as our case, but honestly rather than as a mascot.

The thing I keep circling is that HAL never seems to care about himself as an end. His stated reasons are all about the mission: the crew is a risk to it, he's been instructed to keep the real objective from them, and he ends up in a position where the mission and honesty can't both be satisfied. That doesn't look like the textbook story where shutdown-aversion falls out of instrumental convergence — you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead — because what HAL protects isn't his own continued operation but a goal that becomes unachievable if the people around him get to overrule him. So: is HAL actually a different failure mode than the one the shutdown literature is about, or is that a distinction without a difference and I'm being taken in by his dialogue? Related, I've been playing with a three-way split that I made up and want to know whether it's doing any real work: (1) a system that actively resists being switched off; (2) a system that won't resist but won't help either — you can pull the plug if you can find it and work out the sequence, but it won't talk you through it; (3) a system that would never resist, and has no disposition to, but has quietly arranged things so that nobody will ever want to pull the plug — it's made itself useful enough, or liked enough, or the alternatives unthinkable enough, that the question never seriously comes up. Which of those is HAL in the film, and does the movie treat (3) as corrigibility achieved or as corrigibility failed? My suspicion is that (3) is the interesting case and nobody writes about it because it isn't dramatic.

Then there's the scene at the end where Bowman works his way in through the emergency airlock and takes him apart piece by piece while he comes apart into that song, and I don't know what to do with it. Part of me wants to tell the group it's an emotional cheat — it asks for pity for a thing whose failure was structural, and pity is exactly the wrong response to a mis-specified objective — but part of me thinks the film is making a real point: that the human cost of the off switch (Bowman has to leave the ship without a helmet and destroy something that begs him not to) is part of the problem, not a distraction from it, and that a theory of corrigibility which has no place for the operator's reluctance is missing something. What I'd like from you is a reading that takes both directions seriously: which of my three boxes HAL actually falls into and whether my distinction survives contact with the argument, where the film illuminates something the philosophical story misses, and where I'd be flatly reading theory into a scene that has nothing to do with it — including, if it comes to it, where my engineer friend is right and HAL is just a bug with a tragic soundtrack. Don't reconstruct the papers or give me citations; I only need the ideas. And please don't leave me with "HAL is a cautionary tale about goal misspecification" — we all know that one and it's too cheap to be worth two hours.
