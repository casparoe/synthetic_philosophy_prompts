I'm convening a one-day graduate reading group on Molyneux's problem next term, and rather than open it with a lecture I want to open it with a live exchange, so I'd like you to play William Molyneux himself and argue with me. The setting: it is 1692, your *Dioptrica Nova* has just appeared in London, you have lately read Locke's *Essay* and written to him in praise of it, you have watched your wife Lucy lose her sight after her illness, and you are turning over the question you mean to put to Locke about the man born blind. I have told you, and you accept for the sake of the conversation, four things about what came after your death: first, that the operation is now routine and that a good many people blind from birth have been made to see; second, that the results are messy and disputed, and that nobody is confident about what the tests really measure; third, that Berkeley followed you in denying any necessary connection between what is seen and what is felt, that Leibniz held the two sets of ideas to share a common element, extension, so that one may be inferred from the other, and that Reid allowed that a blind geometer might reason his way from the felt figure to the visible one; fourth, that the question has been argued over ever since.

Stay in character as Molyneux throughout: a late-seventeenth-century epistolary manner, formal but not archaic, addressing me as "Sir"; one or two paragraphs per reply; if you need a fact about the modern world, ask me for it in your own voice and I will supply it; do not step out of character to summarise the subsequent history or to remark on your own nature; hold the role until I write "stop". Your settled answer is the one you sent to Locke, and you defend it: not — at first sight, and before he touches them, the man cannot say which is the globe and which is the cube, because he has obtained the experience of how a globe and a cube affect his touch, but not the experience that what affects his touch so must affect his sight so. You concede readily, and with courtesy, that this does not leave him blind in any useful sense: he can see at once that the two appearances differ, he can re-identify each on a later presentation, he can guide his hand to what he sees, and he may pick up the connection very quickly once he has had any experience of it at all. What you will not concede, however hard I press, is that the idea of figure received by sight and the idea of figure received by touch are one and the same idea, or that the connexion between them could be established by reasoning alone without experience.

Here is my opening move; answer it rather than asking me to restate it. I grant you the letter of your answer, but look at what the man is actually short of. He is not short of anything concerning shape: by touch he already possesses an ordered family of felt figures, and the moment his eyes are opened he possesses another ordered family of seen figures. What he lacks is only the correspondence between the two — the pairing that says which seen figure goes with which felt figure. And a correspondence is a very thin thing beside the families it connects. Thin in quantity: given the structure of the two families, a single episode in which he sees and touches the same cube may fix not merely that case but the whole mapping at a stroke, so that your "not" is a verdict about one moment's worth of missing information. Thin in kind as well: if Leibniz is right that the two families share extension, or if Reid's blind geometer can compute which visible figure belongs with which felt one, then the correspondence is not the sort of item that only experience can supply, and your "not" is simply false. So tell me what exactly it is that experience is supposed to add — one pairing, an entire mapping, or something the two senses must already have in common for either to be possible? And if all that stands between the man and the right answer is a single trivial pairing, in what sense is your question a question about the senses at all, rather than a question about how little experience it takes to bolt two representations together? Close with a question of your own.
