Next month I am giving a work-in-progress paper at a joint session of the philosophy of mind group and the epistemology group in a department I am visiting: forty minutes of talk, forty minutes of discussion, seminar table rather than lectern. The room will be roughly this: two internalists of the older sort, one of whom has argued at length that the extended-mind literature trades on confusing causal coupling with constitution and arrives expecting my paper to be one more instance of that confusion; a younger colleague working in predictive processing and active inference, who thinks the boundary-of-cognition question dissolves once you have Markov blankets and who will be impatient with anything framed as a dispute about where mental states are located; a social epistemologist whose work concerns epistemic injustice and how institutions decide which informants and which instruments get credited with knowledge, and who will want to know what my view implies about artifacts that are not neutrally or equally available; a philosopher of psychiatry working on confabulation and delusion, who is the reason I settled on the case I did; and fifteen or so graduate students who are broadly sympathetic to externalism about content but anxious about whether this literature makes any checkable claim about the world.

The thesis is a reformulation of the extended mind thesis. I argue that the parity principle is close to toothless, because any causal coupling to an environment can be given a functional description that matches some putative internal process, so parity will always return yes; the real work in the classic cases is being done by something the principle never states. I replace it with what I call the delegation condition: a subject counts as having extended a cognitive process into an artifact just in case (i) she has assigned the artifact a specific cognitive sub-task rather than merely using it opportunistically, (ii) she treats its outputs as presumptively authoritative for that sub-task, in the sense that she acts on them without re-deriving them, and (iii) she is disposed to answer for its errors the way she answers for her own slips — that is, she offers corrections rather than excuses, and treats a failure as a reason to revise the artifact or her reliance on it rather than as a bare fact about the world.

The case I build the talk around is what I have been calling the confabulating surrogate. A woman with a degenerative memory condition wears a device that records her days and plays them back on request; the device, like any compression scheme, fills gaps with plausible invented detail; she relies on it entirely, acts on what it returns, corrects it when it fails her, and never considers the possibility that it invents. On my condition the surrogate is part of her memory process, and so the consequence I have to face is that she genuinely remembers, and arguably comes to know, episodes that never happened. My planned response is two-part: first, separate membership in a cognitive process from entitlement to its deliverances, so that externality of process is compatible with failure of justification; second, argue that objections from unreliability, contamination, or opacity are parochial, since structurally identical problems arise for neural memory in the confabulation syndromes, and so cannot be doing the work the internalist needs them to do.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually produce, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the idiom of the person who will raise it rather than in neutral philosophical English, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which of my three clauses is most likely to be the pressure point, and tell me honestly whether the delegation condition quietly collapses into an internalist criterion for an externalist conclusion, since if it does I would rather say so in the talk than be told so at the table. Tell me what I should concede in the body of the paper rather than in discussion, and which single reply has to land if the proposal is to survive the hour. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks. You can set aside the objections I already have stock answers to — the coupling-constitution complaint, the regress about where the boundary stops, and the usual conditions of trust, reliability and portability.
