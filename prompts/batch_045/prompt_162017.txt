I'm trying to work out what I actually think about a certain class of questions, and I'd rather start from my own reactions to cases than from a theory. The questions all have one shape: they ask what there is, and by the nature of the case no information bearing on the answer could ever reach the person asking. Sometimes I am sure the question has an answer and we simply cannot get it. Sometimes I think there is nothing there to be answered at all. I would like to know whether there is a principle that sorts these cases the way I do.

First: a colony ship launched long before cosmic expansion accelerated is now receding from us in such a way that no signal from it can ever arrive, and nothing we send can ever catch it. Just before departure the crew flipped a coin to decide which of two stars to make for. I am confident that the coin landed one way or the other, that there is a fact of the matter, and that we will never learn it. Being cut off from the answer does not, by itself, make the question empty.

Second: a civilization lives out its entire history inside a long-running computer simulation with a very large map. In one build, the whole map is written out in memory at the start; in another, terrain is generated only as inhabitants draw near, from a source whose output is not fixed until the moment it is drawn. The inhabitants may travel where they like, and nothing they can ever measure distinguishes the builds. My reaction is that in the second build, and before anyone travels there, there is no fact of the matter about what lies beyond the next ridge, whereas in the first build there is. I dislike this reaction. It makes whether the inhabitants' question about the far country has an answer at all depend on a detail of implementation they cannot inspect, and it leaves me unable to say which build they are in while still wanting to say something about their question.

Third: a physical theory can be written in two ways that agree on every possible measurement and differ only about a quantity attached to every point of space, a quantity that can be shifted by any smooth amount without changing a single prediction. Here I think there is no fact of the matter about that quantity's real value. It looks like slack in the description rather than a feature of the world.

Fourth: one account of a world containing genuinely chancy events holds that every possible outcome of every such event is realized, in an enormous structure of histories that never interact with one another; another holds that only one outcome is realized and the rest are merely possible. Nothing any observer could ever record tells between them, since by hypothesis the histories do not interact. I want to say there is no fact of the matter about whether the other histories exist, that the claim has come loose from anything. But I notice that in the first account the dynamics does generate them, so by the standard I applied in the first case something settles them, and my verdict looks inconsistent with that standard.

Fifth, the case I keep going back and forth on: whether there are galaxies beyond our cosmological horizon. Our best cosmology can be extended in two ways that fit everything we can observe and that disagree about this. Half the time I think that plainly there either are or there are not. Half the time I think the question belongs with the third and fourth cases.

The principle I keep trying out is that a question about what exists has an answer just in case something in the world settles it, and no answer when nothing does: no difference without something to make the difference. I am fairly sure that principle yields the wrong result in the fourth case, and I cannot repair it by adding a clause about accessibility, because the first case is precisely one where the answer is inaccessible to us and I am certain it exists.

So: is there a principle that captures my verdicts, or comes close? If none does cleanly, tell me which verdict you would overturn and why that one rather than another, and state as precisely as you can the principle you would put in its place. Then tell me what that principle says about two cases I have not settled: whether a previous cycle of a cyclic universe contained cities, given that each cycle erases every trace of the one before it; and whether a solitary animal in a region of an otherwise empty universe that no observer will ever reach is in pain.

Please separate, as you go, what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the standard view; where you are unsure, say so, and if one of my cases rests on a description you think is confused, tell me that as well. Answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long email, and write in American English.
