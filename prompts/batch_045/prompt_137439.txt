Next month's episode of my interview series on the philosophy of logic features Dr. Marisol Vance, a dialetheist working broadly in the Graham Priest tradition, and I want the question list to be considerably sharper than the usual "so you think contradictions can be true?" routine. Her new monograph argues that true contradictions are not a peculiarity of the semantic paradoxes: she extends the glut-theoretic treatment to non-semantic cases, most prominently the instant at which a body begins to move (which she claims is an instant at which the body is at rest and also not at rest) and hard legal conflicts, where two validly enacted statutes impose demands that cannot both be satisfied and where, she argues, it is true both that the agent ought to φ and that the agent ought not to φ. Her stated commitments are: unrestricted disquotation for the truth predicate; the Liar sentence and its negation both true; the correct base logic is the paraconsistent system whose negation behaves as in FDE and in which validity is preservation of truth (so a valid inference may land on a conclusion that is true and also false); classical reasoning recoverable inside "consistent" regions of discourse; and no global triviality.

Draft eight to ten questions, in the order you would ask them, with no throat-clearing items. For each question, state in a sentence or two the reply she is most likely to give, and then give the follow-up that that specific reply invites — I want the sequence to close off her exits rather than let her restate the formalism. At least one question each on:

1. The charge that her negation is not negation: that an operator for which A and ¬A can both be true is at best a subcontrary-forming device, and that calling it "not" is a stipulation.
2. Curry's paradox and the conditional: her logic must block a paradox that uses no negation at all, and the block seems to cost contraction, or modus ponens, or the deduction theorem — press what exactly is given up and whether the surviving conditional is the one used in ordinary mathematical reasoning.
3. Assertion, denial, and disagreement: if A and ¬A can both be true, "¬A" cannot simply be a correction of "A"; anticipate the apparatus she reaches for (a primitive rejection or denial, or an operator for being true and not false) and then press the strengthened denials it invites.
4. Her extralogical cases: what test she has for a genuine contradiction as opposed to a merely apparent incompatibility that a better theory would dissolve — note that for the legal case she will likely deny that "ought" agglomerates over conjunction, which may mean her dilemma is not a contradiction in the first place — and whether her view is a metaphysical one, about an inconsistent reality, or only a semantic one, about our representations.
5. One question built around a case I will put to her on air: the control software of a chemical plant is verified against a formal specification known to entail both that valve V is closed at t and that valve V is not closed at t, the inconsistency traces to two incompatible statutory requirements, no revision is available, and a shift supervisor must decide whether to send a crew into the chamber. What does the dialetheist say the supervisor believes, asserts, and does, and does the truth of "V is not closed" give him any reason at all? If the honest practical answer is to treat the specification as unusable, ask in what her disagreement with the classicist who says an inconsistent theory is worthless then consists.
6. Leakage: given unrestricted disquotation and the ubiquity of truth-talk, how she can tell from the inside whether the region she is reasoning in is one of the "consistent" ones, and what her answer implies about how much of ordinary discourse is insulated from the glut.
7. Methodology: she argues largely abductively, trading on uniformity of solution across the paradoxes; ask what would count as evidence against dialetheism, and whether any development — a non-ad-hoc rival treatment, or a collapse result — could settle the matter rather than merely shift the cost-benefit ledger.

Where a question turns on a technical choice she has not publicly made, flag the fork and write the follow-up for the branch you think is more likely.
