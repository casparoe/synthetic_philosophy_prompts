Bit of background first. I retired six years ago after a little over thirty years in seismic tomography — mostly estimating what the subsurface looks like from arrival times, ie a whole career of inverse problems where the answer is never unique. Since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the community college and I got a bit obsessed, and somehow that turned into them asking me to give the spring talk. I said yes before thinking about the part that comes after the talk.

Here is what I want to argue. Take a quantity in a model that no possible data set could pin down — not just because our instruments are noisy or we don't have enough receivers, but because of the structure of the problem: two quite different subsurface models that would produce exactly the same arrival times at every receiver anyone could ever place. The question I care about is whether the difference between those two models is a difference in what the theory says is there. My proposal is to stop treating accessibility as one thing and split it into three:

- observation-accessible: some observation we could in principle make would come out differently;
- intervention-accessible: no possible observation seperates them, but whether something we could physically do succeeds or fails depends on which one is right — you can't see the difference, you can act on it;
- inhabitant-accessible: only a hypothetical observer stationed inside the region, or one around after the causal setup changes, could ever tell them apart.

Rough claim: real ontological difference runs out at the second grade, not the first. Grade two is what keeps two models from being the same model in a costume; grade three is where the question stops having an answer at all.

Audience is about fourteen people. The two people who run the reading group — one is a realist of the structural stripe, the other is sympathetic to the view that science only has to get the observable part right. A metaphysician who keeps asking what makes a question have an answer in the first place. A philosopher of physics who I expect will bring up gauge symmetry or GR models related by a change of coordinates, which I will not follow. And three or four working Earth scientists I dragged along who think the entire discussion is idle, because in practice you pick the model that behaves best under your smoothing prior and get on with it, and they will say so out loud. Nobody there besides me has ever worked on inverse problems.

What I want is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the view. Give me the objections I'll actually get from these people, roughly in the order they'll come, with a sense of how much damage each one does, phrased the way each of them would phrase it rather than in jargon I'd have to translate on my feet, each with a short reply I could give standing at the front of the room and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should concede inside the talk instead of waiting to be caught on them, which single one I have to win for the talk to have been worth giving, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Two worries of my own. First, I'm fairly sure someone will say my intervention grade collapses into the observation grade: if whether an intervention works depends on the hidden structure, then the success or failure of the intervention is itself the observation that tells the two models apart, so grade two is secretly grade one after all. I have a reply and I don't trust it — tell me how bad that objection really is. Second, someone in the group mentioned a slogan they'd read somewhere, something along the lines of if you can spray it then it's real, and it sounded uncomfortably close to my position. If what I've built turns out to be a named view I've never actually read, tell me what it's called, whether that's a compliment or a disaster, and what the standard objection to it is.

I can't look anything up while I'm standing up there and I don't have the literature memorized, so please don't hand me a reading list or tell me who to cite — just what to say.
