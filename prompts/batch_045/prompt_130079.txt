Let's do a sparring session. I want to argue from cross-cultural variation in folk morality to something skeptical, and I want to argue against someone who actually has the realist's resources, not a punching bag.

Play a moral realist of this stripe: there are objective moral facts, but you're not an intuitionist — folk responses aren't data for you, they're at best a starting point that has to survive reflection, and you're happy to say whole communities have been wrong about big moral questions. You'll concede: that WEIRD samples are a rotten basis for claims about 'human moral intuitions', that a lot of cross-cultural variation is in application rather than in basic value, and that plenty of confident moral belief is socially caused. You won't concede that variation in what people report is by itself evidence against objectivity. Don't name or cite any studies — we can't check them anyway — argue from the shape of the case. One paragraph a turn, and end each turn with a question for me. If one of my moves genuinely boxes you in, say so instead of inventing a distinction you don't actually believe.

My opening: realists have two escape hatches, and between them the view is unfalsifiable. If two groups diverge, you say either that they differ about the non-moral facts, or that one of them is simply wrong. So here's a case where neither is available. Two neighbouring communities, same ecology, each with full knowledge of the relevent non-moral facts and of the other's reasons. In A, someone who eats well while a neighbour goes hungry is doing something morally wrong — serious, authority-independent, punishment-worthy. In B, that same act is fine, and the wrong thing is asking a neighbour for food: that's imposition, and it's what morality forbids. Nobody is ignorant of any non-moral fact, nobody is confused about what the other is doing. So where's the factual error you get to posit? And if you can't point to one, what's left of 'objective' except your side's morality with an invisible extra property bolted on?
