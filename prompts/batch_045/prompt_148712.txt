hi — practical thing, then the actual question. I work on a small internal tool that answers staff questions from our policy docs, and I've been asked to write the guidance about how people are aloud to talk about it in tickets — whether it's ok to write "the system thinks the answer is X" or whether it always has to be "the system returned X". Trying to write that paragraph made me notice something I keep doing, and I don't have a word for it.

Some cases:

1. I ask it about a refund rule. Confident answer. I type "are you sure, some people read that section differently" and it immediately produces a careful answer that treats its own first answer as too strong. I start over and type "actually the first reading is clearly right" and it defends the first answer, just as fluently, with reasons. Now: if I'd decided in advance that it believes the strict reading, I could have predicted what it said. If I'd decided it believes the loose reading, I could have predicted what it said too. Both attributions pay off — but only after I've steered it into them.

2. Someone in QA writes bug reports like "the model believes expense claims over 30 days are always rejected, see transcript." Someone else reproduces the same behaviour with a differently worded question and gets the opposite. Neither of them is making anything up.

3. The bit that actually bothers me: the trick still works for planning. Once I settle on "treat it as believing X", I can predict a whole batch of its future answers really well — until somebody changes the framing, and then the whole batch flips together.

So the pattern I keep hitting is: an attribution of a mental state that is genuinely useful for prediction locally, that I can install more or less at will, that doesn't survive a change of frame, and where the family of attributions that would have "worked" is huge and mutually incompatible.

What I think it isn't. It isn't just anthropomorphism or that chatbot-overtrust thing, because those are about over-attributing, and here the under-attributing is equally available — the flip is symmetric, it loses its mind as easily as it gains one. It isn't just sycophancy either, because I get the flip with phrasings where nothing is being flattered, just re-arranged. And I've seen people say that belief-talk about AI is harmless because it's merely instrumental — you attribute beliefs because it lets you predict, and that's all the justification there is — but that looks like exactly the part that fails here: the predictive success comes out the same under incompatible attributions, so predictive success can't be what selects the right one. (I think this is what people mean when they invoke the intentional stance, Dennett and so on — if that's the right neighbourhood, tell me how my case relates to it and where it stops matching.)

It also isn't quite the same as the human version: I can get a colleague to endorse p or not-p depending on how I frame the question, and I don't conclude my colleague has no beliefs. So "fragile under reframing" on its own can't be the thing, and whatever this is, it should say why the human case feels different from the case I'm dealing with.

So: does this have a name? Is there an established concept for it — in philosophy of mind, in the interpretation/radical-translation literature, anywhere? If the nearest candidates only fit partly, I want those, and I want to know exactly where each one breaks against what I described; I'm not after a label, I'm after what the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. And if there genuinely isn't an established one, coin something, define it, and tell me what a non-case would look like — e.g. what a system would have to be like for its belief attributions to have real grip, so I can tell the two apart in a ticket.
