I spent forty years as an experimental physical chemist — electrochemistry and surface science, mostly — and since retiring I have been reading my way into epistemology and the theory of rational choice. The distinction that keeps being put to work there is the one between the inside view and the outside view, together with the epistemic modesty that is supposed to follow from taking the outside view seriously. I want to know how much weight that pair of ideas can actually bear, and I would rather test them on two episodes from the 1980s that I followed closely as a working scientist than on invented puzzles. One of the two claims was false and one was true, and I have never seen a satisfactory account of what the difference was supposed to be from the inside, at the time.

The two episodes, with the facts I take to be relevant. If I have a detail wrong, correct the detail and carry on; nothing below should turn on it.

Episode A. On 23 March 1989 Stanley Pons of the University of Utah and Martin Fleischmann of Southampton — a Fellow of the Royal Society, one of the world's leading electrochemists, whose earlier work includes the first observation of surface-enhanced Raman scattering — announced at a press conference, before peer-reviewed publication and with almost no experimental detail released, that a tabletop electrolysis cell using heavy water and a palladium cathode had produced excess heat of a magnitude they could not explain except by nuclear processes, along with small amounts of neutrons and tritium. The announcement was made under pressure of a competing claim from a group at Brigham Young University. The objection that mattered was theoretical: if the reported heat came from deuterium–deuterium fusion, the neutron flux implied would have killed the experimenters, and the two branches of that reaction proceed at comparable rates, so there is no way to have the heat without the neutrons. Replications mostly failed, some early positive reports were retracted, the original calorimetry drew methodological criticism, and by late 1989 a Department of Energy panel concluded that the evidence was not convincing and declined to fund the field specifically; a second review in 2004 reached broadly the same conclusion less brusquely. A small research community continues under the name of low-energy nuclear reactions. For the reference class, note the prehistory: in the 1920s Paneth and Peters reported the conversion of hydrogen to helium by finely divided palladium and retracted it, the helium having come from the air; and Tandberg ran an electrolytic cell with palladium electrodes, applied for a Swedish patent for a method of producing helium and useful energy, and was refused.

Episode B. Robin Warren, a pathologist in Perth, saw curved bacilli on gastric biopsies; Barry Marshall, then a junior physician, joined him; they cultured the organism in 1982 and published in 1984. The stomach had been regarded as sterile since a 1954 study that found no bacteria in biopsy material stained in the conventional way and put the earlier reports down to contamination — reports of spiral organisms in the human stomach going back to the end of the nineteenth century. The prevailing doctrine was that ulcers were caused by acid and stress, around which a very large industry of acid suppression had been built, and the old dictum was that without acid there is no ulcer. Koch's postulates were not satisfied in the ordinary way at the time, there being no convenient animal model; Marshall drank a culture and gave himself a gastritis; it took roughly a decade and a consensus conference before the infectious account of peptic ulcer was accepted, and Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in 2005.

What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions: what the inside/outside distinction says these actors faced and should have done, which features of each episode it explains and which it misses or actively misdescribes, and what the pair of them together shows about the distinction itself. Please hold to the following.

First, date-stamp everything. The outcomes are known to us and were not known to them, and hindsight will do all the work if you let it. For each judgment you attribute to an actor, say what information set you are holding fixed and at what date.

Second, do not treat the outside view as one thing. Separate, at least: base-rate reasoning from a reference class; deference to the well-confirmed theories of adjacent fields; and what I would call signal-reading, the assessment of the claimants' credibility from how they proceeded — the press conference before publication, the withholding of details, the self-experiment that proved nothing statistically but changed the conversation. Then tell me how much of the reception in each case each ingredient actually explains. My own suspicion, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that what passed for the outside view in 1989 was mostly the second and third ingredients wearing the outside view's clothes, and that base rates did very little work in either episode.

Third, name the agent and the register. The distinction is usually drawn for a single agent facing a single decision, but here the agents are individuals, laboratories, referees, funders and the community at large, and the decisions are whether to believe, whether to work on it, whether to fund it and whether to publish it. These come apart: a state legislature voted several million dollars for a cold fusion institute within months of the announcement, at the same time as most working scientists thought the claim was dead. Tell me what the outside view governs in each register, and whether epistemic modesty says the same thing in all of them.

Fourth, engage three difficulties I have not seen handled. (a) The retrospective reference class: the class of failed revolutionary tabletop claims is at least partly defined by its outcome, since a successful member would be filed under a different description, and the pre-1954 reports of gastric bacteria were true observations sitting inside a class everyone took to be failures. Is the base rate therefore contaminated, or can it be repaired by an outcome-independent reference class — claims announced by press conference, say, or claims whose originators decline to release their protocol — and would the repair have changed either verdict? (b) The ratchet: if the outside view is applied not only to belief but to pursuit, then no one ever gathers the evidence that would move the base rate, and the base rate can never improve. Is that a reductio of the outside view as a norm, or only a reason to confine it to acceptance? (c) The distributed agent: in a community of many inquirers it may be optimal for most to take the outside view and a few to take the inside view, in which case epistemic modesty for any one of them is a different virtue than it is for a solitary agent — it may require deferring about what to believe while persisting in what to investigate. Say what the theory has available here and what it does not.

Fifth, be explicit about silence. If the inside/outside framework has nothing to say about some feature of these episodes — about the role of the patent and priority pressure, about the asymmetry between the cost of a false positive and a false negative, about why one community reversed within a decade and the other never did — say so plainly rather than stretching the framework to cover it.

Close with a verdict of no more than a short paragraph: does this pair confirm the outside view and the modesty it recommends, present an anomaly to them, or show that they were only ever claims about a restricted domain? And answer one question I have never been able to answer to my own satisfaction: for the community as a whole, which error should have been preferred, and does the answer change depending on whether we score the community on the accuracy of its beliefs or on the allocation of its effort?

Please write about 1,200 to 1,500 words. I am not asking for a literature survey, a reading list or any citations; argue the case from the episodes and define any term of art you use rather than attributing it.
