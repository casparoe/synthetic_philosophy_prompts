I run the grants side of a small health-research foundation — two rounds a year, ~150 applications each, every one read by 4–6 external reviewers who fill in a score sheet, and then a panel of five of us meets on Zoom for about fifteen minutes per application to make a yes/no call. Reviewers are volunteers so I can't just keep adding more of them, I've got no statistician on staff, the panel chair has to be able to sign off on whatever we do, and anything I change has to be explainable to our board in plain english and fit in a spreadsheet. I also can't spend more than a couple of hours per round messing with process.

The thing I keep coming back to is the Condorcet jury theorem — the bit about a lot of imperfect-but-better-than-chance voters beating one careful one, and the nasty flip side where if each voter is worse than chance then adding more of them makes the group worse, not better. I want that turned into something I can actually run, not just admire.

So: write me a decision procedure. The questions to ask in order (some once per round when I set the process up, some per application when the panel is stuck), what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision over to a person because the disagreement is about what we value rather than about what's true.

It has to settle at least these:

- How many reviewers, and when adding one more reviewer is worth it versus spending the same effort on making reviews better-informed (paying a second reader from the *other* field, or a fifteen-minute call with the applicant to clear something up).
- Whether reviewers should see each other's draft reviews before scoring. I have a feeling sharing helps them catch real problems but wrecks the independence the theorem wants, and I have no idea how to tell which effect is bigger. Someone told me that once reviewers' errors are correlated, accuracy stops climbing and flattens out somewhere short of certainty no matter how many people you pile on — if that's even roughly right, tell me what I'm supposed to *do* with it.
- Our panel discusses first and then votes. Should each of us write down a private vote before anyone talks?
- What threshold: simple majority, two-thirds, unanimity, or our current unwritten rule that one "strong reject" kills an application even when the other four are enthusiastic (people are scared of being the one who waved through a dud). I suspect the veto rule is just a very high threshold wearing a disguise, and I want to know what it's costing us and what would be better.
- Reviewers score three separate things — importance, methods, feasibility — and then we aggregate. I've noticed the panel can be majority-yes on each of the three and majority-no overall, or the other way round. Should we vote on the criteria and combine, or vote on the overall call? Which is less likely to be wrong?
- What to do when the split is factual versus when it's about risk appetite (a safe boring project versus a long shot), and when the right move is to hand the decision to one named person instead of the group.
- Some estimate of our reviewers' actual accuracy. We have outcome data for funded projects, obviously not for rejected ones; the only rejected applications we can check are about twenty that got picked up by another funder and we can see how they did. Tell me honestly what I can and cannot infer from that, and what to do when I can't get a number at all.

Then apply the whole thing to three cases: (1) a solid, unexciting application that everybody mildly likes; (2) an interdisciplinary application where all our reviewers sit on one side of the discipline and I genuinely worry their accuracy on it is below chance; (3) an application where two reviewers say the statistical plan is fine and three say it's broken, and nobody can settle it without seeing the raw data.

Please be straight about which steps are really just my judgement dressed up as procedure, and which parts of the theorem I'm quietly violating. And give me at least one worked case where the honest output of the procedure is something I won't like — ideally something our board would probably refuse to approve — and say what you'd do then. And please don't give me a reading list or footnotes; I want the reasoning, not pointers to papers I can't get to.
