I chair the emerging-risk committee of a mid-sized mutual insurer. We meet for two hours every fortnight and vote yes or no on questions of the form should we treat this as a material exposure: a cluster of claims in one region, a new regulation that may or may not reach our book, a technology that may or may not create a liability, a rumoured supply-chain chokepoint. Nine people vote. They are a mixed group — two actuaries, a lawyer, an underwriter, a claims manager, a data scientist, and three rotating business-unit representatives — and for any given question at most one or two of them have real domain knowledge. Our current default is a show of hands at the end of the discussion, decided by simple majority, and I have come to suspect that this default is wrong for several of the decision types we face, though I cannot say which or why.

What I want is a written procedure that can go into our terms of reference and be run in about ten minutes at the start of the item, before anyone speaks. It should be a sequence of gates, in order, each of which says what question is being asked, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a fixed rule, and where it hands the decision on to a named person because the remaining question is one of values or of unmeasurable fact rather than something an aggregation rule can settle. Two constraints on form. First, no member of the committee can do anything beyond simple arithmetic, so if a number matters, give it approximately and say plainly that it is approximate; the procedure has to survive being applied by someone who has never opened a statistics textbook. Second, any rule we adopt has to be fixed in advance and signed off by our chief risk officer, because I am not permitted to choose an aggregation rule after seeing who is in the room or how the discussion went. Also: state in your own words any formal result you rely on rather than appealing to authorities or naming results you will not spell out, and mark clearly which parts of the procedure follow from the result and which are your own stipulations about our situation.

The complications I need the gates to handle, because I do not think I can think about them reliably on the fly:

Whether the question in front of us has a truth that will eventually be revealed at all. Some of our votes are really questions about our risk appetite dressed up as forecasts, and it seems to me that an argument for majority rule from the reliability of groups buys nothing when there is no fact of the matter to be reliable about.

Whether the question is secretly compound. Will this new regulation apply to us and bite within five years is two claims, not one, and it looks to me as though taking a majority on a conjunction can be worse than taking a majority on either conjunct even when everyone's errors are independent. If the package vote can come out the opposite way from the votes on its parts, tell me which one we should act on.

Whether our members are actually better than chance on this kind of question, and what to do when we cannot say. We keep a running record of how each member has done on decisions that have since resolved, but only for six of the nine people and only for the two decision types we have been scoring; for most item types there is no track record at all.

Whether our errors are independent. Everyone reads the same briefing note I circulate; the data scientist's model output is the main input for two other members' views; the lawyer writes the paper that the claims manager reads; most of us read the same two news sources; the most senior person tends to speak first and the room tends to follow. I want a gate that asks how many genuinely separate information channels are in the room rather than how many heads there are, and I want it to say what to do when the honest answer is two or three.

Deliberation. Our regulator requires that the committee's discussion be minuted, so I cannot replace discussion with a silent vote, but I can add a silent poll before it. The awkward part, as I see it, is that discussion probably makes people more accurate and at the same time makes their errors line up, and I do not know how to weigh a pre-discussion poll against a post-discussion show of hands.

Whether one member is enough better than the rest that voting dilutes rather than improves the answer, and how big a gap has to be before deferring to that person beats any vote.

Asymmetric costs. Missing a real exposure is much worse for us than reserving for one that never materialises, and the ratio differs by decision type. Simple majority presumably stops being the right threshold once the two errors cost different amounts, and I would like the procedure to say how the ratio moves the threshold rather than just noting that it does.

Whether members' votes track what they think is true or what they want. Three of our nine represent business units whose results depend on our continuing to write a given line, and I do not trust that their votes are purely judgments.

Small numbers. We have nine people, and where we can measure competence at all it is somewhere around a coin flip plus a little. The result everyone invokes is about groups growing without limit, and I strongly suspect that at our size with our competence levels the guarantee is nearly vacuous; if that is right, I want the procedure to say so in numbers rather than as a caveat.

Then apply the finished procedure to three of our recurring items. The first: a spike in water-damage claims in one coastal region over two quarters, where the vote is signal or reporting artefact, resolution arrives within about six months, six members have scored track records, nobody has a specialist advantage, and the two error types cost roughly the same. The second: whether an incoming disclosure regulation will capture our agricultural book, where our general counsel is the only person with any real purchase on the question and the rest of us would be guessing. The third: a report of a novel respiratory outbreak in a single city abroad, and the question whether we should now treat pandemic-style business interruption as a material exposure and flag it to the board — a compound question, no competence estimate that applies, everyone briefed from the same three sources, an error-cost ratio I would put in the tens, and a truth that may not be settled for years or at all. I expect the procedure to tell me something I do not want to hear on that third one, and I would rather it did.

Finally, close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do and what would change your mind about it: which gate you expect to be answered confidently and wrongly in practice, which empirical finding about our panel would lead you to rewrite the procedure rather than patch it, and where your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong — in particular, whether the step where you judge independence is anything more than a guess with a procedure attached to it.
