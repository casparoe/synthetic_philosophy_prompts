I do a fortnightly column on the science and ethics of food, and I'm drowning in pitches. Roughly three or four times a week some advocacy group or certification scheme sends me a claim about which way of eating is easier on wild animals: grazing is fine because it's just grass, chicken is worse because it's really soy in disguise, crop farming kills field animals by the billion so maybe beef is the humane option after all, and so on. Some of these are clearly rigged, but others feel like they turn on something real that I can't quite put my finger on, and right now I just squint at each one separately, which is getting embarrassing. You're an expert in animal welfare science and environmental ethics: please think carefully about this before writing, brainstorm a bit out loud first about what the underlying structure of these claims even is, and then do the translating work. Don't send me citations or a reading list — I can't check them at the desk and my editor will cut them anyway — I want the reasoning itself.

Here's the pile of half-formed distinctions I've been carrying around, which you should feel free to reorganize, rename, or throw out. First, the denominator keeps getting swapped: per kilogram of meat, per gram of protein, per calorie, per meal on a plate — and because a chicken is small and mostly grain-fed while a cow is big and partly grass-fed, the beef-versus-chicken ordering seems to flip depending on which of these you pick. Second, there's what I've started calling the replacement question: the claim's numbers always depend on an assumption about what the land, and the animals on it, would otherwise be doing — does the pasture revert to scrub, get ploughed for crops, or just carry someone else's herd. Third, the subtraction problem: most of the wild animals on that land would have died anyway, of cold, hunger, or predation, and if their populations are limited by something density-dependent then a death now partly cancels out deaths later, so the honest net effect might be much smaller than the raw body count suggests. Fourth, there's the currency problem of comparing a cow to a mouse to an insect killed by pesticide or by a plough share, which involves both how much suffering we're talking about per animal and how confident we should be that the smaller creatures feel anything at all. I need this as a procedure I can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as an answer good enough to move on from each one, where the procedure just stops and says no verdict available, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because it's a values call and not a factual one. I've got about twenty minutes per pitch, no access to paywalled agricultural datasets, and my editor wants a one-sentence verdict with an honest hedge attached, not a 900-word essay.

Then run it on three things so I can see it working. One: a press kit for a pasture-raised chicken certification arguing that their birds are better for wild animals than beef, since the birds forage on land that stays grassland. Two: an NGO graphic claiming that if everyone who eats beef switched to chicken, some large number of wild animal deaths per person per year would be avoided. Three, and this is the one I'm nervous about: my own recurring line that grass-fed beef is the closest thing to a wild-animal-friendly meat, which I've written in at least three columns and sort of hope survives. I'd especially like the procedure to tell me something I don't want to hear in at least one of these, and I want you to be blunt about what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, which inputs will always be guesswork at my level of access, and how I'm supposed to phrase that in print without either pretending to a precision I don't have or sliding into saying every diet is equally fine.
