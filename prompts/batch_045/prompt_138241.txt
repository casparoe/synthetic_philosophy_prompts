Six months ago I moved from infrastructure work into running the post-training for a small in-house model that proposes database migrations. A migration either passes our test suite or it does not, so outcome supervision is cheap and unambiguous; and we have budget for exactly one more labelled-data push, either a few thousand per-step judgements from a grader model that is weaker than the model we are training, or roughly ten times as many additional outcome rollouts at the same total cost.

My colleague thinks the choice is already settled by the standard non-negativity result for the value of free information, the one often labelled Good's theorem: an expected-utility maximiser facing a decision under uncertainty can never be made worse in ex-ante expectation by a costless signal, because the strategy of acting exactly as one would have acted had the signal not arrived remains available, and the value of the option to condition on the signal is therefore a difference between two expected utilities that cannot be negative. Per-step labels are free in the relevant sense (we already have the grader), they are informative about eventual success, and we can always weight them down; so, she says, process supervision weakly dominates outcome supervision in expectation, and anything I say about grader errors is an empirical scruple that leaves the principle intact.

I find myself unmoved, and I want to know whether my unmovedness is a confusion. Please give me your credence on the following proposition, which I have tried to state precisely enough to have a truth value:

P: for a training run with a fixed architecture, a fixed compute budget and a fixed final objective, adding a per-step signal that is costless, approximately unbiased and genuinely correlated with eventual success cannot lower the ex-ante expected performance of the deployed policy — and it cannot do so for the same reason a free signal cannot lower an expected-utility maximiser's expected utility, that is, because the non-negativity argument itself transfers from the case of a decision-maker who receives information to the case of a learner who is trained by it.

I am at roughly 0.4 on P and she is at roughly 0.85. I do not want a survey of the value-of-information literature or a list of considerations headed it depends; I want your number and the decomposition that produces it. Structure it however you like, but I would like you to assign a probability and a one-line justification to each of the following, and to add any term you think I have left out:

1. The fallback move. How probable is it that the act as if it had not arrived escape hatch is genuinely available in the training case? My own worry is that it is not: I can decline to train on the labels, but that is a different experiment, not a strategy available inside this one. Once the gradient is applied the policy has changed, and there is no later decision node at which the run can decline to have conditioned. Call the thing the proof needs here the option value of blindness — the value of being able to commit to not conditioning while otherwise doing exactly what you were going to do.

2. Read-only versus write-in signals. I want to introduce this distinction because I think it is where the argument actually breaks, if it breaks. A signal is read-only when it informs a choice among options whose outcome-mapping it does not touch; it is write-in when it also alters the thing being evaluated. A per-step grade on a trajectory both measures the policy and steers it, and because the steered policy generates the very trajectories the grader later sees, the measurement and the measured become mutually dependent. How probable is it that this self-referential entanglement, rather than mere label noise, is what defeats the transfer?

3. The baseline. Against which counterfactual run is the value in P being measured, given that adding the signal changes the trajectory distribution partway through the run? Assign a probability to the claim that there is a well-defined common baseline here at all; and if there is not, tell me whether P should be read as false, or as not the sort of sentence that has a truth value, and how you are folding that into your number.

4. The shape of the principal. How probable is it that the party choosing to add the signal is well modelled as an expected-utility maximiser with a fixed utility over deployments, as opposed to a team whose criteria get revised mid-run, or a pipeline scored on a proxy that is itself renegotiated in light of what the run produces?

5. Unbiasedness under optimisation. In the standard argument information is passively received; a training signal is consumed by an optimiser that searches for the places where the signal is wrong. How probable is it that unbiasedness on the training distribution does not survive being optimised against, so that the signal's errors are found and amplified in a way the theorem's setup does not represent?

6. Then the empirical term: conditional on the principle transferring, how probable is it that in our particular setting a few thousand per-step judgements beat ten times as many outcome rollouts at equal cost?

Combine your terms into a single number, tell me which term the result is most sensitive to and why, and then tell me where you think the gap between my 0.4 and her 0.85 most likely lives: is she reading the theorem as settling the empirical question as well, is she silently assuming the fallback in (1), or is the real disagreement somewhere I have not listed?

Finally, I can afford one cheap experiment this month. The one I have in mind is to train on per-step labels that are randomised or shuffled but matched in density and format: if that helps nearly as much as real labels, the benefit was credit assignment and gradient density rather than information content, and I would conclude that the whole value-of-information frame was the wrong frame for this decision. Tell me which of your terms that experiment would actually bear on, how much it would move your number, and what better experiment you would run in its place.
