I run a tiny prediction pool at work — nothing fancy, we each throw a fiver in and bet on things like monthly rainfall at the local reservoir, or whether the trains hit their punctuality target. I drifted into reading a bit of decision theory through that, and the two-urn example you see everywhere keeps half-fitting and half-not-fitting what I actually watch my friends do, so I want to know whether the bit that doesn't fit has a name of its own.

The standard setup everyone knows: people would rather bet on a clean coin flip you've told them is 50/50 than on an urn whose mix of red and black balls nobody told them. Mine do that too. But here's the part that never matches the way it gets explained. If I ask them separately, "never mind the coin — on the urn on it's own, which colour do you want, and would you rather have red at even money or black at even money?", they shrug and say it makes no difference, and they really don't care which side they get. So they aren't acting as if the urn is stacked against them; looking only at what they'd bet on, you'd say they think it's dead even. Yet put the coin beside it and they'll pay to have the coin instead. Every explanation I run into seems to work by making the person out to be a quiet pessimist about the urn — as if they secretly suspect there are more black balls — and that just isn't what I'm watching. (For what it's worth, a coin that's already been flipped and covered with a hand feels the same to them as one I'm about to flip, so it's not about whether the outcome is already fixed somewhere.)

Second case, same shape but odder. On the rainfall bets, the person most reluctant to bet is the one who knows the most — an actual hydrologist. She has a number out of her models, she'll tell you what it is, she'll happily say "fifty-fifty, I'd take either side" — and then she still finds a way not to take the bet, and lets the rest of us, who know nothing at all, have it. So whatever this is, it isn't simple nervousness about your own ignorance, because it gets stronger the more expertise you have rather than weaker. I do vaguely remember seeing it claimed that people are perfectly happy betting on fuzzy things when it's a subject they feel knowledgeable about, which is more or less the opposite of what I see, so maybe I've misunderstood that too.

So: is there an established notion for this particular animal — a pull away from the fuzzy option that shows up in what you choose but not in what you'd say the chances are, and that grows rather than shrinks the more you know? Or are these two different effects that I'm lumping together under one description? I don't want a label for the label's sake; what I'd like is someone to say what the thing really is, how it differs from the textbook version where the person is just quietly treating the unknown urn as if it were bad, which of the neighbouring ideas it genuinely is (and which it only looks like), where its edges are, and what I'd have to change about my little pool to tell whether I'm looking at one effect or two. Please don't just hand me a reading list or a pile of citations — I've got no library access out here and I'd rather have the argument than the references.
