I'm a science journalist working on a piece about a large school system that has begun publishing two figures for every school: the raw attainment gap between its pupils from low- and high-income households, and an "adjusted" gap that statistically controls for each pupil's polygenic score for educational attainment. Officials defend the adjusted figure as fairer to schools in poor catchment areas — a school, they say, should not be blamed or credited for the genetic propensities of the children who happen to enrol. Critics say it licenses schools to treat poor children as fated.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory rather than referee the press release. What does the practice have to assume about what a heritability estimate is — a ratio of variances in one population under one range of environments — and about what a polygenic score is, given that such a score partly records the parents' genes acting through the home they provide, not only the child's own variants? Does the practice strictly presuppose that heritability measures how unmalleable a trait is, or can a defender reconstruct it without that commitment? Does treating the score as a fixed input to be netted out go wrong if the score partly encodes the very disadvantage being measured, and does that matter for what the adjusted number could mean? What would I actually see at a school that scores well on the adjusted gap and badly on the raw one? And where is the practice underdetermined — where would several quite different theories license the same number?

Give me a definite verdict: defensible as it stands, incoherent, or salvageable with specified changes — with your reasons, and a note on which part of the answer you would least want to have to defend to a behavioural geneticist.
