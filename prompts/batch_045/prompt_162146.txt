Maimonides, in his philosophical and legal writings, is on record for the following verdicts about a series of earlier cases concerning worship and sacrifice.

1. Animal sacrifice offered to God at the central sanctuary: he retains and commands it, while insisting that it is not desired by God for its own sake and is instead a concession to the people's deeply ingrained habit of sacrificial worship, intended to wean them from idolatry by redirecting that habit to the true God.
2. Bowing down and burning incense before God in the Temple: he retains and commands these acts, even though prostration and incense were also staple elements of idolatrous ritual, because they can be redirected to God.
3. Sacrifice or incense offered to any being other than God, or to an image or to the heavenly bodies: he prohibits it absolutely; such worship is not to be retained in any redirected form.
4. Any use of a physical image or bodily representation in the worship of God: he prohibits it absolutely, on the ground that God is incorporporeal and that such representations foster false beliefs and idolatry.
5. Prayer, fasting, and petitioning God in time of trouble: he regards these as necessary and appropriate in the present age; he says that a prophet who told people to serve God only in thought, abandoning prayer, fasting, and petition, would be commanding something contrary to human nature.
6. In the era of the Messiah, he rules that the observance of the statutes will return to its previous state and that animal sacrifices will once again be offered.

The new case is this. A philosophically advanced community in the messianic era, living at a time when the Temple has been rebuilt and when idolatry has disappeared from the earth, proposes to replace the statutory animal sacrifices with a non-animal pure offering consisting of recitation of scriptural passages and sustained intellectual contemplation of God. They argue that animal sacrifice was only ever a concession to primitive and idolatrous habits; that those habits have been entirely outgrown; and that the true end of the law is the intellectual love and knowledge of God, so the highest worship now available to them is service in thought rather than the slaughter of animals. Would Maimonides permit this substitution, or would he rule that the animal sacrifices themselves must still be offered?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the rule he seems to be running. The earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one plausible rule. Identify at least two, and preferably three, candidate rules that fit all of the earlier verdicts equally well. For each rule, state what it predicts about the new case, whether the substitution is permitted or forbidden, and why. Then give your best guess about Maimonides' own verdict, with the reasons for preferring the rule that generates that guess over the alternatives. Finally, propose a further case, a variant that has not yet been decided, that would best tell these competing rules apart, and explain how each rule would decide that further case.
