hi — I'm in an upper-level seminar on social norms and practical reason, and last week we did the Lewis-style coordination account of conventions and the Rawls practice-conception bit, and I tried to use it to win an argument with my roommate and instead built something I'm pretty sure can't be right but can't locate the gap in. He's from a country where nobody tips, and his view is that at home he has no obligation to tip and here he also has no obligation to tip, because, as he puts it, "the menu price is the price." I said he does have to tip here, since the servers wages are literally set on the assumption, and when he asked why that generates an obligation rather than just explaining why they're underpaid, this is what I wrote out:

(1) A convention is a regularity in behavior that persists because nearly everyone prefers to conform conditional on others conforming; which regularity gets selected is typically arbitrary — what does the work is that everyone does the same thing.

(2) So when you deviate from a live convention, you make others worse off than they would have been had you conformed: their expectations get frustrated, and the coordination the convention existed to produce partly fails.

(3) Making others worse off than they'd otherwise be just to save yourself a small cost is pro tanto wrong; and where you've taken a benefit the practice produced while letting others shoulder the cost of keeping it up, it's a form of free riding.

(4) So a visitor owes conformity to the local conventions. Since the content is arbitrary, conforming costs them at most minor inconvenience, so they can't complain about it.

(5) Because it's the conformity and not the content that does the work, (4) holds whatever the convention is, whether or not the visitor thinks it's a decent one, and whether they're staying ten years or one afternoon.

(6) Now fill in some actual local conventions. In plenty of cities the real convention is that pedestrians cross wherever they like and drivers have adapted to it, so the marked crossing is where accidents happen, because nobody expects a pedestrian there. Elsewhere the convention is not to tip; or to shove into the scrum instead of lining up (in which case my shoving is exactly what pushes out the person who can't shove); or to slip the clerk something so your paperwork moves this month.

(7) So by (5) the visitor is required to jaywalk, not to tip, to shove, and to pay the bribe.

(8) That's mad. At least the last one is something a visitor plainly shouldn't do, and "when in Rome" isn't a moral principle. So something above is false.

I can see the obvious move — say (2) and (4) only cover conventions that are themselves worth having — but that feels like cheating, because step (1) says the content is arbitrary, which is exactly what made (4) cheap to accept. I'd rather keep (3) than (4) if forced, and I'd love to keep the tipping conclusion for my roommate's case in particular, but not at the price of having to say that a tourist who won't bribe a clerk is thereby doing something wrong.

Where's the break? If it's an equivocation on "convention," tell me precisely which of my steps survives on the narrow reading and which one collapses, and what I'd have to say instead about tipping and about jaywalking once the argument is repaired — including whether the repaired version still makes tipping owed, or whether my roommate was right all along. Under 700 words, please, and please don't just tell me that conventions aren't morality: I want the step, why it looked fine, and what each of the alternatives costs me.
