I teach an undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I am building a session around the two classic paradoxes of rational acceptance: Kyburg's lottery paradox, where a threshold rule for accepting very probable propositions lets you accept, of each ticket, that it will lose, while you know that one will win; and Makinson's preface paradox, where the author who has checked every sentence of her book reasonably believes each sentence and also reasonably believes that somewhere in the book there is an error. I know the standard moves on both — abandon the high-probability acceptance rule and keep only degrees of belief, or keep acceptance and refuse to aggregate it across conjunction — so I don't need a survey of the literature. What I want is to set the paradoxes beside an institution that has to do, in practice, what the theories say cannot be done coherently.

A friend who litigates suggested I use the 1957 film Twelve Angry Men, and I think she is onto something, but I cannot settle which paradox the film is actually a picture of. Read as a lottery, the prosecution's case is a conjunction of individually strong-looking items: the switchblade the boy had bought, the neighbour downstairs who says he heard a body fall and saw the boy run out, the woman across the street who says she saw the killing through the windows of a passing elevated train, the threat heard by other tenants, the angle of the stab wound. Juror 8's early method is to take these one at a time and show that each is individually defeasible — a knife of the same make bought in a nearby pawn shop, the neighbour who could not have reached his door in the time he claimed, the woman who was not wearing her glasses in bed. But by the end something different seems to be happening: the jurors who change their votes mostly do not point at one item and declare it false. They go on accepting that the boy probably did buy a knife, that someone in the apartment probably did shout, that the old man probably did hear something fall — and they vote not guilty anyway. That looks much closer to the preface than to the lottery, and because it is a jury rather than an author it is a preface with twelve co-authors who have to sign it jointly: each contributes to a verdict that no one of them fully endorses, and no one is required to say which claim is the mistaken one. I have started calling this distributed, non-localisable doubt, and I would like to know whether you think it is a genuinely distinct third structure or only the preface in costume.

So I am asking for a reading of the film that takes both paradoxes seriously and stays faithful in both directions. Which scenes instantiate which structure, and does the film move between them as the deliberation proceeds — does it begin as a lottery, dismantling the case item by item, and end as a preface, granting the items while rejecting the conjunction? Is the film's implicit epistemology Kyburg's, keeping high-threshold acceptance of each item and refusing to aggregate, or the orthodox probabilist's, dropping acceptance for graded confidence — or does it quietly trade on both, and does it know the difference? Where does the film cheat? My own suspicion is that its tidiest move, giving every piece of evidence an actual defeater, is precisely what the paradox literature forbids us to lean on, since the puzzle arises exactly when doubt is spread across a conjunction with no identifiable false conjunct; and that the ending's insistence that the last holdout supply a reason converts a problem about degrees of confidence into a binary vote, with unanimity functioning as an aggregation rule that behaves nothing like conjunction and a not-guilty verdict asserting doubt without asserting innocence. And where does the film resist the reading altogether, so that an honest critic has to say so? Finally, does the film add anything to the philosophical debate that the papers do not — about what institutions do with uncertainty they cannot aggregate, about the moral and epistemic cost of refusing to close a set of accepted propositions, or about the function of the one juror who will not sign the preface? My students know the paradoxes but not the film, so please do not summarise the plot beyond what your argument needs, and I have no use for quotations or citations: I want the reading itself.
