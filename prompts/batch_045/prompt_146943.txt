I help run a small reading group at my university — political philosophy adjacent, mostly grad students plus two lawyers who show up mainly to argue — and we've spend the last few weeks on the "equality of what" material: welfare vs. resources vs. capabilities and all the来回 between them. Last night's session ended badly. We were talking about our university's exam accommodations for students with disabilities and chronic illnesses (extra time, flexible deadlines, a separate quiet room, permission to stand and walk around during a three-hour exam), and one member shut the discussion down with "equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome," saying accommodations are a kind of cheating: they hand people equal results rather than equal chances. I said that doesn't follow, but all I managed was a vague "it depends what you mean by opportunity," which wasn't much of a contribution.

What I'd like is a proper menu, because I think both halves of the sentence are doing a lot of hidden work. Take "opportunity": it could mean an option that no one is formally blocking you from; or your actual chance of getting the thing if you try and put in effort; or a real freedom or capacity to turn goods into something you can actually do and be (the capabilities thing, I think?); or merely the absence of other people deliberately interfering; or something about your chances being set by your choices rather than by circumstances you didn't pick. And "outcome" is just as slippery in our context: happiness or preference satisfaction, bundles of resources, what people are actually able to do and be, or your position relative to other people. For each combination that matters, can you say whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, and — importantly — whether the slogan is still interesting when read that way, since I suspect some readings make it something nobody would disagree with.

Two things would make this genuinely useful rather than just tidy. First, the same person is fine with means-tested scholarships for first-generation students and was in favour of our department offering free childcare during conferences, so I want to know which readings let him keep all three views and which readings force him to draw a line — and whether there's a principled distinction nearby (removing a barrier vs. giving someone a boost? compensating for brute luck vs. holding people responsible for their choices? something about who created the obstacle in the first place, given that the exam format is the university's invention?) or whether he's just being inconsistent. Second, someone said — and this is the bit that made my head spin — that if you spell out "equal opportunity" seriously, so that nobody's prospects are dragged down by circumstances they didn't choose, you end up having to equalize something very close to welfare, at which point the slogan eats itself and the opportunity/outcome contrast collapses. Is that actually where the view goes, or is that a bad inference? I'd rather have the reasoning than a name I can google.
