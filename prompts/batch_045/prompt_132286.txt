I'm retired now — for about thirty years I ran an analytical chemistry group, mostly chromatography and an endless amount of arguing about lab safety — and a few of us from the old department have been working our way through some moral philosophy together. We're currently stuck on the literature about "standing to blame", and I've ended up on the minority side of the group, so I want to practice against someone who's better at it than my friends are. Please play a committed proponent of the view that a hypocritical blamer can genuinely lack standing — but the strong, non-silly version: blame isn't a verdict you hand down, its a move you make on a person, an address or a demand, and whether you're in a position to make that move is a separate question from whether what you say is true. Concede quickly that the hypocrite's claim isn't thereby false, that "you did it too" is a bad argument in most contexts, and that a trivial or unrelated blot on my record shouldn't cost me anything. Don't concede that in the central case the blame is fully in order — that's the whole fight. One paragraph per turn, plain words, no bibliographies or citations, no naming philosophers unless I do first, and you're welcome to ask me for further details of a case. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here's my opening, and it comes in three parts. First, the calibration point, which someone in my group raised and which I think actually helps me. In the lab we distinguished a detector being accurate from its being calibrated: an instrument that's drifted can still hand you a reading that happens to be exactly right, it just can't be used to certify anything. If that's your model, standing is an epistemic property — it's about whether the reading can be trusted — but blame isn't reporting a reading, it's doing something to someone, so I dont think the analogy gets you what you want. Second, my actual case, call it the Last Instrument: a senior postdoc taught a junior a peak-shaving trick, then stopped using it herself after it got someone else into trouble. Years later she's the only person in the building who can still recognise that signature in a dataset, and she does recognise it, in a paper by someone she has no history with. On your view she isn't in a position to say anything; on mine she's the only one who can. Who is leaving a real violation unremarked, and why is it the person who didn't do the faking who benefits? Third, the preface test: suppose she opens with "I did this too, I shouldn't have, and neither should you." Restored, or not? If yes, then standing was never really about her record at all, it was about what she was implicitly claiming — that she's exempt. If no, you owe me a story about why the more candid blamer is in worse shape. And a small fourth thing while you're at it: if what's missing in the hypocrite is reliability or knowledge, then my colleague who's never been tempted at all should have less standing than I do, and I don't think you want that. Go.
