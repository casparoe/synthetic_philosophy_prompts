I write novels — eleven now, nine of them published, the last two of which did roughly what my publishers and I expected them to — and last autumn I began planning something different in kind: a long book assembled out of documents, set partly inside a vanished river trade I have been reading about for years, in a town I know well and have written about before. The plan I made was unusually detailed: somewhere near ninety scenes, each with a source, a date and a stated function. As the plan grew, so did my confidence, and not only my confidence about the writing: my confidence that the book would find its readers, that the research would pay off, that the year I was about to spend on it was well spent. A friend who reads scripts for a living asked, not unkindly, what the base rate was for a book like that doing well. I tried to answer honestly. I looked for comparable books, and I found that every comparison I could construct felt slightly wrong — wrong in a way I could specify: those were debuts and this is not; those had no archive behind them; those were published into a different market; those authors were not writing about a subject that already had an organized readership. Each of those was true, and each had the effect of leaving me with a comparison class that contained my project and nothing else. I have since noticed that I do the same thing in the other direction, which is what makes me think it is a single move rather than a wish: when a friend was deciding whether to leave a large publisher and publish independently, I ran through the comparable cases and found reasons to exclude each of the ones that had gone well, until the class contained only the ones that had gone badly, and I urged caution on the strength of a comparison class I could not have defined in advance, before I knew which cases it would need to hold.

What interests me is the shape of the move, not its outcomes. It does not present itself as optimism, and I do not think it is quite what I have heard called the planning fallacy, since it does not seem to rest on any flattering belief about my own diligence or talent: I have performed it on other people's projects, and I have performed it pessimistically, and in both directions I believed at every step that I was being more scrupulous rather than less. It does not feel like ordinary motivated reasoning either, because nothing in it is insincere — every difference I cite is a real difference, and I would defend each exclusion in public. It seems instead to be a consequence of how finely the case can be described: the finer the description, the fewer the comparators survive, and there is no non-arbitrary place to stop.

So my question is whether this has been named and given a shape. I am not after a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. In particular: is it simply the inside view, or a mechanism by which an inside view gets generated, and therefore separable from it? Is it continuous with the old difficulty of choosing a reference class for a single case, or is there something specific about the description arriving already organized as a narrative — as mine did, in scenes with sources and functions? How does one tell an honest restriction of the comparison class from this thing, given that the two can be verbally identical and differ only in whether the restriction was fixed before one knew where it would lead? And are there cases in which the move is not a failure at all, in which narrowing the class really is what the evidence demands? If the honest answer is that nothing established fits, I would rather be told so, and given a name, a definition, and the boundary cases that would let me recognize when I am doing it and when I am not.
