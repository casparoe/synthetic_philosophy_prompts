I'm a philosophy master's student putting together an application for a departmental summer award — twelve weeks, one supervisor, a deliverable of a paper of about six thousand words — and the committee that reads it is mostly unsympathetic to philosophy-of-statistics projects, so the proposal has to earn its place. Here is my half-formed idea. Whenever I raise Simpson's paradox with anyone who works with data, I get the same dissolution: stop asking a probabilistic question and ask a causal one, condition on the confounder, don't condition on the mediator or the collider, and the reversal stops being paradoxical. I think that advice quietly assumes that the variable you are being told to condition on is the confounder itself, and in the examples people actually argue about it almost never is. Severity, department, stone size, triage category, disease stage: these are recorded proxies for something the argument needs to be about, and stratifying on a proxy of a confounder is a different operation from stratifying on the confounder. Depending on how good the proxy is, the stratified estimate can be no better than the aggregate one, and which of the two you'd rather act on can flip with the quality of the measurement rather than with the causal structure. I've been calling this the noisy-partition problem. If it's a real problem, then the standard dissolution of the paradox is incomplete in a way that matters, and the question of which number to act on starts to look less like a question about causal structure with a determinate answer and more like a decision made under measurement uncertainty. A friend in biostatistics, when I tried this out on her, said that this is just measurement error and that there are methods for it; I don't know whether that's a dismissal or a research program, and I'd like the proposal to have an answer to it.
