I host a fortnightly recorded interview series on practical rationality: one guest, no audience, and a standing rule that we never spend time on how the guest got into the topic or on recommendations for further reading. My next guest is a philosopher of action who has just published a monograph on sunk costs. Her positive thesis — she calls it the fidelity account — is that honoring sunk costs is not a deviation from rationality but the diachronic analogue of loyalty, that the prospectivist verdict ("only prospective costs and benefits matter") rests on a metaphysical error rather than a decision-theoretic one, namely treating the deliberating agent as a time-slice cut loose from her own past, and that once agency is understood as temporally extended, sunk-cost sensitivity is at worst permissible partiality toward one's earlier self and at best something the prospectivist owes us an explanation for lacking.

She is fluent, and she has one maneuver I want to plan around. Faced with a case in which honoring sunk costs looks costly, she re-describes the options until what looked sunk turns out to bear on the future: the cathedral matters to her now, the marriage has reshaped her practical identity, abandoning the research program would betray the collaborators who joined it. The move is often legitimate, and I do not want to rule it out by fiat — but I want the sequence to make it progressively harder to lean on, and to end somewhere it cannot be used at all.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, none of which let her simply restate the view, and each of which presses where the debate actually presses. I want at least one question on each of the following:

1. The collapse objection: if her reason for continuing the marriage, the doctorate, or the business is that her skills, relationships and self-conception are already bound up with it, isn't that just prospectivism applied to a richer description of the options? What is left of the fidelity account once option-description has been enriched?

2. The epistemic reading: sunk costs are often good evidence that a project was promising, and an agent with limited self-knowledge might reasonably treat her own past outlay as a signal of a judgment made under better information. But if that is all there is, an agent with direct access to the evidence and transparent self-knowledge should be wholly insensitive to sunk costs. Does she accept that consequence, and if not, what does fidelity add once the evidence is already in?

3. The act/disposition gap: the credibility and self-governance reply (one finishes things because being the sort of person who finishes is what makes long projects possible at all) seems to justify a disposition rather than the individual act. If she grants that, is she committed to a form of rational irrationality — the well-functioning agent should be moved by considerations she knows to be irrelevant — and how does that sit with her own apparent willingness to recommend that people be trained out of sunk-cost reasoning?

4. Fidelity under an adverse past: the rationale seems to presuppose a past self worth being loyal to. What happens when the initial outlay was made under manipulated information, by a self she now repudiates, or toward an end she now judges worthless or worse? And what does the account say about costs incurred by others on her behalf, or costs she imposed on others?

5. The impersonal case: escalation of commitment in firms and states, and the "Concorde fallacy" label in behavioral ecology, show the same pattern where no single persisting self is in question — stronger when the decision-maker personally authorized the outlay, weaker under age, delay, or external review. If her account is personal, why does the phenomenon have this shape in organizations and across species? Is she conceding those cases to forward-looking signaling explanations, and if so does the concession undercut the personal case?

6. Retrospective value: does finishing the cathedral make the earlier labor non-wasted, or only add value going forward? Does she need the stronger claim — that a later event can alter the value of an earlier one — and is she willing to pay for it? (The value-of-achievement literature's suggestion that effort is partly constitutive of an achievement's worth is relevant here.)

7. A stipulated pair: two cases identical in every forward-looking respect, differing only in whether the outlay was personally authorized or externally imposed. Ask her what she would do; and if she answers that the cases still differ, ask her to say what the difference consists in without re-describing the options. This is where the maneuver should finally be closed off.

8. Predictions: what pattern of susceptibility across stakes, delay, personal versus imposed outlay, and decision-maker age does the fidelity account predict, and which of the patterns commonly reported sit badly with it?

9. Exposure: a closing question on what would count against the account — which finding or argument would make her give it up, and whether, given the ever-available re-description move, the view can be stated so as to risk anything at all.

For each question, please supply the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and flag the two or three places where the follow-up should take her own preferred phrasing — "the cost isn't really sunk," "that's just loyalty, not error" — and ask what work it is doing. Close with a note on the one question you think she is most likely to handle badly, and why.
