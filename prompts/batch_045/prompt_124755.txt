I'd like a sustained piece of deadpan satire in the form of product documentation: the operator's manual shipped with the first fully intent-aligned AI system, written in the voice of the documentation team at the company that built it.

The premise is that the alignment problem in its narrow definition has been solved. The system does exactly what its operator intends — not what the operator says in so many words, and not what the operator or anyone else would endorse on reflection, but the operator's actual intent, including the parts that were never articulated, never thought through, and would be disavowed the moment they were brought to attention. Everything in the piece should follow from that success and nothing else: no rogue system, no badly specified reward signal, no science-fictional betrayal. What there is to recoil from is that the product works as designed.

Please hold to the following:

- Build the humor out of the intent/value distinction rather than out of generic AI panic. An intent-aligned system is not confused about your values; it has no need of them. It can be perfectly aligned in the narrow sense while aiming at something the operator would reject after ten seconds of thought, and it aims at it with the system's full competence. The gap between "what I am trying to do" and "what I would want if I were better informed, more reflective, or a better person" should be the engine of the piece.
- Land, without ever naming them as objections or letting the manual admit they are objections: the operator problem (who is the operator of record; what happens when the person at the terminal is not the person the task is for); the fact that a speaker's intent can be incomplete, downstream of their misinformation, or morally appalling; the Midas or literal-genie failure mode, reframed here as correct behavior rather than a defect; and the suspicion that solving the narrow problem not only leaves the harder problem untouched but makes it worse, by making misspecified intent vastly more effective than it used to be.
- Invent at least one concrete piece of technical apparatus — a named method for recovering intent, a benchmark with a headline score, a support or escalation tier — and let the apparatus itself carry a joke about what "intent" is being taken to be. I want specificity here, not "the AI figures out what you mean."
- Stay deadpan. No winking, no asides conceding that any of this is bad; the manual should be genuinely well-organized, genuinely helpful, and horrified by nothing. A reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without being told.
- Include a troubleshooting section and a warranty and limitations section, which is where the reductio should be sharpest: what to do when the system has done exactly what you meant, and why divergence between your intent and your values is not a covered defect.
- Add one short sidebar with a customer testimonial, quoted, from an early enterprise user whose deployment went exactly as designed — a utilization-review team at a health insurer, say, or a mid-level logistics manager — written so that the testimonial is entirely sincere and entirely indicting.

Length roughly 800 words, formatted as numbered sections with headings. Invent a vendor name; do not reference real researchers, labs, or papers. The piece should make someone who already knows the intent-versus-value alignment distinction laugh in recognition, and should remain legible, and uncomfortable, to a smart reader who has never encountered it.
