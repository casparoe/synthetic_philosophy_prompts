I chair the taxonomy panel of a national wildlife listings board. We meet four times a year and decide, on a written record, whether a named population is to be treated as a distinct species for the purposes of a statute that protects species without ever defining the word. It is six to ten petitions a session. The decisions bite: a listing restricts land use across a wide area, unlocks recovery funding, and is appealable to a tribunal that asks only whether our reasons were adequate, not whether we were biologically right. The panel is three people — a systematist, a population geneticist, and me — and in any given case two of us are not specialists in the taxon on the table. We have about ten days per petition, the evidence is typically one mitochondrial gene tree, a handful of nuclear markers if we are lucky, a morphology table and some field notes, and the finding has to be short enough and plain enough that a non-specialist can follow it, and stable enough that we would reach the same answer again in five years on a different taxon.

The panel has provisionally adopted one theoretical commitment, and I want it operationalized rather than argued for: that a species is a separately evolving segment of a population lineage, and that the familiar tests — reproductive isolation and interbreeding, monophyly and fixed diagnosable differences, ecological and adaptive distinctiveness, coalescent depth — are not rival definitions of the word but different kinds of evidence bearing on that single claim, which is why they so often disagree. Build the procedure on that, and do not spend the answer defending it.

What I need is a decision procedure we can run in order, on the record, in the time we have. Specifically: the questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. It must have explicit rules for the situations that actually recur — conflicting gene trees; mitochondrial signal contradicted by nuclear data; discordance that could be either incomplete lineage sorting or introgression; contact zones that were never sampled; names attached to a handful of nineteenth-century specimens with imprecise type localities; single-locus evidence; and straight absence of evidence. It needs a rule for when two independent lines of evidence confirm each other versus when we merely have more of the same kind of evidence, and a rule for how much weight the fact that a group is well studied may carry, given that well-studied groups attract more scrutiny and are therefore easier to split. Where a threshold is conventional — how much gene flow is too much, how much divergence is enough, over what time depth — say plainly that it is conventional and fix a default we can apply without reopening the argument each session. There should be three exits: recognize, do not recognize, and defer pending specified data, with deferral available only once per petition.

Three devices are floating around the board and I want you to rule on them, keeping the ones that earn their place and saying what the others cost: a diagnosability floor, requiring that any recognized lineage be identifiable by a non-specialist from a stated set of characters with a stated error rate, with the absence of a usable diagnostic treated as a hard stop rather than a consideration to be weighed; a defeater clause, requiring each finding to name the single future result most likely to overturn it and to carry a review date; and a strict firewall requiring that the delimitation question and the protection question be voted and written separately, with no leakage from the second into the first — while still saying what the second question is allowed to be once the first is settled.

Then run it on three hypothetical composites. First: two nominal forms of a stream-breeding salamander in one mountain range, with nine percent divergence at a mitochondrial gene, no fixed differences at four nuclear loci, a four-kilometre contact zone in which about a twelfth of animals are genotypically intermediate and laboratory crosses show no loss of hatching success, and a breeding-habitat difference between cold headwater seeps and warmer mainstem pools.

Second: an all-female lineage of a freshwater fish that reproduces by gynogenesis — sperm from a sexual relative is needed to trigger development but, on present evidence, contributes no genes — which is morphologically diagnosable, carries a fixed hybrid karyotype, has been separate for something like sixty thousand years, uses a different habitat, is broadly sympatric with its host, and in which the rare paternal leakage reported in a related lineage has never been looked for.

Third is the one I am dreading. A large, pale insular form of a widespread rodent, described as a full species in 1913, in every regional field guide, the mascot of two fundraising NGOs, protected for thirty years: genome-wide data now place it nested inside the widespread mainland lineage, with an estimated split around eight thousand years ago and gene flow continuing until roughly 1900, and with its three coat-colour loci and its body size plausibly under local selection. Give me the finding the procedure honestly yields. I would rather have a procedure that is sometimes wrong in a known direction than one that lets us steer, so do not bend it to keep this animal listed — tell me what the honest output is, what the protection question can legitimately rescue, and how much of that reasoning I can put in a public written finding.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which kinds of organism defeat it outright, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as method. Write for a reader with no library access, so no citations, no reading lists, and no appeal to what the literature has settled — just the procedure, the three applications, and the honest limits. Two pages for the finding format itself, if you can.
