I'm a philosophy MA student working on formal epistemology, and to keep myself honest I run a small forecasting pool with four friends — twenty-odd questions a month, mostly about departmental life and travel times — which we currently score with the Brier score: for each question with a partition of outcomes, your inaccuracy is the sum of the squared differences between your reported credences and the 0/1 truth vector, and your points are that number rescaled. Last week a friend who works in statistics argued we should switch to what he calls the plain confidence rule: your score for a question is just the credence you assigned to the outcome that actually occurred, so that reporting 0.9 on the true cell earns 0.9 and the season's winner is whoever was right with the most conviction. I said this obviously rewards fanaticism, but when he asked me to say what exactly is wrong with it in the vocabulary I'd been reading — accuracy, dominance, propriety — I got no further than "it isn't strictly proper," and I realized I don't understand what work strict propriety (reporting your real credences uniquely maximizes expected score) is doing over and above merely being sensitive to the truth.

So I have two connected questions about the accuracy-first programme, by which I mean the project of treating accuracy as the sole fundamental epistemic good and deriving probabilism, conditionalization and the rest from it. First: how much of the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism is carried by the particular scoring rule, and what exactly has to be true of a rule for the argument to go through? I take the argument to show that for any credence function violating the probability axioms there is some probabilistic one that is more accurate at every possible world, but which features of a measure like the Brier score are doing the work — its being a squared distance, its additivity or separability across the cells of the partition, continuity, strict propriety, or something else — and what happens to the argument under each variation? If we reran the argument with the logarithmic score (minus the log of your credence in the truth) instead, would anything the programme cares about change? I can follow simple algebra over a three-cell partition, so please don't shy away from sketching the mechanics; what I want is the gut-level reason each condition is in the theorem.

Second, about my friend's rule. I checked a three-cell case by hand and noticed that under it the assignment (0.6, 0.6, 0.6), which isn't even coherent, scores better than (0.4, 0.3, 0.3) at every possible outcome, and that (1, 1, 1) beats everything; his reply was that we should simply restrict the inputs to coherent assignments, and I can see that this blocks the inflation, but it looks to me as though the rule then just instructs you to be certain of whichever outcome you currently like best, since expected score is linear in your reports. Is his rule a genuine measure of accuracy at all — is it truth-directed, merely improper, or something worse — and what would a forecaster governed by it end up doing? My deeper worry is about circularity: if the only reason to exclude it is that it fails strict propriety, and the reason to care about strict propriety is that we want a measure under which your actual credences come out optimal, then we seem to have built the conclusion into the definition of accuracy, and I can't tell whether the literature has a non-question-begging reply to this, whether it concedes the point, or whether I've simply misidentified what the constraints are for. A related thought I'd like your view on: maybe the right response to my friend is that a forecast pool is not a pure accuracy exercise at all, because we also care about calibration, resolution, and not being humiliated in public — but I'm unsure whether that concedes too much to the accuracy-firster or nothing at all.
