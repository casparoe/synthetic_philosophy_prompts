I'm a third-year undergrad in a small cross-listed seminar this term — half Earth sciences, half STS — on periodization: how the geologic time scale gets carved up, who gets to do the carving, and what happens when the thing being dated is us. We spent the last few weeks on the Anthropocene proposal: the working group, the mid-century radionuclide marker, the Crawford Lake section, and the fact that the proposal was finally voted down in 2024 while the term itself went on being used everywhere anyway. What I keep coming back to is the mismatch at the center of it. The apparatus for defining an epoch asks you to pick one boundary, in one physical place, identifiable as a horizon in rock, globally synchronous and preservable for the deep future — and then it asks that apparatus to date a diffuse, diachronous, politically charged process that is still happening and that everyone already agrees on the name of. My TA, who is a stratigrapher, said something I've been chewing on: that the working group wasn't confused, it was doing exactly what the discipline requires, and the discomfort people feel is the discomfort of watching a well-designed tool applied to something it wasn't designed for. I want a piece that gets at that from the inside rather than editorializing about it.

So here's what I'd like as my final project. Write a mock "Call for Nominations" — an official request for proposals issued by a successor body, inviting national geological surveys and research consortia to submit candidate sections for the Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point marking the base of the Anthropocene. About 800 to 1000 words, in deadpan bureaucratic register, structured as numbered clauses with a scoring rubric (allocate actual points across the criteria — accessibility, synchrony, preservability, datability, and whatever else the protocol would demand). Include a short eligibility section on who may submit, a note on the composition of the voting membership, and — this is the part I care most about — at least one wholly invented candidate marker, proposed and defended in exactly the same sober register as the real ones, so that the invented one is indistinguishable in tone from the familiar ones. Somewhere in the document, break briefly into a field note by a geologist in the far future reading our layer; keep it short and keep it straight-faced.

Two constraints on how the humor has to work. First, the jokes should come from getting the practice right and then following it one step further than it wants to go: the demand that a period be defined by a physical marker rather than by a concept; the requirement of global synchrony and the diachroneity problem it creates; the demand that the signal survive and remain legible to a reader tens of millions of years out; the need to protect the type section from the very civilization whose signature it records; the smallness of the electorate that settles the question; and the fact that a unit can be formally rejected and still be the one everybody uses. Exaggerate those, don't invent faults they don't have — the target is the protocol and the style of discourse around it, not any named person, so please don't name real scientists or hinge any joke on a real date or place name being exactly right. Second, no winking: no glossary, no footnotes explaining the irony, no character who steps in to say the quiet part. Let it end Swiftian — the committee resolving the synchrony problem by some straight-faced move that the reader recoils from (defining the base by the date of the ratification vote itself, or requiring the marker to have been manufactured for the purpose, or something better than either of those), with the deadpan never breaking.
