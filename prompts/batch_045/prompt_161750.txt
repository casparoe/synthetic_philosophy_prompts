I have no academic background in philosophy — I read military history and memoirs on my own — and one question has been snagging me for years: whether a soldier fighting on the wrong side of a war does anything wrong merely by fighting, and whether he may be killed for doing it. I have written down my own gut reactions to a series of cases, and I would like to know whether any single principle produces them, what it commits me to in cases I have not settled, and which of my reactions it would force me to give up.

One. A nineteen-year-old conscript in the army of a country that started an aggressive war. Everyone around him, including his officers, believes his country was attacked first, and he has no way to check. He shoots at enemy soldiers in ordinary firefights and takes no part in any atrocity. I do not think he is a murderer, I do not blame him, and I think punishing him afterwards would be wrong.

Two. The same man a year later, after being shown captured documents that make the aggression obvious. He keeps fighting, not because he believes the cause is just but because desertion means prison and his mother loses her pension. I blame him somewhat, but only somewhat, and I still think he may be shot at only while he is actually trying to kill people.

Three. A volunteer who enlisted in the aggressor army out of enthusiasm for the cause and who also fights cleanly. I blame him plainly, and much more than the man in case two. But I do not think he may be killed any more than the conscript in case one may. Blame and being killable seem to me to come apart here.

Four. A civilian engineer who knowingly redesigns the fuses on the aggressor's missiles, for a large salary, and never fires a weapon. I think he does something wrong and I think he may be targeted.

Five. A civilian who voted for the government that started the war and pays his taxes. Not targetable, obviously.

Six. A conscripted cook in the aggressor army who only feeds soldiers. I think he can be captured and interned but not shot — even though he is in uniform and the engineer in case four is not. This is one of the two contrasts I cannot reconcile.

Seven. An aggressor soldier who has been wounded, disarmed, and is waiting for evacuation; he is no longer fighting now, though he will be sent back to his unit after treatment. I think killing him now is murder. The second contrast I cannot reconcile: my reason in case three was that what matters is what you are doing, but this man will be doing it again next month.

Eight. A mercenary from a neutral country who fights for the defending side purely for money. I think he does nothing wrong, I think the aggressor's soldiers may shoot him exactly as they may shoot any defender, and I still feel he is somehow less admirable than the citizen who defends his own country for nothing.

Nine. An aggressor's drone operator sitting in a container two thousand kilometres away, never in any danger, launching munitions by remote control. I think he may be killed, and I also feel he is worse than the conscript rifleman, which I cannot explain — it is not that he kills more people.

Here is the rule of thumb I have arrived at, which I suspect is too crude. First part: you may be killed only if, at that moment, you are an active part of a threat that is wrongful, stopping that threat requires killing you, and you had a fair opportunity to know the cause was wrongful. Your motives and your blameworthiness are irrelevant to this. Second part, kept separate: blame is a different matter entirely, and tracks whether you had decent reasons and whether you could reasonably have refused.

I would like you to test that rule against my cases and repair it where it breaks, and in particular to tell me what clause would have to be added to separate the cook from the engineer without dragging in the taxpayer. I also want verdicts on three cases I cannot decide: a soldier in the defending army who comes to learn, midway, that his government is now pursuing conquest beyond what defense requires, and who stays because his unit would be short-handed; a contractor paid triple to keep the aggressor's trucks running, who tells himself he only fixes engines; and a pair of soldiers, one on each side, with exactly equally good evidence for their own government's version of events, where one happens to be right. Finally, please say which of my verdicts a principle you respect would force me to drop, and which of my two irreconcilable contrasts you would resolve in which direction.

Please answer in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a long email to someone who has asked you a hard question.
