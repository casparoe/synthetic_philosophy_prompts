I am putting together the handout for a ninety-minute graduate seminar on how macroscopic subjects are supposed to arise from microscopic ones, and I would rather it be a decision tree than a survey of named views. Could you build one?

Start from a single commitment everyone in the room can grant: there is something it is like to be an ordinary awake adult human, and whatever else is true, that depends on the physical goings-on in that person's brain. Everything downstream of that should branch on what a view says happens in a concrete case, never on what the view calls itself. In particular, no node may contain the words panpsychism, panprotopsychism, cosmopsychism, Russellian, neutral, qualia, or any form of "combine"; a student should not be able to guess from a node which leaf it leads to. Phrase each node as a question about the case with two or three answers attached, not as a yes/no on a thesis.

The cases I want used as branch points (add at most four of your own):

1. A static configuration: the constituents of a living brain held exactly in the arrangement they occupy during experience, with all causal interaction among them switched off.
2. The count question: at one time, how many subjects does a single living human brain contain, and if the answer is more than one, is any of them the one whose experience the person reports?
3. Overlap: two functioning brains sharing ninety percent of their constituents at the same time, each with its own cognitive life.
4. Grain: the same brain described at the level of molecules, of cells, of neural assemblies, and of the whole organism — a subject at every level, at one level, or is the question ill-posed?
5. Division: severing the corpus callosum, and the same severance imagined as purely functional rather than anatomical.
6. Palette: constituents credited only with extremely simple experiential characters, while the resulting experience has a richly structured sensory field, with spatial arrangement and similarities among colours already built into it.
7. Derivation: someone is handed every microphysical truth, every truth about the experiential character of the constituents, and every a priori truth, and asked which macroscopic experiences exist.
8. Spoils: when constituents give rise to a macroscopic subject, is that subject one of them, something they make up without being identical to it, or something additional to all of them?

Requirements for the finished object. At most twelve nodes. At least eight leaves, including constitutive micropsychism, emergentist panpsychism with a phenomenal-bonding relation, panprotopsychism, cosmopsychism, neutral monism, brute macro-emergentism, and illusionism or some other deflationary dissolution; each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of labels: first the answer combinations your eight questions permit, then the actual positions located in that space. Mark the combinations nobody occupies, and say of each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate instead of inventing a question that begs the difference between them. Close with a short note on which single node is carrying most of the classificatory work and what the tree would collapse into without it.

No bibliography or reading list, please — I have those. What I want is the carving: which questions actually divide the space, and where the edges of each position lie.
