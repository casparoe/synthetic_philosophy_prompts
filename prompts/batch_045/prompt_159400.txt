hey — I'm doing a behavioural public policy / ethics module this term and we keep going back and forth on nudging, and I realised in the last seminar that I have no idea whether the things I keep saying actually fit together. I'm not after a reading list or citations, I just want someone to poke at the package as a whole. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) nudges are fine, and the reason they're fine is that they don't remove or seriously burden any option — automatic enrolment in a workplace pension with an opt-out form is my paradigm case, nobody is forced into anything. (2) but I'm against the heavy stuff even where the welfare case is stronger: no bans, no mandates, no soda tax big enough to actually bite — the state shouldn't coerce competent adults for their own good. (3) nudges have to be transparent; if the only reason a nudge works is that people don't notice the choice architecture has been arranged, that's out. (4) what I object to in manipulation is deception and withheld information — framing, ordering, salience and defaults are OK by me because they don't lie to anyone and don't take anything off the table. (5) I'm much happier with paternalism about means than about ends: help people get what they already want, don't substitute my view of what's good for them. (6) in principle I prefer the "boost" side of the nudges-vs-boosts debate, teach people the skill and let them decide, but in practice I almost always end up voting for the nudge because boosts are slow and don't scale. (7) I'm totally unbothered by private-sector nudging — supermarkets putting fruit at eye level, apps greying out the button they don't want you to press — and I don't think a supermarket owes me the transparency I want from the state.

Some verdicts I've already committed myself to, which I suspect might not all follow from that list: I'm for presumed consent for organ donation but against fining people who never register a preference; I'm for auto-escalation of pension contributions (your rate ticks up every year unless you opt out) even though that's a lot stickier than a one-off opt-out; I'm for mandatory calorie labelling on restaurant menus but against taxing sugary drinks; I'm against a menu that quietly hides the unhealthy options in a separate folder; and I'm against mandatory cooling-off periods for payday loans even though I think people misjudge those really badly.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension with each other, which only look like they are, and can you rank the real ones by severity instead of flagging all seven as equally broken? For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set — and if there's a single revision that would clean up the most, say which one it is. I genuinely dont know where the crack is; I'd have guessed (7) was the weakest link but I'm not at all confident, and I'd rather hear that (1) or (5) is the problem if that's where it is.
