I'm in the lower sixth, and I'm applying for a six-week summer research bursary that my school awards for one independent project a year. The deliverable is an extended essay of roughly 5,000 words, written mostly on my own with about an hour of supervision a fortnight from a history teacher. The application has to include a proposal of no more than 900 words, read by a panel of two teachers and one older student, and I'd like you to draft that proposal for me. Please write it in British English, in a register formal enough for a panel but still one a fairly bookish seventeen-year-old could plausibly have produced. Keep the proposal itself to about 850–900 words, since that is the limit.

Two constraints first. The panel marks proposals down for citations, and my supervisor will supply the reading later, so please don't include any footnotes, references, page numbers or a reading list: describe positions and arguments by what they claim, not by who holds them. And please don't ask me to look anything up, because I won't have access to a university library over the summer and I don't want the project to rest on facts I can't check. Keep to the kind of general European history that gets taught in school, and avoid statistics, precise dates for minor events, quotations, and anything whose truth would need verifying.

The topic is the standing argument about whether history is driven by individuals or by underlying structural forces, and I want to use exactly one case: whether there would have been a Nazi Germany if Hitler had died before he entered politics. My history teacher's complaint about my first attempt was that it was philosophy dressed up as history and that I needed to be honest about which of the two I was doing. I think what I actually want to do is the second-order question: what each side in that argument is asking, and whether they are asking the same thing. Please make the proposal explicit about which of those it is.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a precise question. It seems to me that the two sides run their counterfactual at different levels of detail, and that a good deal of the disagreement may be about that rather than about history. Someone who says that some such regime was bound to come, given the collapse of the economy, the weakness of the republican constitution, fear of communism, resentment at the peace settlement and the habits of the old elites, is usually describing the outcome quite coarsely: a nationalist, authoritarian, anti-communist, rearming, militarist state. Someone who says that without him there would have been no Nazi Germany often means something much finer-grained: that party, that style and pace of decision-making, that particular war, the Holocaust as it actually happened. So my first thought is that the question needs a ladder of outcome descriptions, ordered from the coarsest to the finest, and that the answer may change as you climb it.

My second thought is that the phrase without Hitler is ambiguous in a separate way. You might mean: without that particular human being, other things broadly as they were. Or you might mean: without anyone occupying the role he occupied, that is, without anyone with a certain bundle of capacities — the oratory, the readiness to break the law and use violence, the tactical skill at building coalitions, the refusal of compromise. Call the first a person-counterfactual and the second a capacity-counterfactual. These can come apart, and I suspect they come apart in an interesting way: it may be that the very structures which create the vacancy also tend, over time, to throw up people with the capacities to fill it, in which case the two counterfactuals converge and the debate dissolves into a question about timing; or it may be that they don't, in which case the capacity-counterfactual is the one doing the real work. There is also, I think, a difference between asking whether the capacities existed somewhere in the population and asking whether they were attached to someone who could actually have reached the top, which may be the more demanding question.

What I'd like the proposal to contain:

1. A precise statement of the question, tighter than mine, with the key terms (structure, outcome-description, capacity, role) defined well enough that a marker could tell whether I'd answered it.
2. Why the standard individual-centred and structure-centred answers, as I've sketched them, don't settle it: what each side would have to establish, where each is strongest, and what each side typically helps itself to without argument. Describe these positions, don't attribute them.
3. The method, week by week across six weeks: how I would actually construct the ladder of outcome descriptions and argue for its ordering; how I would test the person-counterfactual against the capacity-counterfactual using only school-level history and conceptual argument; what the essay would look like section by section.
4. Why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — simply picking a side and defending it, or widening out to several case studies, or trying to settle the empirical causal question. Say plainly what my approach gives up, since the panel will ask.
5. What a good result would look like, including the possibility that the honest conclusion is that the original question is ill-posed and here is the better question; and, separately, what would count as the project failing. I'd rather have the real failure conditions than a rhetorical gesture at them: for instance, what if the ordering of the ladder is arbitrary and I can pick whichever rung gives me the answer I want, or what if the two counterfactuals collapse into each other, or what if the remaining disagreement turns out to be purely empirical and therefore beyond what this project can settle.
6. A short paragraph on scope: what I would have to cut to fit six weeks and 5,000 words, and the most likely way for me to run out of time.
7. One paragraph, outside the 900 words if you prefer, identifying the weakest point in the proposal as you've written it — where a sceptical teacher would push hardest — because I'd rather meet it in the interview than discover it there.
