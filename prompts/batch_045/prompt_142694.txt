You are a professional philosopher of mind who has taught the qualia material enough times to know exactly where people stall out: not on the arguments, but on not being able to say what, exactly, they are committed to. I run a small graduate reading group, and last week we spent most of a session on the Chase-and-Sanborn coffee-taster material in Dennett's "Quining Qualia" — the pair of tasters who can't agree whether it is the coffee's taste or merely their reaction to the taste that has drifted over the years — and discovered that the room disagreed in at least four different ways while using the same vocabulary. So I want a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey of the literature: before you start naming views, take a pass at brainstorming the logical space itself — list the independent claims about conscious experience that someone could in principle combine (about whether experience has properties that outrun what a state does or represents, whether introspection reveals the nature of those properties or only that we have them, whether the posit does any explanatory work or is a placeholder, whether experience could be absent or inverted with everything functional held fixed, and so on) — and only afterward locate actual positions in that space. Then give me the tree I can hand out: roughly ten to twelve branch nodes and no more than nine leaves, starting from the thin, shared commitment that ordinary talk about how things seem or feel to a subject is in good standing, and ending at a named position. Every node must be phrased as a concrete case or a substantive claim that a student can answer without knowing which view it leads to — absolutely no "do you think qualia are intrinsic?"-style nodes, and no question that just asks for a label. Please use at least these four cases as branch points: the stepwise (soritical) inversion case, in which a subject is walked through several hundred micro-steps of gradual spectral inversion, each step indiscriminable from the last, so that either nothing changes at any step or something changes with no fact of the matter where; the classic absent-qualia case, where a functional duplicate has no experience at all; a case in which the property is held fixed in itself but its relations to everything else — memory, discrimination, verbal report, downstream preference — are swapped, so that the subject's dispositions change while the property is stipulated not to; and a case where two subjects are stipulated to have identical experiences while disagreeing exhaustively and permanently about what they are like, along with whatever cases you think the space actually requires. I especially want the tree to separate three destinations that my group kept collapsing into one: the Dennett-style view that the traditional posit is so much conceptual lumber and should be dropped in favor of relational, partly extrinsic facts about what a subject is disposed to say and do; the illusionist view that there really is a "what it's like" that introspection seems to reveal, but it is an introspective illusion produced by non-phenomenal quasi-phenomenal states; and the quietist or verbal-dispute view that the fighting is over which concept the word is doing duty for. I also want Russellian-monist and primitivist-realist destinations to come apart from each other, and I want a leaf for someone who accepts the possibility of absent qualia but denies inverted qualia (or vice versa), if the space allows it.

Format it for a one-page handout: a numbered decision tree in plain text or ASCII, each branch labeled with the claim that splits it from its siblings, each leaf carrying the name of the position, one sentence on what it is committed to, and one sentence on its most awkward or least comfortable commitment. After the tree, add three short things. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say why — is the distinction only in rhetorical emphasis, in what each camp thinks still needs explaining, or in a background commitment the tree never asked about? Second, mark any combination of answers the logical space permits but that nobody actually occupies, and diagnose each: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored? Third, and most important, tell me where your own tree is least trustworthy: name the single branch you would expect the profession to be most evenly split on, say which node's phrasing is most likely to be doing argumentative work by presupposing a contested claim, and state plainly what argument or case would lead you to redraw the tree — i.e., what would change your mind about which distinctions are the load-bearing ones. Work entirely from the concepts and cases; no citations, no page references, no further-reading list, and no appeal to how some paper or another phrases things, since my group has no library access and I want the instrument to stand on its own. Take your time and think it through carefully before writing.
