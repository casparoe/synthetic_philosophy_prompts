I've been reporting a piece on why almost nothing in real policy ever seems to count as a genuine Pareto improvement, and I keep getting pulled toward a position I can't quite state cleanly — so before an economist friend of mine picks it apart on his podcast, I'd like a consistency check. For context: I've been talking to a bunch of market-design economists who throw around the phrase "safe Pareto improvement" as if everyone knows what it means, and my working understanding is: a reform that is still a Pareto improvement even under the most pessimistic assumption about the private valuations you can't observe. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Pareto or bust — no Kaldor–Hicks, no interpersonal utility comparisons, no social welfare function; if a change leaves anyone worse off, it isn't an improvement. (2) The standard complaint that the criterion is inert in practice is basically right about actual policy, and my response is to redesign policies until they compensate rather than weaken the test — I'm fine with the implication that most reforms shouldn't happen. (3) That's exactly why I like the safe-Pareto-improvement move: when you don't know people's private valuations, build the reform so it's still an improvement under the worst case — over-compensate by construction, leave people an opt-out, that sort of thing. (4) The baseline should be the actual status quo, existing legal entitlements and all, because that's what people have organised their lives and expectations around; I'm suspicious of hypothetical baselines, they let you pick you conclusion. (5) Methodologically I want to stay inside revealed preference — no happiness surveys, no cardinally measured utility, no one's subjective well-being scale. (6) My favorite example of (3) is the self-assessed property tax: you declare a price at which you'd hand the asset over, you're taxed on that number, and anyone can take it at that number, so nobody is ever forced to give up something for less than they themselves said it was worth. That looks safe by construction to me. (7) And I also think the behavioural stuff is real — loss aversion, the endowment effect, status quo bias, the gap between what people will pay for a mug and what they'll sell it for.

Can you audit the whole set? I honestly can't tell where the trouble is, and I'd rather not find out on tape. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — my guess is that (1) and (3) look more in conflict than they really are, but I suspect something worse is going on elsewhere, possibly with (6). Rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging them all as equal, say for each one what would have to give and what that would cost me, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the mess at once.
