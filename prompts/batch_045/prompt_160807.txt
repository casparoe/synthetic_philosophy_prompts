My father died in October, and I am the one clearing his study. That has turned a piece of intellectual furniture into a practical question: where, exactly, does a message's meaning live? I'd been reading Hofstadter's distinction in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* between a message's frame message, its outer message, and its inner message that winter for exactly this reason — I have to decide what to keep, what to give to the local historical society, and what to throw out, and my verdicts on whether a thing still carries a message don't seem to hang together.

A postcard he wrote me in plain English: unproblematic, meaning in the object and readable.

Roughly four hundred index cards in a shorthand of his own invention; a single card carries a partial key, a dozen-odd symbols matched to letters. I want to say there is a real message here, that its meaning is genuinely in the cards whether or not anyone ever cracks them, and that destroying them would destroy more than paper.

One card of columns of digits. Set next to different notebooks of his, they read as shortwave frequencies, as dates, or as chess notation. I find myself saying the frame fixes which message this is — yet I also want to insist that reading them as a shopping list is simply wrong.

A Polaroid of a rock face on the property, which he captioned he's watching. I say the rock carries no message, but the photograph and caption together do: here an act of framing seems to create a message that nobody put into the rock.

Forty years of daily weather readings in identical ledgers. I don't want to call these a message from him, though a meteorologist could still use them — but one entry ringed in pencil with look at this!! is a message. A pencil ring seems to be doing all the work.

A card in a sealed envelope reading the key is in the other box; the other box went to the dump in 1998. I want to say this was a message and now isn't, although nothing about the card itself has changed.

My rule of thumb has been that a thing carries a message when its structure does work that the story of how it was produced doesn't already do: the shorthand isn't explained by anything about how the cards got written, whereas the weather ledgers are fully explained by the weather plus a forty-year habit. But I can't square the second case with the third and fourth — in one I want meaning sealed inside the object with no decoder in sight, and in the others I want it supplied by a frame or even by a receiver's act.

Is there a single account of where meaning is located that delivers all six verdicts, or nearly all? State it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me what it predicts about the thing I keep going back to: a locked cash box for which no key exists, and whether drilling it open could destroy something. Use the frame/outer/inner vocabulary where it helps, but don't just restate Hofstadter at me. And be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what I would have to give up.
