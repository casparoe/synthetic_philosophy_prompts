QUESTION (from the problem set)

Consider three accounts of what makes a token a token of the letter a:

(G) Geometric/feature: a token is an a in virtue of having certain shape features (or a weighted majority of them) — a closed bowl, a stem on the right, an open aperture, and so on.

(H) Causal-historical: a token is an a in virtue of standing at the end of an appropriate chain of copying that goes back to earlier a's.

(P) Perceptual-functional: a token is an a in virtue of being perceived and used as an a by a fluent reading community, within a working writing system.

Using these three accounts, discuss the following cases and say which account you favour.

1. The single-storey a found in many geometric sans faces and in italic handwriting, versus the ordinary double-storey a. Same letter, or two letters?
2. A hypothetical geometric sans, Kestrel Sans, in which the b glyph is the exact 180-degree rotation of the q glyph, the p is the exact 180-degree rotation of the d, and the n the exact 180-degree rotation of the u. Does Kestrel Sans contain fewer distinct letterforms than a normal font? Does it contain fewer letters?
3. The Chalk River script: twenty-six forms invented from scratch for a fictional language by a designer who worked only from drawings of local plants and never looked at the Roman alphabet. Shown English words written in it, English readers read them aloud correctly about 90 percent of the time. Are those forms instances of the Roman letters?

STUDENT ANSWER

All three accounts can agree on one thing: there is no list of intrinsic shape features that every a has. Name any feature — a closed bowl, a stem on the right, an open aperture, a given number of terminals — and it can be removed while the result is still perfectly recognisable as an a. So (G) in its naive form is out, and the real question is what replaces it.

Case 1. The single-storey and double-storey forms are the same letter. (H) handles this easily: both descend, through long chains of copying, from earlier a's, so both are a's, and their looking different is just historical drift. (P) handles it too, and better: fluent readers read both as a and use them interchangeably, which makes them two allographs of one letter rather than two letters.

Case 2. Kestrel Sans's b and q are literally the same shape, since rotating a shape does not change which shape it is — it only changes which way it is pointing. (G) is therefore committed to saying that b and q are the same letter, which is absurd, and on the same reasoning every font would turn out to have about eighteen letters. So (G) collapses entirely and the real choice is between (H) and (P). (H) does fine here: the b and the q have separate copying histories, so they are different letters even though they share a shape.

Case 3. (H) says the Chalk River forms are not Roman letters, since their designer never intended to produce an a. But notice that this shows (H) is really just the intentionalist view in disguise: what does the work is the intention, and the chains-of-copying talk is decoration, because the copier has to intend to reproduce the letter he sees. On (P), by contrast, the Chalk River forms are Roman letters: a reading community perceives them as such and uses them to read, and that is all (P) asks for.

I favour (P). Being an a is not the same as being readable as an a — that distinction matters — but on (P) letter identity is fixed by what a reading community actually does with a form, and that is what lets us say both that the Chalk River forms are a's and that a badly written token nobody can decipher is not one.
